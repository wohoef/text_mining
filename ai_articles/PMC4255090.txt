To mitigate the toxic side effects of anthracycline antibiotics, a strategy involves conjugating anti-neoplastic agents with human proteins. We have created a novel doxorubicin-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate designed to target cancer drugs specifically to malignant cells, which often exhibit different protein expression levels compared to normal cells. Previous research indicated that the conjugate's distribution within cells is influenced by the interplay of influx and efflux mechanisms. This study aimed to determine if P-glycoprotein (P-gp) expression impacts how DOX-TRF accumulates in cells and its resulting cytotoxicity. Experiments were conducted using human erythromyeloblastoid cells, specifically a P-gp over-expressing line (K562/DOX) and its drug-sensitive parent line (K562). Assessments using MTT cytotoxicity assays, flow cytometry, fluorescence microscopy, and RT-PCR demonstrated that the DOX-TRF conjugate exhibited greater cytotoxic potential than free doxorubicin. These findings suggest that the DOX-TRF conjugate is less reliant on P-gp for its action than free doxorubicin, potentially making it a valuable tool for enhancing treatment effectiveness in drug-resistant human tumors.

Anthracyclines, a significant group of anti-neoplastic agents, demonstrate efficacy against both hematologic and solid tumors. Nevertheless, their clinical utility is frequently hampered by dose-limiting cardiac toxicity and myelosuppression. Doxorubicin (DOX), an anthracycline antibiotic, induces cancer cell apoptosis through a multifaceted mechanism involving DNA intercalation, reactive oxygen species generation, topoisomerase II inhibition, and DNA damage. Effective chemotherapy with DOX necessitates delivering adequate drug quantities to neoplastic cells while minimizing exposure to healthy tissues. Consequently, the concept of attaching anthracycline antibiotics to polymers or tumor-associated antigens has been explored to achieve more selective drug delivery. DOX has been incorporated into various delivery systems, including polymeric hydrogels, liposomes, dendrimers, nanoparticles, and antibodies. Human serum proteins, particularly serum albumin and transferrin (TRF), have emerged as promising candidates for drug delivery in cancer chemotherapy. These proteins offer advantages such as biodegradability, low toxicity, and non-immunogenicity, alongside a tendency for preferential uptake in tumor tissues. Furthermore, protein conjugates can accumulate in solid tumors due to their often-enhanced microvasculature, a phenomenon known as the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect.
Transferrin (TRF) has demonstrated potential for delivering anticancer drugs to malignant cells with overexpressed TRF receptors. DOX can be covalently linked to TRF, forming a DOX-TRF conjugate. This linkage, created via a Schiff base using glutaraldehyde, remains stable at physiological pH 7.4 and is unaffected by human enzymes. Within cells, DOX is rapidly released from the conjugate in the acidic environment of endosomes or lysosomes (pH 5).
The emergence of drug-resistant cancer cells, often due to the overexpression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) proteins, highlights the critical need for targeted anticancer drug delivery. A key player in this resistance mechanism is P-glycoprotein (P-gp), a 170-kDa membrane transporter glycoprotein encoded by the ABCB1 gene. P-gp expression is a significant contributor to chemotherapy resistance, particularly in breast cancer, and is also found in other cancer cells and normal tissues, where it plays a role in transporting various substances. When P-gp is overexpressed, it actively pumps anticancer agents out of cells, leading to lower intracellular drug concentrations and increased resistance, especially to drugs that are P-gp substrates. Free doxorubicin (DOX) is among the more than 100 compounds identified as P-gp substrates.
This study investigated how the doxorubicin-transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) affects the P-glycoprotein (P-gp) transport system and its relationship with the conjugate's cytotoxicity. Experiments were conducted on doxorubicin-sensitive (K562) and resistant (K562/DOX) leukemia cells, both with and without the P-gp inhibitor Cyclosporin A (CsA). The findings indicated varying sensitivities to the DOX-TRF conjugate between these cell lines. Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy revealed changes in Rhodamine 123 (R123) accumulation. Furthermore, analyses of ABCB1 gene expression at both mRNA (RT-PCR) and protein (MDR-1 shift assay) levels demonstrated distinct time- and concentration-dependent responses to free doxorubicin (DOX) and DOX-TRF.
Doxorubicin (DOX) was sourced from Sequoia Research Products in the UK. RPMI 1640 bicarbonate came from Cambrex in Switzerland, while fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, and streptomycin were acquired from PAA in Scotland. Sigma (Germany) provided R123, Cyclosporin A, and materials for the conjugation process. The DOX-TRF conjugate was synthesized using a modified procedure by Berczi et al., with its integrity confirmed by mass spectrometry. Additional chemicals and solvents were of high analytical grade, obtained from Sigma or POCH S.A. in Poland.
Human erythromyeloblastoid leukemia cell lines, K562 (DOX-sensitive) and K562/DOX (DOX-resistant), were generously provided by Prof. J. Robert of the Institute Bergonie, France. These cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 bicarbonate medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 units/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml), under a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C. The K562/DOX cells were maintained in the same medium, with the addition of 0.02 μM DOX. For experimental procedures, cells were plated in either 96-well plates (10,000 cells/well) for cytotoxicity and related assays, or 6-well plates (100,000 cells/well) for flow cytometry, real-time PCR, and MDR-1 protein shift assays. Prior to treatment, cells were allowed to grow for 24 hours, and cultures were regularly checked for Mycoplasma contamination.
Cell growth inhibition by the DOX-TRF conjugate and free doxorubicin (DOX) was assessed in K562 and K562/DOX cells using the MTT assay. Cells, plated at 1x10^4 density, were exposed to varying concentrations of either the DOX-TRF conjugate or free DOX for 72 hours at 37 °C. Following this incubation period, MTT reagent was added to each well. Viable cells produced dark formazan crystals, which were then solubilized using 30 mM HCl in 2-propanol. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm to determine the concentration of drug required for 50% growth inhibition (IC50), derived from dose-response curves. To investigate the impact of Cyclosporin A (CsA) on the cytotoxicity of both free DOX and the DOX-TRF conjugate, cells were pre-incubated with 30 μM of this P-gp inhibitor for 30 minutes. The chosen CsA concentration was validated by assessing its toxicity on cell viability.
To evaluate P-glycoprotein (P-gp) activity as a transmembrane transporter, Rhodamine 123 (R123), a fluorescent probe whose intracellular concentration is influenced by this efflux protein, was employed. Experiments commenced with cells, plated at a density of 1x10^6 cells/ml, being pre-incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C in culture medium, either without or with 30 μM Cyclosporin A (CsA). Following this, free doxorubicin (DOX) or the DOX-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate, at their respective IC50 concentrations, were added, and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 hours. The culture medium was then removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS before being detached from the culture dishes. After centrifugation at 300×g for 10 minutes at 4°C, the cells were re-suspended in ice-cold PBS. R123 was then added to all samples at a final concentration of 0.1 M. Cellular R123 uptake was monitored for 20-minute intervals using a flow cytometer. Fluorescence microscopy was subsequently utilized to assess the fluorescence of the analyzed samples, with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 497 nm and 520 nm, respectively.
To evaluate the impact of free doxorubicin (DOX) and the DOX-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate on P-glycoprotein (P-gp) expression, we analyzed ABCB1 transcript levels. Cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 1x105 cells per well and treated with an IC50 concentration of the compounds for 48 hours. Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent, and its concentration was determined with a UV spectrophotometer. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from equal RNA amounts using a Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. Real-time PCR was performed using specific primers for ABCB1, along with primers for the housekeeping genes hydroxymethylbilane synthase (HMBS) and hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (HPRT1). Gene expression levels were measured using SYBR Green I Master Mix and a LightCycler 480. The threshold cycle numbers (Ct) were then used to calculate the expression of each target gene, normalized against the housekeeping genes.
To assess how P-glycoprotein (P-gp) functions when transporting MDR1 substrates into K562 and K562/DOX cells, a MDR Shift assay was employed. This method utilized an R-phycoerythrin (R-PE)-conjugated mouse anti-human P-gp monoclonal antibody (UIC2). The assay determined the extent to which DOX or DOX-TRF acted as MDR1 substrates for P-gp. Since UIC2 binding is enhanced by MDR1 substrates, this assay provided a sensitive and specific way to detect P-gp expression.
Following 48 hours of incubation with free DOX or the DOX-TRF conjugate, cells were gathered and washed. They were then incubated with an R-PE conjugated anti-human P-gp monoclonal antibody (UIC2 PE) for 15 minutes at 37 °C. After a further 15-minute incubation at 4 °C, cells were washed again and kept on ice. Vinblastine-treated cells served as a positive control. Measurements were conducted using a flow cytometer's FL2 channel.
Statistical analysis involved expressing data as means ± S.D. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while Levene's test evaluated variance homogeneity. Multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to determine statistically significant differences between variables, with Tukey's test for post-hoc analyses. All statistical calculations were performed using STATISTICA version 11, with a p-value of <0.05 considered significant.
To evaluate the impact of free doxorubicin (DOX) and its transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) on both drug-sensitive and drug-resistant K562 cells, a MTT assay was performed. The results, depicted in Figure 1, demonstrated a concentration-dependent and statistically significant decrease in formazan-producing cells when exposed to either DOX or DOX-TRF. Notably, treatment with DOX-TRF led to a more pronounced reduction in the survival of both K562 and K562/DOX cells compared to treatment with free DOX. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50), representing the drug concentration required for 50% cell survival after 72 hours of incubation, were determined. For K562 cells, the IC50 values were 263.0 ± 6.2 nM for DOX and 70.3 ± 4.6 nM for DOX-TRF. In the case of K562/DOX cells, the IC50 was 2814.0 ± 33.4 nM for DOX and 278.4 ± 1.4 nM for DOX-TRF. Further experiments assessed cell viability after a 72-hour exposure to Cyclosporin A (CsA), a P-glycoprotein (P-gp) inhibitor, across a concentration range of 0.5–50 μM. It was observed that CsA at various concentrations did not significantly affect cell survival, with viability remaining above 90% (data not shown). Consequently, a CsA concentration of 30 μM was selected for subsequent experiments, aligning with established literature values. When cells were pre-incubated with this 30 μM CsA concentration and subsequently treated with either free DOX or DOX-TRF, an enhanced cytotoxicity was observed for both agents (Figure 1). In K562 and K562/DOX cells treated with free DOX, CsA reduced cell survival by approximately 25%. A similar reduction in survival was noted in cells treated with DOX-TRF, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two treatment groups in the presence of CsA.
To investigate how free doxorubicin (DOX) and the doxorubicin-transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) affect P-glycoprotein (P-gp) activity, we measured the intracellular accumulation of Rhodamine 123 (R123). R123 is a known substrate for P-gp, and its cellular uptake is inversely related to P-gp activity. Our experimental approach involved comparing the R123 accumulation in cells treated with free DOX or DOX-TRF to that of positive control cells pre-treated with Cyclosporin A (CsA), a P-gp inhibitor. The data, visualized in Figure 2a, revealed distinct patterns of R123 accumulation. Fluorescence ratios were calculated for each experimental condition. Notably, K562/DOX cells, which are resistant to DOX, exhibited a significantly higher accumulation of R123 compared to their sensitive counterparts, K562 cells, even in the absence of drug treatment (Figure 2b). This observation underscores the elevated P-gp activity in the resistant cell line. When examining the impact of the drugs, we found that free DOX markedly increased R123 cellular influx in K562/DOX cells, with an influx factor of 1.72 ± 0.14. In contrast, the DOX-TRF conjugate resulted in a less pronounced increase, with an influx factor of 1.29 ± 0.13. This suggests that free DOX has a greater capacity to either inhibit R123 efflux or enhance its influx, thereby increasing intracellular R123 levels more effectively than the conjugate. Further analysis of P-gp activity in the DOX-sensitive K562 cells, using flow cytometry, confirmed that both free DOX and DOX-TRF influence P-gp functionality. In these sensitive cells, free DOX increased R123 accumulation by a factor of 1.38, while DOX-TRF increased it by a factor of 1.16, relative to control samples without drug treatment or CsA pre-incubation. These findings from flow cytometry were corroborated by fluorescence microscopy observations (Figure 3). The microscopy images showed maximal R123 accumulation in K562/DOX cells that had been pre-treated with CsA, consistent with P-gp inhibition. However, when comparing cells treated with free DOX versus DOX-TRF, a higher fluorescence intensity, indicative of greater R123 accumulation, was observed in cells treated with the free drug. This observation aligns with the flow cytometry data, reinforcing the conclusion that free DOX has a more significant impact on R123 intracellular levels compared to the DOX-TRF conjugate.
To investigate how free doxorubicin (DOX) and the DOX-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate influence P-glycoprotein (P-gp) expression, we quantified ABCB1 transcript levels in both the drug-sensitive K562 cell line and its resistant counterpart, K562/DOX. Using RT-PCR, we confirmed that untreated K562/DOX cells exhibited a higher baseline mRNA expression of ABCB1 (318.9) compared to K562 cells (146.8). Treatment with free DOX resulted in a statistically significant increase in ABCB1 transcript levels compared to treatment with DOX-TRF. Specifically, free DOX boosted ABCB1 transcription by 6.2-fold in K562 cells and 1.7-fold in K562/DOX cells. In contrast, DOX-TRF induced more modest increases of 1.6-fold and 1.3-fold in K562 and K562/DOX cells, respectively. Furthermore, pre-incubating cells with Cyclosporin A (CsA), a P-gp inhibitor, led to a reduction in ABCB1 transcript levels in both cell types, regardless of whether they were treated with free DOX or DOX-TRF. These findings, illustrated in Figure 4, highlight the differential impact of free DOX and its conjugate on P-gp gene expression.
Figure 5 illustrates changes in P-glycoprotein (P-gp) expression following treatment with either free doxorubicin (DOX) or the DOX-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate. Analysis using the monoclonal antibody UIC2 revealed that the K562/DOX cell line, which is resistant to DOX, exhibited a P-gp level three times higher (7.2%) than the parental K562 cell line (2.4%). When these cells were exposed to the tested compounds, the most substantial alterations in P-gp expression were observed in K562/DOX cells treated with free DOX, showing a 30% increase compared to untreated controls. The DOX-TRF conjugate also induced an increase in P-gp expression in both cell lines, but to a significantly lesser degree than free DOX. Furthermore, pre-treatment with Cyclosporin A (CsA) indicated that free DOX induced greater changes in P-gp expression compared to the DOX-TRF conjugate. The use of vinblastine as a positive control corroborated these findings, confirming a 2.6-fold higher P-gp expression in the DOX-resistant K562 cell line relative to the parental line.
This study aimed to determine if a doxorubicin-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate could effectively bypass P-glycoprotein (P-gp) mediated drug resistance, thereby enhancing anticancer therapy efficacy. To achieve this, we compared the activity, transcription, and expression of P-gp in erythromyeloblastoid cells exposed to either free doxorubicin (DOX) or the DOX-TRF conjugate. These investigations into various P-gp parameters were conducted concurrently with assessments of the cytotoxic effects of both anticancer agents. Furthermore, the cell-killing capabilities of free DOX and the DOX-TRF conjugate were evaluated in combination with Cyclosporin A (CsA), a known P-gp inhibitor. Our findings align with previous research by Lo et al. [28], which demonstrated that certain anthracyclines and their derivatives, such as aclarubicin, can influence P-gp function in non-small cell lung cancer cell lines (A549). We also observed that combining free DOX with CsA yielded greater cytotoxicity in the studied leukemia cells compared to using either agent alone. Notably, the DOX-TRF conjugate exhibited a more significant increase in cytotoxicity in the P-gp over-expressing K562/DOX cells than in the DOX-sensitive K562 cells. Existing literature highlights that conjugating anticancer drugs with nano-sized delivery systems, including dendrimers, nanoparticles, micelles [29–31], and plasma proteins, can be a viable strategy. These carriers, when linked with hydrophobic drugs, have the potential to increase cumulative drug dosage and improve accumulation within the tumor microenvironment over time [32, 33]. More recently, a transferrin-linked liposomal DOX formulation containing verapamil (VER), termed Tf-L-DOX/VER, was developed and demonstrated the ability to overcome multi-drug resistance (MDR) [34]. Targeted liposomal delivery has also been shown to enhance tumor cell-selective cytotoxicity and facilitate P-gp bypass and MDR reversal. Wu et al. [34] further supported this by confirming that TRF-targeted liposomal delivery of co-encapsulated DOX and VER (L-DOX/VER) represents an effective approach for selectively targeting and reversing drug resistance in K562 cells. Their results indicated that Tf-L-DOX/VER possessed an approximately five-fold lower IC50 value compared to non-targeted L-DOX/VER. To investigate the hypothesis that the enhanced toxicity of the DOX-TRF conjugate stems from reduced P-gp mediated efflux, we utilized Rhodamine 123 (R123), a known P-gp substrate. We assessed the impact of both free DOX and the DOX-TRF conjugate on R123's intracellular transport. Our experiments revealed an increase in P-gp activity in both sensitive and resistant K562 cells when treated with free DOX. In contrast, our work suggests that the DOX-TRF conjugate does not significantly modulate P-gp functionality, particularly in K562/DOX cells. These flow cytometry findings were corroborated by subsequent fluorescence microscopy observations of R123 accumulation. Specifically, after a 20-minute incubation with R123, cells treated with the DOX-TRF conjugate displayed higher fluorescence intensity compared to those treated with free DOX.
The influence of human plasma protein-conjugated drugs on P-glycoprotein (P-gp) activity remains a subject of debate. While the DOX-TRF conjugate's anti-tumor efficacy in drug-resistant cells has been linked to direct P-gp binding, our findings suggest a different mechanism. P-gp's heightened activity is associated with conformational changes during the transport of MDR1 substrates. Our MDR1 shift assay demonstrated that free DOX significantly increased UIC2 antibody binding, indicating substantial conformational changes in P-gp. In contrast, the DOX-TRF conjugate induced less pronounced changes. Pre-treatment with Cyclosporin A (CsA) further supported P-gp's role in the efflux of both free DOX and DOX-TRF, albeit with varying kinetics and affinities. These observations aligned with our real-time PCR findings on ABCB1 transcript levels. Previous research has established P-gp's involvement in mediating multi-drug resistance in chronic erythromyeloblastoid leukemia cells. Our study revealed that ABCB1 gene transcription was approximately threefold higher in K562/DOX cells compared to the sensitive K562 parental line. Notably, free DOX treatment led to a more substantial increase in ABCB1 gene transcription than the DOX-TRF conjugate. In P-gp over-expressing K562 cells, the increase in ABCB1 transcription induced by DOX-TRF was not statistically significant.
Our findings demonstrate a link between the enhanced cytotoxicity of the DOX-TRF conjugate and its impact on P-gp function and expression. Unlike the DOX-TRF conjugate, free DOX results in elevated P-gp expression. The conjugate's greater cytotoxicity likely stems from its reduced affinity for P-gp. Consequently, we propose that the DOX-TRF conjugate's prolonged cellular accumulation and higher intracellular concentration do not trigger increased ABCB1 gene expression or enhanced P-gp-mediated efflux.
