This study investigated the anatomical suitability of different screw trajectories for treating atlanto-axial instability (AAI), a condition characterized by instability or subluxation of the atlantoaxial joint, particularly in patients with a narrow C1 posterior arch. To achieve this, researchers analyzed 3D-CT scans from 60 patients, acquired using an Aquilion Multi 64 helical CT system. Two distinct C1 pedicle screw trajectories, L and M, were defined and measured from these CT images. Key anatomical features were assessed for both trajectories, including medullary cavity width, posterior arch height, and pedicle length. The findings indicated that trajectory L offered a wider medullary cavity compared to trajectory M; specifically, only 2.5% of pedicles along trajectory L had a medullary cavity width less than 3.5 mm, versus 12.5% for trajectory M. Furthermore, trajectory L demonstrated a significantly greater posterior arch height than trajectory M. A posterior arch height below 3.5 mm was observed in a substantial 74.2% of pedicles for trajectory M, but only 16.7% for trajectory L. Trajectory L also featured a significantly longer pedicle length. The average trajectory angles were 9.08° ± 4.66° for trajectory L and 14.53° ± 4.03° for trajectory M. Notably, the optimal entry point for trajectory L was positioned 5.4 mm medial to that of trajectory M. The study concludes that a C1 pedicle screw trajectory with lateral inclination, such as trajectory L, shows promise for favorable outcomes in AAI treatment.

The atlanto-axial joint, also known as the atlanto-axial junction, is located in the upper neck, connecting the first cervical vertebra (C1), or atlas, and the second cervical vertebra (C2), or axis [1]. When abnormal movement occurs at this joint, due to either bony or ligamentous issues, it results in a condition called atlanto-axial instability (AAI). While AAI can be congenital, in adults it often arises from trauma or inflammatory conditions like rheumatoid arthritis [2]. Posterior atlantoaxial fixation is recognized as an effective treatment for AAI [3]. Over recent years, various posterior screw fixation methods have been developed for AAI, including bilateral C1 lateral mass and C2 pedicle screw fixation, posterior C1-2 transarticular screw fixation, and posterior atlantoaxial pedicle screw fixation [[4], [5], [6]]. The concept of C1 pedicle screw placement was initially proposed by Resnick and Benzel [7]. This technique involves inserting a screw into the lateral mass through the posterior arch, or "pedicle" [8]. A key advantage of this starting point is that it bypasses the extensive venous plexus surrounding the C2 nerve root beneath the C1 posterior arch [9]. The C1 pedicle screw technique has gained popularity for achieving rigid and stable C1 fixation due to several benefits: it offers a high fusion rate with fewer complications, reduces bleeding from the venous plexus and irritation of the C2 nerve root, and provides greater cortical purchase and a longer screw trajectory compared to the atlas lateral mass screw technique, making it particularly suitable for short-segment fixation [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. However, the C1 pedicle screw technique does have limitations. A primary concern is the thin outer bone of the C1 posterior arch, which can lead to the screw or drill slipping during surgery and potentially damaging the vertebral artery. To mitigate this risk in patients with a C1 posterior arch measuring 4 mm or less, some surgeons opt for alternative techniques [12]. Yet, these alternatives may not always provide reliable biomechanical fixation. To address this challenge, we developed "trajectory L," characterized by an entry point closer to the posterior tubercle and a lateral inclination angle. This trajectory is designed for patients with an unusually thin outer bone in the C1 posterior arch. Our study involved morphological measurements to assess the anatomical feasibility of trajectory L and to identify an optimal screw trajectory for treating AAI in patients with a narrow C1 posterior arch.
This study included sixty patients (38 males, 22 females; mean age 54.1 years, range 30–81) whose cervical 3D-CT scans were randomly selected from the hospital's PACS between 2013 and 2015. Excluded were individuals under 18 or those with conditions like fractures, tumors, infections, rheumatoid diseases, or malformations impacting atlas bone morphology.
CT examinations and measurements were conducted using an Aquilion Multi 64 helical CT scanner (Toshiba Tungaloy Co. Ltd., Nirasaki, Japan). Patients were placed supine with their cervical spine in a neutral position. The scanning parameters included a thickness of 1 mm, a pitch of 0.938, and a distance of 0.5 mm. Tube tension was set at 120 kV, with an electric current of 250 mA. The window width was 1000 and the window level 300. Further CT scanning specifics involved 120 kV, 54 mA, a 10 × 10 cm field of view, a 512 × 512 matrix, and a table speed of 1 mm/0.5 s.
CT images revealed two distinct screw trajectories, L and M, for each C1 pedicle. Trajectory L's central axis, as seen on the C1 axial image, ran parallel to the medial tangent of the transverse foramen and the lateral tangent of the vertebral canal. In contrast, trajectory M's central axis, also on the C1 axial image, was parallel to the lateral tangent of the vertebral canal and the odontoid foramen. The posterior arch height was determined from C1 coronal images. All image analysis was conducted using the Picture Archiving Communication System (PACS).
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA), which also included a variance component analysis. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for measurements and as percentages for enumeration data. To compare C1 pedicle characteristics—specifically, medullary cavity width, external pedicle wall, posterior arch height, pedicle length, trajectory angle, and the distance between the posterior tubercle and optimal entry point—between different trajectories, a paired t-test was employed. Differences between male and female participants were assessed using an independent samples t-test. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Measurements from sixty patients, detailed in Table 1, compared C1 pedicle screw insertion parameters for trajectories L and M. A significant difference was observed in several key metrics. The medullary cavity width, for instance, was notably narrower along trajectory M compared to trajectory L (p < 0.001). Specifically, 12.5% of pedicles following trajectory M had a medullary cavity width under 3.5 mm, whereas only 2.5% of those along trajectory L fell below this threshold. Despite all external pedicle wall widths exceeding 3.5 mm, no statistically significant difference was found between the two trajectories in this regard (p > 0.050). Furthermore, trajectory L demonstrated a significantly greater posterior arch height than trajectory M (p < 0.001). A posterior arch height less than 3.5 mm was identified in a substantial 74.2% of pedicles along trajectory M, contrasting sharply with only 16.7% for trajectory L. The pedicle length was also significantly longer for trajectory L compared to trajectory M (p < 0.001). Regarding trajectory angles, C1 pedicles averaged 9.08° ± 4.66° for trajectory L and 14.53° ± 4.03° for trajectory M. The optimal entry point for trajectory L was determined to be 5.4 mm medial to that of trajectory M, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.
The vertebral arch is composed of the pedicles and laminae. Each pedicle is a short, thick projection extending backward from the posterolateral upper surface of the vertebral body [15]. From each pedicle, a lamina projects backward and medially, completing the vertebral arch and forming the posterior boundary of the vertebral foramen [16]. Anatomically and biomechanically, the C1 posterior arch and lateral mass connection resembles other vertebral pedicles [12]. The C1 pedicle screw technique offers several benefits over direct lateral mass screw insertion, such as enhanced fixation strength, reduced irritation of the C2 nerve root and associated occipital neuralgia, and less bleeding from the paravertebral venous plexus [[17], [18]]. Consequently, the C1 posterior arch screw technique is gaining popularity for achieving rigid C1 stabilization, particularly in short-segment cervical fixation [[19], [20]].
When the C1 posterior arch has a thin outer bone, surgeons face a challenge: the drill or screw can slip during entry point creation, potentially damaging the vertebral artery. Tan et al. [12] addressed this by exposing the C1 "pedicle" through the removal of approximately 4 mm of the posterior arch's outer bone. This procedure aims to increase the height of the screw entry point along the C1 posterior arch screw trajectory. However, removing cortical bone from the C1 posterior arch might compromise the screw's pull-out strength. Another approach, the notching technique, was proposed by Lee Xin et al. [10]. This involves inserting a screw into a narrow posterior arch by notching its inferior aspect, positioning the screw partially outside the inferior wall and the posterior arch itself. Conceptually, this technique carries risks similar to the lateral mass screw technique, such as potential injury to the C2 nerve root and venous plexus, particularly during dissection of the C1 posterior arch's inferior margin. Furthermore, penetrating the inferior cortex of the posterior arch through notching could negatively impact the screw's pull-out strength. In contrast to these methods, our study's measurements revealed that the posterior arch heights along trajectory L averaged 5.47 ± 1.84 mm, a measurement significantly greater than that observed for trajectory M. Our data also indicated that 16.7% of the pedicles measured less than 3.5 mm, a finding consistent with other research [21].
Numerous studies have indicated that the C1 pedicle isthmus possesses a mediolateral diameter sufficient for the insertion of a 3.5-mm screw [[9], [18]]. Our investigation found that the external pedicle wall width for trajectory M averaged 9.96 ± 2.11 mm, while for trajectory L, it was 9.34 ± 2.53 mm. Both measurements consistently exceeded the 3.5 mm or 4.0 mm screw diameters. Despite these external dimensions, the stability of cervical pedicle screws is primarily determined by the internal characteristics of the pedicle, rather than its external size [22]. The advent of multiplanar CT has enabled precise measurement of both internal and external pedicle diameters [23]. Consequently, evaluating the C1 pedicle's medullary cavity width using axial CT images has become crucial for selecting an optimal and safe C1 pedicle screw trajectory. Our findings revealed that the medullary cavity along trajectory L was 1.71 mm wider than that along trajectory M. Furthermore, a medullary cavity width under 3.5 mm was observed in 12.5% of pedicles for trajectory M, but in only 2.5% for trajectory L. This suggests that if trajectory M cannot accommodate a 3.5 mm pedicle screw, trajectory L, with its more medial entry point, offers a broader medullary cavity, thereby reducing the risk of screw perforation.
The C1 posterior arch screw technique offers superior pull-out strength and a longer screw trajectory compared to the lateral mass screw technique, as supported by multiple studies. This method is particularly effective for achieving stable short-segment rigid fixation and promoting successful fusion due to its inherent biomechanical stability. The length of the screw trajectory is a key determinant of both pull-out strength and overall biomechanical stability. Our research specifically found that trajectory L had a significantly greater pedicle length (28.46 ± 2.20 mm) than trajectory M (27.37 ± 1.93 mm). Consequently, we infer that a pedicle screw following trajectory L would likely offer enhanced pull-out strength and improved biomechanical stability over one placed via trajectory M.
While previous research by Florian et al. and Kwang et al. indicated that a medial screw trajectory of approximately 10° improved safety and prevented vertebral artery injury in the C1 transverse foramen, our study presents a different perspective. We observed a wider medullary cavity along trajectory L compared to trajectory M. To further enhance safety, our approach involved preoperatively measuring the maximum lateral inclination angle on CT images and then implementing a trajectory angle during surgery that was 5° less than this maximum. This technique, we believe, mitigates the risk of screw perforation into the vertebral foramen. Consequently, the use of trajectory L's pedicle is theoretically not associated with an elevated risk of vertebral artery damage.
This study has limitations. A key one is the absence of a biomechanical comparison between trajectory L and trajectory M regarding fixation strengths. Further clinical research is also necessary to fully assess the feasibility and safety of pedicle screw placement along trajectory L.
Our findings indicate that a C1 pedicle screw trajectory with lateral inclination offers several advantages over a medial inclination approach. Specifically, trajectory L demonstrated greater posterior arch height, increased lateral mass height, a wider medullary cavity, and a longer pedicle length. These characteristics contribute to enhanced biomechanical stability and superior pull-out strength, while also minimizing the risk of screw perforation and improving surgical safety. Furthermore, the entry point for trajectory L is positioned closer to the posterior tubercle compared to trajectory M. This proximity reduces the extent of dissection required during surgery and lowers the potential for vertebral artery injury. Given the significant variability in trajectory angles, a preoperative CT scan is crucial for successful implementation of this fixation method.
