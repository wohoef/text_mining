This study utilized a noninvasive ultrasound system to investigate differences in the cross-sectional area (CSA) of flexor digitorum tendons and the thickness of the first annular (A1) pulleys. The comparison was made between trigger digits (TDs) and healthy contralateral digits, specifically at two metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint flexion angles: 0° and 60°. The research involved 17 patients, comprising 6 men and 11 women, each presenting with a TD in one hand. For comparison, 17 corresponding normal digits from the opposite hand, free of trigger finger symptoms, were also examined. The findings revealed that both the CSA of flexor digitorum tendons and the A1 pulley thickness were considerably greater in TDs than in normal digits, a statistically significant difference observed at both 0° and 60° MCP joint flexion (p < 0.01). Furthermore, within the TDs themselves, the A1 pulley demonstrated a significant increase in thickness when the MCP joint was flexed to 60° compared to 0° (p < 0.01). Interestingly, the CSA of the flexor tendons in TDs did not show a significant change between these two flexion positions. In conclusion, the study suggests that TDs are characterized by thicker A1 pulleys and larger flexor digitorum tendon CSAs. The observed discrepancy in how the A1 pulley thickness and flexor digitorum tendon CSA change during MCP flexion might be a contributing factor to the trigger phenomenon.

Trigger digit (TD) is a common hand condition characterized by limited movement in the affected fingers at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint. The characteristic "triggering" sensation, involving locking, catching, and snapping, occurs as the finger transitions from a flexed to an extended position, specifically at the proximal part of the first annular (A1) pulley. While a disparity within the A1 pulley-tendon sheath system is thought to be the underlying cause, the precise pathological mechanism of TD remains undefined, despite various proposed factors (idiopathic, intrinsic, extrinsic). Investigating the A1 pulley-tendon sheath's structure across different finger flexion and extension positions could therefore illuminate the pathophysiology of TD.
In clinical settings, ultrasound offers a noninvasive and readily available method for clearly visualizing the A1 pulley-tendon sheath system within the fingers. While traditional diagnosis of Trigger Digit (TD) relies on clinical indicators like tenderness, triggering, palpable nodules at the A1 pulley, and patient history, ultrasonography has recently become a valuable tool. It is now extensively used in TD cases to assess alterations in the A1 pulley and flexor tendons, as well as to monitor the effectiveness of local injections. This imaging technique proves beneficial for exploring disease severity, potential underlying pathological mechanisms, and treatment outcomes.
Previous research utilizing sonography has consistently demonstrated that trigger fingers exhibit increased thickness in both the flexor tendons and the A1 pulley at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint. Furthermore, other investigations suggest a correlation between the severity of Trigger Digit (TD) and the thickness and elasticity of the A1 pulley and flexor digitorum tendons. The phenomenon of triggering is potentially explained by a size discrepancy within the A1 pulley-tendon sheath system, which could arise from either a thickened A1 pulley or swollen flexor digitorum tendons. Despite these insights, the precise relationship between the ultrasound characteristics of the A1 pulley and flexor digitorum tendons across different finger flexion and extension positions in TD remains unclear. To address this gap and enhance our understanding of TD pathophysiology, this study aims to noninvasively evaluate and compare the cross-sectional area (CSA) of flexor digitorum tendons and the thickness of the A1 pulley in both trigger digits and contralateral normal digits at various finger flexion and extension positions. Our central hypothesis is that a mismatch exists between the CSAs of the flexor tendons and the A1 pulley thickness during MCP joint flexion.
From January to December 2015, adult patients diagnosed with idiopathic Trigger Digit (TD) were recruited for this study, which received approval from the National Cheng Kung University Institutional Review Board (IRB: A-ER-103-096). All participants provided written informed consent. The study included 17 patients, comprising 6 men and 11 women, with an average age of 49.0 ± 7.2 years. Each patient presented with a single affected finger exhibiting TD, with 10 of these cases occurring in dominant hands. Concurrently, 17 healthy, contralateral digits from these same patients served as controls. The affected digits comprised four index fingers, nine middle fingers, and four ring fingers. The average symptom duration for TD was 11.4 ± 7.4 months. Clinical grading using the Froimson classification revealed four patients with Grade 2 TD and thirteen with Grade 3 TD. Additionally, five patients (29%) had co-occurring hypertension, and another five had diabetes mellitus. Strict inclusion criteria mandated a clinical diagnosis of a single TD and no prior history of TD in other digits. Exclusion criteria were comprehensive, encompassing trigger thumb, severe TD (Froimson Grade 4), bilateral TD, and any affected finger with a history of trauma, surgery, local injections, dialysis, gouty arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or carpal tunnel syndrome.
For the ultrasound examination, patients' hands and forearms were positioned with the palm facing upwards, ensuring full supination, and the wrist joint was kept in a neutral alignment. A 10 MHz linear-array ultrasound transducer (SonoSite, Inc., Bothell, WA, USA, 5-10 MHz range) was gently applied to the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint area. The examination commenced with a longitudinal view, allowing for clear identification of the MCP joint, the volar plate, and the flexor digitorum tendons. Subsequently, the transducer was reoriented to a transverse view at the center of the MCP joint, aligning with the long axis of the flexor tendons. Measurements included the A1 pulley thickness and the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the flexor digitorum tendons. The A1 pulley's thickness was defined as the distance spanning its inferior to superior boundaries, encompassing the hyperechoic region. This measurement was taken at the point of maximum thickness, typically located near the apex of the metacarpal head's curve, though its exact position could vary. In the same transverse section where the A1 pulley's maximum thickness was determined, the CSAs of the flexor digitorum tendons were manually outlined. This tracing was performed along the hyperechoic rim of the tendons using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). A hypoechoic synovial fluid space, appearing as a thin layer, was noted between the hyperechoic A1 pulley and the hyperechoic flexor digitorum tendons. Observations of both the flexor digitorum tendons and the A1 pulley were made at two distinct MCP joint flexion angles: 0° and 60°. A specialized fixture was employed to accurately maintain the 60° flexion position during examination. An orthopedic surgeon, experienced in musculoskeletal ultrasonography for over eight years and blinded to patient details, conducted all examinations in the axial view. This included both the trigger digits in the affected hand and the corresponding normal digits in the contralateral hand. To ensure reliability, all ultrasound measurements were repeated within the same session and then averaged, yielding an intraobserver agreement of 0.91.
Due to the small sample size, the Wilcoxon signed rank test analyzed ultrasonographic differences, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.
Comparing trigger digits (TDs) to normal digits, significant differences were observed in both flexor digitorum tendon cross-sectional area (CSA) and A1 pulley thickness. At 0° MCP joint flexion, the flexor tendon CSA measured 0.24 ± 0.05 cm2 in TDs, notably larger than the 0.19 ± 0.06 cm2 in normal digits. Similarly, at 60° flexion, TD flexor tendon CSA was 0.25 ± 0.05 cm2, exceeding the 0.19 ± 0.06 cm2 found in normal digits (p < 0.01). Regarding A1 pulley thickness, TDs exhibited a thickness of 0.78 ± 0.20 cm at 0° flexion, considerably greater than the 0.21 ± 0.10 cm in normal digits. At 60° flexion, TD A1 pulley thickness increased to 0.91 ± 0.21 cm, while normal digits measured 0.22 ± 0.10 cm. Across both flexion angles, the A1 pulley in TDs was significantly thicker than in normal digits (p < 0.001).
Flexor digitorum tendon cross-sectional areas remained consistent in both trigger and normal digits, regardless of MCP joint flexion (0° vs. 60°). Conversely, the A1 pulley in trigger digits showed a significant increase in thickness when flexed to 60° compared to 0°. This thickening was not observed in the A1 pulleys of normal digits.
Our investigation focused on how different finger positions influence the soft tissue appearance at the MCP joint, specifically comparing trigger digits (TDs) with healthy contralateral digits at 0° and 60° MCP joint flexion. We observed that both the cross-sectional area (CSA) of flexor digitorum tendons and the thickness of the A1 pulleys were significantly greater in TDs at both flexion angles when contrasted with normal digits. Furthermore, while the A1 pulley thickness in TDs increased when the MCP joint flexed to 60°, the flexor digitorum tendon CSAs remained unchanged. This specific response was absent in normal digits.
Repeated forceful compression and friction within the soft tissues are hypothesized to cause the thickening of the A1 pulley or swelling of the flexor digitorum tendons. This, in turn, can constrict the proximal part of the flexor digitorum tendons, hindering their smooth movement at the base of the trigger finger. Evidence suggests that trigger digit (TD) could be a form of tendinosis or A1 pulley stenosis. Ultrasound examinations of TDs in a neutral position reveal that both the A1 pulleys are thicker and the flexor digitorum tendon cross-sectional areas (CSAs) are larger compared to healthy digits. These increased CSAs may result from collagenase degeneration and synovial proliferation.
The A1 pulley, a crucial component in finger mechanics, is a dense, arc-shaped connective tissue. Its primary role is to stabilize the flexor digitorum tendons by encasing them and securing the phalanges, thereby preventing bowstringing during finger flexion. While cadaver studies have identified the A1 pulley as hyperechoic tissue situated above the hypoechoic synovial fluid space, there's no universal agreement on how to precisely measure its thickness using ultrasound. Some define it as the hypoechoic bundle over the flexor tendon rim, while others refer to the area above the synovial fluid space. Despite this measurement discrepancy, numerous prior studies consistently report that trigger digits (TDs) exhibit significantly thicker A1 pulleys compared to normal digits. Our own research also found significant increases in both A1 pulley thickness and flexor digitorum tendon cross-sectional areas in TDs, but not in healthy contralateral digits, across both flexion positions. This reinforces the utility of ultrasonography for confirming TD diagnoses and tracking treatment efficacy.
The phenomenon of triggering typically manifests in the proximal region of the A1 pulley as the finger extends. While a continuous, dynamic assessment of A1 pulley thickness and flexor digitorum tendon cross-sectional area (CSA) would offer valuable insights into the pathophysiology of Trigger Digit (TD), current ultrasound technology does not permit such real-time observation. Consequently, researchers have investigated alterations in A1 pulleys and flexor tendon CSAs across various static finger positions. For instance, Chuang et al. observed that both the A1 pulleys and flexor digitorum tendon CSAs were considerably thicker in TDs compared to normal contralateral digits when viewed transversely across three distinct finger postures. Similarly, Sato et al. noted that A1 pulleys were thicker and flexor tendon CSAs were larger in TDs than in healthy digits, regardless of whether the hand was in a neutral or hook grip position. Their work also indicated a significantly greater anteroposterior CSA of flexor tendons in the hook grip (full interphalangeal joint flexion) compared to the neutral position, although A1 pulley thickness remained unchanged between these two postures. Our current study, however, specifically concentrated on position changes at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint. A substantial discrepancy between flexor tendon CSAs and A1 pulley thickness during MCP flexion could represent a key mechanism behind triggering. The increased thickness of the A1 pulley in TDs may lead to heightened stress, friction, and obstruction during finger flexion, more so than during extension. This, in turn, could contribute to the enlarged CSAs of the flexor digitorum tendons and impede their smooth gliding, ultimately preventing effortless movement.
Several limitations were identified in this study. The investigation did not include patients with Froimson grade 4 Trigger Digits, as accurate ultrasound examination is not feasible in a fixed flexion. While the sample size was small, the significant differences observed between groups indicate its sufficiency for hypothesis testing. Furthermore, the study exclusively focused on the metacarpophalangeal joint position, unlike other research exploring various interphalangeal joints. Flexion exceeding 60° at the MCP joint was not assessed due to difficulties in proper ultrasound probe placement.
Trigger digits exhibited both thicker A1 pulleys and larger flexor digitorum tendon cross-sectional areas compared to normal digits. A potential mechanism for triggering involves the observed mismatch during MCP joint flexion: A1 pulley thickness increased, while flexor tendon cross-sectional areas remained unchanged.
