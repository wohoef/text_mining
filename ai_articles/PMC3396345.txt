This study investigated the prevalence of EGFR and KRAS mutations in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) within the Dutch population of Western Europe. Previous research had primarily focused on East Asian and North American populations, revealing considerable variations in mutation frequencies. The objective was to establish these frequencies in both primary tumors and various metastatic sites. Data was gathered from NSCLC samples across 13 hospitals, utilizing EGFR (exons 19, 20, 21) and KRAS (exons 2, 3) mutation test results. These tests were conducted on paraffin-embedded tissue or cytological material from primary and metastatic lung carcinomas. The findings showed EGFR mutations in 9.1% (71/778) of tested patients, with a higher incidence of 10.6% (66/620) in adenocarcinomas. Notably, female patients exhibited a significantly higher EGFR mutation rate (13.4%) compared to males (5.5%, p < 0.001). KRAS mutations were identified in 33.3% (277/832) of patients, reaching 36.9% (244/662) in adenocarcinomas. A significant increase in EGFR mutations (26.5%; OR 2.80, 95% CI 1.22–6.41) was observed in patients with malignant pleural/pericardial effusions, while KRAS mutations were significantly lower in this group (18.8%; OR 0.35, 95% CI 0.14–0.86). In conclusion, the Dutch cohort demonstrated an elevated frequency of EGFR mutations in lung adenocarcinoma patients with malignant pleural/pericardial effusions. The overall EGFR mutation frequency in lung adenocarcinomas in this West European group aligned with North American and South European populations, but the KRAS mutation frequency was higher than previously reported in any other population.

Advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) typically lacks curative treatment options, and chemotherapy offers limited efficacy, leading to a median overall survival of about one year. Recent therapeutic advancements include small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like erlotinib and gefitinib, which target specific signaling pathways by blocking the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). These TKIs are particularly effective against tumors with activating EGFR gene mutations, but they are ineffective for tumors with activating KRAS gene mutations.
Research on EGFR mutation frequency has been most comprehensive in East Asian populations, where it ranges from 36.4% to 66.3% in adenocarcinoma. Conversely, North American and South European populations exhibit significantly lower EGFR mutation rates, between 6.0% and 14.0%. The trend for KRAS mutations is the opposite: East Asian populations show a low frequency (2.3% to 9.4%), while North American and South European populations have a higher frequency (11.7% to 31.0%). However, extensive studies on EGFR and KRAS mutation frequencies in the West European population are currently lacking.
While East Asian and South European studies have extensively explored the link between patient characteristics and EGFR mutation status, the relationship with metastatic sites is less understood. In these populations, a higher EGFR mutation rate is consistently observed in female patients, never-smokers, and those with adenocarcinoma. These investigations typically analyze EGFR mutations in primary lung tumor tissue.
This study investigated EGFR and KRAS mutation frequencies in primary and metastatic NSCLC within the Dutch population.
Data on EGFR and KRAS mutations in NSCLC were gathered from routine diagnostic analyses conducted between January 2008 and April 2011. This information originated from 13 Dutch hospitals, including St. Antonius Hospital, University Medical Center Utrecht, Rijnstate Hospital, Medical Center Alkmaar, Jeroen Bosch Hospital, Radboud University Nijmegen Medical Center, Zuwe Hofpoort Hospital, Slingeland Hospital, Gemini Hospital, Hospital Gelderse Vallei, Hospital Bernhoven, Maas Hospital Pantein, and Elkerliek Hospital. Four pathology departments, specifically St. Antonius Hospital, University Medical Center Utrecht, VU University Medical Center, and Radboud University Nijmegen Medical Center, were responsible for performing these mutation analyses centrally.
Samples for analysis, comprising primary lung tumors and metastases, were prepared from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue or cytological slides. Smoking history, including pack-years when available, was collected for a subset of patients whose samples were processed at St. Antonius Hospital and University Medical Center Utrecht. Patients were classified as never smokers (<100 lifetime cigarettes), former smokers (cessation ≥1 year ago), or current smokers (still smoking or cessation <1 year ago).
Ethics committee approval was not required because all mutation analyses were routine diagnostic procedures, and patient and tumor data were collected anonymously.
EGFR (exons 19, 20, 21) and KRAS (exons 2, 3) gene mutations were analyzed using two distinct methods. Most samples underwent polymerase chain reaction followed by sequencing, referencing GenBank Accession Numbers NM_005228.3 and NM_004985.3. However, samples from Utrecht, Alkmaar, and 's Hertogenbosch hospitals were initially screened with high-resolution melting analysis, with sequencing performed only if an abnormal melting curve was detected.
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 15.0). To assess the relationship between EGFR and KRAS mutation status and patient/tumor features, Fisher’s exact test was employed. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test determined the normality of age and pack-year distributions, with subsequent mean comparisons made via Mann–Whitney or T tests as suitable. Binary logistic regression analysis facilitated the comparison of mutation frequencies between various metastatic sites and primary tumors, yielding odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided p-value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance across all tests.
EGFR mutation status was assessed in 816 samples, yielding interpretable results for 791. These samples originated from 803 individual patients, with 778 having an interpretable result. For 13 patients, both primary tumor and metastatic samples were analyzed, and they were classified as EGFR mutation positive if either sample showed a mutation. The samples comprised 655 adenocarcinomas (ADC), 42 squamous cell carcinomas (SCC), and 119 other types (including 102 large cell, 8 sarcomatoid, 5 adenosquamous, and 4 unspecified carcinomas). Of the total samples, 462 were primary tumors and 342 were from metastatic sites. Twelve pleural biopsies/excisions could not be definitively categorized as primary or metastatic. Overall, EGFR mutations were identified in 71 (9.1%) patients. A breakdown by histology showed that 66 of these mutations occurred in ADC, representing a 10.6% frequency within this subtype. The remaining 5 mutations were distributed among 3 large cell carcinomas, one adenosquamous carcinoma, and one sarcomatoid carcinoma (specifically, a spindle cell subtype). Statistical analysis revealed a significantly higher incidence of EGFR mutations in ADC (10.6%) compared to SCC (0%) or other tumor types (4.3%) (p = 0.006). Furthermore, female patients exhibited a significantly higher EGFR mutation frequency (13.4%) than male patients (5.5%) (p < 0.001). Smoking history was available for 288 patients with known EGFR mutation status. Never smokers demonstrated a significantly elevated EGFR mutation frequency (48.3%) compared to former (8.5%) and current smokers (4.9%) (p < 0.001). Among former and current smokers, EGFR mutation-positive individuals had a significantly lower average number of pack-years (23.6) than those who were EGFR mutation-negative (35.6) (P = 0.031).
The analysis of EGFR mutations revealed that deletions in exon 19 were the most prevalent, accounting for 52.7% (39 cases). The L858R point mutation in exon 21 was the next most frequent, observed in 28.4% (21 cases). Beyond these common types, a single insertion (V769-D770 ins ASV) was identified. Additionally, four distinct point mutations were found in exon 20 (S768I occurring twice, S768N, G779S, and H805Y), and another four in exon 21 (R831H, V845A, D855N, and L861Q, which appeared twice). Three T790M point mutations in exon 20, known for TKI resistance, were also detected; each of these was found alongside another activating EGFR mutation (one exon 19 deletion and two L858R mutations in exon 21). Furthermore, the A840T single nucleotide polymorphism in exon 21 was observed twice.
Analysis of KRAS mutation status involved 873 samples, with 845 yielding interpretable results. These samples originated from 860 distinct patients, and 832 of these patients provided interpretable data. Among the samples, 497 were primary tumors and 363 were from metastatic sites. For 13 pleural biopsies/excisions, it was not possible to differentiate between primary tumor and metastasis. In 13 cases, both a primary tumor and a metastasis from the same patient were analyzed; a patient was deemed KRAS mutation positive if either sample showed a mutation. Overall, KRAS mutations were identified in 277 (33.3%) patients. Specifically within adenocarcinomas (ADC), 244 patients had KRAS mutations, representing a frequency of 36.9%. This frequency was significantly higher in ADC compared to squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and other tumor types (36.9%, 2.5%, and 24.6% respectively, p < 0.001). A trend indicated more KRAS mutations in female patients (36.4%) than in male patients (30.6%), though this difference (p = 0.090) was not statistically significant. Smoking history was available for 287 patients with known KRAS status. Never smokers exhibited a lower KRAS mutation frequency (12.0%) compared to former (33.9%) and current smokers (36.6%), a statistically significant finding (p = 0.044). However, no significant difference was observed in the average number of pack-years between KRAS mutation-positive and KRAS mutation-negative patients (30.7 vs. 29.9, p = 0.808). The detected KRAS mutations were predominantly point mutations, with 86.3% occurring in codon 12 (239 cases), 7.2% in codon 13 (20 cases), and 6.5% in codon 61 (18 cases).
Of 752 patients, EGFR and KRAS mutation statuses were predominantly mutually exclusive. A mere two cases (0.3%) exhibited both mutations: EGFR exon 20 S768N with KRAS exon 2 G12A, and EGFR exon 20 G779S with KRAS exon 2 G12C. These combined mutations, found in bronchial biopsies, occurred in male patients with adenocarcinoma.
An investigation into mutation frequency differences between primary tumors and metastatic sites was conducted, focusing exclusively on adenocarcinomas (ADC) due to the predominant presence of EGFR and KRAS mutations in this subtype. Pleural biopsies and excisions were omitted from this analysis, as distinguishing between primary tumor growth and metastatic spread in these samples proved challenging. The study assessed EGFR status in 360 primary tumors and 261 metastatic locations. For KRAS, 394 primary tumors and 268 metastatic sites were analyzed. No significant variation in EGFR mutation frequency was detected when comparing primary tumors (11.4%) to metastases (10.0%) (p = 0.602). However, a statistically significant difference was observed for KRAS mutations, which were found more frequently in primary tumors (40.1%) than in metastatic sites (31.3%) (p = 0.026).
To further investigate mutation distribution, metastatic locations were grouped into categories, each containing at least 10 samples. These categories included lymph nodes, pleural and pericardial effusions, liver, brain, bone, soft tissue, and other sites. Tables 2 and 3 provide a summary of these findings. A significant variation in the presence of both EGFR and KRAS mutations was observed across these different metastatic categories (p = 0.034 for EGFR and p = 0.017 for KRAS). Binary logistic regression analysis identified pleural and pericardial effusions as the specific category driving this difference in mutation frequency when compared to primary tumors. In this particular metastatic site, EGFR mutations were found at a frequency of 26.5%, demonstrating a significantly increased odds ratio (OR = 2.80, 95% CI 1.22-6.41, p = 0.015). Conversely, KRAS mutations were less frequent, at 18.8%, with a significantly decreased odds ratio (OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.14–0.86, p = 0.022). Brain metastases also exhibited a similar, though not statistically significant, trend, showing an increased EGFR mutation frequency (25.0%) and a decreased KRAS mutation frequency (9.1%) relative to primary tumors.
Among the 13 patient samples that included both a primary tumor and a matched metastasis, four pairs (30.8%) exhibited discrepancies in their EGFR and/or KRAS mutation status. This discordance resulted in a clinically significant difference in mutation status for three of these 13 pairs (23.1%). Specifically, one case involved a KRAS mutation found only in the metastasis, not the primary tumor. Another instance showed an EGFR mutation exclusively in the metastasis, while its corresponding primary tumor had a KRAS mutation absent in the metastasis. A third case presented with an EGFR exon 19 deletion and a T790M point mutation in exon 20 in the primary tumor, but only the exon 19 deletion in the metastasis. Additionally, one pair displayed different KRAS mutations between the primary tumor (exon 1 G12V) and its metastasis (G12R).
This study is the first in a European population to link EGFR mutation rates with metastatic locations in lung adenocarcinoma patients. Our findings revealed a higher EGFR mutation frequency (27%) and a lower KRAS mutation frequency (19%) in individuals with malignant pleural or pericardial effusions. A comparable, though not statistically significant, pattern was observed in patients with brain metastases. These outcomes align with two East Asian studies that reported significantly elevated EGFR mutation rates in tumors with malignant pleural effusions and a relatively high EGFR mutation rate in brain metastases. Such observations hold significant implications for managing patients with metastatic lung adenocarcinoma. For those with malignant pleural or pericardial effusion, clinicians might prioritize obtaining tissue for mutation analysis or, if tissue is unavailable, consider a trial TKI treatment. The genetic makeup of a tumor dictates its biological characteristics, and the EGFR and KRAS mutation status may significantly impact metastasis formation, warranting further research.
The presence of EGFR and KRAS mutations can differ between a primary tumor and its corresponding metastases, a phenomenon observed in various studies [14, 15]. Within our cohort, we analyzed 13 pairs of primary tumors and their matched metastases. Of these, 4 pairs (31%) exhibited distinct mutations, and in 3 of these pairs (23%), this discordance resulted in a clinically significant difference in mutation status. Such variations in mutation status between primary and metastatic sites hold potential clinical implications, particularly concerning a patient's response to TKI treatment. We lack data on whether these patients received TKIs or chemotherapy between the collection of primary and metastatic samples. Such treatments might impose selective pressure, favoring the proliferation of certain tumor clones and thus altering mutation status. Alternatively, the process of metastasis itself could lead to the outgrowth of specific tumor cell clones, even without external selective pressures.
This research represents the initial comprehensive investigation into EGFR and KRAS mutation frequencies within a substantial West European cohort. Previous studies predominantly focused on East Asian and North American populations, with some attention given to South European groups, revealing significant regional variations. Our findings in adenocarcinomas showed an EGFR mutation rate of 11%. This figure is considerably lower than the 36–66% observed in East Asia [2, 3]. However, it aligns with the 6–24% reported for North America [4, 5], the 6–16% for Southern Europe [9, 16], and the 11% noted in a recent, smaller North European (Norwegian) study [17]. This consistency might stem from the ethnic makeup of our study participants, likely more akin to North American and South European populations than East Asian ones, despite potential environmental differences. It's important to note that exon 18 of the EGFR gene was not consistently analyzed across all participating laboratories, potentially leading to a slight underestimation of the EGFR mutation frequency, though mutations in this exon are generally a minor proportion of all EGFR mutations [4, 18].
In our West European cohort, KRAS mutations were identified in 37% of adenocarcinomas. This frequency significantly surpasses the 2–9% observed in East Asian populations [3, 6]. Furthermore, it exceeds the rates reported for North America (12–31%) [4, 8] and Southern Europe (11–29%) [7, 9]. This finding aligns with a 39% frequency noted in a smaller Dutch validation study utilizing HRM [19]. A contributing factor to this higher observed frequency might be our comprehensive analysis, which included codon 61 in addition to the commonly studied codons 12 and 13. Codon 61 accounted for a substantial 7% of the mutations, which would have been overlooked in studies focusing solely on the primary hotspot codons.
In the West European population studied, female patients exhibited a significantly higher frequency of EGFR mutations compared to males (13% vs. 6%, p < 0.001). This finding aligns with previously reported differences in EGFR mutation rates between sexes in Asian and South European populations. While a trend of increased KRAS mutations in females (36%) compared to males (31%) was observed, this difference was not statistically significant. Earlier research on KRAS mutations, particularly in East Asian populations where overall KRAS mutation numbers are low, similarly did not establish a significant correlation with sex, though some studies hinted at a male predominance.
Never smokers exhibited an EGFR mutation frequency more than five times greater than that of former and current smokers, a negative correlation consistently observed across Asian, North American, and South European populations. Additionally, a link between KRAS mutations and smoking history has been previously documented.
Adenocarcinoma (ADC) showed a higher prevalence of both EGFR and KRAS mutations than squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and other tumor types, consistent with prior findings.
In this study, the most prevalent EGFR mutations were deletions in exon 19 and the L858R point mutation in exon 21, collectively accounting for 81% of all observed EGFR mutations. This figure is slightly lower than the 85-90% reported in other research, potentially because some prior studies focused exclusively on more common mutation sites. Beyond these frequent mutations, two TKI-sensitive L861Q point mutations were identified in exon 21. Additionally, seven distinct, rarer point mutations were detected: S768I (twice), S768N, G779S, and H805Y in exon 20, and R831H, V845A, and D855N in exon 21. While most of these rare mutations have been previously documented, H805Y and V845A are novel, though other mutations at their respective positions have been noted. The clinical relevance and TKI sensitivity of these rare point mutations remain undetermined.
Three T790M point mutations, located in exon 20, were identified. These mutations, which are known to confer resistance to TKI treatment, consistently appeared alongside an activating EGFR mutation: one was with an exon 19 deletion, and two were with L858R point mutations in exon 21. While the T790M mutation is typically associated with secondary resistance developing post-TKI treatment, some studies indicate its presence prior to therapy. Additionally, an exon 20 insertion was observed, a type of mutation previously documented and linked to TKI resistance.
While EGFR and KRAS mutations are typically considered mutually exclusive, our study identified two instances where both were present. These combined mutations involved rare EGFR point mutations (S768N and G779S) of uncertain clinical importance. It's plausible these specific EGFR mutations lack significant clinical impact, potentially explaining their co-occurrence with known activating EGFR mutations in prior research.
The study's findings, while relevant for TKI treatment, are subject to a selection bias. Due to analyses primarily being physician-requested, the cohort disproportionately represents advanced non-small cell lung carcinoma cases. This means the study doesn't offer a comprehensive overview of all NSCLC, as physicians typically wouldn't order these tests for surgically cured patients. However, this aligns with real-world clinical practice.
The distribution of EGFR and KRAS mutations varies across metastatic sites, with significant implications for patient care. In the West European Dutch population, pleural and pericardial effusions showed a relative increase in EGFR mutation frequency and a decrease in KRAS mutation frequency when compared to primary tumors. While the overall EGFR mutation frequency in this cohort aligns with those reported for North American and South European populations, KRAS mutation frequencies were notably higher than any previously documented population. Furthermore, both EGFR and KRAS mutations were more prevalent in adenocarcinomas, and EGFR mutations were observed more frequently in female patients.
