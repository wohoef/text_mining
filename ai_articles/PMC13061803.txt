Addressing energy and material resource challenges can be aided by the sustainable utilization of wood. This study aims to deepen the understanding of wood's environmental impacts by examining the entire value chain of wooden products manufactured or utilized within Switzerland. Our approach begins with a material flow analysis of current wood consumption in the country. Subsequently, life cycle assessment (LCA)-based environmental indicators are employed to evaluate the environmental consequences linked to these material flows. In terms of climate change, our findings indicate an average net benefit of 0.5 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per cubic meter of wood consumed. Significant environmental advantages are frequently observed when wood replaces conventional methods of heat generation and energy-intensive materials used in construction and furniture manufacturing. However, the environmental performance of wood is considerably influenced by its specific application and the chosen environmental indicators. To fully harness wood's potential for environmental mitigation, we propose three key recommendations: Firstly, prioritize wood's application in areas offering substantial substitution benefits, such as replacing fossil fuels for energy or energy-intensive building materials. Secondly, implement targeted measures to reduce adverse effects, such as particulate matter emissions. Thirdly, maintain a holistic systems perspective to comprehensively assess and balance competing effects like substitution and cascading use. These findings can serve as a foundation for subsequent detailed investigations and forward-looking analyses of wood-use scenarios.

Wood, a renewable and versatile resource, acts as a significant carbon dioxide sink and offers diverse applications in both material and energy sectors. Its sustainable utilization can mitigate climate change impacts and reduce reliance on nonrenewable resources. However, current trends in wood usage present challenges to these benefits. In areas like the European Union and Switzerland, wood stocks are growing due to underutilized forests, meaning forests' climate mitigation potential is not fully realized. Growing wood stocks sequester less carbon than when utilized at their growth rate. Furthermore, the carbon sequestration capacity of older forests or wood products is often less substantial than the climate benefits derived from wood substituting non-wood products. These circumstances necessitate new strategies for sustainable wood mobilization and use, underpinned by a thorough quantitative analysis of its environmental benefits and impacts.
Previous research on wood's environmental impact has largely focused on individual indicators, primarily climate change, and specific products or sectors. However, a broader perspective is crucial for grasping the full consequences of wood utilization. This involves expanding system boundaries to encompass effects like material substitution, where one product replaces another, and resource cascading, which involves the sequential use of a resource based on its quality. While resource cascading can enhance overall resource efficiency, including for wood, its environmental benefits are often overshadowed by the advantages gained from substituting non-wood products. Understanding these broader system-level interactions is essential for a comprehensive assessment of wood's environmental performance.
This study aims to evaluate the environmental consequences and advantages linked to the entire lifecycle of wood products in Switzerland, from production to disposal. To achieve this, we will conduct a comprehensive assessment of each stage within the wood value chain. This involves quantifying the environmental contributions of wood to final products and estimating the potential benefits derived from wood replacing alternative materials. Ultimately, the findings will inform future research and strategic planning for sustainable wood utilization.
To model environmental impacts and benefits, this study integrates material flow analysis (MFA) with life cycle assessment (LCA). Data on wood product production comes from annual statistics and reports from federal offices and industry associations. Environmental impact calculations utilize life cycle inventories from ecoinvent 3.1, employing a cut-off allocation system model.
The study's initial phase involved defining products and quantifying their quantities. This began with an established material flow analysis (MFA) of Switzerland's wood consumption in 2011, offering a coherent overview of wood movement throughout its value chain, measured in cubic meters (m³) of solid wood equivalents. However, the broad scope of this MFA led to a reduction in the precision of product and process details when compared to available statistics. To enhance the model's resolution while maintaining MFA consistency, statistical data were proportionally distributed based on the output volumes of MFA processes. For instance, data on various fiber and particle board types were aggregated into the "board production" MFA process. Subsequently, each board type was assigned a portion of this process's output, corresponding to its production share. This refined MFA provided the required granularity for environmental impact assessments. The quantities of products, distinguishing between domestic production and consumption, imports, and exports, were detailed (refer to table S6 in the supplementary information). Furthermore, product flows within this expanded MFA were converted to m³ solid wood equivalents using specific factors for each product (detailed in table S5 of the supplementary information).
Environmental impacts for calculated product quantities were then assessed. This involved using several impact assessment methods, including climate change over a 100-year period (IPCC 2013), ReCiPe mid- and endpoints (Goedkoop et al. 2009), cumulative energy demand (Hischier et al. 2010), and ecological scarcity 2013 (Frischknecht and Büsser Knöpfel 2013).
Directly applying life cycle inventory data at the unit process level to the extended material flow analysis (MFA) proved challenging due to discrepancies in the resolution of inventory and MFA process chains. To overcome this, a modular life cycle assessment (LCA) approach was employed. This method allowed for the representation of MFA supply chain processes through interconnected LCA modules. Each module was constructed by combining pre-existing inventory chains from the ecoinvent 3.1 database with upstream cut-off points to define system boundaries. As illustrated by the example of sawmilling, all relevant LCA unit processes associated with an MFA process were consolidated into a single, interconnected LCA module. For instance, the "sawmilling" MFA process was represented by a comprehensive LCA module. Inputs to this module, such as "sawlogs, softwood," were excluded from the module itself, with their production being accounted for in preceding LCA modules. Subsequently, these interconnected modules were scaled according to the output quantities of the corresponding MFA process, using "sawnwood, softwood" as an example. Similarly, the input quantities for products like "sawlogs, softwood" were adjusted based on these output amounts.
Each module's environmental impacts include the process itself and its upstream inputs, excluding wood-based ones. This exclusion prevents double-counting, as impacts from wood inputs are already covered by the modules that produce them.
For imported and exported products, upstream wood processing impacts are included, unlike for domestically produced items. Switzerland's domestic consumption impacts are derived by subtracting exports from domestic production and adding imports. Discrepancies in system boundaries for domestic production and trade introduce some uncertainty into these consumption impact calculations.
Switzerland's wood use is modeled through 52 processes yielding 40 wood-based products, encompassing various market sectors up to semifinished goods. Final products like houses and furniture, along with energy, were excluded due to insufficient material flow analysis (MFA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) data. A comprehensive list of the model's products and processes is available in Table S4 of the supplementary information.
In Switzerland, forest growth currently outpaces wood utilization, with an annual net increment of 7.4 m³/ha compared to an annual use of 6.6 m³/ha. This underuse is largely concentrated in mountainous areas where harvesting is more costly. Consequently, increased cascade use of wood would likely result in reduced forest extraction, a trend unlikely to shift without a significant rise in wood product demand. To assess the potential benefits of enhanced cascading under these conditions, we measured the impact reduction achievable by comparing primary wood-related impacts to total product impacts. This measure is termed the "short-term cascading potential." This designation reflects the expectation that future environmental pressures, such as climate change, will drive increased long-term demand for wood, leading to the substitution of non-wood materials.
To evaluate the current material and energy substitution effects stemming from wood product usage, this study adopted a retrospective approach. In Switzerland, the primary applications for wood are energy generation, accounting for roughly 50% of its use, and paper production, at approximately 25%. The remaining wood is allocated to various other products, including buildings (around 10%), furniture (about 3%), and packaging (also around 3%). For each of these applications, the production impacts associated with wood were compared against those of non-wood alternatives. Paper was excluded from this comparison because its substitution products often represent a significant technological advancement, and readily available non-wood alternatives were scarce historically. For all applications except energy, the point of substitution was considered at the level of semi-finished products, representing the inputs to the final service. Based on estimated demand, wood products were assigned to their respective services, and corresponding non-wood substitute products were identified. The benefits derived from current wood utilization were then calculated across three distinct scenarios. These scenarios encompassed a likely benefit case, a minimum benefit case, and a maximum benefit case. The variations among these scenarios were driven by the composition of the substitution products, allowing for the incorporation of variability and uncertainty in the underlying assumptions.
The lifespan of wooden products and their alternatives was assumed to be equivalent. This is because product longevity is typically dictated by aspects such as overall quality, consumer trends, and affordability, rather than the material composition alone. Research on furniture, for instance, indicates that these consumer-driven factors significantly influence disposal patterns. Detailed information regarding the selection of substitute materials can be found in Tables S8 and S9 within the supplementary online material.
The end-of-life (EoL) treatment was incorporated into the substitution effect calculations. For each product, a combination of inert landfilling, recycling, and incineration was estimated, drawing on waste stream data from Zurich and researcher assumptions. The ecoinvent 3.1 cut-off allocation system already accounts for recycled content in some product manufacturing, so additional recycling benefits were not applied to prevent double counting. For other products undergoing recycling or incineration, EoL effects were assessed through system expansion. Landfilling was not credited, and potential losses during material collection were excluded from consideration. Detailed information on EoL calculations can be found in the supplementary information.
The following section examines the contribution of wood products and processes to Switzerland's overall domestic consumption impacts. The analysis presents findings for several environmental indicators, including ecological scarcity (eco scarcity 2013), ReCiPe total, particulate matter formation (ReCiPe, PMFP), climate change (IPCC 2013), and cumulative fossil energy demand (CED, fossil). It is important to note that the climate change impact of biogenic CO2 emissions is excluded from this analysis. While these emissions can be significant in the short term, particularly with direct wood use for heating, their discussion is deferred to the "Further Limitations" section. Abbreviations for these indicators are provided in parentheses. Additional results across various life cycle impact assessment methods and categories can be found in figures S1 to S6 within the supplementary information.
In Switzerland during 2011, wood processing was dominated by harvesting and energy production, handling approximately 6.5 and 5.3 million cubic meters of wood, respectively. Sawmilling, waste paper and wood collection, and paper production followed in terms of volume. In contrast, board and pulp production utilized significantly smaller quantities, around 0.8 and 0.2 million cubic meters each. Looking at product applications, heat and paper consumed the majority of wood, with a substantial portion also passing through sawmilling processes. While domestic production and consumption volumes of wood were closely aligned, Switzerland engaged in considerable international trade of wood products. The primary exports consisted of paper, waste wood, waste paper, and sawnwood. Conversely, wood imports were largely concentrated in paper products and finished goods. Figure 2 illustrates these wood flows within Switzerland for 2011, with arrow thickness indicating volume. The dashed line delineates the Swiss system boundary, while the dotted line represents the broader global perspective including trade. Although the figure shows the production of wood-based products, the model itself only accounts for their inputs, such as semi-finished materials, due to data limitations regarding final processing steps.
When evaluating the wood value chain, switching from a focus on material volume to environmental impact significantly alters the perceived importance of different products and processes. Paper production, for instance, accounts for a substantial portion of overall consumption impacts, ranging from 23% to 58% depending on the assessment method used. Energy production also emerges as a major contributor, with impacts between 6% and 57%. Board production and harvesting are also identified as significant factors, contributing 8% to 18% and 8% to 44% of overall impacts, respectively. This shift in perspective, illustrated in Figure 3, highlights the critical role of impact assessment in understanding the true environmental significance of wood-based products.
Similar patterns emerge when examining particulate matter formation and ecological scarcity. Particulate matter emissions primarily stem from combustion processes, and as these are a significant factor in eco-point calculations, they align with ecological scarcity method results. Climate change impacts and cumulative fossil energy demand also show comparable trends. This similarity is attributed to processes like board and paper production, which frequently require substantial fossil energy inputs, leading to elevated greenhouse gas emissions. Furthermore, the ReCiPe total method assigns high impacts to land-use processes such as harvesting due to its strong weighting of land occupation. However, this method does not differentiate regional variations in forest management intensity or biodiversity.
Analyzing the impact of primary wood within a product reveals two key observations. Primarily, the ReCiPe total method shows substantial primary wood impact shares, potentially due to its strong emphasis on land occupation, which may not accurately reflect Swiss conditions. Similar high shares are observed with the ecological scarcity method, largely due to primary energy consumption. Conversely, when accounting for particulate matter and greenhouse gas emissions, along with cumulative fossil energy demand, these shares significantly decrease. Consequently, the benefits derived from wood cascading, which replaces primary wood, are minimal for these specific environmental indicators.
As wood products undergo more processing, their material-related environmental impacts become less significant compared to other materials and process impacts, reducing wood's overall environmental relevance.
Wood consumption generally leads to reduced greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across various applications. Significant advantages are observed when wood replaces fossil fuels like oil and gas for heating, or when it substitutes energy-intensive materials such as primary metals, plastics, and concrete in construction and furniture manufacturing. Furthermore, the end-of-life (EoL) management of wood products can yield substantial environmental gains. This is particularly true for waste wood, which, when incinerated, can generate benefits that even surpass its initial production impacts. Figure 4 visually represents these substitution effects on climate change impacts. It details the contributions from both the production phase and the EoL treatment for three distinct substitution scenarios: minimum, likely, and maximum benefit. The analysis adopts a "wood perspective," where the production impacts of substitute materials are considered negative, signifying savings achieved by using wood instead. Conversely, EoL benefits derived from substitutes are presented as positive, indicating losses incurred when wood is used. The cumulative net effect for each scenario is depicted by a blue line, illustrating the overall climate impact of wood utilization.
The environmental benefits of wood-based materials differ significantly based on the assessment method and the materials they replace. For example, when evaluating ecological scarcity and particulate matter formation, the advantages of wood diminish. In some instances, such as with land occupation, wood products may even lead to greater environmental impacts than non-wood alternatives. Detailed results for these specific impact categories can be found in figures S8 through S11 within the supplementary information.
Considering all substitution options, the use of wood provides an average climate benefit of 0.5 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per cubic meter. This benefit is comprised of 0.3 t CO2-eq/m3 from substituting other materials and 0.4 t CO2-eq/m3 from replacing conventional energy sources. Furthermore, the end-of-life treatment of wood contributes an additional 0.2 t CO2-eq/m3 benefit. This latter benefit is less pronounced for waste wood incineration compared to primary wood due to the relatively low thermal efficiency of Swiss municipal waste incineration plants.
Energetic use of wood generally yields greater direct substitution benefits. Even more advantageous is a material use followed by energetic end-of-life utilization, assuming the waste wood is combustible and losses are minimal. These findings underscore the significant influence of substitution assumptions, as evidenced by variations across different scenarios. Detailed displacement factors for products are available in Table S3 of the supporting information.
Figure 5 presents factors that quantify the environmental performance of two key strategies: substituting wood for other materials and cascading wood use. These factors illustrate the extent to which environmental impacts are either reduced (indicated by positive numbers) or increased (indicated by negative numbers) when these patterns are applied, relative to a baseline scenario where they are not. For substitution, the factor is calculated by comparing the environmental impacts of a wood product against those of its non-wood alternative. Specifically, it measures the difference in impacts between the substitute and the wood product, then expresses this difference as a proportion of the substitute product's impact. A positive factor signifies a reduction in environmental impact due to wood substitution. In the context of short-term cascading, the factor is derived by comparing the environmental impacts of a wood product that includes primary wood with those of the same product when only waste wood is used. This factor represents the difference in impacts between these two scenarios, divided by the impact of the wood product that includes primary wood. A positive factor here indicates an improvement in environmental performance through cascading. Negative factors, in both substitution and cascading, suggest that the environmental impacts are higher than in the reference scenario. Conversely, factors exceeding zero, particularly those with a green background, highlight situations where substitution or cascading leads to significantly better environmental outcomes compared to the baseline. For instance, the example of plywood production shows a 90% reduction in climate change impacts through substitution, while short-term cascading in the same context only achieves a 10% reduction.
When examining greenhouse gas emissions and fossil fuel demand, wood substitution yields greater environmental savings than short-term wood cascading. Conversely, land occupation and particulate matter formation show the opposite trend. Therefore, wood should be utilized efficiently through cascading while prioritizing the replacement of non-wood products through substitution. However, the environmental impacts vary significantly across different wood products. For instance, substituting fiberboard is less beneficial due to the lower environmental impacts of gypsum fiberboard alternatives. The Swiss wood value chain exhibits diverse environmental characteristics.
Wood harvesting generally has minor environmental impacts, especially concerning greenhouse gas emissions and fossil energy consumption. However, the ReCiPe total method assigns significant weight to land occupation, leading to high biodiversity loss impacts for land-intensive activities like harvesting. This method fails to account for regional variations in forest management intensity or biodiversity. The high impacts it identifies are primarily associated with intensive harvesting and management practices, such as clear-cuts and plantations, which heavily affect biodiversity. In contrast, more extensive harvesting methods, like selective harvesting, have minimal impact on overall biodiversity. Given that Switzerland legally mandates sustainable forest use and exclusively employs selective harvesting, the ReCiPe total method likely overestimates the impacts in this study.
Wood used for energy production generates low greenhouse gas emissions. However, its combustion produces significant particulate matter, a known problem particularly with small heating systems. While solutions like particle filters exist, their widespread adoption in smaller facilities is still pending.
Producing wood-based boards, regardless of the impact method, incurs significant environmental costs per cubic meter. These impacts stem primarily from chemicals, resins, and processing energy. However, due to the relatively small quantities of boards used in Switzerland, their overall environmental footprint remains minor.
The pulp and paper sector, a significant user of wood, contributes substantially to environmental impacts across numerous categories. This is largely attributed to the sector's high energy demands, a finding consistent with previous research. In response, paper manufacturers are actively seeking to mitigate these impacts. One strategy involves substituting fossil fuels with biomass-based energy, often sourced internally during production. Chemical agents used in manufacturing also contribute to the environmental footprint. However, the effectiveness of Switzerland's robust recycling system, which utilizes 73% recycled paper for pulp, significantly lessens these impacts. Furthermore, the ongoing shift towards electronic media is expected to reduce the demand for graphic paper in the future.
Cross-border wood trade significantly shapes Switzerland's environmental impacts from wood use, meaning production abroad heavily influences domestic consumption impacts. This aligns with existing research. However, the study's reliance on non-regionalized life cycle inventories limits its ability to fully capture variations in impacts due to differing international production conditions. Consequently, the assessment of location-specific factors, such as biodiversity impacts which are highly sensitive to geographical context, is insufficient.
The MFA process modules are designed around outputs. This means each module aligns with the wood output of its process, not necessarily the wood input. Consequently, inventory chains may not precisely mirror material flows.
This study did not account for impacts during the product use phase. Indoor exposure to volatile organic compounds from adhesives and wood itself can occur, potentially leading to health effects if products are not managed properly.
For assessing short-term cascading potential, the study assumed waste wood was supplied without associated impacts from collection or sorting. Likewise, any necessary process adjustments for handling waste wood were not considered. The point at which primary wood is substituted by waste wood was not fixed at harvesting; savings could potentially be realized further down the production chain, such as through reduced drying energy requirements. However, research by Höglmeier et al. (2014) indicates that these energy savings from reduced drying are minimal, suggesting process adjustments have little impact on cascading benefits. Consequently, the methodology employed provides a consistent evaluation of short-term cascading potentials.
The potential for short-term cascading of wood to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and fossil energy demand appears limited. For wood boards, production impacts are primarily driven by chemical additives and process energy. While short-term cascading shows better results for ReCiPe total and ecological scarcity, this is largely due to land occupation impacts. These methods do not account for regional differences, and selective harvesting, common in Switzerland, has minimal impact on land use. Consequently, the ReCiPe method likely overestimates land-use-related impacts in this context.
The effectiveness of cascading wood use diminishes rapidly if waste wood collection becomes inefficient, as this impedes its optimal use for energy. Therefore, minimizing waste and ensuring effective end-of-life energy recovery are crucial. Moreover, expanding wood cascading, particularly by increasing the use of waste wood in material applications, must not result in greater reliance on primary wood for energy. Such a shift would mean substituting lower-quality waste wood with higher-quality primary wood in energy generation. Currently, from a resource management perspective, it is more advantageous to utilize high-quality wood for material purposes.
Wood cascading is becoming more significant, especially as wood resources grow scarcer. This approach can unlock more material and energy services by enabling more intensive use of limited biomass. Consequently, wood cascading can significantly bolster substitution efforts in such future scenarios.
Renewable resource substitution frequently lowers non-renewable consumption, thereby significantly mitigating specific environmental impacts. Studies consistently recognize these substitution benefits for wood, particularly concerning cumulative fossil energy demand and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. A meta-analysis by Sathre and O'Connor (2010) examined GHG displacement factors for wood product substitutions, estimating an emission reduction of approximately 3.9 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per metric tonne of dry wood. This translates to roughly 2 tonnes of CO2 equivalent reduction per cubic meter of wood, assuming a density of 500 kg/m³. A prior Swiss study reported a reduction potential of 1.3 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per cubic meter of wood used (Werner et al. 2010). While the average reduction calculated in the current study, 0.5 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per cubic meter, appears lower, direct comparisons between studies are challenging due to variations in system boundaries, assumptions regarding product lifespans, and the selection of products. Nevertheless, all research indicates a clear net GHG benefit when wood replaces equivalent non-wood materials, largely because wood products typically require less fossil energy during their production.
When examining impact categories beyond greenhouse gas emissions, the substitution benefits of wood become more varied and less consistently favorable. For energy applications, these other categories can substantially reduce the significant greenhouse gas savings. Similarly, in material use, particularly in construction, the perceived benefits of wood fluctuate considerably depending on the specific impact method employed. Wood's advantages diminish when considering aspects like air emissions from combustion, such as particulate matter, or land-use impacts, especially when forests are not managed sustainably. Therefore, careful management of wood and its associated flows is crucial to fully realize its environmental advantages.
In Switzerland, the current abundance of forest wood over demand means that short-term wood cascading primarily results in reduced forest extraction, rather than significant greenhouse gas or fossil energy savings. This situation changes if primary wood becomes scarce. In such a scenario, cascading becomes crucial for supplying wood that can replace less sustainable alternatives, thereby yielding environmental benefits. For greenhouse gas emissions, a "long-term cascading performance" approximation is presented in Table 3. When material losses are minimal, using wood for materials first and then for energy can be more advantageous than direct energy use. However, it's important to recognize that wood cascading inherently involves material downcycling. Therefore, to enhance the overall environmental benefits of wood use through combined substitution and cascading, minimizing losses and ensuring efficient final energy utilization are paramount.
This study's focus on current conditions overlooks future environmental implications. Wood products, particularly in construction, have extended lifespans, meaning their environmental impacts will manifest later. Delaying the energy use of wood, for instance, might mean it replaces cleaner future energy alternatives, diminishing its benefits compared to immediate use. Furthermore, the analysis does not incorporate the carbon storage within wood products. While this storage offers some environmental benefit, it is generally outweighed by the advantages gained from wood substituting fossil-fuel-based emissions.
In this analysis, the environmental impacts of final products were largely estimated using data from semifinished products. This methodological choice may affect comparisons based on functional units, as suggested by Gustavsson and Sathre (2010). However, the primary sources of environmental impact are likely still represented, as later processing stages, such as in furniture production, typically contribute only a small portion to the total impact (Wenker and Rüter 2012). The key environmental burdens often stem from the sourcing of raw materials or components rather than the final assembly.
The current study's exclusion of climate change impacts from biogenic CO2 emissions means that the environmental benefits of using wood for energy may be overstated. While the long-term climate effects of biogenic CO2 are minimal compared to fossil CO2, their impact is significant over shorter periods, such as 100 years. This temporal aspect is crucial when assessing biogenic carbon sequestration, which can offer additional advantages for wood used in long-term material applications like construction. Modeling the temporal distribution of these emissions is necessary for a complete assessment but was outside the scope of this research. Consequently, while the study's findings regarding long-term climate effects are robust, the short-term climate impacts of wood could be substantially different if biogenic carbon emissions were factored in. In certain scenarios, this could even diminish the perceived advantages of wood over alternative materials or energy sources. Future research is planned to address these short-term climate impacts by modeling the temporal patterns of wood use and the effects of biogenic carbon emissions in Switzerland.
This research examines the environmental performance of wood across various impact categories, using Switzerland as a case study. The findings indicate that wood utilization aids in mitigating environmental impacts, notably by reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and decreasing fossil energy consumption. These primary savings stem from substituting conventional materials and energy sources with wood. However, wood is not without its environmental drawbacks. To fully leverage wood's environmental benefits and minimize its negative aspects, a thoughtful and strategic approach to its use is essential. Key considerations include addressing environmental "hotspots" such as the significant particulate matter emissions from wood combustion, and the impacts of harvesting on land occupation and biodiversity. Implementing solutions like particle filters and sourcing wood from sustainably managed forests employing selective harvesting practices can help alleviate these issues. Furthermore, adopting a cascade-like approach to wood utilization can enhance resource efficiency. Crucially, the environmental benefits of cascading depend on the specific products being substituted and the completeness and efficiency of the final energy recovery from wood. Given the current underutilization of wood resources in Switzerland, increasing the use of primary wood is a priority. Finally, a comprehensive systems perspective that encompasses the entire value chains of wood products and their alternatives is vital. This holistic view enables a balanced assessment of various use options, ultimately guiding decisions towards minimizing overall environmental impacts.
