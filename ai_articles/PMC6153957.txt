This study aimed to functionally characterize the 3q28 bladder cancer risk locus, building upon previous genome-wide association studies (GWAS) that pinpointed a bladder cancer susceptibility variant, rs710521. This variant is located in a non-coding intergenic region between the TP63 and LEPREL1 genes on chromosome 3q28, suggesting its involvement in regulating these genes' transcription. To achieve this, fine-mapping was conducted on the area around rs710521, and variants were selected for further investigation based on ENCODE regulatory data. The enhancer activity of this identified region was then assessed using dual-luciferase assays. Subsequently, CRISPR/Cas9 technology was employed to delete the enhancer region, and the impact of this deletion on cell proliferation and gene expression was measured using CellTiter-Glo and RT-qPCR, respectively. The fine-mapping process revealed twenty single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a stronger association to bladder cancer risk than rs710521. Leveraging publicly available regulatory data, an enhancer region containing these risk variants was identified. Reporter assays demonstrated that this enhancer region significantly boosted ΔNTP63 promoter activity in bladder cancer cell lines. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of this enhancer reduced the viability of bladder cancer cells by lowering the expression of ΔNTP63 and its target genes. In conclusion, the findings indicate that bladder cancer risk-associated variants on chromosome 3q28 reside within an active enhancer region. Future research focusing on the allele-specific activity of this enhancer and its target genes could uncover new signaling pathways crucial to bladder carcinogenesis.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have recently pinpointed several bladder cancer risk loci, with 14 such regions, including TP63, c-MYC, and TERT/CLPTM1L, now documented. However, the genetic variants identified by GWAS are typically not directly functional. These single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) instead often correlate with unmeasured causal variants, necessitating further fine-mapping. A significant challenge arises because most of these variants are located in non-coding intronic or intergenic regions. To date, only four of these GWAS-identified loci—PSCA, UGT1A, CCNE1, and APOBEC3B—have undergone functional characterization, revealing allele-specific gene expression regulation.
Non-coding genomic regions play a crucial role in gene expression regulation, as highlighted by the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) initiative. ENCODE data indicate that single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) frequently reside within active regulatory elements, which often exhibit cell-type and disease specificity. A non-coding SNP can influence gene expression both locally (in cis) and at distant sites (in trans) through various mechanisms. A well-researched example is the colorectal cancer risk SNP rs6983267, situated in a gene-poor region on chromosome 8q24. This SNP was observed to boost TCF4 transcription factor binding within an enhancer. This enhancer subsequently interacts with and allele-specifically modulates the c-MYC promoter, resulting in elevated c-MYC expression when the risk allele is present. Further research revealed that rs6983267 is located within the long non-coding RNA CCAT2. Colorectal cancer cells expressing the risk G allele of CCAT2 showed significantly higher CCAT2 transcript levels. CCAT2, in turn, positively regulates c-MYC transcription, influencing colorectal cancer cell growth, metastasis, and energy metabolism in an allele-specific manner.
The intergenic region between the TP63 and LEPREL1 genes on chromosome 3q28 harbors a bladder cancer risk SNP, rs710521. This A > G variant, where A is the risk-increasing allele, is situated within a linkage disequilibrium block that includes the TP63 gene. TP63, a member of the p53 transcription factor family, produces two isoforms, TATP63 and ΔNTP63, which exert opposing effects on cell cycle control and apoptosis. Beyond its role in epithelial development and squamous epithelium formation, p63 expression is also implicated in cancer progression. Specifically, in non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), changes in p63 expression are inversely related to pathological grade, while in muscle invasive tumors (MIBC), p63 expression is often reduced. The other gene in this 3q28 locus, LEPREL1, codes for a Leprecan family proteoglycan crucial for collagen's posttranslational modification and protein stability. LEPREL1 expression patterns vary across cancers, being downregulated in breast and hepatocellular carcinoma, but upregulated in thyroid cancer.
This study functionally characterized genetic variants linked to bladder cancer risk within the TP63/LEPREL1 gene locus, specifically fine-mapping the rs710521 GWAS signal. Leveraging public regulatory data, an enhancer region housing these fine-mapped risk SNPs was identified. Our findings demonstrate that this enhancer boosts ΔNTP63 promoter activity and influences cell viability. Therefore, we conclude that bladder cancer-associated SNPs at the 3q28 locus reside in an enhancer that impacts bladder cancer progression by regulating gene expression.
The fine-mapping process involved an association analysis of single nucleotide variant (SNV) data. This analysis focused on a 500 kb region surrounding rs710521 and utilized data from 1601 bladder cancer patients and 1819 controls within the Nijmegen Bladder Cancer Study (NBCS). Genotyping of NBCS participants was conducted using either the Illumina Human370CNV-Duo or Quad BeadChip. Imputation of this data was performed with IMPUTE v2.1 software, referencing a training set that combined 1000 Genomes low-coverage pilot haplotypes (June 2010 release, 120 chromosomes) and HapMap3 haplotypes (February 2009 release, 1920 chromosomes). Exclusions from imputation included individuals with less than 96% yield for 292,650 autosomal SNPs on both chips, and SNPs exhibiting a minor allele frequency below 0.01, deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P < 10–5), or differing frequencies between chip types (P < 10–5). Case-control association analyses for all SNVs were executed using SNPtest (version 2.4), with the most frequent homozygous genotype serving as the reference.
To identify active regulatory regions, variants with stronger bladder cancer risk association than rs710521 were prioritized using ENCODE, Regulome DB, and Haploreg data. Selection criteria included histone modifications indicating active chromatin, DNaseI hypersensitive sites, and transcription factor binding.
Five bladder cancer cell lines—5637, 647 V, RT-112, SW800, and T24—were cultivated in RPMI-1640 medium, enriched with 10% fetal calf serum and L-glutamine. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified environment. In 2016, these cell lines underwent authentication via the PowerPlex 21 PCR kit and were confirmed free of mycoplasma contamination.
To assess chromatin accessibility, FAIRE analysis was conducted following established methods. Initially, 647 V and RT112 bladder cancer cells were cultured in 10-cm dishes until they reached confluence. These cells were then cross-linked for 5 minutes with 37% formaldehyde from Sigma-Aldrich. Following cross-linking, the cells were lysed, and the resulting lysates were sonicated on ice for 15 minutes (comprising 15 cycles of 30 seconds on and 30 seconds off) using a Bioruptor sonicator from Diagenode. Both the input control DNA and the FAIRE DNA were subsequently isolated according to the manufacturer's instructions. FAIRE enrichment was quantified via FAIRE-qPCR, utilizing 5 μl (approximately 5 ng/μl) of DNA as input, SYBR Green qPCR reagent from Roche, and a LightCycler LC480 instrument, also from Roche. Relative FAIRE enrichment for each amplicon was determined by calculating the ratio of the FAIRE sample signal to the input control DNA signal. Supplementary Table 1 provides a list of the primers employed.
To prepare for reporter assays, DNA was first extracted from fresh frozen normal bladder tissue (adjacent to tumors) using a QIAamp DNA Mini kit. Subsequently, the ΔNTP63 promoter, LEPREL1 promoter, and the intergenic enhancer region were amplified via PCR with PrimeSTAR HS DNA polymerase and GC buffer; specific primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The E1 enhancer, which includes both risk (AA) and non-risk (GG) haplotypes, was isolated by amplifying the segment containing rs4687103 and rs4687104. Haplotypes were identified using Haploview software. For constructing reporter vectors, the ΔNTP63 and LEPREL1 promoter regions were initially ligated into a pGL3-Basic vector, positioned upstream of the luciferase gene, utilizing BglII and HindIII restriction sites. Following this, the amplified enhancer region was inserted downstream of the luciferase gene in these promoter-containing pGL3 vectors, employing BamHI and SalI restriction sites. Plasmid DNA was then isolated using a QIAGEN Plasmid Midi kit. All steps adhered to manufacturer guidelines, and Sanger sequencing confirmed the integrity of the resulting vectors.
For enhancer reporter assays, bladder cancer cells were cultured in 96-well plates until reaching 70–80% confluence. They were then transfected with 900 ng of reporter vector and 100 ng of the pRL-TK control vector (Promega), using X-tremeGENE 9 transfection reagent (Roche). After two days, a Dual-Luciferase reporter assay system (Promega) was employed to measure luciferase activity with a Victor3 multilabel reader (PerkinElmer). Firefly luciferase signals were normalized against Renilla luciferase signals. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate and repeated at least three times, with results compared to pGL3-ΔNTP63 or pGL3-LEPREL1 promoter activities.
To target the TP63 enhancer region, guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed using the CRISPR Design tool (http://crispr.mit.edu); their sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The gRNA cloning vector, originally from George Church (Addgene plasmid #41824), underwent prior modifications. Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Invitrogen and then annealed following the Church Laboratory's gRNA design and cloning protocol, utilizing Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB). The gRNA cloning vector was linearized with AflII (NEB), and target sequence inserts were integrated via Gibson assembly (NEB), as per manufacturer instructions. The resulting cloning products were transformed into DH5α competent E.coli (NEB) cells. Subsequently, plasmid DNA was isolated and sequenced as previously detailed.
To generate cell lines with the E1 enhancer region deleted, 5637 bladder cancer cells were initially cultured in 6-well plates until they reached 70–80% confluence. These cells then underwent co-transfection with an hCas9 expression vector (Addgene plasmid #41815, from George Church) and two gRNA vectors designed to target the sequences flanking the enhancer. X-tremeGENE 9 transfection reagent from Roche was used, following the manufacturer's guidelines. Three days post-transfection, the cells were trypsinized and subsequently re-seeded at lower densities into 10-cm dishes. DNA was extracted from the remaining cells using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN), and deletion-specific PCR, employing SuperTaq DNA polymerase (ThermoFisher Scientific), was performed to confirm the 2.2 kb E1 enhancer deletion. This confirmation involved agarose gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing of the PCR products. The efficiency of the CRISPR/Cas9 system was assessed by determining the ratio of deleted to non-deleted cells (Supplementary Fig. A). After a two-week period, individual cell colonies were collected, and their DNA was isolated using a NucleoSpin Tissue XS kit (Macherey-Nagel). Finally, deletion-specific PCR was again utilized to identify and select the desired E1-deleted clones (Supplementary Fig. B).
To assess gene expression, CRISPR/Cas9-modified cell lines were grown in 6-well plates until 70–80% confluent. Total RNA was then extracted using TRIzol reagent, and its yield quantified with a Nanodrop ND-1000 system. Following DNaseI treatment, cDNA was synthesized from the RNA using random hexamer primers and Superscript II reverse transcriptase. Gene expression levels were subsequently determined via SYBR Green qPCR analysis on a LightCycler LC480 instrument. HP1BP3 mRNA expression served as a normalization control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with comparisons made against wild-type, non-deleted cells. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.
Cell viability was determined at various time points using a Promega CellTiter-Glo luminescence assay after seeding cells in 96-well plates. A PerkinElmer Victor3 multilabel reader measured the luminescence. Experiments were conducted in triplicate, repeated at least three times, and results were compared to non-deleted cells.
For eQTL analysis, rs4687103 genotype data were obtained from 412 MIBC patients within The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project. To ensure population homogeneity, only 297 patients of Northern and Western European ancestry (CEU) from Utah were selected. Gene expression data for these patients were sourced from the Broad Institute GDAC Firehose. Patients exhibiting alterations in the TP63, LEPREL1, or p63 target genes (FGFR3, NOTCH1, KRT5, KRT6A), as documented in cBioPortal, were excluded from the eQTL analysis. This resulted in the exclusion of 27, 35, 23, 31, 15, and 15 patients, respectively, for these genes. The study then compared gene expression levels across different rs4687103 genotypes (GG, GA, and AA). Additional clinical data were also acquired from cBioPortal.
For FAIRE, enhancer reporter, cell proliferation, and gene expression assays, statistical analysis utilized GraphPad with a one-way ANOVA test. IBM SPSS and Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank test) were employed to assess survival associations with the rs4687103 genotype. A p-value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance for all analyses.
Fine-mapping efforts focused on the 3q28 locus, initially identified by the GWAS hit rs710521. Analysis of bladder cancer patients and controls revealed that the rs710521 GA genotype had an odds ratio (OR) of 0.78 (95% confidence interval 0.68–0.90) compared to the AA genotype. For the GG genotype versus AA, the OR was 0.69 (95% CI 0.52–0.91), with a significant p-value of 0.00034741 for the general genotype model, indicating an association with reduced bladder cancer risk. Beyond rs710521, an additional 20 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) demonstrated an even stronger association with a decreased risk of bladder cancer. These SNPs exhibited high linkage disequilibrium (LD; r2 > 0.8) with rs710521, sharing comparable allele frequencies and ORs, as detailed in Figure 1a and Supplementary Table 2. A detailed examination of the TP63 and LEPREL1 gene locus, which encompasses these 20 fine-mapped bladder cancer risk SNPs, was conducted. The identified SNPs were found to cluster within or near two distinct enhancer regions, designated E1 and E2. These regions were classified as enhancers based on ENCODE regulatory data, characterized by the presence of H3K27Ac histone marks, DNaseI hypersensitive sites, and transcription factor binding sites. Specifically, rs4687100 was located in the last intron of the TP63 gene. Enhancer region 1 (E1) contained several SNPs, including rs1839072, rs35868376, rs1447932, rs1447933, rs4479569, rs4687103, rs4687104, rs34666239, rs35076301, and rs35075630. Rs13089435 was situated near E1, but in an area lacking H2K27Ac marks. Other SNPs, such as rs62278269, rs35392448, rs13059523, rs12491886, rs12485497, and rs4687108, were located in a non-active region. Rs837775 was found upstream of E2, also in a non-active region. The original GWAS SNP, rs710521, along with rs56031831, were located within enhancer region 2 (E2). The numbers within the DNase clusters indicate the count of ENCODE cell lines where the region showed DNase sensitivity. Regulatory data specific to urothelia further confirmed a DNaseI hypersensitive peak and enrichment in an open chromatin structure, as revealed by FAIRE analysis. Transcription factor binding (Txn) was determined through ChIP analysis. These genomic images were sourced and adapted from the UCSC Genome Browser (hg19).
Identifying functional non-coding variants from GWAS results presents a challenge, though specific DNA region characteristics can guide interpretation. To prioritize 21 bladder cancer-associated SNPs within the TP63 region, publicly available regulatory data were analyzed. This analysis revealed that between 57.2% and 85.7% of these SNPs were situated in enhancer-characteristic histone mark regions, while 19.1% to 33.3% were in DNaseI hypersensitive sites. Furthermore, 17 SNPs were predicted to modify transcription factor binding sites. Specifically, rs4687103 and rs4687104 were found within multiple transcription factor binding sites. Notably, rs4687104 was uniquely predicted to alter transcription factor binding motifs (Regulome DB) and was located in an active chromatin region in urothelium, characterized by a DNaseI hypersensitive, nucleosome-free site, indicating potential regulatory function. Consequently, based on these indicators of active chromatin, a putative enhancer region (E1) encompassing several SNPs, including rs4687103 and rs4687104, was chosen for subsequent functional investigations.
To validate the active, open chromatin state of the predicted enhancer region, as indicated by ENCODE data (Fig. 1b), we conducted FAIRE analysis to assess nucleosome occupancy in bladder cancer cells. This analysis revealed a significant FAIRE enrichment in the identified enhancer region E1 within two bladder cancer cell lines, RT112 and 647 V. This enrichment confirms a nucleosome-depleted, and therefore open, chromatin structure. Specifically, the E1 region, particularly around rs4687103, exhibited greater FAIRE enrichment compared to a non-active heterochromatin region within the TP63/LEPREL1 locus and the non-expressed prostate-specific PCA3 gene locus. Furthermore, the active chromatin enrichment observed in this enhancer region was comparable to that of the c-MYC promoter region. These findings collectively support the potential regulatory function of the identified enhancer in bladder cancer cells (Fig. 2a). The subsequent Fig. 2 also details the experimental setup for analyzing ΔNTP63/LEPREL1 promoter activity, including a schematic of the reporter vector and the results of enhancer activity using both risk and non-risk alleles in different cell lines.
Given the established role of enhancer regions in gene expression regulation, we posited that the identified enhancer, housing bladder cancer risk SNPs, might specifically activate the ΔNTP63 and/or LEPREL1 gene promoters in a cis-acting manner. ΔNTP63 is the predominant TP63 isoform in normal urothelium and bladder cancer. To test this, we constructed reporter vectors incorporating either the ΔNTP63 or LEPREL1 promoter alongside the E1 enhancer region, which included both risk and non-risk haplotypes. Our experiments revealed that the E1 fragment, encompassing most transcription factor binding sites (including rs4687103 and rs4687104), substantially boosted ΔNTP63 promoter activity across all three cell lines, irrespective of whether minor or major alleles were present. However, no such regulatory influence of E1 was observed on LEPREL1 promoter activity. Furthermore, we did not detect any significant allele-specific effects on E1 enhancer activity.
To investigate the cellular impact of the identified enhancer region, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 technology to delete it within 5637 bladder cancer cells (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). This deletion significantly reduced cell viability. Specifically, homozygous deletion of the E1 enhancer resulted in a notable decrease in cell viability when compared to wild-type, non-deleted cells (Fig. 3b). A similar, though less pronounced, reduction in cell viability was observed with heterozygous E1 deletion using gRNA set 1 (Fig. 3b). Beyond cell viability, the deletion of E1 also impacted gene expression in cis. Both homozygous and heterozygous E1 deletions led to decreased levels of ΔNTP63 (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, the expression of LEPREL1, a gene adjacent to TP63, was also found to be lower in E1-deleted cells (Fig. 3d). We also examined the expression of two known p63 target genes, FGFR3 and NOTCH1. Previous findings indicated a positive correlation between ΔNTP63 expression and the expression of both FGFR3 and NOTCH1 in normal urothelium and MIBC tissue samples (Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistent with these observations, we found that FGFR3 and NOTCH1 expression levels were likewise reduced in cells where E1 had been deleted (Fig. 3e and f, respectively). The experimental approach involved targeting the enhancer region with two independent sets of gRNAs (gRNA 1 and gRNA 2). All data from E1-deleted single-cell colonies were compared to the average values from wild-type, non-deleted cells in corresponding experiments. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate and repeated at least three times.
Genetic variations, specifically single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), can lead to allele-specific differences in gene expression among individuals. Our investigation into the rs4687103 genotype revealed several such correlations in gene expression. For instance, individuals homozygous for the G allele exhibited elevated TP63 expression compared to heterozygous individuals (Fig. 4a). Conversely, LEPREL1 expression levels did not show significant variation based on rs4687103 genotypes (Fig. 4b). Further analysis of p63 target genes demonstrated a notable correlation between NOTCH1 expression and the rs4687103 genotype. Individuals carrying the non-risk G allele in a homozygous state displayed significantly higher NOTCH1 expression than those with the risk A allele (Fig. 4c). However, no allele-specific expression was observed for FGFR3, another p63 target gene (Fig. 4d). Additionally, KRT5 expression was significantly higher in individuals homozygous for the non-risk G allele (Fig. 4e), while KRT6A expression did not show this pattern (Fig. 4f). Regarding clinical outcomes, we assessed the impact of the rs4687103 genotype on survival in 297 muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) patients from the TCGA project. While no statistically significant differences in overall survival (OS) were found (p = 0.178, Supplementary Fig. 3A), there was a trend towards shorter OS in MIBC patients with GG genotypes compared to GA/AA genotypes (p = 0.065, Supplementary Fig. 3B).
Bladder cancer susceptibility is influenced by intricate genetic and environmental interactions. Prior research has pinpointed various genetic variants linked to bladder cancer risk, revealing new genes and mechanisms in its development. Our study focused on fine-mapping a GWAS signal at the 3q28 locus, demonstrating that a region with bladder cancer-associated SNPs regulates gene expression and impacts cell survival.
An enhancer region was identified using publicly available regulatory element data. This region significantly boosted ΔNTP63 promoter activity in bladder cancer cells, though this effect was not allele-specific. The modest impact of genetic variants found in GWAS on gene expression, coupled with the limited sensitivity of current assays, might explain the absence of observed allele-specific effects. Additionally, an enhancer can influence multiple target genes, and a single gene can be regulated by several enhancers. CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing offers a way to evaluate the impact of individual genetic variants within specific genomic or cellular environments. Future advancements in CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homology directed repair (HDR) efficiency could improve functional studies of GWAS variants. Transcriptional factor expression is tightly regulated, which might preclude observing significant allele-specific effects on their promoter activity or expression. Nevertheless, allele-specific differences can emerge through the expression of target genes regulated by these transcription factors. Indeed, a significant correlation was found between the risk SNP genotype and the expression levels of NOTCH1, a ΔNTP63 target gene. The Notch signaling pathway plays a role in bladder cancer development, although its precise oncogenic or tumor-suppressive function remains unclear. For instance, elevated Jagged2 expression has been linked to aggressive tumors and metastasis, while NOTCH inactivating mutations have been shown to accelerate bladder cancer development and promote squamous cell carcinoma formation.
The correlation between ΔNTP63 levels and the risk SNP genotype has been observed in MIBC tissue samples. Given that smoking is a primary bladder cancer risk factor, contributing to half of all tumors, it's conceivable that subtle allele-specific variations in ΔNTP63 expression emerge following DNA damage, such as that induced by smoking. Earlier research indicated that genotoxic stress causes ΔN-p63 to bind to a p53-binding element in its own promoter, thereby silencing ΔNTP63 expression. Furthermore, identifying eQTLs in tumor tissue is often difficult due to numerous genetic and epigenetic changes impacting transcript levels.
Our investigation revealed that deleting the enhancer region in 5637 bladder cancer cells reduced cell viability, alongside decreased mRNA expression of both ΔNTP63 and its target genes. This aligns with prior research demonstrating that silencing ΔNTP63 in 5637 cells, through shRNA, causes cell cycle arrest, slows proliferation, and downregulates Cyclin D1. Furthermore, ΔNTP63 knockdown in a 5637 xenograft mouse model inhibited tumor growth by inducing apoptosis. Other studies have shown that reduced ΔNTP63 expression can sensitize 5637 cells to DNA damage-induced apoptosis, independent of p53, and that ΔNTP63 knockdown increased adhesion and decreased migration by elevating F-actin levels. However, one study reported that ΔNTP63 knockdown led to increased N-cadherin and enhanced motility and invasion in 5637 cells.
Deletion of the E1 enhancer region resulted in reduced expression of both ΔNTP63 and LEPREL1. While LEPREL1's role in bladder cancer remains unexplored, its significance has been noted in other cancers. For instance, in thyroid carcinoma, LEPREL1 acts as a target for the TWIST1 transcription factor, which is linked to metastasis and poor prognosis. Downregulating LEPREL1 has been shown to curb thyroid cancer cell growth. Conversely, overexpression of LEPREL1 in hepatocellular and breast cancers has been observed to hinder cell proliferation and colony formation.
Research has categorized MIBC into luminal and basal subtypes. The basal subtype is notably linked to reduced overall survival and exhibits dysregulation of p63 target genes. A key indicator of this basal subtype is the elevated expression of basal keratins like KRT5 and KRT6A. ΔNp63 is known to regulate the expression of both KRT5 and KRT6A across various tissues. Interestingly, the rs4687103 non-risk allele showed a significant correlation with increased KRT5 expression, though not KRT6A. This suggests the 3q28 risk locus promotes basal/squamous differentiation in tumors, contributing to a shorter overall survival.
The TP63 E1 enhancer region contains rs4687103 and rs4687104 SNPs, which overlap with several transcription factor binding sites. These include STAT3 and c-MYC, implicated in basal/squamous bladder cancer subtype development. Notably, STAT3, FOS, c-MYC, and CEBPB also bind to the KRT5 and KRT6A gene promoter regions. Future research should investigate allele-specific binding of transcription factors to the E1 enhancer in both healthy urothelium and bladder cancer cells.
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully pinpointed genetic variations that influence gene expression in bladder cancer. For instance, the missense variant rs2294008 in the PSCA gene results in a truncated PSCA protein due to a modified start codon. This variant allele is linked to reduced PSCA promoter activity. Similarly, the protective T allele of rs17863783, located within the UGT1A locus, is associated with elevated UGT1A6.1 expression, which enhances carcinogen clearance from the urothelium and lowers bladder cancer risk. In another example, carriers of the risk T allele of rs10775480, found in an intron of SLC14A1, exhibit significantly reduced urine concentration. Within the CCNE1 locus, both the original GWAS SNP rs8102137 and the CCNE1 promoter variant rs7257330 are linked to increased bladder cancer aggressiveness, characterized by higher CCNE1 protein expression. Furthermore, APOBEC3B expression and the prevalence of APOBEC3B-signature mutations correlate with rs1014971. A SNP in the non-coding region upstream of APOBEC3A also impacts protein binding in an allele-specific manner. More recently, a bladder cancer risk variant in the 3’-UTR of the TP63 gene was identified, which disrupts miR-140-5p binding and leads to allele-specific TP63 expression. Further functional research is needed to fully understand the molecular mechanisms through which these and other GWAS-identified SNPs contribute to bladder cancer risk.
Our study highlights the significance of GWAS-identified signals in non-coding regions for bladder cancer development. We pinpointed an enhancer region within the TP63/LEPREL1 intergenic locus, housing bladder cancer risk SNPs. This region regulates gene expression in cis, thereby influencing tumor cell viability. Further investigation of this area could reveal new allele-specific pathways impacting bladder cancer susceptibility.
