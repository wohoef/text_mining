This study investigated whether early and advanced ovarian cancers represent distinct molecular entities or simply different stages of the same disease, given their differing clinical presentations. The researchers analyzed genomic aberrations in 16 early-stage and 16 advanced-stage ovarian carcinomas, carefully matched for histological subtype and differentiation grade, using array comparative genomic hybridization. The findings revealed that while the patterns of genomic aberrations were similar between early and advanced stages, these aberrations were significantly more prevalent in advanced-stage cancers (p = 0.012). Further analysis involved clustering tumors based on their genomic aberration profiles. This unsupervised clustering produced two distinct groups that effectively differentiated between early and advanced stages (p = 0.001) and also showed a significant difference in patient survival (p = 0.002). Notably, these genomic aberration-based clusters provided a more precise prognosis than traditional factors like histological subtype or differentiation grade. In conclusion, the study suggests that advanced ovarian cancer likely develops either from an earlier stage or from a shared precursor, rather than originating from entirely separate molecular carcinogenic pathways. The results also highlight the potential of array comparative genomic hybridization as a tool for identifying patients with clinically relevant distinctions.

Ovarian cancer remains the most fatal gynecological malignancy in Western countries, despite recent improvements in survival rates. While early-stage diagnoses boast a 93% five-year survival, only 19% of patients are diagnosed at this stage. Conversely, advanced-stage ovarian cancer has a significantly lower five-year survival rate of just 25%.
The precise molecular mechanisms driving the development of epithelial ovarian cancer, like other malignancies, remain unclear, though it is believed to stem from an accumulation of genomic aberrations. While precursor lesions like intraepithelial neoplasia are identified in cancers such as cervical and anal cancer, a definitive precursor for ovarian cancer has not been established. Consequently, the earliest clinical manifestation suitable for studying ovarian cancer carcinogenesis in patients is minimal localized cancer (FIGO stage I). Given that only a small fraction of patients receive an early-stage diagnosis, it has been proposed that early and advanced ovarian cancers constitute separate subtypes. Early-stage ovarian cancer, characterized by a favorable prognosis, might represent a distinct biological entity with limited metastatic potential, suggesting a different carcinogenic pathway. There is a hypothesis that these early cases are molecularly distinct from advanced-stage ovarian cancer, which carries a poorer prognosis.
To investigate if early and advanced ovarian cancers represent distinct molecular entities, we utilized array comparative genomic hybridization (array CGH). This technique, applicable to formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue (FFPE), allowed us to identify and compare genomic aberrations in matched tumor samples. Copy number changes are a significant indicator of genetic instability in cancer. Our study also examined the relationship between these aberrations and the clinical characteristics of the tumors.
From a larger pool of 448 participants in the EORTC ACTION trial, 52 early-stage ovarian cancer patients (FIGO stage I) provided formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary ovarian tumor tissue. Out of these 52, seventeen early-stage samples, possessing high-quality DNA, were carefully selected. These selected samples were then paired with advanced-stage (FIGO stage III–IV) ovarian cancer samples obtained from the Pathology Departments of the University Medical Center Utrecht and VU University Medical Center Amsterdam in the Netherlands. This matching process ensured consistency in histological subtype and differentiation grade between the early and advanced stage groups. The FIGO staging for the advanced-stage cancer patients was primarily determined through optimal staging procedures, with the exception of four cases. The staging protocols varied, including optimal (7 patients), modified (3 patients), and minimal (7 patients) approaches, as detailed in previous research. Optimal staging involved a comprehensive examination, including visual inspection and palpation of all peritoneal surfaces, biopsies of any suspicious metastatic lesions, peritoneal washing, infracolic omentectomy, and blind biopsies of various anatomical sites, along with lymph node sampling. Modified staging encompassed procedures falling between optimal and minimal. Minimal staging, in contrast, was limited to visual inspection and palpation of peritoneal and retroperitoneal areas, biopsies of suspicious lesions, peritoneal washing, and infracolic omentectomy.
An experienced gynaecological/oncological pathologist (PvD) re-examined all samples. The pathologist determined the tumor percentage for each case, with a mean of 77.8% (SD 7.5), a median of 80%, and a range of 65–95%. Adhering to institutional ethical guidelines, samples were processed anonymously. Follow-up data for early-stage tumors came from the EORTC ACTION trial database, while advanced-stage tumor data were obtained from the hospital information system. Overall and progression-free survival were calculated from randomization (early-stage) or surgery date (advanced-stage).
The study participants, detailed in Table 1, exhibited an age range of 27 to 80 years. The average age for individuals with early-stage carcinomas was 52 years, while those with advanced-stage carcinomas had a higher average age of 62 years. While efforts were made to precisely match cases by tumor grade, an exact match was not always feasible. In six instances where an exact grade match was unavailable, a single grade difference was accepted. Specifically, four cases involved matching a grade 1 early-stage tumor with a grade 2 advanced-stage tumor, and two cases involved pairing a grade 2 early-stage tumor with a grade 3 advanced-stage tumor. Table 1 also provides additional patient characteristics, including histological subtypes (clearcell, endometrioid, mucinous, serous), adjuvant chemotherapy administration, staging details, and survival outcomes (overall and progression-free survival), along with their assigned cluster from the unsupervised analysis.
The experimental procedure began with the preparation of tumor tissue. Sections of 10 μm from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples were cut, and the tumor regions were verified through concurrent haematoxylin and eosin staining. Subsequently, DNA extraction was carried out using the QIAamp DNA micro kit from Qiagen, following established methods [7, 12]. The quality of the DNA, including its concentration and suitability for labeling, was assessed with a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). For array Comparative Genomic Hybridization (CGH), DNA was labeled with cyanine 3-dUTP (Cy3) and cyanine 5-dUTP (Cy5) nucleotides, utilizing the array CGH labeling kit for oligo arrays as per the manufacturer's instructions (Enzo Life Sciences). A pooled DNA sample from eighteen healthy women served as the normal reference [13]. Unincorporated nucleotides were then removed using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). The prepared DNA was hybridized overnight to 105 K whole-genome Oxford design microarrays (Agilent Technologies), which feature over 99,000 unique 60-mer oligonucleotides, using the oligo array CGH/Chip-Chip Hybridization Kit 25 (Agilent). Each slide accommodated two arrays, with one reference sample hybridized for every three tumor samples [13]. After hybridization, slides underwent washing and were scanned with an Agilent G2505B microarray scanner. All raw data from these arrays are accessible in the GEO database (accession number GSE24418).
The quality of the array CGH data was evaluated by calculating the median absolute deviation (MAD) of log2 ratios for a chromosome arm lacking breakpoints [14]. While most samples exhibited MAD values between 0.17 and 0.43 for copy number changes on the q-arm of chromosome two, one advanced stage sample presented a MAD value of 0.70. Consequently, this outlier sample and its paired early stage counterpart were removed from subsequent analysis, resulting in a final dataset of 16 early and 16 advanced stage cases. A power analysis was conducted on this reduced set of 32 samples. Data processing involved several steps: log2 ratios underwent median normalization across the array [13], and wave patterns were smoothed [15]. Circular binary segmentation (CBS) was employed for segmentation, a method chosen for its effectiveness in minimizing false positives arising from local data trends [16, 17]. Before the automatic detection of DNA losses, gains, and amplifications, mode normalization was applied to the segmented data [16]. To reduce dimensionality, data were summarized into regions with a 0.01 threshold, allowing for a maximum of 1% information loss. The frequency of aberrations was estimated for both groups. To identify differences in aberration occurrence between the groups, false discovery rate (FDR) corrected p-values (Chi-square) were computed after dividing the genome into distinct regions [18, 19]. An FDR of 15% was used, ensuring the detection of over 50% of discriminative aberrations [20]. To investigate if advanced stage disease develops from early stage, the study assessed whether advanced stage samples generally displayed higher odds for aberrations compared to early stage samples (further details are available in supplementary materials). Weighted unsupervised clustering of the array CGH data, as previously described [21, 22], was then performed. The resulting clusters were subsequently correlated with overall and progression-free survival using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank testing (SPSS software package version 17.0, Chicago, IL, USA). All other analytical procedures were carried out using the R statistical framework [23].
Genomic aberration patterns in advanced stage ovarian cancer closely resembled those observed in early stage disease. However, a notable difference was the significantly higher frequency of these aberrations across the entire genome in advanced stage cases compared to early stage cases (p = 0.012), as illustrated in Figure 1a. While individual patient tumor profiles exhibited heterogeneity when viewed at a granular level (Figure 1b), a greater degree of similarity emerged when these profiles were grouped by FIGO stage and histological subtype (Figure 2). This stratification further revealed that the increase in aberrations was particularly prominent within the clearcell and endometrioid subtypes. Figure 1a specifically details the frequencies of both copy number gains (top panel) and losses (bottom panel) in the 16 early and 16 advanced stage samples, along with the false discovery rate corrected p-values for differences between stages. Figure 1b provides a visual representation of array CGH profiles for all samples, color-coded to indicate DNA copy number changes, while Figure 2 organizes these profiles by histological type and FIGO stage.
As anticipated, advanced stage ovarian cancer patients exhibited a significantly poorer survival rate compared to those with early stage disease (p < 0.001). Interestingly, grouping tumors by histological subtype did not reveal any significant differences in survival. Conversely, patients with well-differentiated (grade one) tumors demonstrated markedly improved survival when compared to individuals with tumors of intermediate or poor differentiation (grades two and three combined) (p = 0.044).
To investigate the relationship between genomic aberrations and tumor clinical behavior, a weighted clustering analysis was conducted using array CGH data. This analysis generated two distinct groups: Cluster A, comprising 19 tumor samples, and Cluster B, with 13 samples (Fig. 3). Cluster A predominantly consisted of early-stage tumors, with 14 early-stage and 5 advanced-stage cases. Among these, 4 advanced-stage and 2 early-stage tumors experienced recurrence. Conversely, Cluster B was characterized by a higher proportion of advanced-stage tumors, containing 11 advanced-stage and 2 early-stage cases. Recurrence was observed in 10 advanced-stage and 1 early-stage tumor within this cluster. The distribution of FIGO stages between these clusters was statistically significant (Pearson Chi-square p = 0.001). A notable difference in survival was observed between the two clusters. Patients in Cluster A demonstrated significantly better survival, with a mean survival of 142.0 months (95% CI: 112.7–171.2), compared to patients in Cluster B, who had a mean survival of 43.0 months (95% CI: 27.4–58.6) (Mantel Cox log rank p = 0.002) (Fig. 4). Figure 3, a heatmap of the unsupervised clustering, visually represents DNA copy number changes (blue for loss, black for normal, yellow for gain) and includes tumor characteristics like FIGO stage, histological type, and differentiation grade. The dendrogram illustrates the similarity of array CGH profiles. Figure 4 presents a Kaplan-Meier plot, highlighting the survival disparity between the two clusters.
Our study utilized array CGH to compare chromosomal copy number aberrations in early and advanced stage ovarian carcinomas. We initially hypothesized that their differing clinical presentations implied distinct molecular developmental pathways. However, our findings revealed that advanced stage cancers exhibited more frequent aberrations, with nearly all aberrations present in early stage cancers also appearing in advanced stage. This indicates that advanced stage disease likely evolves from early stage disease or a shared precursor, supporting previous low-resolution array CGH data.
Genomic aberration clustering distinguished early from advanced stages. However, further independent, high-resolution studies are needed to confirm molecular subtypes using array CGH data.
Microsatellite instability (MSI) is reported in 0-24% of ovarian cancers, but its biological and clinical importance remains unclear. This study didn't assess MSI, so its potential influence on the results cannot be ruled out.
Shih and Kurman have categorized ovarian carcinomas into two main subtypes, Type I and Type II, based on their clinical and histological features. Type I tumors are less common and are characterized by slow growth, genetic stability, and typically remain localized to the ovary when diagnosed. These include low-grade micropapillary serous, mucinous, endometrioid, and clear cell carcinomas, often linked to mutations in genes like KRAS, BRAF, PTEN, and beta-catenin. In contrast, Type II tumors represent the more prevalent form, known for rapid growth, aggressive behavior, and genetic instability. This category encompasses high-grade serous, malignant mixed mesodermal, and undifferentiated carcinomas, frequently associated with TP53 mutations. While our study focuses on FIGO stage 1 tumors, preventing a direct comparison of our clusters (A and B) with these Type I and II classifications, our findings demonstrate that array CGH can further stratify our study population, predominantly Type I tumors, into groups with differing overall survival rates.
The significant survival difference between clusters suggests that ovarian carcinoma comparative genomic hybridization data could hold future clinical value.
This study concludes that advanced ovarian cancer likely develops from either an early stage or a shared precursor, refuting the idea that early and advanced stages originate from separate molecular events. Grouping patients based purely on genomic aberrations revealed significant differences in survival between these groups. Given that array CGH data can be obtained from readily available pre-treatment FFPE ovarian cancer tissue, this method holds promise for identifying patients with a poorer prognosis.
