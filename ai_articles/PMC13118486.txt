The high costs of pharmaceutical research and development, estimated at over $1 billion for innovative drugs, are often cited to justify their high prices. While initial prices for new, effective drugs may be prohibitive for many patients, market principles suggest that demand should lead to greater accessibility once market exclusivity ends and generic competition begins. This premise, established by the 1984 Drug Price Competition and Patent Term Restoration Act, balances innovation with access: high prices during patent protection reward innovation, and subsequent generic competition lowers prices.

Economists estimate that bringing an innovative drug to market costs over $1 billion, a figure that continues to climb. These substantial pharmaceutical research and development expenses are frequently cited to rationalize the high prices of new medications. While the initial cost of effective new drugs, such as those for hepatitis or heart failure, can be prohibitive for many patients, market principles suggest that strong demand should lead to increased accessibility once market exclusivity concludes and generic competition emerges. This premise has underpinned the balance between pharmaceutical innovation and access since the 1984 passage of the Drug Price Competition and Patent Term Restoration Act. Under this framework, innovative drugs command high prices during their patent protection period, with generic competition subsequently driving down costs.
Insulin, discovered in 1921, was celebrated as a "wonder drug" for its ability to transform diabetes from a fatal illness into a manageable chronic condition. Despite its critical role in public health, with 21 million Americans living with diabetes and 6 million using insulin, this essential medication remains exclusively available in brand-name forms. Uninsured patients face monthly out-of-pocket costs ranging from $120 to $400. This contrasts sharply with many other vital medications, which are accessible as $4 generics. The absence of an affordable, low-priced generic insulin means that for numerous patients, particularly those without insurance, the cost is prohibitively high. This situation has dire consequences, impacting both individual patient care and the broader systemic management of diabetes.
Insulin, discovered nearly a century ago, lacks a low-priced generic version. This historical anomaly has significant implications for current policy and medical practice.
The discovery of insulin in 1921 at the University of Toronto, a celebrated moment in biomedical history, involved an unconventional team: Frederick Banting, a young orthopedic surgeon lacking laboratory experience, and Charles Best, a medical student. Their initial efforts focused on refining the extraction of active insulin, then called isletin, from animal pancreases. By 1922, they had successfully produced enough to treat their first patient, Leonard Thompson. Interestingly, the team delayed patenting their discovery. This was partly due to academic medicine's prevailing skepticism regarding the commercialization of biomedical research. However, when they eventually sought a U.S. patent in January 1923, which they subsequently sold to the university for a nominal $1, their stated objective was not financial gain. Instead, they aimed to ensure the rapid and safe public availability of insulin. They communicated to their university president that the patent served as a form of publication, explaining that once the preparation method was public, anyone could produce the extract, preventing any single entity from establishing a profitable monopoly.
Control over insulin's manufacturing was maintained through its patenting, ensuring product quality. Initially, university researchers attempted to produce insulin on campus but soon recognized their inability to meet North American market demand. Consequently, in 1923, they partnered with Eli Lilly, a pharmaceutical company with established expertise in glandular extract production. This collaboration granted Lilly U.S. patents for any manufacturing process enhancements. However, the university retained patent rights for all other global markets. The Toronto team subsequently licensed insulin production rights to various international companies. Among these was Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium of Denmark, which later merged to become Novo Nordisk, eventually emerging as a significant innovator in the field of insulin products.
Initially, animal-extracted insulin was seen as a vital, life-saving drug. However, it had limitations despite its clinical and public health importance.
Early insulin formulations, characterized by their brief duration, required frequent injections. However, significant advancements in the 1930s and 1940s addressed this limitation. In the early 1930s, Hans Christian Hagedorn and his Nordisk team found that adding protamine to insulin extended its action by altering absorption. While this protamine insulin was innovative, its physical properties prevented combination with fast-acting crystalline insulin. A further breakthrough occurred in 1946 with the patenting of neutral protamine Hagedorn (NPH) insulin. This innovation, involving the addition of small amounts of zinc to create crystalline protamine-isophane insulin, enabled the combination of long-acting and short-acting insulins. Consequently, many diabetic patients could manage their condition with just one daily injection. The mid-1950s saw another development: the introduction of lente ("slow") insulins, which prolonged insulin's action without protamine. These discoveries expanded options for tailoring insulin regimens, but also prolonged the duration of insulin patents into the 1970s.
Early beef and pork insulins, derived from animal tissues, presented issues like impurities causing local reactions and nonhuman proteins triggering allergic responses or reducing efficacy. Manufacturing innovations in the early 1970s addressed these problems, leading to improved purity and fewer side effects. Specifically, Novo introduced "monocomponent" insulins, and Lilly launched "single-peak" insulins. These safety advancements prolonged insulin patents into the late 1980s.
By the late 1970s, advancements in recombinant technology shifted the focus from improving insulin purity. This new biotechnology allowed for the creation of human insulin, a development that attracted significant investment. In 1978, Genentech scientists pioneered this by inserting the cloned insulin gene into *Escherichia coli* bacteria, marking a significant breakthrough. This innovation enabled Lilly to introduce the first recombinant human insulins, Humulin R and NPH, to the U.S. market in 1982. Concurrently, Novo and Nordisk developed chemical conversion methods to transform bovine insulin into human insulin, allowing them to also enter this emerging market. Novo Nordisk subsequently launched its own recombinant insulin in 1988. This period saw the establishment of a new network of insulin patents, primarily held by Lilly, Novo Nordisk, and Genentech, which were projected to extend well into the 21st century.
Recombinant technology enabled scientists to modify insulin's structure, aiming for improved physiological effects. By the late 1980s, research demonstrated that single amino-acid changes could significantly accelerate insulin absorption. This innovation allowed injected insulin to better mimic the pancreas's natural prandial release. Consequently, the first short-acting insulin analogue, lispro, gained approval in 1996, followed by aspart in 2000 and glulisine in 2004.
Long-acting insulin analogues were developed following the principles used for fast-acting versions. NPH insulin's unpredictable peak and under 24-hour duration prompted the creation of synthetic long-acting insulins to potentially improve glycemic control and reduce hypoglycemia. Glargine, introduced in 2000, was the first, with detemir following in 2005. Patents for these products expired in June 2014.
Despite insulin's initial patent in 1923, a generic version was unavailable by 2014. This is partly due to biological drugs being larger and more complex than the small-molecule drugs that formed the basis of the generic drug industry, making them harder to replicate. However, the expiration of recent insulin patents has led to expectations of more competitive pricing with the advent of biosimilar insulins. For instance, the FDA tentatively approved a biosimilar insulin glargine from Lilly and Boehringer Ingelheim last summer, and other companies are planning similar products in the US. The European Medicines Agency is also nearing approval for its first biosimilar glargine. Furthermore, less-regulated markets like China, India, Mexico, and Peru already see unregulated biosimilar insulins.
Biologic drugs, being significantly larger than small-molecule drugs, pose challenges in determining atom-by-atom equivalence. Consequently, off-patent biotech drugs are termed "biosimilar" instead of "generic." Production system variations can cause differences in protein folding and glycosylation, potentially affecting efficacy or safety. However, regulatory bodies like the FDA and EMA, alongside the generic drug industry, anticipate that emerging regulatory sciences of biosimilarity will resolve these concerns.
Economists caution that the introduction of biosimilar insulins, despite ongoing regulatory finalization in the US and Europe, may not yield price reductions comparable to those observed with conventional generic drugs. The abbreviated approval process for biosimilars demands significantly more original data than the typical abbreviated new drug application for small-molecule generics, including human immunogenicity and other safety studies. Consequently, projected price reductions for biosimilar insulins in the US range from only 20% to 40%, a stark contrast to the 80% or greater reductions common for most small-molecule generics. Europe, having already approved numerous non-insulin biosimilars, has experienced similarly modest price decreases in its biosimilar market. This suggests that the cost-saving potential of biosimilar insulins may be limited.
The absence of generic insulin stems from historical factors, not solely current biosimilarity debates. Unlike typical pharmaceutical innovation where patent expiration leads to generic competition, insulin's development involved continuous incremental improvements. These innovations consistently prevented a generic insulin industry from forming in North America as earlier patents lapsed.
Historically, insulin isn't a singular drug, but a family of products continually refined. Each new version offered improvements in safety, effectiveness, or convenience. Despite these ongoing innovations over generations, insulin's affordability hasn't necessarily improved. Ironically, it may be no more accessible now than when its original patent holders sold their rights for a nominal sum, intending to ensure widespread access to this vital medicine. This continuous evolution, while beneficial clinically, has paradoxically hindered its affordability.
The practice of "evergreening" extends a product's market life beyond its initial patent expiration through a series of related patents, often on metabolites or optical isomers, as described by pharmaceutical industry analysts. This strategy can maintain market share for a product family. For instance, Pfizer, after losing gabapentin (Neurontin) patent exclusivity in 2004, retained significant market presence by patenting its metabolic cognate, pregabalin (Lyrica). Critics frequently argue that the incremental innovations leading to such "me-too" drugs are insignificant, citing pregabalin as an example, which is not demonstrably safer or more effective than gabapentin.
The successive advancements in insulin formulations, far from being mere "me-too" drugs, have introduced tangible improvements. For instance, protamine insulin significantly surpassed regular insulin, and NPH insulin, in turn, offered clear benefits over protamine. Consequently, contemporary insulin products are undeniably safer and more user-friendly compared to those first available in 1923. However, the true value of each incremental innovation, particularly given that many diabetic patients still cannot afford insulin, remains debatable. For example, the introduction of lente insulin in the 1950s prompted questions regarding whether its minor theoretical benefits over NPH justified the added market complexity. Similarly, the theoretical advantages of monocomponent extract insulins were occasionally overshadowed by the practical challenges and risks associated with switching patients to different potency insulins. Despite extensive advertising in the 1980s promoting recombinant insulin as clinically superior, there was a notable lack of evidence to substantiate its superiority over the best animal-extract insulins then available. While long-acting analogues do reduce hypoglycemia compared to NPH, their impact on long-term patient outcomes, when compared to standard recombinant human insulin, has yet to be conclusively demonstrated. This raises critical questions about the cost-benefit ratio of these continuous innovations for patients.
While incremental insulin innovations likely benefited many patients, it is surprising that older insulin products did not foster generic competition. Instead, these older versions, such as pork and beef insulins, became obsolete and were removed from the U.S. market, making them unavailable for human use. Consequently, even when healthcare providers prescribe more affordable NPH and R insulin, patients receive newer, brand-name recombinant products that maintain market monopolies. This situation raises concerns about patient welfare. Contemporary patients unable to afford insulin, along with the patent-protected glucometers and test strips essential for its proper use, are arguably underserved. They are left with only marginally more effective agents, despite the potential for cheaper, generically available alternatives that could have existed 10, 30, or even 50 years ago if generic manufacturers had introduced them upon patent expiration. This lack of generic options underscores a significant gap in accessibility and affordability for a critical medication.
Generic drug companies historically avoided insulin production, prioritizing easier-to-manufacture small-molecule drugs. They deemed investing in updated manufacturing for potentially obsolete insulin versions unprofitable. However, with insulin-analogue patents expiring and no new products emerging, major generic manufacturers are now seriously considering the insulin market.
Generic drugs significantly benefit public health and the economy by increasing access to affordable, safe, and effective medications in the United States. In the early 1960s, generic medications were uncommon, with under 10% of dispensed drugs being generics and most prescription drugs operating as monopolies. Currently, over 80% of prescriptions are filled with generics, leading to billions of dollars in annual healthcare savings. These savings are crucial for both payers facing escalating healthcare costs and patients, as reduced medication expenses correlate with improved treatment adherence and better health outcomes.
The insulin market illustrates that generic competition isn't an automatic stage in a drug's lifespan. Recent generic drug shortages underscore that not all medications attract widespread generic manufacturers. Insulin's history reveals the limitations of relying solely on generic competition for public health. Despite being discovered almost a century ago, an affordable insulin supply remains unavailable in North America. This forces Americans to pay a high cost for the continuous revitalization of this foundational medicine, demonstrating how market dynamics can hinder access even for essential, long-established treatments.
