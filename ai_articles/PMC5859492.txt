The Wnt/β-catenin pathway's influence on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and antitumor immunity remains a subject of debate, despite suggestions that it promotes immune evasion by inhibiting immune cell function and T-cell infiltration. This study investigated the impact of intratumoral Wnt/β-catenin signaling on lymphocyte infiltration in breast cancer. Researchers analyzed 96 breast cancer specimens, evaluating the distribution of stromal TILs, specifically CD8+ and FOXP3+ TIL subsets, and β-catenin expression on consecutive tissue sections. The findings revealed that both stromal TIL infiltration and β-catenin expression were elevated in hormone receptor-negative HER2-enriched and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtypes. Furthermore, a significant association was observed between high levels of stromal TILs, including both CD8+ and FOXP3+ subsets, and β-catenin overexpression in breast cancer. These results challenge previous reports in melanoma, which indicated that Wnt/β-catenin signaling excludes lymphocyte infiltration. Instead, this study demonstrates a positive correlation between high TIL levels and β-catenin overexpression in breast cancer. This suggests that Wnt/β-catenin signaling may play a crucial role in breast cancer immunity, particularly in HER2-enriched and TNBC subtypes. Consequently, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway could represent a promising therapeutic target for modulating immune infiltrates in breast cancer.

Breast cancer remains a significant global health concern for women, despite advancements in its 5-year survival rates over the last three decades. It is the primary cause of cancer-related mortality in less developed nations and the second leading cause in more developed regions. The interplay between tumor cells and the immune system is crucial for cancer development and progression. While breast cancer has not historically been viewed as immunogenic, a growing body of research, including retrospective and prospective studies, indicates that a high level of lymphocyte infiltration in breast cancer correlates with improved patient outcomes. This positive association is particularly evident in early-stage triple-negative and HER2-enriched breast cancers. Furthermore, the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) has been shown to positively impact treatment response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Higher TIL levels have also been linked to enhanced benefits from trastuzumab in HER2-positive breast cancer. Consequently, suppressing lymphocyte infiltration and activity within the tumor microenvironment can hinder the antitumor immune response and diminish treatment effectiveness in breast cancer.
The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a crucial regulator of hematopoietic system development and function, particularly in peripheral immunity where it governs the polarization, differentiation, and survival of mature T lymphocytes. However, its precise role in tumor immunity remains a subject of debate. Some research suggests that Wnt/β-catenin signaling can negatively impact anti-tumor responses. For instance, it has been shown to inhibit the differentiation of naive T cells into effector T cells in humans. Furthermore, this pathway can suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production by dendritic cells (DCs) and promote the generation of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, thereby fostering immune tolerance. These immune-suppressive effects, often observed in various cancers where Wnt signaling is frequently activated, may contribute to tumor immune evasion and progression. In melanoma, specifically, β-catenin accumulation in tumor cells has been linked to increased IL-10 expression, which impairs DC maturation and induces a regulatory DC phenotype. Additionally, both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that Wnt/β-catenin activity can inhibit the anti-tumor effects of melanoma-specific cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Clinical data also indicate that immunotherapeutic strategies, such as checkpoint blockade, are most effective in T cell-inflamed tumor microenvironments. In line with this, studies in melanoma have reported that intrinsic Wnt/β-catenin signaling can prevent T-cell infiltration, leading to resistance to anti-PD-L1/anti-CTLA-4 therapies. Conversely, other evidence points to a potentially beneficial role for Wnt/β-catenin signaling in anti-tumor immunity. The pathway is essential for the reconstitution and quiescence of hematopoietic stem cells. A mouse model study revealed that Wnt pathway activation prevented CD8+ T cells from differentiating into effector T cells, instead generating a subset of CD8+ T cells with a young/memory phenotype similar to stem cell memory T cells (TSCM). These TSCM cells exhibited enhanced proliferation and anti-tumor activity compared to central memory T cells (TCM) or effector memory T cells (TEM). Moreover, Wnt/β-catenin signaling has been shown to improve the persistence of human CD8+ T cells by upregulating Bcl-2 and preventing active caspase-3. These findings suggest that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway may contribute to maintaining the stemness of mature CD8+ T cells, highlighting its potential utility in adoptive T cell therapy for cancer. Therefore, the exact contribution of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to anti-tumor immunity, whether positive or negative, requires further investigation.
Previous research links high β-catenin activity to poor breast cancer prognosis and progression. Our own work indicates abnormal Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in breast cancer, suggesting its mechanistic and pharmaceutical importance. However, the exact role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling within the tumor immune microenvironment remains unclear, necessitating further investigation into its involvement.
This study investigated the relationship between Wnt/β-catenin signaling and T lymphocyte infiltration in breast cancer, specifically examining how β-catenin expression in tumor tissues correlates with various inflammatory infiltrates. Prior research indicates that CD8+ T cell infiltration in breast cancer is linked to improved patient survival, while elevated FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) generally predict poorer outcomes, except in triple-negative breast cancer. Given the clinical importance of these lymphocyte subsets, our analysis focused on their infiltration patterns and association with β-catenin expression within breast cancer. Our novel findings reveal that, contrary to observations in other cancers where Wnt/β-catenin signaling might exclude lymphocytes, its activation in breast cancer is positively associated with increased overall lymphocyte infiltration. This positive correlation extends to both CD8+ and FOXP3+ T cell subsets, suggesting a distinct role for Wnt/β-catenin in the breast cancer immune microenvironment.
This study included 96 paraffin-embedded tumor samples from female patients diagnosed with primary operable breast carcinoma. Patients were excluded if they had clinical distant metastasis, or a history of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or preoperative radiation therapy. All tumor samples were identified as infiltrating ductal or lobular breast carcinoma. The median age of participants was 52 years, with a range of 28 to 77. Clinicopathological data for these samples were obtained from the Department of Pathology at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Qualified pathologists conducted immunohistochemical analyses, with ER and PR cutoffs set at 1%. HER2 status was determined according to the 2013 ASCO/CAP guideline, utilizing fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) amplification for equivocal (IHC 2+) samples. The ethics committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University reviewed and approved this study, and all participants provided informed consent for the use of their biological tissues.
Breast cancer tissue sections, 5 µm thick, underwent standard hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (Str-TILs) were subsequently identified and quantified within the stromal compartment. This assessment adhered to established guidelines for TILs analysis, specifically those outlined by Salgado et al. [35]. Two independent, experienced pathologists from the hospital's Pathology Department conducted the histopathological evaluation of Str-TILs. To ensure objectivity, both pathologists were blinded to the clinical and pathological data of the samples. Any significant discrepancies in their findings were resolved through discussion. The Str-TILs were categorized into three groups based on their percentage: Low (less than 10%), Intermediate (10–40%), and High (40% or more). These categorization thresholds were consistent with previously published guidelines [30].
Immunohistochemical staining was employed to assess β-catenin protein expression within tumor tissue and the distribution of CD8+ T cells and FOXP3+ Tregs in the cancer stroma. This analysis utilized 5 µm consecutive paraffin-embedded tissue slides with an IHC Detection Kit (CWBiotech, Beijing, China). Prior to antibody application, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer. Primary monoclonal antibodies were used at specific dilutions: β-catenin (ab32572, clone E247, Abcam, Cambridge, MA; 1:500), CD8α (#70306, clone C8/144B, CST, Danvers, MA; 1:200), and FOXP3 (ab20034, clone 236A/E7, Abcam, Cambridge, MA; 1:100), following manufacturer protocols. Control slides were incubated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) instead of the primary antibody. Full-face slide images were captured using an automated digital slide scanner (Zeiss Axio Scan .Z1, Jena, Germany).
Two qualified pathologists independently evaluated β-catenin expression and CD8+ or FOXP3+ T-cell counts, blinded to clinical data. Discrepancies were resolved through joint re-assessment. For each slide, ten random, non-overlapping high-power fields (x400 magnification) within the tumor stroma were selected for analysis.
Figure 1 displays representative breast cancer sections (200x magnification). β-catenin expression was scored 1-3 based on immunohistochemical intensity.
Tumor slides were evaluated for β-catenin expression by assessing both staining intensity (scores 1-3) and the percentage of positive cells in the nucleus and/or cytoplasm. A score of 0 indicated less than 5% positive cells. Scores 1 through 4 corresponded to positive cell percentages of 6-25%, 26-50%, 51-75%, and over 75%, respectively. These individual scores were then combined into a histoscore (H) by multiplying the percentage score by the intensity score. The final staining score was derived from the histoscore: a score of 1 for H values of 0-1, 2 for H values of 2-4, 3 for H values of 6 or 8, and 4 for H values of 9 or 12. Ultimately, final scores of 0-2 were categorized as β-catenin negative, while scores of 3-4 were considered β-catenin positive.

Immunohistochemical staining was used to quantify stromal CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and FOXP3+ regulatory T cells. CD8+ T cells were identified by membrane and nuclear staining, while FOXP3+ cells showed dark-brown nuclear staining. Average counts of these positive lymphocytes were recorded to represent their infiltration levels in the stroma.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0. Pearson Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests assessed relationships between clinicopathological factors and the counts of TILs, CD8+, or FOXP3+ T cells, as well as cytoplasmic/nuclear β-catenin expression. Kruskal-Wallis and Pearson Correlation tests further evaluated the correlation between TILs, CD8+, or FOXP3+ T cell counts and β-catenin expression. A significance level of 0.05 was adopted, with all p-values being two-sided.
Analysis of 96 primary breast cancer patients revealed significant correlations between stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and several clinicopathologic parameters. Increased TILs were significantly associated with higher histological grade (p=0.020). Specifically, 60% of low-grade tumors exhibited low TIL density, whereas over 70% of poorly differentiated tumors (grades II and III) had higher TIL levels. Furthermore, strong correlations were observed with estrogen receptor (ER) status (p=0.001) and progesterone receptor (PR) status (p=0.029). Seventy-five percent of ER-negative tumors showed intermediate to high TIL infiltration, contrasting with ER-positive tumors, where 60.3% had low TIL percentages. Similarly, PR-negative breast cancers displayed significantly higher stromal TIL density (64.3%) compared to PR-positive tumors. No significant association was found between HER2 overexpression and stromal TILs.

A notable association was found between increased lymphocyte infiltration in the tumor stroma and higher histological grades (p=0.020). Significant correlations were also observed with estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status. Among ER-negative breast tumors, 75% demonstrated intermediate to high levels of lymphocyte infiltration. Conversely, most ER-positive tumors (60.3%) had low stromal TIL percentages. A similar pattern emerged for PR status: PR-positive tumors generally exhibited less lymphocyte infiltration, while PR-negative breast cancers showed a significantly higher density of stromal TILs (64.3%). Interestingly, no significant link was established between HER2 overexpression and stromal TILs (Table 1).
This study categorized breast cancer specimens into four molecular subtypes: luminal A (42.7%), luminal B (20.8%), HER2-enriched (19.8%), and triple-negative (TNBC) (16.7%), based on immunohistochemical analysis. Significant variations in stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were observed across these subtypes (p=0.004). Specifically, TNBC and HER2-enriched subtypes exhibited notably higher TIL infiltration, with 77.8% and 73.8% respectively showing intermediate to high levels. In contrast, luminal A and B subtypes displayed considerably lower infiltration rates, at 34% and 52.4%. These findings align with prior research indicating a greater density of TILs in estrogen receptor/progesterone receptor (ER/PR) negative breast cancers, highlighting elevated stromal lymphocyte infiltration in HER2-enriched and TNBC subtypes.

Our investigation utilized consecutive tissue sections from breast cancer specimens to assess the relationship between β-catenin expression and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Immunohistochemical analysis allowed for the evaluation of both β-catenin distribution and TILs within identical regions of these serial sections. Visual inspection of representative images (Fig. 3) revealed a consistent pattern: tumors exhibiting elevated lymphocyte infiltration also demonstrated overexpression of β-catenin. Conversely, tumors with low TILs showed reduced β-catenin levels, suggesting a positive correlation between these two factors. To statistically validate this observation, a Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted, confirming a significant association between both nuclear and cytoplasmic β-catenin expression and the extent of lymphocyte infiltration in breast cancer (p<0.01, Fig. 4A and B). Further analysis, employing a positive/negative cutoff for β-catenin expression rather than scores, reinforced these findings, indicating that tumors with β-catenin overexpression consistently displayed higher levels of lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. 4C and Table 4). A Pearson correlation analysis further established a strong linear relationship between β-catenin expression and TILs levels (r=0.710, p<0.001, Fig. 4D).

In 96 breast cancer samples, we investigated the relationship between β-catenin expression and two specific subsets of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs): CD8+ T cells and FOXP3+ Tregs. Both CD8+ T cell and FOXP3+ Treg infiltration demonstrated a significant association with overall stromal TILs in breast cancer (r=0.682, p=0.000 and r=0.450, p=0.000, respectively). Furthermore, a linear correlation was observed between the expression of β-catenin by tumor cells and the infiltration levels of both CD8+ T cells (r=0.577, p=0.000) and FOXP3+ Tregs (r=0.476, p=0.000). Interestingly, the presence of FOXP3+ Tregs within the tumor stroma also showed a positive association with CD8+ T cell infiltration (r=0.606, p=0.000).
Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) play a crucial role in intratumoral immune responses through their interactions with other immune cells within the tumor microenvironment. Immunohistochemical detection of TILs can serve as an indicator of the immune system's response to cancer progression. Furthermore, the presence of TILs offers predictive insights into patient responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, aiding oncologists in identifying patients who may benefit most from this treatment. However, the clinical significance and predictive value of TILs are not universal across all breast cancer populations. Instead, their utility appears to be more pronounced in specific molecular subtypes, particularly triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and HER2-enriched subtypes. Our clinicopathological findings demonstrate a strong correlation between estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status and stromal TILs. Specifically, ER/PR-negative breast cancers, such as TNBC and HER2-positive subtypes, exhibit higher levels of lymphocyte infiltration compared to luminal type A and B breast cancers. These observations align with prior research, underscoring the importance of TILs in hormone receptor-negative breast cancers. This suggests that future research should extend beyond using hormone receptors solely for cancer subtyping to investigate how hormonal activity influences lymphocyte recruitment in breast cancer.
The Wnt/β-catenin pathway's central component is β-catenin, a protein crucial for cell adhesion and gene transcription. Normally, in the absence of Wnt signaling, a destruction complex continuously degrades β-catenin. However, when Wnt ligands activate Frizzled and LRP receptors, this complex is inhibited, leading to β-catenin stabilization within the cytoplasm. Subsequently, β-catenin moves into the nucleus, where it influences the expression of numerous target genes. This accumulation of β-catenin is frequently observed in malignant tumors, indicating abnormal Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation during tumor development and progression. Notably, over 60% of breast cancers exhibit alterations in Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which can be a predictor of poor disease outcomes.
The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is crucial for hematopoietic stem cell self-renewal and lymphocyte development, influencing the tumor immune system. Studies indicate that this signaling pathway can suppress immunity and foster resistance by modulating cytokine activity and immune cell function. For instance, in melanoma, intrinsic β-catenin activation leads to T-cell exclusion, hindering the effectiveness of anti-PD-L1/anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapies. Consequently, dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in malignant tumors may promote immune evasion and resistance to various cancer treatments.
Our immunohistochemical analysis of breast cancer specimens revealed a novel association between Wnt/β-catenin signaling and lymphatic infiltration. Across all 96 invasive breast cancer samples, we observed cytoplasmic and/or nuclear accumulation of β-catenin within tumor nests. Contrary to findings in melanoma, where Wnt/β-catenin signaling has been linked to T-cell exclusion, our consecutive section staining demonstrated that β-catenin overexpression in cancer cells correlated with increased tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density in the stroma. This positive relationship was further supported by a strong linear correlation between β-catenin expression and TIL levels (r=0.710, p<0.001). This unexpected finding challenges the prevailing understanding of Wnt/β-catenin's role in anti-tumor immunity, particularly when compared to melanoma research. The discrepancy might stem from the distinct biological contexts of different cancers, suggesting that the Wnt-β-catenin pathway's function can vary significantly. Additionally, methodological differences, such as our use of IHC staining for TILs versus comparative gene expression profiling in other studies, could contribute to the divergent results. Our observations prompt a re-evaluation of whether tumor-intrinsic Wnt-β-catenin signaling exclusively exerts detrimental effects on anti-tumor immune responses. Previous research by Forget et al. and Gattinoni et al. indicates a crucial role for Wnt/β-catenin signaling in maintaining stemness and promoting the T_SCM subset in mature memory CD8+ T cells, both within TILs and in peripheral circulation. This suggests that the in vivo function of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway could potentially enhance adoptive immunotherapies. However, since the H&E staining method primarily identifies mononuclear TILs without elucidating their functional status, further investigations are essential to fully understand the biological characteristics of human TILs and their intricate relationship with Wnt/β-catenin activity.
The diverse functions of immune cell subgroups within a tumor's microenvironment are critical to understanding anticancer responses. For instance, while dendritic cells (DCs), Th1 CD4+ T cells, and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are recognized for their roles in anticancer immunosurveillance, Th17 CD4+ T cells and FOXP3+ CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) are associated with protumorigenic inflammation [54]. Recent research highlights that distinct infiltrating cell types possess unique prognostic and predictive significance [54]. In breast cancer (BC), a high infiltration of CD8+ lymphocytes typically indicates a more favorable patient prognosis, whereas a high density of FOXP3+ Tregs is often linked to poorer survival outcomes [42]. These observations lead to the hypothesis that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway might exert differential effects on these two specific subsets of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Our investigation of 96 breast cancer specimens revealed that CD8+ T cell densities consistently surpassed those of FOXP3+ lymphocytes. Both CD8+ and FOXP3+ TIL densities exhibited a positive correlation with the total stromal TILs (r=0.682, P<0.001 for CD8+; r=0.450, P<0.001 for FOXP3+). This aligns with arguments by West et al. [55], who, despite acknowledging the contradictory functions of FOXP3+ and CD8+ T cells in anti-tumor immunity, emphasized that the prognostic value of FOXP3+ TILs in breast cancer is contingent on the presence of CD8+ TILs. Our findings further indicated a significant association between the number of FOXP3+ TILs and the total CD8+ infiltrates in the tumor stroma (r=0.606, P<0.001), consistent with prior reports [52]. The precise reasons why CD8+ TILs maintain their anti-tumor efficacy and correlate with improved prognosis even when co-infiltrating with a substantial number of FOXP3+ Tregs remain unclear. In vitro models suggest that Tregs necessitate direct contact with target cells to induce immune suppression [56]. Furthermore, the interactions among TILs, tumor cells, and stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment are exceptionally intricate. Prat et al. [57], using CD25 as a molecular marker for Tregs, demonstrated that breast tumors with high CD25 expression also showed enrichment in markers of dendritic cell activation, inflammatory cytokine production, and CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity. This suggests that a complex anti-tumor immune response can be active in breast tumors despite the presence of Tregs. Therefore, interpreting the functional status of TILs necessitates a comprehensive analysis of Tregs-CD8+ TILs interactions within the tumor site, advocating for the examination of multiple TIL components rather than focusing on isolated lymphocyte populations. Finally, we assessed the relationship between CD8+/FOXP3+ TIL subsets and tumor-intrinsic β-catenin overexpression. Our results did not definitively conclude whether the Wnt/β-catenin pathway differentially impacts these two TIL subgroups, as both CD8+ T cell numbers (r=0.577, P<0.001) and FOXP3+ T cell numbers (r=0.476, P<0.001) were positively associated with abnormal β-catenin expression in breast cancer. Importantly, our findings contradict reports suggesting that Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation excludes T-cell infiltration in tumors [21]. Instead, our study reveals a significant association between the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and both total TILs and these two specific TIL subgroups in breast cancer. This indicates a potential role for the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in tumor immunosurveillance and anti-tumor immunity. Further research is essential to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
Our findings indicate that the degree of lymphocyte infiltration within breast cancer tumors is influenced by hormone receptor status, tumor grade, and molecular subtype. Specifically, both stromal lymphocyte infiltration and β-catenin expression were elevated in triple-negative and HER2-positive breast cancers. Contrary to observations in melanoma, high levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in breast cancer correlated with β-catenin overexpression. This association extended to both CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes and FOXP3+ T cells, which also showed a positive correlation with abnormal β-catenin expression. Further research into the Wnt/β-catenin pathway's role in breast cancer immunity and the functional state of these T cell subsets across different breast cancer subtypes is essential. This will enhance our understanding of tumor-TIL interactions and identify potential therapeutic targets for improving breast cancer immunotherapy.
