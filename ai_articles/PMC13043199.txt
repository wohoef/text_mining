Despite existing metadata standards for clinical studies, a complete framework for tracing data origin and transformations remains undefined. This paper investigates how metadata is utilized in clinical studies, identifying and detailing seven distinct sources of data value-level metadata. These sources encompass diverse research designs, aligning with the National Institutes of Health's broad definition of clinical research. The identified metadata sources enable a categorization based on a data value's origin, its definition, and the operations it undergoes. These operations are further broken down into changes, movement, retrieval, and quality assessments. The paper then delves into the consequences of monitoring and managing this granular level of metadata.

When research findings cannot be replicated or reproduced, the methods of data collection and management often come under scrutiny. While the therapeutic development sector, regulated by the FDA, benefits from established guidelines and regulations, academic studies outside this industry are only beginning to address documentation expectations. Moreover, the past decade has witnessed a tenfold surge in the diversity and volume of data sources across all clinical studies. This proliferation of new data, frequently involving direct electronic capture, alongside the growing trend of secondary data use in research, challenges current regulatory frameworks.
The perceived burden of extra documentation has likely deterred many academic studies from adopting comprehensive metadata. While understandable given increasing demands for reproducibility and limited resources, we argue that computable documentation offers significant advantages for researchers and institutions.
While the International Standards Organization (ISO) defines metadata as data describing other data within its Metadata Registries standard (ISO/IEC 11179), this standard primarily addresses the lifecycle management of data element definitions, not the actual lifecycle events of individual data values. Similarly, other fields like library science and public records management typically focus on resource-level metadata, such as bibliographic details for managing information resources (e.g., acquisition, storage, discovery, access). Various disciplines, including business, environmental science, social media, and information technology, have developed their own metadata definitions and categorizations tailored to their specific needs and functions. In the realm of clinical research, existing initiatives like ClinicalTrials.gov provide study-level metadata to facilitate the discovery of ongoing studies, results reporting, and information retrieval. Organizations such as the Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium (CDISC), the Biomedical Research Integrated Domain Group (BRIDG), the Ontology of Clinical Research (OCRe), and the Human Studies Database Project (HSDB) have also contributed to harmonized study-level metadata. However, a significant gap remains: there is currently no comprehensive or agreed-upon metadata standard specifically for the individual data values that form the basis of clinical study conclusions. Our focus here is precisely on this data value-level metadata and its potential to enhance the utility and reusability of clinical study data.
The growing need for interoperability and automation in clinical studies highlights the importance of robust metadata. Metadata has proven instrumental in automating various processes. For instance, it can configure data entry screens for web-based Electronic Data Capture (EDC) systems, leveraging descriptive information about data elements like prompts, collection formats, and discrete choice options. Similarly, descriptive metadata facilitates automated data exchange, as seen with Health Level Seven (HL7) messages. Beyond configuration and exchange, metadata plays a crucial role in tracking data changes, enabling the visualization of data value histories. Information embedded in metadata regarding data discrepancies aids in status reporting and quality assessment. Furthermore, metadata supports the automated comparison of data files and provides traceability through algorithms detailing data operations. Metadata associated with data transfers automates the reception and integration of incoming files. Collectively, these metadata types enable automated data visualization, illustrating data origin, provenance (the path data takes), processing status, and quality metrics.
Achieving widespread improvements in quality and efficiency within clinical studies hinges on software that utilizes standardized metadata. Current metadata often falls short, lacking crucial details such as the rationale behind data element generation, the observer responsible for a datum, the methodology for dataset assembly or retrieval, and any subsequent modifications to an element. This absence of comprehensive metadata restricts the scope and potential of automation. Conversely, a thorough grasp of the automation capabilities linked to specific metadata allows for a direct evaluation, weighing the expenses of acquiring and managing additional metadata against the anticipated advantages.

For clinical studies, the classification of data value-level metadata is shaped by its various uses and origins. Each data value possesses a singular origin. Additionally, a data value's history, encompassing all previous states and operations (such as changes, assessments, movement, and retrieval) from its inception to its present condition, is a direct outcome of these operations. Crucially, every data value is also associated with a clear and unambiguous definition.
The foundational elements of data value-level metadata enable the derivation of "second-generation" metadata. This includes information like a data value's age, the degree of modifications it has undergone, and its complete historical journey. Such derived metadata facilitates novel applications, such as distinguishing institutional data within health records from external contributions, or highlighting outdated or questionable data values.
The increasing volume of biomedical research data necessitates a clear definition and categorization of data value-level metadata, which in turn supports various metadata applications. This type of metadata is inherently tied to a data value, meaning it's an inseparable part of it, regardless of how that data value is used. Implementing data value-level metadata will inevitably lead to a significant increase in data volume and storage needs, at minimum doubling them as each data value would have at least one associated metadata piece. While this discussion offers an initial framework for classifying and defining such metadata, it is not yet a standardized approach for biomedical research software. A substantial portion of this metadata will be automatically generated, demanding new software capabilities beyond current audit trail functions. Furthermore, while data element level metadata requires human input, this area has generally seen limited success in biomedical research, with only a few exceptions.
Significant advantages arise from robust metadata implementation. Metadata defined at the data element level offers reusability, applying to numerous data values associated with that element. Furthermore, advanced software can largely automate the generation of data value-level metadata. When standardized software utilizes this metadata, it unlocks substantial benefits, such as enhanced signal detection and visualization. This includes the ability to generate heat maps highlighting frequently changed, older, or highly discrepant data. Additional applications encompass documenting data usage, reconstructing information retrieval following source data alterations, comparing data transfers, and automatically validating incoming data against specifications. Despite the proven value of these capabilities, the current absence of standardized data value-level metadata limits their widespread adoption and availability.
The growing need for secondary use of electronic health data, driven by increasing costs in medical therapy development, highlights the importance of comprehensive metadata for confident research applications. We propose expanding the biomedical research vocabulary to include two specialized categories of metadata: "paradata" and "orthodata." The term "metadata" itself originates from the Greek "meta-," signifying "about" or data that describes other data. Drawing from the Greek "para-," meaning "alterations" or "modifications," we define "paradata" as information detailing how a data value was generated, changed, or otherwise processed. This concept is already present in survey research, where "paradata" describes aspects like survey duration, delivery time, or interviewee hesitation, and is recognized as a type of metadata. Similarly, from the Greek "ortho-," meaning "straight" or "upright," we introduce "orthodata" to refer to the logic behind data quality checks and assessments, which determine if data values meet expectations. Both paradata and orthodata are distinct classes of metadata crucial for robust research.
The expanding volume, speed, and diversity of data and its origins present a significant hurdle for current biomedical research. While tasks like data checking, integration, and cleaning were once manageable manually or with limited automation, they now demand full automation. This automation, particularly at scale, necessitates standardized metadata and software designed to utilize it. Consequently, defining a comprehensive, accurate, and unambiguous classification for data value-level metadata is an urgent requirement, as the quantity of data collected and processed for research continues to grow.
