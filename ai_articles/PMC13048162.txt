Acute lung injury frequently leads to mortality after severe trauma and ischemia-reperfusion (IR) events. This study aimed to assess the protective benefits of N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and atorvastatin (ATOR) pretreatment in a rat model of mesenteric IR. The methodology involved dividing male rats into five groups: a sham group, a mesenteric IR group, and three pretreatment groups (ATOR, NAC, and a combined ATOR + NAC, or A+N) followed by IR. Various parameters were analyzed, including blood gas and cytokine levels, biochemical markers, and cell counts. Lung damage was quantified using histopathological examination and the wet-to-dry lung weight (W/D) ratio. Post-IR, significant alterations were observed across biochemistry, cytokine profiles, and lung injury indicators. All pretreatment groups demonstrated reduced neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, lactate, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels compared to the IR group. However, only the A+N group showed decreased creatinine and alkaline phosphatase (ALKP) levels. Blood pH and base excess (BE) were elevated, and venous partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PvCO2) was lowered in the ATOR and A+N groups relative to the IR group, with bicarbonate (HCO3-) levels increasing exclusively in the A+N group. The A+N group also exhibited notable reductions in lung injury scores and W/D ratios. Furthermore, tissue tumor necrosis factor-α levels were significantly lower in all pretreatment groups, while interleukin-1β levels were reduced only in the A+N group, when compared to the IR group. In conclusion, both NAC and ATOR effectively mitigated inflammation and lung injury in rats subjected to mesenteric IR. The combination of NAC and ATOR appears to offer superior protection against lung injury than either agent alone.

Ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury represents a critical and frequent complication following resuscitation from major surgery, severe trauma, and hemorrhagic shock. This type of injury is often exacerbated by the restoration of mesenteric blood flow, a process frequently delayed during early shock. The underlying mechanisms of IR injury primarily involve the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid peroxidation, and inflammatory responses, which collectively lead to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). SIRS, in turn, can cause secondary damage to organs located remotely from the initial injury site, potentially resulting in organ failure, multiple organ dysfunction, or even mortality. These remote injuries are characterized by the release of inflammatory cytokines, the production of oxygen free radicals, and the infiltration of neutrophils into affected tissues. Among these, acute lung injury stands out as the most severe form of remote organ damage and a leading cause of death after IR injury. Specifically, ROS formation is identified as the main pathological driver of lung injuries stemming from mesenteric IR. During intestinal IR, ROS are produced in damaged cells and tissues, activating various signaling pathways, promoting inflammation, and impairing the intestinal mucosal barrier. Subsequently, these ROS can escape the gut microvasculature, entering the systemic circulation and initiating a cascade of biological events, including acute lung injury.
To mitigate tissue damage, a range of anti-inflammatory agents, including those with antioxidant and antiapoptotic properties, are employed. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and atorvastatin (ATOR) are two such agents, both possessing broad-ranging effects and potent antioxidant capabilities that combat oxidative stress and avert ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury. NAC, a sulfhydryl group donor frequently utilized in medical settings, functions as a powerful antioxidant by directly neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) via its thiol groups and elevating intracellular glutathione levels. Research has demonstrated that NAC treatment significantly lessens IR injury and reduces remote organ dysfunction. Consequently, NAC's therapeutic benefits extend to various conditions, addressing oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. Atorvastatin, an antidyslipidemic, is prescribed for cholesterol reduction and the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases. Its diverse actions encompass strong anti-inflammatory effects, immune system modulation, antithrombotic activity, enhanced endothelial function, and antioxidant properties. While both NAC and ATOR are believed to exert anti-inflammatory effects, they do so through distinct mechanisms. This study specifically investigated the protective efficacy of NAC, ATOR, and their combination as pretreatments against lung injury induced by superior mesenteric artery IR in rats.
The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at National Yang-Ming University (approval number: 1021294) approved this study. Animal care adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.
Thirty male Sprague Dawley rats, weighing between 250-300 grams and aged 7-8 weeks, were acquired from BioLasco Taiwan Co. Ltd in Taipei City. These animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions, including a 12-hour light-dark cycle, 55% ± 5% humidity, and a consistent room temperature of 23°C ± 2°C, with access to standard rat chow and filtered water. Prior to surgical manipulation, the rats were randomly assigned to one of five experimental groups, each containing six animals. For three days leading up to surgery, specific drug regimens were administered, with the final dose given 12 hours before the procedure. The groups included: a sham group, which received no pretreatment and underwent a sham mesenteric IR; an IR group, given a placebo (0.9% NaCl) via oral gavage and intraperitoneal injection before mesenteric IR; an ATOR group, receiving 40 mg/kg body weight/day of atorvastatin (Pfizer Inc., New York, NY, USA) orally before IR; a NAC group, treated with 300 mg/kg body weight/day of N-acetylcysteine (Nang Kuang Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Tainan, Taiwan) via intraperitoneal injection before IR; and an ATOR + NAC group, which received both drugs prior to mesenteric IR.
The experimental procedure for mesenteric ischemia-reperfusion (IR) mirrored a previously established protocol. Anesthesia was induced in the animals using 3% isoflurane gas. Prior to surgery, the rats were positioned supine, their anterior abdominal area was shaved and cleaned, and a subcutaneous injection of 1-2 mL of 2% lidocaine hydrochloride was administered for pain relief. A controlled heating blanket maintained the animals' body temperature between 37°C and 38°C throughout the surgical and recovery phases. A 2 cm midline incision was made in the abdomen. The superior mesenteric artery (SMA) was carefully isolated, and a microvascular clamp was applied for 60 minutes to induce ischemia. Loss of mesenteric pulsation and paleness of the intestinal segment confirmed successful ischemia. The bowel was then repositioned within the abdominal cavity, and the incision was closed with interrupted 4-0 nylon sutures. Following the 60-minute ischemic period, the abdomen was reopened, and the vascular clamp was removed. Reperfusion was verified by the return of pulsation and normal color to the mesentery, after which the bowel was returned to the abdominal cavity and the incision re-closed. After another 60 minutes of reperfusion, the animals were humanely euthanized by exsanguination via the inferior vena cava. To ensure blinding during subsequent sampling and analysis, numerical tags were used instead of group identifiers.
For analysis, whole blood was collected in Eppendorf tubes. A Sysmex XT-1800iv performed whole-blood analysis. Serum was isolated by centrifuging the blood at 3000 g for 10 minutes at 4°C. This serum then underwent chemical analysis using a VetTest Chemistry Analyzer. Blood gas analysis was conducted with a VetStat Electrolyte and Blood Gas Analyzer.
To assess lung injury, samples were collected from specific regions: the superior aspect of the right middle lobe (nondependent) and the inferior part of the superior-posterior segment of the right lower lobe (dependent). A constant positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 cm H2O was maintained during sample acquisition. These tissue samples underwent fixation in buffered 4% formaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning. Subsequently, they were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. One of the authors was responsible for harvesting the lung tissues from the rats. A specialist then prepared the histology slides, which were numerically coded. A pathologist, unaware of the experimental groups, then examined and scored these slides.
To assess injury severity, a 5-point grading scale was applied to histology slides. This scale evaluated the presence of abnormal observations like alveolar hemorrhage, interstitial inflammatory cells, edema, and necrosis. Scores ranged from 0 (no abnormalities) to 4 (≥3/4 of the microscopic field affected). To account for regional differences, at least five fields were analyzed per slide.
To assess pulmonary edema, the wet-to-dry (W/D) lung weight ratio was calculated. The left lower lung was removed and its initial "wet" weight recorded. After drying the tissue at 60°C for five days, its "dry" weight was measured. The W/D ratio was then determined by dividing the wet weight by the dry weight.
For cytokine analysis, the left upper lung was first removed and washed with phosphate-buffered saline to eliminate residual blood. A 0.5-gram portion of this lung tissue was then manually homogenized in 1 mL of tissue protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The total protein concentrations were subsequently measured using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit, also from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. To quantify the levels of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (DuoSet rat; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was employed, following the manufacturer's guidelines. Optical density readings were taken at 450 nm, with a 540 nm reference wavelength, using a TECAN Infini 200 ELISA reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). Standard buffers, diluents, and substrates were utilized for all assays. Tissue cytokine levels were determined by comparing the readings to a standard concentration curve, generated in MS Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), from an eight-point, two-fold dilution series of rat IL-1β (4000-62.5 pg/mL), IL-6 (8000-125 pg/mL), and TNF-α (4000-62.5 pg/mL) standards.
To determine statistical significance, a p-value of less than 0.05 was used. Data were presented as medians with their ranges. Inter-group differences were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA by ranks and Dunn's post hoc test. For intra-group comparisons of lung injury scores between nondependent and dependent sites, a paired t-test was employed. SigmaPlot 13 for Windows facilitated all statistical analyses.
While total leukocyte counts were consistent across all groups, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was significantly reduced in all pretreatment groups compared to the IR group, with no notable differences among the pretreatment groups themselves.
Assays were conducted for serum lactate, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, ALT, and ALKP. All pretreatment groups showed significantly reduced lactate and ALT compared to the IR group. However, only the A+N group exhibited significantly lower creatinine and ALKP levels.
The IR group exhibited altered venous gas exchange compared to the sham group, with reduced pH, bicarbonate (HCO3-), and base excess (BE), alongside elevated partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PvCO2). In contrast to the IR group, both the ATOR and A+N groups showed significantly higher pH and BE, and lower PvCO2. Notably, only the A+N group demonstrated a significant increase in HCO3-.
Figure 2 illustrates the histopathological observations of lung injury across the experimental groups. Following IR, increased leukocyte infiltration, edema, and hemorrhage were evident. While all pretreatment groups exhibited reduced injury compared to the IR group, only the A + N group demonstrated significant alleviation in lung injury scores.
Following IR, the W/D ratio increased. While all pretreatment groups showed a reduced W/D, only the combined ATOR + NAC group exhibited a statistically significant decrease (Fig. 3).
Lung tissue biomarkers of acute inflammation, specifically proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, were assessed. Following IR, cytokine levels rose compared to the sham group. All pretreatment groups showed significantly reduced TNF-α levels relative to the IR group, while IL-1β levels were significantly lower exclusively in the A + N group.
In this rat model, mesenteric ischemia-reperfusion (IR) induced remote organ injury, which was mitigated by NAC, ATOR, and their combination. The combined administration of NAC and ATOR (A+N group) demonstrated superior protection compared to individual treatments. This was evidenced by enhanced biochemical outcomes, improved gas exchange, and better lung function preservation. Specifically, the A+N group exhibited elevated HCO3– levels and reduced lung injury scores, wet-to-dry ratios (W/Ds), and tissue IL-1 levels, indicating the anti-inflammatory or antioxidant properties of the combination in preventing acute lung damage.
Ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury is characterized by elevated oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which are central to its pathophysiology. This process leads to endoplasmic reticulum stress, with the resulting reactive oxygen species (ROS) initiating an apoptotic cascade and an acute inflammatory response. Intestinal IR, a common occurrence in clinical scenarios like shock and trauma, renders the lungs particularly susceptible to damage. Early symptoms often precede multi-organ failure, potentially culminating in acute respiratory distress syndrome. While inflammation and oxidative stress are recognized as primary drivers of IR-induced damage, the exact mechanisms are not fully understood. In this study, we employed a previously established and validated surgical method to induce intestinal IR through complete superior mesenteric artery (SMA) occlusion.
Our study utilized a high dose of N-acetylcysteine (NAC) at 300 mg/kg body weight, consistent with previous research by Kalimeris et al. Their findings indicated that this higher dosage, administered before ischemia, was more effective in enhancing liver and lung function compared to a lower 150 mg/kg dose. NAC functions as a precursor to glutathione and scavenges free radicals. Other studies, such as Cerqueira et al., have also demonstrated effective protection with NAC delivered via continuous infusion (430 mg/kg body weight/h). In our investigation, NAC administration following mesenteric ischemia-reperfusion (IR) proved beneficial in safeguarding distant organs, especially the lungs. Specifically, NAC-treated rats exhibited reduced cytokine levels and diminished neutrophil accumulation in the lungs after IR injury, contrasting with untreated animals.
Atorvastatin (ATOR), a statin, exhibits protective effects beyond its cholesterol-reducing capabilities. These diverse actions, known as pleiotropic effects, encompass improved endothelial function, decreased oxidative stress, immune system modulation, and anti-inflammatory properties. Our study utilized a substantial daily dose of ATOR (40 mg/kg body weight), guided by prior research. This higher concentration is often required in animals due to their rapid upregulation of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme reductase in response to statins. Human studies have also shown that high ATOR doses (80 mg/d) can significantly lower C-reactive protein levels without severe side effects. In our intestinal IR models, ATOR successfully mitigated remote organ damage, aligning with previous findings that highlight its protective role against IR injuries in the lungs, liver, and kidneys.
Our hypothesis centered on the combined protective effect of NAC and ATOR against remote organ damage, specifically in the lungs. These drugs were chosen due to their widespread clinical application. Statins exhibit diverse pharmacological characteristics, including varying absorption, affinity, solubility, and excretion, which contribute to their distinct lung-protective capabilities. Nevertheless, this study demonstrated that administering NAC + ATOR prior to IR injury in rats was more effective in preventing remote lung injury than using either drug alone.
Previous research on the combined protective effects of NAC and ATOR against ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, particularly in the lungs, is limited to two known studies. Cusumano et al. (2007) investigated a rat kidney IR model, administering NAC (140 mg/kg) and ATOR (40 mg/kg). Their findings indicated that both drugs influenced oxidative activity, and notably, the combination of NAC and ATOR resulted in a comparatively low incidence of tubular ischemic lesions. Separately, Alexandropoulos et al. (2010) utilized an intestinal IR model, providing NAC (160 mg/kg) and ATOR (10 mg/kg). This study observed that NAC offered greater protection to the kidneys, while ATOR was more effective for the liver, and their combined application led to a reduction in systemic inflammatory cytokine levels. In contrast, our current study, employing NAC and ATOR in combination, demonstrated a significant reduction in creatinine and ALKP levels, though other biochemical markers remained unchanged. These variations in outcomes could be attributed to differences in blood sampling techniques and timing. Despite these potential discrepancies, our research reinforces the conclusion that the combined administration of NAC and ATOR mitigates lung injury, regulates blood gas parameters, and lessens peripheral blood inflammation.
Several limitations exist within this study. The absence of baseline blood volume measurements, taken prior to the experiment, means we couldn't account for potential blood loss effects on our findings, possibly obscuring the true impact of the drug treatments. To minimize animal sacrifice, we omitted a group receiving sham treatment without sham IR, which could have helped clarify any medical sham effects. Furthermore, our investigation did not delve into the molecular mechanisms behind the protective effects of individual or combined drug use, though the observed additive effects suggest distinct pathways are involved. Lastly, the high drug dosages and the need for pretreatment might limit the applicability of these findings in acute injury or other emergency clinical situations.
Pretreatment with NAC, ATOR, or their combination offers potential protection against remote organ injuries, specifically to the lungs. In our mesenteric IR rat model, the combined NAC + ATOR pretreatment notably improved gas exchange, lessened inflammatory cytokine secretion, and decreased lung injury severity. These findings hold clinical significance due to the widespread use and relative safety of NAC and ATOR.
