Effective parenting is crucial for children's healthy social-emotional development, necessitating robust assessment tools, especially in clinical contexts. This study investigated the Caregiver Wish List (CWL), a novel 53-item interview-based scale designed to evaluate parenting practices. We analyzed its factor structure using data from 348 parents of children aged 4-11, comprising 220 from the general population and 128 from clinical settings. Exploratory factor analysis identified five meaningful factors: adequate discipline, controlled responding, focus on positive behavior, consistency, and monitoring. These factors, however, did not entirely align with the CWL's initial hypothesized structure. All five factors exhibited adequate internal consistency. The factor structures were consistent across both community and clinical samples, suggesting broad applicability. Notably, these factors successfully distinguished between the two groups, with the community sample displaying superior parenting skills. Furthermore, all identified factors showed a significant negative correlation with child psychopathology, with a more pronounced relationship observed for externalizing problems compared to internalizing issues. Only 23 of the original 53 CWL items contributed to these five factors, indicating potential for a more concise version of the scale without compromising essential content. Future research should explore the sensitivity of these CWL factors to change. The CWL's interview format offers unique advantages, fostering rapport and facilitating parental reflection on their parenting strengths and vulnerabilities.

Childhood externalizing behavior problems are consistently linked to ineffective parenting, which is recognized as a significant risk factor. Specifically, harsh and inconsistent discipline, inadequate monitoring, and limited positive involvement have been empirically shown to correlate with oppositional-defiant and conduct issues in young individuals. Consequently, many interventions for these behavioral challenges prioritize enhancing effective parenting strategies and reducing detrimental rearing practices. The theoretical premise that improved parenting is the primary driver for reducing children's behavioral problems is widely supported by research. This improvement not only benefits the children but also alleviates parental stress and depression. Such a reduction in parental distress, in turn, fosters the sustained application of effective parenting techniques, thereby reinforcing the long-term efficacy of therapeutic interventions. This reciprocal relationship underscores the critical role of parental well-being in maintaining positive behavioral changes in children. Therefore, interventions that target parenting practices offer a comprehensive approach to addressing child behavior problems, yielding benefits for both children and their caregivers.
The Social Interaction Learning (SIL) model serves as the foundation for numerous parent training initiatives. This model posits that a child's psychosocial development is directly shaped by their parents and their chosen parenting strategies. However, external factors, such as parental divorce, can negatively affect parenting quality, thereby indirectly impacting the child. The SIL model identifies five key effective parenting practices: skill encouragement, discipline, monitoring, problem-solving, and positive involvement. Skill encouragement focuses on fostering prosocial development through scaffolding techniques, like breaking down behaviors into smaller steps and prompting appropriate actions, alongside providing positive reinforcement, such as complimenting good behavior. Discipline aims to reduce problematic behaviors by consistently and appropriately applying mild sanctions, such as time-outs or privilege removal. Monitoring involves parents being aware of their child's friendships and activities to safeguard them from negative peer influences. Problem-solving assists families in navigating disagreements, establishing clear rules, and defining consequences for rule violations. Lastly, positive involvement encompasses various ways parents demonstrate affection and engage in enjoyable shared activities with their child.
Parenting skills are frequently assessed through observations of parent-child interactions, such as during structured playroom sessions or in naturalistic home or school settings. However, self-report measures can also be employed to evaluate parenting. Research suggests that combining observational and self-report methods in a multi-method assessment offers enhanced validity compared to relying on a single measurement approach. This integrated strategy provides a more comprehensive understanding of parenting practices.
Various self-report instruments exist for evaluating parenting, though they differ significantly in their theoretical underpinnings and definitions. Among these, the Caregiver Wish List (CWL), developed by Hodges in 2002, stands out as a structured, interview-based tool. Its design specifically allows parents and other caregivers to articulate their parenting experiences and reflect on their own practices. Notably, the CWL was conceptualized to assess the core parenting behaviors outlined in the Social Interaction Learning (SIL) model, making it especially valuable in clinical settings. Its utility stems from several key aspects. Firstly, it provides clinicians with a means to gather parents' perspectives on their parenting strengths and areas needing improvement. Secondly, the interview format is expected to foster a stronger therapeutic alliance and positive engagement between parents and clinicians. Lastly, the CWL aids in defining and clarifying treatment objectives. Ultimately, the CWL aims to empower parents by positioning them as primary agents of change for their children, as highlighted by Hodges in 2005. The CWL originally encompasses six predefined domains of parenting skills: providing direction and follow-up, encouraging positive behavior, discouraging undesirable behavior, monitoring activities, fostering positive connections with the child, and problem-solving orientation.
The Caregiver Wish List (CWL) is currently utilized in the Netherlands as an intake assessment for the Parent Management Training–Oregon (PMTO) model. This training program, designed for parents of children aged 4 to 12, aims to reduce externalizing behavioral problems by teaching effective parenting skills aligned with the Social Interaction Learning (SIL) model. The CWL's direct measurement of these target parenting skills makes its use in PMTO logical. Despite its application in PMTO, including a large-scale Dutch study evaluating the program's effectiveness (Thijssen et al., 2017), the CWL's psychometric properties have been largely unexamined. The aforementioned study, for instance, revealed poor reliability for the CWL, with only one of its six original parenting domains demonstrating adequate internal consistency. This lack of empirical investigation into the CWL's psychometric soundness represents a significant gap in the literature. To address this, the present study undertook an initial examination of the CWL's psychometric qualities. We administered the CWL to a substantial sample of parents, encompassing both a general community population and a clinical group whose children exhibited disruptive behavior problems. This comprehensive approach allowed for a thorough exploration of the instrument's factor structure, its reliability (specifically, internal consistency and inter-factor correlations), and its validity (by analyzing the relationships between parenting scores and children's psychopathological symptoms). This research marks a crucial step in understanding and potentially improving the CWL as a valuable assessment tool.
A total of 348 parents participated, with 92.1% being mothers. Their children, aged 4-11 (mean age 8.07 years, SD 1.61), comprised 185 boys (53.2%) and 163 girls (46.8%). The primary caregivers averaged 39.06 years old (SD 5.15), with most being biological parents (92.8%), Dutch nationals (89.1%), and employed (72.4%). Over two-thirds (63.8%) of these caregivers lived with a partner. Educational attainment varied, with 73% holding a college or university degree, 17.9% having completed high school, and 1.1% having finished only elementary school. Demographic comparisons between community and clinical samples revealed notable differences. Community sample children and parents were significantly older (t(346) = 7.94, p < .01; t(327) = 4.95, p < .01, respectively). Furthermore, the community sample included a higher proportion of girls (χ2(1) = 16.00, p < .01) and more two-parent households (χ2(1) = 8.57, p < .01) compared to the clinical sample. Community sample parents were recruited through elementary schools. Information letters detailing the study and consent forms, alongside a brief demographic survey (covering ethnicity, education, employment), were distributed. Participating parents signed consent, completed the survey, and returned these via their child's teacher. Subsequently, the primary caregiver was contacted to schedule a 30-minute Caregiver Wish List (CWL) interview, conducted at the child's school. A €10 gift voucher was provided as compensation. This study received approval from the Medical Ethical Committee of Maastricht University's Academic Hospital (NL19855.068.07/MEC 07-3-091).
Participants for the clinical sample were recruited from families referred to a child service agency for disruptive behavior problems, as part of a larger study on PMTO effectiveness (Thijssen et al., 2017). Families were screened against specific inclusion criteria. Those who qualified received detailed study information and an invitation to participate. Written consent was obtained from parents who agreed to join. The Caregiver Wish List (CWL) interview was then administered to the primary caregiver, predominantly at the child service agency. Crucially, only CWL data collected before any treatment commenced were utilized for the current investigation. This ensured that the assessment reflected baseline parenting practices.
The Caregiver Wish List (CWL), an interview-based instrument developed by Hodges (2005) and Hodges, de Ruiter, & Thijssen (2009), comprises 53 items designed to assess parental skills. During the interview, parents respond to questions read by the interviewer, selecting the most appropriate option from a 5-point Likert-type scale. While response options vary by question, they primarily gauge the frequency of specific behaviors. Parents can also indicate "not applicable" if a situation does not pertain to their family. The CWL includes 29 positively worded items and 24 negatively phrased items. The scoring for negative items is reversed, ensuring that higher overall CWL scores consistently indicate more effective parenting practices. Hodges' (2002) conceptual framework categorizes these items into six predefined domains of parenting skills. These domains are: "providing direction and following up" (four items), "encouraging good behavior" (five items), "discouraging undesirable behavior" (24 items), "monitoring activities" (13 items), "connecting positively with child" (three items), and "problem solving orientation" (four items). Each domain is conceptualized as a continuum, with one extreme representing weak parenting skills and the other strong parenting skills.
The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) is a well-established instrument for evaluating behavioral and emotional issues in children aged 6 to 18. This widely utilized rating scale, available in a Dutch version, comprises 120 items. Responses are recorded on a 3-point Likert-type scale, ranging from "not true" (0) to "very or often true" (2). For this study, only the internalizing and externalizing subscales were employed. The CBCL is recognized for its strong reliability and validity.
Given the absence of prior research on the Caregiver Wish List's (CWL) factor structure, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was deemed more suitable than a confirmatory approach. This EFA was guided by the CWL's existing conceptual framework. The analysis utilized Mplus (version 5.21) and robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to account for non-normal item distributions. An oblique rotation method (geomin) was employed, anticipating correlations among the positive parenting factors. Unlike principal components analysis, EFA does not quantify the proportion of common variance explained. Therefore, to assess the quality of our factor solution, we examined several model fit indices typically reported in confirmatory factor analysis: the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). While these indices offer complementary information and should be interpreted cautiously in an EFA context, they provide valuable insight into the factor solution's quality. An accurate representation of the original correlation matrix would be indicated by a TLI exceeding 0.95, an RMSEA below 0.05, and an SRMR less than 0.08, as per Hu and Bentler (1999).
Despite the relatively small size of individual samples compared to the number of items, an initial factor structure exploration was conducted for each sample separately. This approach, while potentially increasing solution instability, mitigated confounding from heterogeneous populations. A combined analysis was deemed appropriate only if factor solutions demonstrated sufficient similarity across separate samples. This methodology aimed to ensure that any observed factor structure was robust and not an artifact of combining diverse groups prematurely. Consequently, this sequential analysis strategy prioritized the integrity of findings by first examining within-sample consistency before generalizing across the broader dataset.
The CWL factors' ability to differentiate between community and clinical samples was evaluated using independent-samples t-tests. Internal consistency of the extracted factors was determined by Cronbach's alpha. Convergent validity was established through correlational tests linking CWL factors to CBCL scores. Additionally, an adapted Steiger's formula was employed to compare correlation coefficients, specifically investigating differences in correlations between CWL factors and internalizing versus externalizing problems.
Prior to factor analysis, several preliminary decisions were necessary. We addressed "not applicable" responses in the CWL, which parents selected when situations were irrelevant. These were treated as missing values, and robust maximum likelihood estimation allowed their retention in the analysis. However, items with over 10% "not applicable" responses were excluded, a threshold chosen because traditional missing data methods yield similar results below this point. This led to the removal of three items. Next, we screened item distributions for excessive skewness. Items with scores of 4 or 5 in 95% of one sample and at least 90% in the other were excluded due to low discriminatory power; six such items were removed. Additionally, items with mean scores below 2.50 in the community sample were excluded, as this indicated infrequent practice of the behaviors. This criterion removed six more items, as they did not differentiate between community and clinical samples. In total, 13 items were removed before conducting the exploratory factor analysis.
Prior to conducting the exploratory factor analysis (EFA), a rigorous item screening process was implemented, leading to the exclusion of 13 items from the Caregiver Wish List (CWL). This process involved two primary stages: identifying skewed distributions and low mean scores. First, items exhibiting excessively skewed distributions were removed. Specifically, any item where 95% of responses in one sample and at least 90% in the other sample were concentrated at scores of 4 or 5 was deemed to lack sufficient discriminatory power and thus excluded. This criterion led to the removal of six items that displayed such extreme distributions across both the community and clinical samples. Second, items with conspicuously low mean scores, suggesting infrequent practice of the associated parenting behaviors, were also excluded. The expectation was that parenting skill scores in the community sample would, at minimum, be around a "neutral" rating of 3 or higher. Consequently, all items with a mean value below 2.50 in the community sample were removed. A mean below 2.50 signifies that the majority of responses indicated "almost never" or "now and then" (where "now and then" denotes a lower frequency than "sometimes," coded as 3). While a low mean in the community sample doesn't inherently invalidate an item as a diagnostic marker, as it could still be significantly lower in a clinical sample, this was not generally observed. With the sole exception of Item 21, all items initially flagged by this criterion actually showed higher mean scores in the clinical sample compared to the general population sample. This counterintuitive finding indicated that parents in the clinical sample reported performing these behaviors more frequently than those in the general population, rendering these items unsuitable for diagnostic differentiation. Item 21, while having a lower mean in the clinical sample, did not show a statistically significant difference from the general population sample. Therefore, these six items were also excluded due to their inability to differentiate between the general and clinical populations. In total, these two screening criteria resulted in the removal of 13 items before the EFA was performed.
Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to investigate the Caregiver Wish List's (CWL) factor structure. Initial assessment of sampling adequacy using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure yielded acceptable values for both the community (KMO = 0.67) and clinical (KMO = 0.64) samples, indicating suitability for factor analysis. However, the initial EFA attempt for the community sample encountered convergence issues in parameter estimation. Examination of the output revealed unusual mean and covariance estimates for four specific items. While multiple factors can impede convergence, the most probable cause identified was low initial communality values for these four items. Consequently, these items were removed from the analysis, resolving the convergence problem. The EFA was then re-executed on the community sample with the remaining 36 items, and also performed on the clinical sample. Applying the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1) initially suggested 12 factors for both samples, a number deemed impractical. Further analysis involved inspecting scree plots, which indicated potential five, six, or seven-factor solutions. Subsequent evaluation of fit indices for both the community and clinical samples narrowed the possibilities to either a five- or six-factor solution. The sixth factor proved challenging to interpret meaningfully, leading to the selection of a five-factor solution as the most appropriate. This five-factor model yielded interpretable factors with acceptable Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values, although Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) values remained somewhat low. Given the notable similarity in factor loading patterns observed across both samples, a decision was made to re-run the EFA on the combined dataset. This consolidated analysis also resulted in a five-factor solution. For this combined sample, the five-factor solution demonstrated good fit values for the RMSEA and SRMR measures. The TLI value also showed improvement in the combined analysis, though it still did not reach the conventionally acceptable threshold. This comprehensive approach aimed to leverage the larger sample size for more stable parameter estimates while confirming the consistency of the factor structure across different populations.
The five-factor solution, derived from the total sample, excluded 13 items due to insignificant factor loadings or loadings below .40 across all factors. Table 3 details the final factor loadings for the remaining items in the community, clinical, and combined samples. Factor 1, termed "adequate discipline," comprised five items exclusively from the original "discouraging undesirable behavior" domain, all reflecting parental disciplinary actions following a child's misbehavior. Factor 2, "controlled responding," contained four items. These primarily originated from the "discouraging undesirable behavior" scale, with one exception (Item 3) from "providing direction and following up," and described parents' calm and neutral responses to a child's misbehavior. "Focus on positive behavior" was the label for Factor 3, which consisted of four items. Although these items originated from various domains, they consistently measured parents' attentiveness and positive reactions to their child's prosocial conduct. Factor 4, named "consistency," included six items, all drawn from the "discouraging undesirable behavior" domain. This factor captured parents' adherence to consequences after disciplining their child. The fifth and final factor, "monitoring," comprised four items, all belonging to the original "monitoring activities" scale. While items 17 and 45 exhibited notable secondary loadings, they were ultimately assigned to Factor 3 and Factor 5, respectively, based on their highest loadings. This refined five-factor structure provides a clearer understanding of the underlying dimensions of parenting practices measured by the CWL.
Internal consistency of the five identified factors was assessed using Cronbach's alpha across community, clinical, and combined samples. All alpha values ranged from .61 to .82. Applying a .70 cutoff, four of the five factors demonstrated good internal consistency within the clinical and total samples. The overall CWL score also showed good internal consistency across all three sample types. However, the community sample exhibited generally lower Cronbach's alphas, with only the "adequate discipline" and "monitoring" factors reaching acceptable levels. It is important to note that despite these lower values in some instances, all CWL factors comprised a limited number of items. Therefore, alpha values exceeding .60 for such concise item sets are generally considered acceptable.
We calculated Pearson's product-moment correlations among the five factors for both individual samples and the combined total sample (Table 5). Generally, these correlations were low. However, two specific correlations were consistently significant across all three analyses: the positive relationship between controlled responding and focusing on positive behavior, and the positive relationship between adequate discipline and consistency. These positive correlations suggest that parents who effectively manage their emotions during disciplinary actions also tend to highlight their child's positive behaviors. Similarly, parents who maintain consistency in setting limits are also more adept at applying adequate discipline. While we anticipated correlations among the three factors originating from the "discouraging undesirable behavior" domain (adequate discipline, controlled responding, and consistency), this was only observed in the total sample. This indicates a more nuanced interplay among these parenting dimensions than initially hypothesized.
The factor loading patterns were largely consistent across both community and clinical samples, leading to a re-analysis using the combined total sample to enhance parameter estimate stability. Mean scores and standard deviations for each factor were then calculated for both sample types. Significant differences emerged between the samples across all factors and the overall CWL score (comprising 23 items). Specifically, parents in the community sample consistently exhibited significantly higher mean scores on all factors compared to their clinical counterparts. This indicates that parenting skills were generally more developed in the community sample. This consistency in factor structure across samples, coupled with the observed differences in skill levels, supports the generalizability of the CWL's factor structure while also highlighting its ability to differentiate between these distinct populations.
The study examined the relationship between the five CWL factors and child psychopathology, specifically internalizing and externalizing behaviors, using Pearson's product-moment correlations. All CWL factors and the overall CWL score showed significant negative correlations with both internalizing and externalizing scales of the CBCL, indicating that better parenting skills were associated with fewer child behavior problems. The CWL monitoring factor exhibited comparatively weaker, though still significant, negative correlations. Notably, correlations with externalizing problems were stronger than with internalizing problems for three CWL factors (controlled responding, focus on positive behavior, and consistency) and for the total CWL score. This outcome aligns with expectations, as the parenting skills assessed by the CWL are considered crucial for addressing externalizing behavior issues in children.
This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Caregiver Wish List (CWL), an interview-based tool designed to assess parenting behaviors within the Social Interaction Learning (SIL) model. We conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using data from parents of elementary school-aged children, drawing from both community and clinical populations. Our investigation focused on determining the CWL's factor structure and the internal reliability of the identified factors. Additionally, we assessed if these factors meaningfully correlated with children's behavioral problems and could distinguish between community and clinical samples. This research represents an initial effort to understand the CWL's measurement qualities.
An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) identified five distinct factors: adequate discipline, controlled responding, focus on positive behavior, consistency, and monitoring. While two conventional fit indices indicated a reasonable fit for this five-factor structure with the original correlation matrix, one index suggested a poor fit. Each factor represents specific parenting behaviors. Adequate discipline refers to parental responses to child misbehavior. Controlled responding evaluates a parent's reaction to misbehavior, such as maintaining a calm and neutral tone. Focus on positive behavior measures a parent's capacity to recognize and reinforce desirable child behaviors. Consistency reflects a parent's commitment to enforcing stated consequences, even when faced with a child's negative reactions. Lastly, monitoring assesses a parent's awareness of their child's activities and social circles.
The factor structure identified in this study for the Caregiver Wish List (CWL) only partially aligns with the six domains originally proposed by Hodges (2002), the CWL's developer. Our analysis yielded five factors, in contrast to the hypothesized six. Notably, three of these five factors—adequate discipline, controlled responding, and consistency—were exclusively derived from items within the CWL's original "discouraging undesirable behavior" domain. This outcome was anticipated, given that this particular domain encompassed approximately half of all initial CWL items, representing a diverse array of parenting behaviors aimed at addressing undesirable child conduct. Furthermore, the "monitoring" factor, while consistent with the original "monitoring activities" domain, incorporated only four of its thirteen initial items. The "focus on positive behavior" factor, however, drew items from various CWL domains, indicating a broader conceptualization of positive behavioral reinforcement. Significantly, the "problem solving" domain, originally comprising four items, did not emerge as a distinct or cohesive parenting factor in our exploratory factor analysis and was subsequently discarded. This suggests that direct observational methods may be more effective for assessing this specific parenting skill. Despite these deviations from the original conceptualization, the five factors identified in our study largely correspond to four of the five core parenting practices within the Social Interaction Learning (SIL) model: effective discipline, encouragement, positive involvement, and monitoring. This alignment suggests that, even with a revised factor structure, the CWL retains its relevance to key theoretical constructs of effective parenting.
The Caregiver Wish List (CWL) could be substantially shortened without sacrificing essential information, as only 23 of its initial 53 items contributed to the identified five factors. Despite this reduction in item count, the resulting factors demonstrated adequate internal consistency, with Cronbach's alphas ranging from .61 to .77. Furthermore, the overall CWL score, derived from these selected items, exhibited strong internal consistency across all samples, with coefficients between .71 and .82. However, the inter-factor correlations revealed some unexpected patterns. Only two relationships consistently appeared across community, clinical, and combined samples: a positive correlation between "controlled responding" and "focus on positive behavior," and another between "adequate discipline" and "consistency." Other factor correlations were notably weak. Particularly surprising was the low correlation between "adequate discipline" and "controlled responding," given their shared origin in the CWL's "discouraging undesirable behavior" domain. This suggests these two aspects of parenting, though conceptually linked, operate with greater independence than anticipated within the CWL framework.
The Caregiver Wish List (CWL) factor structure demonstrated generalizability across different parent populations, as the factor structures derived from both community and clinical samples were highly similar. This suggests the CWL's utility for assessing parenting practices in diverse settings. However, notable differences emerged in the absolute scores between these groups. Parents in the community sample consistently showed higher scores on all five CWL factors and the total score, indicating superior parenting skills compared to those in the clinical sample. Furthermore, a significant inverse relationship was observed between all CWL factors, including the total score, and children's behavioral issues. Improved parenting practices, as measured by the CWL, correlated with reduced internalizing and externalizing problems in children. Specifically, the factors of "controlled responding," "focus on positive behavior," and "consistency" exhibited a significantly stronger association with externalizing problems than with internalizing symptoms. This finding supports the external validity of the CWL, particularly given its focus on parenting practices considered crucial for addressing externalizing behavioral challenges.
Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the findings of this study. Firstly, the demographic characteristics of the study participants restrict the generalizability of the results. The sample predominantly comprised biological Dutch parents in two-parent households with stable employment. Consequently, the applicability of these findings to diverse family structures, such as single-parent or blended families, or to populations from different ethnic backgrounds or lower socioeconomic strata, remains undetermined. Secondly, the sample size presented a challenge for the factor analysis, particularly in relation to the original 53-item Caregiver Wish List (CWL). Adhering to the established guideline of at least five cases per item for factor analysis (Hair, Black, Babin, & Anderson, 2009), a minimum of 246 participants would have been necessary for the full CWL. While 17 items were excluded prior to the final factor analysis, reducing the analyzed items to 36, a sample of 180 participants was still required. Although the community and total samples met this criterion, the clinical sample, with only 128 participants, did not. This smaller clinical sample size could potentially impact the stability and reliability of the factor structure derived for that group. Thirdly, the study did not encompass an assessment of construct validity or test-retest reliability. Incorporating these psychometric evaluations would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the CWL's overall utility and consistency over time. Finally, a notable discrepancy emerged in the frequency of "not applicable" responses between the community and clinical samples, with the clinical sample exhibiting a higher rate. This difference could stem from two potential factors. It is plausible that certain parenting scenarios, such as giving an allowance, are genuinely less prevalent in clinical populations compared to community samples. Alternatively, the variation might be attributable to interviewer effects. While both community and clinical parents were interviewed by trained personnel (students for the community sample, clinicians for the clinical sample), and both groups received instructions to minimize "not applicable" responses and encourage the most appropriate answer, the clinical sample's recruitment and interviewing process spanned a longer period. This extended timeframe could have led to a drift in adherence to the interviewing instructions among the clinicians, potentially influencing the observed differences in response patterns.
While the Caregiver Wish List (CWL) currently presents limitations for research, its unique design makes further investigation into its psychometric properties valuable. The CWL, an interview-based tool, offers distinct advantages over self-report questionnaires like the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (APQ) for clinical applications. Unlike the APQ, which may not encompass all parenting skills outlined by the Social Interaction Learning (SIL) model, the CWL was specifically developed to assess these dimensions. This interview format is particularly beneficial in clinical settings, as it facilitates therapists in understanding parents' perceptions of their own parenting strengths and weaknesses. This interactive approach also fosters a stronger therapeutic alliance and positive engagement between parents and clinicians, a crucial aspect that a standard questionnaire cannot achieve. Therefore, despite its current research shortcomings, the CWL holds promise as a clinical instrument. To enhance the CWL's suitability for research, several improvements are necessary. The factor structure identified in this study requires replication to establish definitive conclusions regarding its structural validity. Additionally, to improve the internal consistency of certain factors, new items could be incorporated. Future research should also explore the sensitivity to change of the CWL's extracted factors. Our previous evaluation of Parent Management Training–Oregon model (PMTO) effectiveness, where the CWL served as an outcome measure, indicated that only the original "discouraging undesirable behavior" domain and the overall CWL score demonstrated adequate internal consistency and significant responsiveness to parent training. Beyond these structural and consistency considerations, future studies should investigate the predictive validity of CWL-assessed parenting practices. Specifically, it is important to determine if these practices can predict the emergence or persistence of externalizing behavior problems in children. Furthermore, research should examine the correlation between self-reported parenting practices measured by the CWL and actual parent-child interactions observed in both naturalistic and laboratory settings. This comparison would provide crucial insights into the ecological validity of the CWL. While other, more validated self-report measures like the APQ exist for assessing parenting skills, the CWL's alignment with the SIL model and its interview format for clinical utility warrant continued effort to refine its psychometric qualities for broader research application.
Our study indicates that the Caregiver Wish List (CWL) requires modification for research utility. We could not replicate its hypothesized factor structure, and several identified factors need more items to enhance psychometric properties. While the CWL was developed to measure Social Interaction Learning (SIL) parenting skills, it did not effectively assess problem-solving, a core SIL skill. Nevertheless, the CWL's interview format offers advantages. It facilitates rapport between parents and interviewers, and encourages parents to reflect on their parenting strengths and weaknesses. This reflective aspect could be valuable in clinical settings, even as the instrument's research applicability needs refinement.
