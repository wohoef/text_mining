Pleomorphic invasive lobular cancer (pleomorphic ILC) is a rare subtype of ILC. It presents with a growth pattern similar to classic ILC but exhibits high nuclear atypicality characteristic of invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). The origin of pleomorphic ILC is debated, with ongoing discussion about whether it represents a dedifferentiated form of ILC or an IDC that has lost cohesion. Gene promoter hypermethylation is an early event in cancer development and can offer insights into tumor progression. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the methylation patterns of pleomorphic ILC, classic ILC, and IDC, and to analyze the specific methylation status of pleomorphic ILC. To achieve this, promoter methylation profiling of 24 tumor suppressor genes was conducted using methylation-specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MS-MLPA) on samples from 20 classic ILC, 16 pleomorphic ILC, and 20 IDC cases. The results indicated that pleomorphic ILC had lower TP73 and MLH1 methylation levels and higher RASSF1A methylation levels when compared to classic ILC. Furthermore, pleomorphic ILC showed lower MLH1 and BRCA1 methylation levels in comparison to IDC. Hierarchical cluster analysis revealed that pleomorphic ILC and IDC shared a similar methylation pattern, which differed from that of classic ILC. In conclusion, this study is the first to identify TP73, RASSF1A, MLH1, and BRCA1 as potential biomarkers for distinguishing pleomorphic ILC from classic ILC and IDC.

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) represents the second most common histological subtype of breast cancer, comprising 10–15% of all cases. ILC distinguishes itself from invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) through differences in its biological characteristics, histological appearance, clinical presentation, and therapeutic response. A key feature of most lobular tumors, unlike ductal tumors, is the loss of E-cadherin expression, often attributed to gene mutations, subsequent loss of heterozygosity, or promoter hypermethylation. Experimental models involving conditional E-cadherin inactivation in mice have demonstrated its role in ILC development and progression. Within the spectrum of ILC, classic and pleomorphic ILC are the most frequently encountered variants. While precise figures are not widely available, classic ILC constitutes approximately 60% of all ILC cases, with pleomorphic ILC accounting for about 13%. Classic ILC is characterized by small, regular, low-grade cells with intra-cytoplasmic vacuoles and small nuclei, exhibiting a trabecular infiltrative growth pattern often surrounding uninvolved ducts. In contrast, pleomorphic ILC shares a similar growth and invasion pattern but is composed of high-grade polygonal cells featuring eccentric and highly pleomorphic nuclei. Furthermore, pleomorphic ILC tumors tend to be larger than their classic counterparts, and patients with pleomorphic ILC more frequently present with lymph node involvement and a higher incidence of metastatic disease. These factors contribute to poorer overall survival and higher recurrence rates for pleomorphic ILC patients compared to those with classic ILC, underscoring its more aggressive nature.
Molecularly, classic and pleomorphic invasive lobular carcinomas (ILCs) exhibit both shared characteristics and distinctions. Both subtypes are characterized by a lack of basal markers such as cytokeratin (CK)5 and CK14, while simultaneously expressing luminal epithelial markers like CK8 and CK18. Typically, ILCs fall into the ‘luminal’ type of breast cancer, indicated by the expression of the estrogen receptor (ER) gene and related genes, including the progesterone receptor (PR) gene. A defining feature of ILC is the cytosolic translocation of p120-catenin, a consequence of E-cadherin inactivation. Neither classic nor pleomorphic ILCs over-express the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene. A notable difference arises in HER2 (ERBB2) expression: while most classic ILCs do not express HER2, a significant proportion, up to 81%, of pleomorphic ILCs do. Furthermore, somatic TP53 mutations are considerably more common in pleomorphic ILC (up to 46%) compared to classic ILC (approximately 6%), suggesting a potential role for p53 loss in the development of pleomorphic ILC. This observation is corroborated by studies in mouse models with mammary-specific E-cadherin and p53 knock-out, which have been shown to develop a mouse variant of pleomorphic ILC. Additionally, pleomorphic ILC often displays expression of the apocrine differentiation marker gross cystic disease fluid protein 15 (GCDFP15) and the androgen receptor (AR), features not typically seen in classic ILC. The precise origin of pleomorphic ILC remains a subject of ongoing discussion. While some research suggests evolutionary links among all lobular cancer types, it is not yet definitively established whether pleomorphic ILC represents a dedifferentiated form of classic ILC or arises from ductal-type tumors. Accurate differentiation between these subtypes is crucial for surgical planning, as ILC management often necessitates pre-operative MRI due to its tendency for diffuse, multifocal growth and a higher incidence of contralateral tumors.
DNA methylation is frequently altered in cancer and plays a role in tumor progression. This process involves the enzymatic addition of a methyl group to cytosine, often within CpG dinucleotides, which can lead to gene silencing. Promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes is recognized as an early event in cancer development, as elevated methylation levels are observed in early breast cancer precursors. Consequently, methylation patterns can offer insights into tumor progression and the origins of pleomorphic ILC. The potential for detecting DNA hypermethylation in various biological samples, such as biopsies, blood, nipple fluid, and urine, positions it as a promising area for clinical biomarker development. Furthermore, DNA hypermethylation analysis is compatible with formalin-fixed tissues, making it suitable for molecular profiling and identifying markers that predict treatment response.
This study aimed to identify promoter methylation patterns in pleomorphic invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) compared to classic ILC and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) to discover potential biomarkers for pleomorphic ILC. Methylation analysis was performed using methylation-specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MS-MLPA). This technique is highly reproducible, requires minimal DNA input (10 ng), and demonstrates strong agreement with other established methods like quantitative multiplex methylation-specific PCR. A key advantage of MS-MLPA is its ability to accurately assess methylation status even in samples containing mixed cell populations, as long as 30% of the DNA is methylated. The promoter methylation status of 24 tumor suppressor genes was evaluated in 16 pleomorphic ILC, 20 classic ILC, and 20 IDC cases. The findings revealed that classic ILC and IDC exhibited comparable methylation patterns, while pleomorphic ILC displayed a mildly distinct profile. Notably, the methylation status of the RASSF1A, TP73, MLH1, and BRCA1 gene promoters emerged as significant stratification markers capable of differentiating pleomorphic ILC from both classic ILC and IDC.
The patient samples for this study were collected from pathology departments at the University Medical Centre Utrecht, Radboud University Medical Centre, the Institute of Pathology in Paderborn, Germany, and the Bács-Kiskun County Teaching Hospital in Hungary. Classic invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), pleomorphic ILC, and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) cases were identified by at least two pathologists examining hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides. The use of leftover tissue specimens was approved by the Tissue Science Committee of the UMC Utrecht. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics for all patient samples are presented in Table 1. Histological grading was performed using the Nottingham modification of the Scarff-Bloom-Richardson grading system. Estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) positivity were defined as at least 10% of cells exhibiting positive nuclear staining. HER2 status was determined using the modified DAKO scoring system, with a score of 3+ considered positive. The mitotic activity index (MAI) was assessed as previously described. Table 1 provides data on the age range, histological grade distribution, MAI, lymph node status, receptor status (ER, PR, HER2), and tumor size for each of the three breast cancer groups.
To prepare tissue samples for DNA analysis, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained sections were examined to identify pre-invasive lesions, necrosis, and lymphocytic infiltrates. These areas, along with any regions of necrosis, dense lymphocytic infiltrates, and pre-invasive lesions, were deliberately excluded from further analysis. The selected micro-dissected areas consistently contained at least 70% tumor cells. Tumor tissue was obtained from 4 μm thick sections, comprising 5 to 10 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded slices. DNA isolation was achieved through overnight incubation in a lysis buffer containing proteinase K at 56°C, followed by a 10-minute boiling step. A 5-minute centrifugation at 12,000 g was performed, and 5 μl of the supernatant was then used for MLPA analysis. This analysis was conducted according to the manufacturer's instructions using the ME001-C2 methylation kit from MRC-Holland. The methodology of MS-MLPA has been previously detailed, with PCR and data analysis procedures following established protocols. The ME001-C2 MS-MLPA probe mix includes 26 probes designed to assess the methylation status of promoter CpG sites within 24 established and putative tumor suppressor genes. These genes are known to be frequently silenced by hypermethylation in tumors, unlike in DNA derived from healthy individuals' blood. Additionally, 15 reference probes were incorporated. The cumulative methylation index (CMI) was calculated by summing the methylation percentages of all analyzed genes, as previously described.
Statistical analyses, including Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analyses, were conducted using IBM SPSS statistics v20.0. A significance level of p < 0.05 (two-sided) was applied. To compare absolute methylation levels between classic ILC, pleomorphic ILC, and IDC samples, Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U Test, and the Kruskal-Wallis test were employed. Multiple comparisons were accounted for by applying Bonferroni-Holm correction to all p-values, thereby minimizing false positives. Logistic regression analysis, utilizing a backward stepwise method, identified the optimal gene combinations for differentiating pleomorphic ILC from classic ILC and/or IDC. Furthermore, unsupervised hierarchical clustering, based on Z-scores and employing a Euclidean metric in the R statistical program, was performed to delineate relevant sample clusters.
To evaluate differences in methylation patterns across classic ILC, pleomorphic ILC, and IDC, a Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA was performed on the methylation data. Out of the 24 genes analyzed, 16 exhibited significant variations between the groups, including BRCA1. Following adjustments for multiple comparisons, only the methylation patterns of TP73, MLH1_b, and RASSF1A_x demonstrated statistically significant differences among the three breast cancer subtypes. Further analysis using a post hoc Mann-Whitney test revealed specific distinctions. Between pleomorphic and classic ILC, TP73, MLH1_y, and RASSF1A_x methylation patterns were significantly different. Specifically, pleomorphic ILC showed reduced promoter methylation in MLH1_y and TP73 compared to classic ILC, while RASSF1A promoter methylation was elevated in pleomorphic ILC. When comparing pleomorphic ILC to IDC, MLH1_y and BRCA1 methylation levels were found to be significantly lower in pleomorphic ILC. Logistic regression analysis indicated that BRCA1 methylation alone possessed independent discriminative value for distinguishing pleomorphic ILC from IDC. The hierarchical cluster analysis, based on the methylation data of all genes and the four significantly differentially methylated genes, supported these findings by showing a tendency for pleomorphic ILC samples to cluster with IDC samples, while classic ILC samples often formed separate clusters.
Following the initial Kruskal-Wallis analysis, a post hoc Mann-Whitney test was performed on the 16 identified genes to pinpoint specific methylation differences between classic and pleomorphic invasive lobular carcinomas (ILCs). This analysis revealed significant distinctions in the methylation patterns of TP73, MLH1, and RASSF1A between these two subtypes. Specifically, pleomorphic ILCs exhibited reduced promoter methylation in MLH1 (p = 0.003) and TP73 (p = 0.001) compared to classic ILCs. Conversely, RASSF1A promoter methylation was found to be elevated in pleomorphic ILCs (p = 0.001). The cumulative methylation index (CMI) did not show a significant difference between pleomorphic and classic ILCs (353.3 vs. 390.0, p = 0.437). Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that TP73 (p = 0.017) and RASSF1A (p = 0.005) together provided independent discriminative value for distinguishing pleomorphic from classic ILCs. This combination achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.888 (CI 0.764-1.000, p < 0.001), with a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis yielding a sensitivity of 81% and a specificity of 100%.
Comparing pleomorphic ILC to IDC, significantly lower methylation levels were observed for MLH1_y (p = 0.001) and BRCA1 (p = 0.002) in pleomorphic ILC. Despite these specific gene differences, the overall methylation patterns, as indicated by the mean cumulative methylation index (CMI), were similar between pleomorphic ILC (353.3) and IDC (392.6), with no significant difference (p = 0.357). Logistic regression analysis identified BRCA1 methylation as an independent marker for distinguishing pleomorphic ILC from IDC, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.781 (CI 0.623-0.939, p = 0.004). This marker demonstrated a sensitivity of 75% and a specificity of 81% in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.
To ascertain whether the MS-MLPA methylation values could effectively differentiate the three breast cancer subtypes, hierarchical Euclidean cluster analysis was conducted. This analysis was performed using the methylation data from all tested genes (Figure 3a) and subsequently, focusing on the four genes that exhibited significant differences in the Mann-Whitney tests (Figure 3b). Both clustering approaches demonstrated a tendency for pleomorphic ILC samples to group with IDC samples. In contrast, classic ILC samples predominantly formed distinct clusters, often alongside other IDC samples. This clustering pattern suggests a degree of similarity in methylation profiles between pleomorphic ILC and IDC, setting them apart from classic ILC.
DNA methylation analyses offer significant potential for disease stratification and prognostication, leading to numerous studies, particularly in breast cancer. However, most existing research has concentrated on invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), with a lack of studies investigating DNA methylation in pleomorphic invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC). Previous comparisons of ILC and IDC have revealed highly similar DNA methylation patterns between these two subtypes. Our findings align with this, indicating that while pleomorphic ILC shares a similar cumulative methylation index (CMI) with classic ILC and IDC, it presents a distinct methylation profile compared to classic ILC.
BRCA1, MLH1, and RASSF1A are recognized tumor suppressor genes, while TP73 is considered a putative one. Both BRCA1 and MLH1 play roles in DNA repair, and their loss of function leads to an accumulation of genetic defects. Notably, we observed a significant link between MLH1_x and BRCA1 promoter methylation and a high mitotic activity index (MAI > 12). BRCA1 promoter methylation has been previously reported in 10-15% of sporadic breast cancers. In contrast, only a small percentage of lobular tumors harbor BRCA1 mutations, and prior studies found no BRCA1 hypermethylation in ILC samples. Given that MLH1 and BRCA1 methylation levels were lower in pleomorphic ILC compared to IDC, these genes are less likely to be therapeutic targets but show potential as biomarkers.
To assess the reliability of the MS-MLPA technique, 10 primary breast tumor samples were analyzed in duplicate across at least eight separate runs. This evaluation revealed average standard deviations for the TP73, MLH1_y, RASSF1A_x, and BRCA1 probes of 0.01, 0.01, 0.05, and 0.02 per sample, respectively. Consequently, the observed differences between groups for TP73 and RASSF1A_x are expected to be reproducible and dependable. However, the less pronounced differences noted for MLH1_y and BRCA1 may stem from technical variability. Therefore, independent validation using a highly sensitive and quantitative method is recommended for these findings.
TP73 exhibits alternative splicing, leading to various p73 isoforms with opposing impacts on tumor development due to alternative promoter usage. While TP73 promoter methylation has been linked to poorer breast cancer patient survival, it also hinders the binding of the transcriptional repressor ZEB1, potentially increasing TP73 expression. Limited studies exist on TP73 methylation in normal breast tissue, and these are not integrated with protein or RNA expression data. Furthermore, TP73 methylation has not been previously investigated in ILC. Our findings indicate that TP73 promoter methylation is relatively low in pleomorphic ILC compared to classic ILC, suggesting its utility as a biomarker rather than a therapeutic target. Additional research is necessary to elucidate the effect of TP73 methylation on protein expression and its functional implications in pleomorphic ILC.
In pleomorphic ILC, RASSF1A promoter methylation levels were elevated compared to classic ILC. While uncommon, RASSF1A polymorphisms and deletions have been noted, and RASSF1A promoter hypermethylation is a frequent occurrence across various tumor types. This hypermethylation is observed in a substantial majority of both ILC and IDC cases, ranging from 70% to 85%. Furthermore, RASSF1A hypermethylation in pre-operative serum has been identified as an independent prognostic marker associated with poorer overall survival in breast cancer patients. Given its infrequent presence in normal breast tissues, RASSF1A hypermethylation is regarded as an early event in breast cancer development, positioning it as a potentially valuable biomarker. RASSF1, part of the RASSF gene family (RASSF 1–8), produces eight distinct isoforms through alternative splicing and promoter utilization. Alongside RAF and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), RASSF proteins function as RAS effectors, binding to the GTP-bound form of RAS. Unlike RAF and PI3K, which regulate proliferation and survival, RASSF genes operate as tumor suppressors. Studies involving RASSF1A null-mice have revealed an increased incidence of spontaneous tumor formation, reduced survival rates, and heightened susceptibility to mutagens. Additionally, introducing RASSF1A into various tumor cell lines has been shown to decrease their viability, proliferation, and invasion. These collective findings, coupled with the observed increase in RASSF1A promoter methylation in pleomorphic ILC, highlight RASSF1A as a compelling and functionally relevant biomarker for lobular breast cancer.
Our findings suggest that the promoter methylation patterns of TP73, MLH1, RASSF1A, and BRCA1 can differentiate pleomorphic ILC from classic ILC and IDC. Given pleomorphic ILC's aggressive nature and the differing utility of pre-operative MRI for ILC versus IDC, these methylation biomarkers could aid treatment planning, particularly when distinguishing between these subtypes is challenging. Further validation in an independent cohort is necessary, along with an assessment of these markers' potential as therapeutic targets.
