While Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is a known cause of anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers, its biological behavior in various mucosal sites, particularly the oral cavity, remains poorly understood. Crucially, whether oral tissues support HPV infection and replication has been an open question. Our study, for the first time, examined HPV16 titers, infectivity, and maturation in oral epithelial tissue compared to genital epithelial tissue. We successfully demonstrated that oral tissue can produce infectious HPV16 virions, indicating the oral cavity's potential role in viral transmission. Furthermore, HPV16 from oral tissue utilizes a redox gradient within the tissue, which promotes virion maturation over time. This maturation is characterized by enhanced virion stability and increased vulnerability to neutralization by anti-HPV16 L1 antibodies. Conversely, the susceptibility to neutralization by anti-HPV16 L2 specific antibodies diminishes as HPV16 virions mature in oral tissue.

While overall cancer death rates have declined across various demographics and cancer types, a notable exception is the rising incidence of oropharyngeal human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated cancers (Jemal et al., 2013). This trend is particularly significant given the evolving cancer burden among HIV-infected individuals in the U.S. Following the introduction of highly active anti-retroviral therapy (HAART), non-AIDS-defining malignancies now constitute the majority of cancers in this population (Engels et al., 2008). Among these, HPV-associated cancers of the oral cavity and pharynx have shown a marked increase after an AIDS diagnosis (Engels et al., 2008; Grulich et al., 2010). HPV is profoundly altering the epidemiology of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Despite its growing impact, the specific mechanisms and natural progression of oral HPV infection, as well as its role in oncogenesis, remain largely unclear. Understanding oral HPV disease cannot be adequately achieved by simply applying knowledge gleaned from HPV-associated infections and oncogenicity in the genital tract. For instance, while HPV is essential for invasive cervical cancer, it alone is not sufficient. Similarly, although HPV is a key contributor to oropharyngeal cancer, its presence is neither necessary nor sufficient for all HNSCC cases, as some occur independently of HPV. Consequently, HPV-related HNSCC warrants dedicated investigation as a distinct disease entity, separate from other HNSCC types and genital HPV infections, to fully elucidate its unique biological characteristics and clinical implications.
HPV16, the predominant HPV type in over 90% of HPV-associated head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs) and a common cause of anogenital cancers, has a well-documented lifecycle in anogenital tissues. However, its lifecycle within oral tissues remains poorly understood. Crucially, direct evidence demonstrating that oral tissue can support the replication of infectious HPV particles has been absent until now.
To establish HPV16-positive cell lines, primary human tonsil (HTLK) and foreskin keratinocytes (HFK) were initially isolated and cultured. Subsequently, 10 μg of pBSHPV16 plasmid DNA was linearized via BamH1 digestion to separate the viral DNA from its vector. This viral DNA was then introduced into either HTLKs or HFKs through electroporation using a Gene Pulser. The resulting immortalized keratinocytes, which stably retained the HPV16 genomes after electroporation, were maintained in E-medium alongside J2 3T3 feeder cells. This process allowed for the generation of cell lines capable of supporting HPV16 studies.
To assess the HPV16 genome within immortalized HTLK keratinocytes, Southern blot hybridization was performed. This involved isolating total cellular DNA from the cell line. The viral genome was then linearized by digesting 5 μg of this DNA with BamHI. Subsequently, samples underwent gel electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel. The separated DNA was transferred to a GeneScreen Plus membrane. Finally, the membrane was probed with an HPV16-specific, whole-genome probe. This method, previously established, allowed for the characterization of the HPV16 genome's state and quantity within the cells.
To investigate HPV16 infection, organotypic raft cultures were established. Immortalized human tonsil (HTLK) and human foreskin keratinocyte (HFK) cell lines, chronically infected with HPV16, were seeded onto collagen matrices. These matrices, composed of rat-tail type 1 collagen and incorporating J2 3T3 feeder cells, supported cell attachment and growth to confluence. Subsequently, the matrices were elevated onto stainless steel grids and nourished with E-medium, supplemented with 10 μM 1,2-dioctanoyl-sn-glycerol, supplied by diffusion. Cultures were maintained for 10, 15, or 20 days to allow for stratification and differentiation.
Raft culture tissues were collected at various intervals. These samples were then fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, four-micrometer sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).
To prepare viral samples, HPV-infected raft tissues were first harvested. These tissues underwent homogenization in 500 μl of phosphate buffer (0.05 M sodium phosphate [pH 8.0]/2 mM MgCl2), with an additional 250 μl of buffer used to rinse the homogenizers. Subsequently, unprotected viral genomes were digested by adding 1.5 μl (375 U) of benzonase to 750 μl of the viral preparation, followed by a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C. To concentrate the samples, 188 μl of ice-cold 5 M NaCl was added, bringing the solution to 1 M NaCl. The samples were then vortexed and centrifuged at 10,500 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. For further purification, OptiPrep density gradients were prepared by layering 27%, 33%, and 39% OptiPrep solutions, allowing them to diffuse for 1 hour at room temperature. A 600 μl aliquot of the clarified, benzonase-treated viral preparation was then carefully layered onto the top of each gradient. These gradients were then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 234,000 × g for 3.5 hours at 16 °C in a SW55 rotor. Following centrifugation, 11 fractions, each 500 μl in volume, were meticulously collected from top to bottom from each tube.
Viral genome quantification was performed using a Thermo Scientific Maxima SYBR Green qPCR kit. First, viral genomes were extracted from benzonase-treated preparations. This involved resuspending the preparations in HPRT DNA extraction buffer (400 mM NaCl/10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4]/10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), then adding 2 μl of 20 mg/ml proteinase K and 10 μl of 10% SDS. This mixture was incubated for 2–4 hours at 37 °C. After incubation, the DNA was purified through phenol-chloroform extraction, followed by ethanol precipitation overnight at −20 °C. For amplification, 0.3 μM of both forward (5′-CCATATAGACTATTGGAAACACATGCGCC-3′, nucleotides 2839 to 2868) and reverse (5′-CGTTAGTTGCAGTTCAATTGCTTGTAATGC-3′, nucleotides 2960 to 2989) primers were used to target the HPV16 E2 open reading frame (ORF). A standard curve was generated using serially diluted pBSHPV16 DNA, ranging from 10^8 to 10^4 copies/μl. A Bio-Rad iQ5 Multicolor Real-Time qPCR machine and its associated software were employed for both PCR amplification and subsequent data analysis.
HaCaT keratinocytes were used to assess infectivity. Cells were plated at 50,000 cells per well in 24-well plates, and infectivity assays were conducted as previously detailed (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013). For these assays, cells were exposed to the virus for 48 hours at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following this incubation, mRNA was extracted using an RNeasy kit (Qiagen). The extent of infection was then determined by quantifying the E1∧E4 splice transcript levels via a reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay. Specifically, HPV16 E1∧E4 was detected using a forward primer (5′-GCTGATCCTAGCAACGAAGTATC-3′), a reverse primer (5′-TTCTTCGGGTGCCCAAAGGC-3′), and a probe (5′-(6-FAM)CCCGCCGCGACCCATACCAAAGCC(BHQ-1)-3′). For neutralization studies, the virus was pre-incubated for one hour at 37 °C with specific antibodies before infecting the cells. These antibodies included the conformation-dependent anti-L1 antibody H16.V5 (1:1000 dilution, provided by Neil Christensen, Penn State College of Medicine) and the anti-L2 antibodies RG-1 (1:100 dilution, from Richard Roden, Johns Hopkins) and L2#4 (1:100 dilution, from Tadahito Kanda, RIKEN institute). After antibody incubation, the standard infectivity assay was performed as described previously (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013).
To quantify HPV16 L1, OptiPrep fractions were prepared for gel electrophoresis. Aliquots were boiled in 2-ME loading buffer, then loaded onto polyacrylamide gels. The Camvir-1 anti-HPV16 L1 monoclonal antibody (1:2500 dilution) detected L1. A Bio-Rad G8-800 densitometer with Quantity One software then determined the relative L1 quantity in the resulting bands.
To visualize sulfhydryls, HPV16-infected tonsil and foreskin epithelial tissue sections were stained with DACM. Free and disulfide bond-specific sulfhydryls were detected. Imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope, capturing images with a CoolSnap Cf2 camera and NIS-elements 3.10 software.
To investigate human papillomavirus 16 (HPV16) infection in oral versus genital tissues, producer cell lines were established from primary human tonsil keratinocytes (HTLK) and human foreskin keratinocytes (HFK). Wild-type HPV16 genomes were introduced into multiple batches of both HTLKs and HFKs via electroporation. This approach allowed for the selection of several immortalized cell lines for each tissue type, defined by their ability to undergo more than 20 population doublings, thereby mitigating potential host-specific variations. Southern blot analysis confirmed the stable presence of episomal HPV16 genomes within these immortalized cell lines; specifically, the generated HPV16 HTLK lines consistently maintained approximately 100 episomal copies of HPV16 DNA per cell (Fig. 1A). These persistently HPV16-infected cell lines were subsequently cultivated in organotypic raft cultures. The resulting organotypic tissues were harvested at 10, 15, and 20 days post-cultivation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that HPV infection caused minor alterations in the differentiation morphology when compared to uninfected tissues (Fig. 1B). A notable characteristic of these organotypic cultures, differing from in vivo epithelia, is the horizontal orientation and lack of dead cell shedding. This leads to an accumulation and thickening of the cornified layer over time, exceeding what would be observed in natural epithelial tissues. HPV16-infected tonsil tissues exhibited numerous vacuolated cells in their upper strata, a feature also present in uninfected tonsil tissue (Fig. 1Bi–iv). Similarly, HPV16-infected foreskin tissues frequently displayed a thickened cornified top layer (Fig. 1Bv–viii), consistent with prior observations.
We compared HPV16 virion production in tonsil and foreskin epithelia over 10, 15, and 20 days of organotypic tissue growth. Tonsil tissue showed a time-dependent increase in encapsidated, endonuclease-resistant HPV16 genomes. In contrast, HPV16 replication in foreskin tissue peaked at 15 days, then decreased by approximately one-third after 20 days. These findings highlight distinct production dynamics between the two tissue types.
We assessed the infectivity of HPV16 produced in tonsil and foreskin tissues by infecting HaCaT cells with equivalent viral genome quantities (5 VGEs) and quantifying infectivity via RT-qPCR. Our findings revealed a time-dependent increase in HPV16 infectivity for viruses grown in both tissue types. This pattern aligns with previous observations for HPV16 in foreskin tissue. Specifically, HPV16 derived from foreskin tissue cultured for 15 days exhibited a 50% increase in infectivity compared to 10-day old virus. Furthermore, 20-day old foreskin-derived HPV16 demonstrated nearly double the infectivity of 15-day old virus. In tonsil tissues, the relative infectivity changes were even more pronounced. HPV16 from 15-day tonsil cultures showed a seven-fold increase in infectivity relative to 10-day virus, and 20-day tonsil-derived HPV16 displayed a ten-fold increase. Historically, the oral cavity's capacity to replicate and transmit infectious HPV has been a subject of speculation. However, prior investigations primarily focused on detecting viral genomes, lacking direct evidence of infectious virus production. Our study provides the first direct evidence that oral tissues are capable of replicating infectious HPV, confirming the oral cavity as a potential source for viral spread. This finding is crucial for understanding HPV transmission dynamics and disease progression in oral contexts.
To investigate differences in HPV16 stability and maturation between tonsil and foreskin tissues, we employed OptiPrep step gradient fractionation, subsequently quantifying viral titers and infectivity within each fraction. Previous research indicates that this method can destabilize HPV virion capsids. Our analysis of HPV16 from 10-day foreskin tissue revealed that most viral genomes were concentrated in fractions 1-4, rather than the highly infectious fractions 6-8. This distribution suggests that the high shear forces and hydrostatic pressure inherent in ultracentrifugation might have damaged the capsids. However, when HPV16 from 15- and 20-day foreskin tissue was similarly fractionated and its viral genomes quantified, we observed a shift: fewer genomes were found in fractions 1-4, and more were present in the denser fractions 5-9. This indicates that 15- and 20-day virions possess greater stability compared to 10-day virions, aligning with our prior findings. In contrast, HPV16 derived from tonsil tissues exhibited a distinct OptiPrep gradient profile. After 10 days of growth in tonsil tissue, viral particles were uniformly distributed across all gradient fractions, implying a consistent mix of stability levels. As the tonsil tissue growth extended to 15 and 20 days, a notable change occurred: the highest proportion of viral particles migrated to fractions 6-8, which represent more stable virions. This divergence points to a fundamental difference in HPV16 maturation between tonsil and foreskin tissues. Specifically, HPV16 maturation in tonsil epithelium appears to commence earlier and encompass a larger proportion of the total viral population than what is observed in HPV16 grown in foreskin tissues. This suggests that the oral environment may facilitate a more rapid and extensive maturation process for HPV16 virions.
To quantify the relative stability of HPV16 virions, we calculated the ratio of unstably encapsidated viral genomes (found in fractions 1–4) to stably encapsidated genomes (found in fractions 6–8) within viral preparations. For HPV16 derived from foreskin tissue, these ratios were 14.8, 1.0, and 0.56 for 10-, 15-, and 20-day old tissue, respectively (Fig. 3C). This indicates that 10-day foreskin tissue contained significantly more unstable virions compared to 15- and 20-day tissues, consistent with prior observations. When analyzing HPV16 from tonsil tissues, the stability ratios were 2.7, 0.48, and 0.34 for 10-, 15-, and 20-day old tissue, respectively (Fig. 3C). These results suggest that HPV16 maturation into a more stable virion occurs more rapidly in tonsil epithelium than in foreskin epithelium. The declining ratios across increasing time points in both tissue types demonstrate a progressive increase in virion stability over time, with tonsil-derived virions achieving higher stability earlier in their development.
Infectivity of HPV16 virions, isolated from foreskin and tonsil tissues after 10, 15, and 20 days of growth, was assessed using RT-qPCR. For these infectivity assays, a consistent viral genome equivalent (VGE) of 10 was applied across all fractions. Interestingly, the infectivity profiles of OptiPrep-fractionated HPV16 from both tissue types, across all growth periods, demonstrated remarkable similarity, contrasting with their stability profiles. Specifically, HPV16 consistently exhibited its highest infectivity levels in fractions 5–8, regardless of the tissue source or growth duration. This finding aligns with our previous observations concerning foreskin tissues. Furthermore, as anticipated from the stability data, virions grown for 20 days displayed the highest relative infectivity. Conversely, the infectivity detected in the less stable fractions (1–4) mirrored levels previously observed for free viral genomes. These results suggest that while stability may vary, the peak infectivity of HPV16 is consistently found within a specific range of fractions for both oral and genital tissues.
To investigate if virion stability influenced specific infectivity, Western blot analysis was conducted on the L1 capsid protein from HPV16 preparations, fractionated by OptiPrep, at 10, 15, and 20 days. The L1 protein quantity in gradient fraction 7 from these preparations served to normalize their relative infectivity, establishing an "infectivity-to-protein ratio" as a measure of specific infectivity. Our previous work demonstrated that 20-day foreskin-derived HPV16 exhibited 2.9-fold greater infectivity compared to 10-day HPV16. This suggested an inherent difference between these time points, rather than variations in overall particle stability across the foreskin tissue preparations. Similarly, Western blot analysis of L1 from OptiPrep-fractionated 10, 15, and 20-day tonsil-derived HPV16 was performed. The resulting infectivity-to-protein ratio for tonsil-derived HPV16 indicated that 20-day HPV16 was 3.1-fold more infectious than its 10-day counterpart (Fig. 4C). These findings collectively suggest that maturation processes, rather than solely stability, contribute to the increased infectivity observed over time in both tissue types.
Western blot analysis of HPV16 L1 protein, extracted from both foreskin and tonsil tissues, revealed two distinct bands in OptiPrep fractions 6–8. This indicates that L1 protein from HPV16 particles, regardless of tissue origin, exists in two different sizes.
The distinct redox environments within epithelial tissues, specifically the highly oxidizing stratum corneum compared to the more reducing suprabasal layers, led to the hypothesis that these varying conditions contribute to the observed temporal phenotypic changes in virions. Previous research on foreskin epithelial raft tissues, mirroring in vivo observations, established the presence of a natural redox gradient. In this gradient, basal and suprabasal compartments are characterized by reducing conditions, while the cornified compartment exhibits an oxidizing environment, as evidenced by the differential localization of free versus disulfide-bonded sulfhydryls. To determine if tonsil epithelia possess a similar redox gradient, HPV16-infected tonsil and foreskin epithelial sections, grown for 15 and 20 days, were stained with DACM, a fluorescent sulfhydryl-alkylating reagent. DACM fluoresces only upon reaction with free sulfhydryl groups. Staining with DACM alone revealed significant concentrations of free sulfhydryl groups across all epithelial strata, with the notable exception of the stratum corneum. This suggests a predominantly reducing environment in these layers. Further investigation involved treating samples with DTT to disrupt all disulfide bonds before DACM staining. This treatment demonstrated the presence of sulfhydryl groups throughout all strata, indicating that these groups exist in both free and disulfide-bonded forms. To specifically identify disulfide bonds, free sulfhydryls were irreversibly blocked using N-ethylmaleimide (NEM), followed by reduction of disulfide-bonded sulfhydryls with dithiothreitol, and then DACM staining. Under these conditions, only the stratum corneum displayed significant amounts of detectable disulfide bonds, confirming its oxidizing nature. Faint autofluorescent signals were observed only in unstained samples or those blocked with NEM, validating the specificity of the DACM staining. These findings confirm the existence of a natural redox gradient within human tonsil epithelial tissue, analogous to that found in foreskin tissue. The basal and suprabasal compartments maintain a reducing environment, while the cornified compartment is oxidizing. This gradient plays a crucial role in viral maturation. At 10 days, most of the virus remains within the reducing compartment of the host tissue. However, by 15 days, the virus has migrated into the oxidizing (cornified) compartment. This migration facilitates disulfide bonding within the viral capsid, a critical step in stabilizing the viral particle. This temporal and spatial exposure to differing redox conditions within the host tissue is thus a key factor influencing the stability and maturation of HPV16 virions.
We investigated the neutralization susceptibility of HPV16 from tonsil tissue, comparing it to previously observed patterns in foreskin tissue. Our prior work demonstrated that HPV16 virions from foreskin tissue exhibited varying susceptibility to anti-L1 and anti-L2 antibodies depending on the isolation time. To assess if tonsil-derived HPV16 showed similar differential neutralization, we conducted assays using a viral genome equivalent (VGE) of 10. Specifically, we employed the conformation-dependent anti-L1 MAb H16.V5 and anti-L2 antibodies, RG1 and L2#4, to characterize the neutralization profiles of HPV16 virions grown in tissues for 20 days.
Our previous findings indicated that HPV16 derived from foreskin tissue, after 20 days of growth, was effectively neutralized by all three antibodies tested. However, a different neutralization pattern emerged for HPV16 from tonsil tissue. While the L1 antibody, H16.V5, efficiently neutralized tonsil-derived HPV16, the two L2-specific antibodies showed only moderate neutralization (Fig. 6A). Significant variability in neutralization efficacy was also observed across all three antibodies when applied to tonsil tissue, as evidenced by large error bars. To further investigate this, we assessed infectivity and neutralization using L1 and L2 specific antibodies on virus from four distinct HTLK cell lines, each originating from a unique patient sample (Fig. 6B). Although the conformation-dependent anti-L1 H16.V5 antibody did neutralize tonsil-derived HPV16, its effectiveness was lower compared to its neutralization of foreskin-derived HPV16 (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, H16.V5 neutralization varied among individual tonsil preparations, with only two out of four samples being neutralizable (Fig. 6B). Similarly, neutralization by both L2-specific monoclonal antibodies was generally moderate (Fig. 6A) and exhibited high variability across different virus preparations, particularly those from individual patient tissues (Fig. 6B). Despite tonsil-derived HPV16 being highly infectious and displaying an OptiPrep gradient profile similar to foreskin-derived HPV16, these neutralization disparities strongly suggest a conformational difference between HPV16 originating from foreskin and tonsil tissues.
Viral capsids, fundamental to viral structure and function, are constructed from repeating protein subunits, either identical or a few distinct types, arranged in precise, repeating patterns. These subunits initially form capsomeres, which then assemble into the complete viral capsid. This structural simplicity offers significant advantages: it minimizes the genetic information required for capsid coding within the viral nucleic acid and enhances assembly efficiency. The capsid serves two primary roles: safeguarding the viral genome from external environmental threats and facilitating its transfer from one cell or host to another. While the fundamental principles governing capsid assembly might seem straightforward, the host environment can profoundly influence the stability and efficiency of this process. Furthermore, both the host cell and the extracellular environment can induce alterations in the viral surface conformation. Such changes can, in turn, impact the virus's ability to bind to new host cells, its interactions with the immune system, and its disassembly once inside a new host cell. This phenomenon is well-documented in enveloped viruses, where modifications to envelope proteins directly affect infectivity and immune evasion. However, comparable observations in non-enveloped viruses have received less attention. Our current investigation into Human Papillomavirus 16 (HPV16), a non-enveloped virus, reveals notable differences in its phenotype depending on whether it is propagated in foreskin or tonsil tissue, despite many similarities. We identify two significant distinctions. Firstly, a higher proportion of HPV16 virions grown in tonsil epithelium achieve full maturity and stability, and this maturation occurs more rapidly compared to virions cultivated in foreskin tissue. Both tissue types exhibit similar differentiation-associated redox potential gradients, which our previous research has established as crucial for HPV16 stability and maturation (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011b). However, the observed disparities in viral stability and maturation between foreskin and tonsil-derived viruses suggest that host cell factors beyond redox gradients may play a role in virion assembly and maturation. It is conceivable that cell-type-specific chaperones contribute to viral capsid assembly, a hypothesis warranting further exploration. The second major phenotypic difference lies in the overall resistance of tonsil-derived HPV16 to neutralization by L2-specific antibodies. This finding implies that the final surface conformation of tonsil-derived HPV16 differs, at least concerning the capsid conformation and the accessibility of the L2 protein. Moreover, variations in neutralization profiles among virus preparations from different HTLK cell lines indicate that not all tonsil-tissue-derived virions are structurally identical. Interestingly, the surface conformation of the L1 capsid protein appears consistent across both foreskin and tonsil-derived virions, as both are susceptible to neutralization by the conformation-dependent L1 antibody. These findings underscore the complex interplay between the virus and its host environment in shaping viral characteristics and highlight the need for further research into the specific mechanisms governing HPV16 maturation and immune evasion in different mucosal locations.
The life cycle of Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is intricately linked to the differentiation processes within its host squamous epithelial tissue. Our laboratory has previously utilized an organotypic culture system to investigate the life cycle of various HPV types in genital tissues. However, the current study represents the first investigation into the HPV16 life cycle within oral tissue, specifically comparing it to foreskin tissue. Despite a long-held belief that the oral cavity could replicate and transmit infectious HPV particles, direct evidence for this phenomenon has been lacking. Our research now demonstrates that tonsil epithelial tissue cultures effectively support the replication of high titers of infectious, native HPV16. This finding is significant as it provides the first direct evidence that oral tissues can indeed replicate infectious HPV, suggesting a potential pathway for viral spread through the oral cavity. The study primarily focused on the assembly and infectivity of these infectious viral particles. While many characteristics of HPV16 grown in both foreskin and tonsil tissues appear similar, our findings reveal notable distinctions concerning the virus's maturation and its final conformational structure when propagated in these two different tissue environments. These differences highlight the complex interplay between the virus and its specific host tissue, particularly in the context of oral HPV infection.
