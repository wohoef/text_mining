This study aimed to understand how horizontal eye movements and age affect choroidal volume near the optic disc, specifically on the macular side. The optic nerve typically tethers during adduction, causing disc deformation. Researchers used optical coherence tomography (OCT) to image the optic disc and peripapillary choroid in two groups of healthy individuals: 25 younger subjects (18–33 years) and 15 older subjects (50–73 years). Imaging was performed in central gaze, 35° adduction, and 35° abduction. The choroid temporal to the optic disc, spanning from the Bruch's membrane opening to the fovea, was divided into segments based on disc radius to measure local choroidal thickness. Changes in regional choroidal volume from central gaze were then calculated for both adduction and abduction. The findings showed a significant decrease in regional choroidal volume during adduction. Younger subjects experienced a reduction of 42.4 ± 3.4 nanoliters (nL) (P < 0.0001), while older subjects saw a smaller decrease of 6.2 ± 2.6 nL (P < 0.02). This represented a relative volume reduction of 7.5% ± 0.6% in younger individuals (P < 0.001) and 1.3% ± 0.6% in older individuals (P < 0.02). This volume reduction was most pronounced closest to the disc, extending up to three disc radii in younger subjects and one radius in older subjects. In contrast, abduction did not result in significant volume changes. In conclusion, horizontal eye movements, particularly adduction, lead to compression of the temporal peripapillary choroid. This effect is more pronounced in adduction compared to abduction, and more significant in younger individuals than in older ones. This suggests that optic nerve tethering during adduction contributes to peripapillary tissue deformation.

Previous research using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has revealed that during significant adduction, the optic nerve (ON) and its surrounding sheath anchor the eyeball, pulling on the optic disc. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) studies have further shown that this adduction causes the peripapillary Bruch's membrane (ppBM) to deform and the optic nerve head (ONH) to tilt forward or backward. This deformation becomes more pronounced around 26° of adduction, which is the point where the ON's limited length forces it to tether. While some disc tilting also happens in abduction, the more severe deformation seen in adduction is likely a result of this ON tethering.
Simulations employing finite element analysis have explored mechanical stress and strain in the optic nerve head (ONH) and peripapillary retina during horizontal eye movements. Our research specifically highlights that this is particularly true during adduction. These simulations indicate that the force from optic nerve (ON) tethering is primarily focused on the temporal disc and surrounding peripapillary tissue. This area is notable as it's where peripapillary atrophy frequently develops in individuals with primary open angle glaucoma (POAG). Furthermore, these analyses suggest that the deformation of the optic disc caused by adduction tethering can surpass that resulting from severe, sudden increases in intraocular pressure (IOP). This offers a potential partial explanation for numerous POAG cases where glaucomatous optic neuropathy is present despite normal IOP levels. Repetitive deformation of the optic disc and peripapillary retina due to horizontal eye movements has been hypothesized to contribute to the common peripapillary chorioretinal atrophy seen in glaucoma. Moreover, in POAG, choroidal microvascular dropout in areas of peripapillary atrophy is linked to advanced visual field defects.
Previous OCT research by our group demonstrated that horizontal eye movements cause deformation in the peripapillary Bruch's membrane (ppBM) and the superficial optic nerve (ON) vasculature. However, the impact of gaze direction on choroidal volume and the influence of subject age on these alterations had not been explored. This study aimed to investigate potential deformation within the peripapillary choroid (ppC), building upon prior findings that the ppC thins during both adduction and abduction. Given that choroidal thinning, resulting from inner choroidal vessel loss, is associated with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and normal tension glaucoma (NTG), and that older individuals generally exhibit thinner choroids, we sought to quantify choroidal volume changes induced by horizontal duction across different age groups. This allowed us to examine choroidal deformation and its potential links to age-related ocular conditions.
The study involved 40 volunteers, comprising 19 males and 21 females, with an average age of 37 ± 20 years (ranging from 18 to 73). These participants were divided into two groups: a younger cohort of 25 individuals (15 males, 10 females; mean age 24 ± 5, range 18–33) and an older cohort of 15 individuals (4 males, 11 females; mean age 62 ± 8, range 50–73). None of the subjects had prior involvement in similar research. Before participation, all volunteers provided written informed consent, adhering to a protocol approved by the University of California, Los Angeles, Institutional Review Board and the Declaration of Helsinki. Comprehensive ophthalmic examinations were conducted to ensure normal corrected visual acuity and to rule out any ocular abnormalities, with the exception of refractive error and pseudophakia. Participants also exhibited normal intraocular pressure (below 21 mm Hg), proper binocular alignment, and healthy optic nerves. For those whose refractive error was measured, the mean spherical equivalent was −1.5 ± 2.9 (ranging from +2 to −8.3), though this data was unavailable for 24 volunteers.
Choroidal imaging was conducted using a Spectralis spectral domain OCT scanner from Heidelberg Engineering, Germany, employing enhanced depth imaging. Sequential volume scans were acquired for both eyes in three gaze positions: central, 35° abduction, and 35° adduction. Each scan comprised 25 horizontal B-scans, covering a 30° x 5° rectangular area positioned between the fovea and the optic disc. The vertical spacing between these B-scans was approximately 62 μm, adjusted based on focal depth. For eccentric gaze imaging, the OCT imager was rotated on its gimbal using a goniometric scale. To ensure consistency, the scan raster was rotated for every scan to align the optic disc center with the fovea. This rotation served to counteract ocular torsion, a phenomenon that varies with gaze direction and individually, thereby making image sets from different gaze positions superimposable.
The OCT imager's design restricted its rotation to a maximum of 35°. To ensure a consistent angle of the scanning light beam entering the eye relative to the visual direction, the axial tilt of the B-scans was kept constant. This was achieved because the eye maintained fixation on the scanner's internal target, rotating along with the scanner head. Consequently, the beam's entry angle remained unchanged regardless of gaze direction. Participants' heads were secured straight ahead using straps and cushions. They were instructed to keep this head position while fixating on the scanner's internal target, which was positioned 12° nasally from straight ahead.
After acquisition, images were initially processed using Adobe Photoshop, where they were saved as TIFF files. To ensure consistency across different gaze positions for the same eye, scans underwent cropping, scaling, and, if needed, rotation to rectify the aspect ratio and align peripheral retinal features precisely. Subsequently, these images were transformed into a volumetric stack within Materialise Mimics. This conversion maintained the original scaling: 3.87 μm/pixel horizontally, an average of 11.6 μm/pixel in depth, and an average vertical spacing of 62.1 μm between B-scans. For each volumetric stack, the central 12 out of 25 acquired slices were selected for analysis, as these consistently encompassed the central optic disc, allowing for evaluation of the peripapillary choroid (ppC). The choroid's nasal boundary was defined by the Bruch's membrane opening (BMO). The ppC was then divided into three adjacent regions, each having a horizontal dimension equivalent to the BMO radius, starting from the BMO's temporal edge. To determine the volume for each eye in each subject, a three-dimensional model of the temporal ppC was constructed from these 12 traced cross-sections. The volume was calculated by multiplying the summed areas by the distance between the B-scans. Each eye was considered an independent sample in the subsequent statistical analyses.
For each eccentric gaze, we assessed two parameters against central gaze: choroidal volume change across the three regions, and differences between these segmented regions. These measurements were then compared across younger and older age groups.
To address potential correlations between a subject's two eyes and within different choroidal regions of a single eye, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were employed for statistical analysis. This was performed using SPSS software (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), treating both the eye and choroidal region as nested, within-subject variables. While the SPSS package typically provides significant values rounded to three decimal places based on a χ2 distribution, it's important to note this isn't a straightforward χ2 test of proportions. For instances where SPSS indicated P = 0.000, we additionally provided the χ2 value and degrees of freedom, as true zero probabilities for random significant differences are impossible.
Figure 2 visually demonstrates the reduction in choroidal volume as gaze moves from a central position to adduction, comparing both younger and older individuals. Generally, younger subjects exhibited a thicker choroid than older subjects. Specifically, the average choroidal volume in central gaze was 556 ± 25 nL for younger individuals, significantly higher than the 460 ± 21 nL observed in older subjects (P < 0.01). While choroidal volume remained higher in younger subjects across eccentric horizontal gazes, it consistently decreased in adduction compared to central gaze in both age groups. In adduction, the volume in younger subjects fell to 514 ± 24 nL, whereas older subjects experienced a smaller decrease to 454 ± 21 nL, diminishing the statistical difference between the groups. During abduction, younger subjects' choroidal volume showed an insignificant increase to 559 ± 26 nL, while older subjects' volume stayed at 460 ± 21 nL, thus restoring a significant difference between the groups (P < 0.01).
To assess the consistency of the measurements, test-retest variability was determined by conducting the experiment again on separate days. This involved 10 eyes from 5 young subjects, utilizing the same methods for data collection and analysis to calculate choroidal volume in both central gaze and adduction. Bland-Altman analysis, as depicted in Figure 3, revealed the agreement between repeated measurements. For central gaze, the average choroidal volume was 562 nL, with the repeat measurement showing a small bias of 5 nL (less than 0.9%) compared to the initial measurement. The 95% limits of agreement for central gaze ranged from -34 to 45 nL, indicating a maximum individual measurement variation of 8%. Similarly, in adduction, the average volume was 514 nL, with a bias of 4 nL (less than 0.8%). The 95% limits of agreement for adduction were between -27 and 36 nL, representing a maximum individual variation of 7%. These findings suggest that test-retest variability contributed to less than a 0.9% difference in the average measurements. Furthermore, linear regression was employed to compare the first and second sets of measurements for each eye in both central gaze and adduction. The goal was to determine if the regression slope deviated from unity, which would signify identical measurements. For both central gaze and adduction choroidal volumes, neither regression slope significantly differed from unity (P > 0.75), confirming that the repeated experiments produced statistically comparable results to the original ones.
Gaze position significantly influenced the change in choroidal volume from central gaze (P = 0.000, GEE χ2 = 47, 2 df). A consistent reduction in choroidal volume was observed during adduction in both younger and older participants, while abduction showed minimal alteration (Fig. 2, Fig. 4). For younger subjects, the average choroidal volume decreased by 42 ± 3 nL in adduction (P < 0.0001), a change approximately eight times larger than the test-retest variability. Conversely, abduction in younger individuals led to an insignificant increase of 2 ± 3 nL. Older subjects experienced a smaller decrease of 6 ± 3 nL in choroidal volume during adduction (P < 0.02), and an insignificant increase of 1 ± 4 nL in abduction. Furthermore, a notable interaction between gaze position and age was identified (P = 0.000, GEE χ2 = 17, 2 df).
Comparing choroidal volume changes from central gaze, younger subjects experienced a substantial 7.5% mean decrease during adduction, with only a negligible 0.4% increase in abduction. Older subjects showed a smaller 1.3% mean volume reduction in adduction, and a similarly insignificant 0.2% increase in abduction. These findings highlight age-related differences in choroidal response to eye movements.
For younger individuals, the most pronounced relative reduction in choroidal volume during adduction occurred closest to the optic disc, diminishing further away (χ2 = 19). Specifically, within one disc radius, the volume decreased by 12% ± 1%; between one and two radii, it fell by 2% ± 1%; and between two and three radii, the reduction was 1% ± 1%. In abduction, a significant relative volume decrease was observed only within one disc radius in younger subjects (P < 0.01). Conversely, older subjects showed no significant changes in relative choroidal volume in any region during either adduction or abduction.
The current study reveals that temporal peripapillary choroid (ppC) volume decreases more significantly during adduction than abduction. This finding expands upon previous research indicating that adduction deforms the peripapillary retina. Prior studies have also established that horizontal eye movements, especially adduction, deform the optic disc and surrounding tissues. Such adduction-induced deformation involves the temporal shift of the disc's nasal portion, anteroposterior displacement of the ppBM, and disc tilting. The concentration of temporal choroid compression near the disc suggests that optic nerve (ON) or its sheath transmits forces during eye movements, leading to this deformation.
Deformation of the peripapillary retina and optic nerve (ON) fibers is linked to compression of the temporal choroid. While both younger and older individuals experience temporal choroid compression during adduction, younger subjects show approximately 15 times more compression in adduction compared to abduction. This pronounced adduction-related compression aligns with optical imaging data, which reveals greater deformation of the disc and peripapillary retina in these gaze directions. Furthermore, MRI studies confirm ON tethering exclusively in adduction, supporting this observation. The compression of the temporal choroid also correlates with the temporal shifting of peripapillary vessels and the overall temporalward compression of optic disc tissue that occurs during ON tethering in adduction. In contrast, abduction presents a different scenario, characterized by minimal choroidal compression and reduced ON and peripapillary deformations in young adults, with no such deformations observed in older subjects. This distinction between adduction and abduction is further corroborated by MRI findings indicating that the ON typically remains slack during abduction.
Age-related stiffening of surrounding tissues like the sclera, Bruch's membrane, and lamina cribrosa may explain why older individuals exhibit less peripapillary choroid (ppC) deformation compared to younger individuals, whose tissues are generally more compliant. This reduced deformation in older subjects could also be linked to their typically thinner choroids, which might contribute to the smaller choroidal volume changes observed during eccentric gazes. The connection between reduced choroidal thickness and age-related ocular neuropathies is noteworthy. For instance, individuals with normal tension glaucoma (NTG) have significantly thinner choroids in specific regions (inferonasal, inferior, inferotemporal). Similarly, those with both NTG and high myopia show thinner choroids in various areas, including the fovea and temporal regions, compared to the optic nerve head. This suggests that choroidal thinning could play a role in glaucomatous optic neuropathy, either as a cause or a consequence, given its subnormal thickness in different forms of NTG.
The optic disc and surrounding peripapillary retina experience mechanical strain, including shifting and tilting, during adduction. This compression of the peripapillary choroid (ppC) during adduction could potentially disrupt the optic nerve's blood supply, which partially originates from the choroid. Such interference might lead to tissue damage, especially if the posterior ciliary circulation is also compromised. Consequently, choroidal vascular insufficiency could be a factor in optic neuropathies like glaucoma. While some studies suggest a thinner ppC in glaucomatous eyes, others find no link between choroidal thickness and glaucoma. This study, however, demonstrates immediate ppC compression during adduction in healthy individuals. Further research on glaucoma patients is needed to explore the connection between gaze-induced choroidal deformation and the disease.
The reduced choroidal compression seen in older individuals during adduction, compared to younger subjects, may have pathological significance. This difference could stem from tissue remodeling caused by repeated strain during adduction eye movements. Older choroids, being thinner, are also likely sclerotic, possessing a higher proportion of rigid connective tissue than younger choroids. Consequently, a greater resistance to the mechanical strain from the tethering optic nerve would reduce choroidal volume changes during adduction. Our MRI research has established that the optic nerve tethers when adduction surpasses 26 degrees in all age groups. This means that the strong medial rectus muscle's reactive force invariably deforms or displaces the globe to allow further adduction. The limited length of the optic nerve inherently leads to local deformation within the eye or orbit. Ideally, healthy deformation, or "strain," should occur only in compliant tissues that can dissipate mechanical force without functional compromise. We suggest the choroid is one such tissue that safely absorbs mechanical strain during adduction. Normally, choroidal deformation acts as a cushion, preventing the transfer of mechanical strain to vital structures like the lamina cribrosa and optic nerve. However, sclerosis of the choroid and surrounding peripapillary tissues would compromise this cushioning effect, thereby transferring potentially damaging strain to critical tissues, including the optic nerve. Thus, a choroid that is relatively thin and poorly compressible could contribute to optic neuropathy, such as glaucoma.
The study's scope was confined to the temporal peripapillary region, meaning choroidal areas further away could not be assessed. While no substantial eye movement-induced choroidal volume changes were observed beyond three disc radii from the BMO, directly confirming that eye movement effects are restricted to this area was not possible. Furthermore, the research did not incorporate varying duction angles, preventing the identification of a potential threshold for gaze-induced choroidal deformation, unlike what has been established for Bruch's membrane. Lastly, choroidal blood flow was not quantified in this investigation.
