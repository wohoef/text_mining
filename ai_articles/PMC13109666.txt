This study investigated the relationship between body dissatisfaction, body appreciation, and attentional biases towards weight-related stimuli in 167 undergraduate women, aged 17–39, who reported high levels of body dissatisfaction. Attentional biases, which are believed to perpetuate body dissatisfaction and eating disorders, were measured using eye-gaze tracking. Participants viewed words and images categorized as thin-related, fat-related, or neutral, with their eye fixations recorded over 8-second intervals. Our central hypothesis was that body appreciation, as assessed by the Body Appreciation Scale, would modify the established link between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases, specifically for thin-related information. We predicted that as body appreciation increased, the strength of the positive relationship between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases would diminish. The findings revealed that body appreciation indeed moderated the association between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases, but only for thin-related words. When body appreciation was low, a positive correlation existed: higher body dissatisfaction was linked to increased attention to thin-related words. Conversely, for individuals with high body appreciation, an inverse relationship emerged, where greater body dissatisfaction was associated with reduced attention to thin-related words. These results suggest that fostering body appreciation could be a valuable strategy in preventing or mitigating maladaptive attentional biases.

Body dissatisfaction, a significant risk factor for eating disorders, is prevalent among college women, with rates reaching 80%. This pervasive issue is linked to various health problems, including depressive symptoms, low self-esteem, and emotional distress. It also plays a crucial role in the development and persistence of eating pathology. Given these negative consequences, understanding factors that both worsen and protect against body dissatisfaction is vital. Appearance is fundamental to self-evaluation for women, making body image a critical area of study. Investigating elements that promote body satisfaction and mitigate dissatisfaction is therefore essential for addressing these widespread concerns.
Cognitive models of body dissatisfaction and eating disorders posit that an individual's mental frameworks concerning appearance, weight, and body shape influence how they process body image-related information. Specifically, maladaptive body image schemas are thought to heighten selective attention to such environmental cues. This phenomenon, known as attentional bias, is well-documented in individuals experiencing body dissatisfaction, with eye-gaze tracking emerging as the primary method for its assessment. Numerous studies have identified attentional biases towards body- and weight-related information in body-dissatisfied individuals. Research consistently shows that women with higher body dissatisfaction tend to focus more on weight-related content, encompassing both thin-related and fat-related stimuli, when compared to control groups. For instance, one study observed that body-dissatisfied women fixated longer on both fat- and thin-related words during an 8-second display. Another investigation revealed that women with greater body dissatisfaction not only paid more attention to images of thin and overweight bodies but also did so more rapidly than their less dissatisfied counterparts. While body-dissatisfied women exhibit prolonged gaze at both fat- and thin-related information, the underlying motivations for these biases may differ. The heightened attention to fat-related stimuli, for example, could stem from a "fear of fatness," a cognitive factor widely believed to contribute significantly to body dissatisfaction. Conversely, increased attention to thin-related stimuli might be more closely linked to the propensity of body-dissatisfied individuals to engage in upward social comparisons, where they compare themselves unfavorably to perceived ideals. These attentional biases are considered crucial in the development and persistence of body dissatisfaction, which itself is a recognized risk factor for eating disorders. Understanding these cognitive processes is vital for developing effective interventions.
Body dissatisfaction is a recognized risk factor for eating disorders, and attentional biases are thought to play a role in its development and persistence. Research indicates a reciprocal relationship, where focusing on non-preferred appearance stimuli can heighten body dissatisfaction. Given the negative outcomes linked to body dissatisfaction, identifying protective factors that reduce susceptibility to these attentional biases is crucial. This study investigated whether body appreciation serves as such a protective factor. Understanding this relationship could inform interventions aimed at mitigating maladaptive attentional biases and their impact on body image.
Body appreciation, distinct from mere body satisfaction, may shield individuals from the detrimental effects of thin-ideal media, which is known to exacerbate body dissatisfaction and eating disorder symptoms. Unlike body satisfaction, body appreciation encompasses a broader, more profound regard for one's physical self. Individuals demonstrating high body appreciation do not derive their self-worth solely from external appearance, thereby reducing their propensity for social comparison. Key components of body appreciation include positive self-perception of the body, unconditional acceptance of one's physique despite perceived flaws, attentiveness to bodily needs, and a proactive rejection of unrealistic beauty standards. This comprehensive approach to body image allows body appreciation to independently predict aspects of well-being, even when accounting for body satisfaction. Therefore, fostering body appreciation could be a crucial strategy in mitigating the negative impacts of media on body image.
Research indicates that increased body appreciation can mitigate the adverse impacts of thin-ideal media. For instance, studies by Andrew, Tiggemann, and Clark (2015) demonstrated that women with low body appreciation reported higher body dissatisfaction after media exposure, a trend not observed in women with greater body appreciation. Further research highlights that women exhibiting high body appreciation are less prone to dieting behaviors and attach less significance to appearance disparities between themselves and thin-ideal models, as documented by Andrew, Tiggemann, and Clark (2016) and Halliwell (2013). Additionally, Gillen and Dunaev (2017) found that women with high body appreciation prioritized individual uniqueness over conforming to media appearance standards. Collectively, these findings suggest that higher body appreciation offers protection against the detrimental effects of media exposure. This protective role implies that individuals with greater body appreciation may be less susceptible to developing maladaptive attentional biases associated with body dissatisfaction.
Body appreciation may act as a protective factor against maladaptive attentional biases, potentially making it a valuable target for prevention strategies. Previous research indicates that higher body appreciation correlates with reduced increases in body dissatisfaction after exposure to thin-ideal media (Andrew et al., 2015) and fewer negative impacts from media exposure overall (Halliwell, 2013). This suggests that individuals with greater body appreciation might allocate less attention to thin-ideal cues, thereby mitigating their influence on body dissatisfaction. If body appreciation indeed modulates attentional biases linked to body dissatisfaction, it could be a crucial element in prevention programs focused on attention. Interventions designed to prevent eating disorders have successfully boosted self-reported body appreciation in various groups, including high school girls (Halliwell et al., 2015), women over 25 with obesity (Olson et al., 2018), and undergraduate men (Jankowski et al., 2017). Investigating how body appreciation affects maladaptive attentional biases related to body dissatisfaction offers direct support for the idea that decreased attention to thin-ideal cues can lessen the detrimental effects of thin-ideal media. This study aims to explore this very mechanism.
This study investigated if high body appreciation could mitigate attentional biases toward thin-ideal cues in women with body dissatisfaction. These women were specifically chosen due to their increased vulnerability to negative media effects and documented attentional biases concerning weight-related words and images. Therefore, participant recruitment focused on individuals exhibiting significant body dissatisfaction. This approach allowed for an examination of body appreciation's potential protective role against maladaptive attentional patterns.
This study aimed to investigate the interplay between body dissatisfaction, body appreciation, and attentional biases. Our primary objective was to determine if higher body appreciation could mitigate attentional biases towards thin-ideal cues in women experiencing body dissatisfaction. We formulated several hypotheses. Firstly, we anticipated confirming prior research indicating that women with significant body dissatisfaction display attentional biases toward words and images related to both thinness and fatness. Specifically, we predicted a positive correlation between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases for these types of stimuli. Secondly, we hypothesized that increased body appreciation would reduce susceptibility to attentional biases linked to thin-ideal cues, which are typically associated with body dissatisfaction. Our prediction was that body appreciation would moderate the relationship between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases for thin-related words and images. As body appreciation grew, we expected the link between body dissatisfaction and these attentional biases to weaken. Furthermore, we theorized that at very high levels of body appreciation, the association between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases for thin-related cues would effectively disappear. This moderation was specifically expected for thin-related cues, drawing on existing literature that shows high body appreciation lessens increases in body dissatisfaction after exposure to thin-ideal media and reduces social comparison tendencies. Finally, considering that previous studies have only demonstrated a protective role for body appreciation against the negative effects of thin-ideal media, we predicted that body appreciation would not moderate the relationship between body dissatisfaction and attention to fat-related words or images.
Participants in this study completed three stages. Initially, an online survey gathered image ratings to aid stimulus selection. Next, potential participants were screened for eligibility using another online survey. Finally, qualified individuals attended a laboratory session to complete an eye-tracking task and various questionnaires. The University of Calgary Conjoint Faculties Research Ethics Board approved this research. These phases are detailed further in subsequent sections.
For the current eye-tracking study, word stimuli were adapted from a prior investigation by Tobin et al. (2018), which focused on attentional patterns towards weight-related words in body-dissatisfied undergraduate women. In that foundational study, psychology students evaluated 190 words, randomly chosen from a larger pool of 380. Participants categorized each word as "thin," "fat," "neutral," or "unsure," and assigned a valence rating from "very negative" to "very positive." The initial evaluation involved 300 neutral, 40 thin-related, and 40 fat-related words. For a word to be considered valid for the Tobin et al. study, a minimum of 70% of students needed to agree on its category, with no more than 10% indicating "unsure." These validated words formed the basis for the present study's eye-tracking task. Specifically, sets of four words were constructed, each containing either one thin-related word (e.g., "lean") or one fat-related word (e.g., "tubby"), alongside three neutral words (e.g., "humid"). To ensure consistency and minimize confounding variables, words within each set were carefully matched for the number of letters. Furthermore, their printed frequency—how often a word appears per million words—was kept similar across the set. For instance, an example set might include "massive" (seven letters; printed frequency 10.0), "intense" (seven letters; printed frequency 10.2), "lecture" (seven letters; printed frequency 10.5), and "twisted" (seven letters; printed frequency 10.6). Valence ratings were also utilized to group words with comparable emotional associations into these four-word sets. Further methodological specifics regarding the word stimuli can be found in Tobin et al. (2018).
An online survey, distributed via Qualtrics, validated five image categories for this study, adapting Gao et al.'s (2014) methodology. These categories included "fat" and "thin" body images, average-sized women, and gardening/household scenes. All images were sourced online. One hundred undergraduate women provided informed consent and participated for course credit.
Participants in the survey were presented with images from five distinct categories, displayed individually and in a randomized sequence for each participant. The image categories included 45 "thin-related" images, depicting women with smaller physiques in swimwear or underwear, and 45 "fat-related" images, showing women with larger bodies similarly attired. Additionally, 90 images featured average-sized women engaged in everyday activities like reading, conversing, walking, or using a computer, where body shape and weight were less prominent. These average-sized woman images served to differentiate between a specific attentional bias toward fat or thin information and a more general attentional bias toward images of women, a methodology consistent with Gao et al. (2014). Control images comprised 70 gardening-related items and 70 household-related items. From a total pool of 320 images, each participant randomly received either 106 or 107 images for evaluation. Participants rated the emotional valence of each image on a scale from -3 ("extremely negative") to +3 ("extremely positive"), with 0 as the neutral midpoint. Furthermore, the "thin-related," "fat-related," and average-sized woman images were assessed for body size, using a scale from -3 ("extremely thin") to +3 ("extremely fat"), with 0 indicating an average size. Attractiveness was also rated on a scale from -3 ("extremely unattractive") to +3 ("extremely attractive"), again with 0 as the midpoint. This comprehensive rating process allowed for detailed characterization of the visual stimuli used in the study.
To finalize the stimulus set, images were chosen based on body size ratings. Thirty-two images categorized as "thin-related" were selected, specifically those with the lowest average body size ratings, indicating they were closest to "extremely thin." Similarly, 32 "fat-related" images were chosen, representing those with the highest average body size ratings, nearest to "extremely fat." For average-sized women, 64 images with body size ratings closest to zero were included. Additionally, 64 gardening-related and 64 household-related images were drawn from the initial pool of 70 per category. These non-body images were specifically chosen to match the valence ratings of the women's images, ensuring that each four-image display presented to participants maintained consistent emotional tone. This careful selection process aimed to control for potential confounding variables in the eye-tracking task.
Over ten months, potential participants were recruited through an online survey. All individuals gave written informed consent. Participants rated their body dissatisfaction on an 11-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (extremely satisfied) to 10 (extremely dissatisfied). This measure was adapted from established visual analog scales for appearance, weight, and body dissatisfaction (Heinberg & Thompson, 1995). Only women whose scores fell within the top third of body dissatisfaction ratings were invited to the laboratory. This method of using a tertile split to identify body-dissatisfied women has been successfully employed in prior research on attentional biases for body-related stimuli (Tobin et al., 2018). The highest tertile corresponded to ratings of 7–10 on the scale.
The study recruited 167 female undergraduate students from the University of Calgary, aged 17 to 39 years (mean age = 20.6). Participants were selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. To be included, individuals had to be psychology students, self-identify as female, report no uncorrected visual impairments, and score within the top tertile (7-10 on an 11-point scale) for body dissatisfaction on an initial online screening. This ensured a focus on women with high levels of body dissatisfaction (mean score = 7.52, SD = 0.86). Excluded were individuals who had participated in prior online surveys for stimulus development or were under 17 years old. Initially, 177 women volunteered for the laboratory visit, with 168 attending. One participant's data was excluded due to poor eye-tracking quality, resulting in the final sample size of 167. This sample size exceeded the 77 participants determined necessary by an a priori power calculation (G*Power 3.1; Faul et al., 2007) to detect a medium effect size in the moderation analysis (linear multiple regression, fixed model, R-squared increase) with an alpha level of .05 and 80% power. This calculation was based on effect sizes observed in previous research.

Age, education, and ethnicity data were gathered through a questionnaire.
BMI, an adiposity estimate, was calculated from participants' self-reported heights and weights. Although self-reported measurements correlate highly with actual values, they can underestimate weight and overestimate height, potentially affecting BMI accuracy.
Body dissatisfaction was measured using the 34-item Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ). This instrument evaluates concerns about body shape and feelings of "fatness" over the preceding four weeks. Responses are recorded on a 6-point Likert scale, from "never" to "always," with higher scores indicating greater dissatisfaction. The BSQ demonstrates strong concurrent and discriminant validity, and its reliability is well-established, including a test-retest reliability of .88. For the present study's sample, Cronbach's α was .96, confirming its internal consistency.
Body appreciation was measured using the Body Appreciation Scale 2 (BAS-2), a 10-item questionnaire. Participants rated statements on a 5-point Likert scale, from "never" to "always." The BAS-2 assesses various aspects of body appreciation, including respect for one's body, attentiveness to its needs, comfort, and recognition of its unique characteristics. Higher scores on this scale indicate greater body appreciation. The BAS-2 demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including good internal consistency, construct validity, and test-retest reliability, particularly in university women. For the current study's sample, the Cronbach's α was .93, indicating excellent internal consistency.
Participants' eye movements were recorded using an EyeLink 1000 system, which employs video-based infrared tracking. This system captured eye gaze data at a rate of 1,000 Hz, achieving a temporal resolution of 2 milliseconds and an average gaze error below 0.5 degrees of visual angle. To ensure precise tracking, participants utilized a headrest while viewing stimuli on a 24-inch LCD monitor positioned approximately 60 cm away. Each trial began with a central fixation marker. Stimuli presentation was contingent on the eye-tracking system confirming the participant's gaze remained on this marker for at least one second. This procedure ensured that participants were consistently focused on the center of the display before any stimuli appeared, thereby optimizing the accuracy and reliability of the collected eye-tracking data.
Participants viewed 29 sets of four words and 32 sets of four images, each displayed for 8 seconds, during which eye-tracking data was continuously recorded. The word sets comprised 12 "thin-neutral" sets, each containing one thin-related word and three neutral words, and 12 "fat-neutral" sets, each with one fat-related word and three neutral words. An additional five sets consisted solely of four neutral words. These neutral word sets were included to diminish the prominence of the thin- and fat-related words within the other sets. All word sets had been previously utilized in a study by Tobin et al. (2018). To ensure consistency, words in the thin-neutral and fat-neutral sets were matched for letter count, printed frequency, and valence. Similarly, words in the neutral sets were matched in letter count to those in the thin-neutral and fat-neutral sets. This meticulous matching aimed to control for extraneous variables that might influence participants' attention during the 8-second presentations.
Participants viewed 16 sets of thin-neutral images and 16 sets of fat-neutral images. Each set contained four images: one weight-related (thin or fat), one average-sized woman, one gardening, and one household item. Image valence within each set was matched to prevent it from influencing participants' attention to different image types. This ensured that any observed attentional biases were due to the weight-related content, not emotional associations.
Participants viewed stimuli arranged in four quadrants on a display. Each quadrant contained one word or image. To mitigate positional biases, fat- and thin-related words and images were distributed equally across all quadrants. The study incorporated two distinct blocks of word and image sets. The order in which these blocks were presented was balanced across participants, and the sequence of sets within each block was randomized. In total, there were 58 word sets and 64 image sets. However, each participant was exposed to only one block, comprising half of the total word and image sets. The entire data collection process, including eye-tracker calibration, took approximately 10 minutes per participant. This methodical presentation ensured controlled and unbiased exposure to the diverse stimuli.
Participants first provided informed consent upon arrival at the laboratory in groups of three to five. Individually, they then completed demographic questionnaires, the BSQ, and the BAS-2 via Qualtrics, a process taking approximately 15 minutes. Following this, eye-tracking data was collected in a private room. The entire procedure, including eye-tracking, lasted between 45 minutes and one hour. Finally, participants were fully debriefed.
Eye-tracking data underwent processing using SR Research's EyeLink Data Viewer software, which applied default settings to filter out blinks, missing data, and other recording artifacts. For data inclusion in the analysis, a minimum fixation duration of 100 milliseconds was required. Any consecutive fixations shorter than this threshold were merged into a single fixation. All participants' eye-tracking data were subjected to quality checks to ensure their suitability for analysis. To quantify attentional maintenance, a bias score was computed for each participant. This score represented the percentage of total display fixation time that was spent fixating on a specific image or word type during the 8-second presentations, averaged across all trials. For instance, to derive attentional maintenance bias scores for thin-related images, the duration of fixations on these images was calculated as a proportion of the overall fixation time across the entire display, then averaged over all relevant trials. Consequently, elevated scores indicated a greater degree of attentional maintenance, signifying longer cumulative fixation times on the particular stimuli.
To analyze the relationships between body dissatisfaction, body appreciation, and attentional biases, two primary statistical methods were employed. First, Pearson's bivariate correlations were calculated. This step served a dual purpose: to confirm previously observed positive associations between body dissatisfaction and attention to weight-related stimuli (thin- and fat-related words and images), and to determine if moderation analyses were warranted. Additionally, these correlations assessed the general interrelationships among all study variables. Second, hierarchical multiple regressions were conducted. The main objective was to investigate if body appreciation moderated the link between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases toward thin-related words and images. Furthermore, these regressions were applied to other stimuli (fat-related words/images, neutral words, average-sized women images, gardening, and household images) as control analyses, as no moderation effects were anticipated for these categories. These control analyses aimed to ascertain if any observed associations with body dissatisfaction were dependent on body appreciation levels.
The study employed hierarchical multiple regression analyses, utilizing PROCESS (Hayes, 2012) within SPSS Statistics (IBM, Version 24). This regression-based analytical tool facilitated the estimation of bias-corrected and 95% confidence intervals through a bootstrapping procedure involving 10,000 resamples and a product of coefficients approach, as detailed by Hayes (2013). This methodology was chosen to optimize statistical power and validity, addressing potential Type I and Type II errors. Predictor variables included body dissatisfaction scores (BSQ), body appreciation scores (BAS-2), and an interaction term combining these two. To mitigate potential multicollinearity with the interaction term, both body dissatisfaction and body appreciation scores were mean-centered. A variance inflation factor of 1.56 (tolerance = .639) confirmed that multicollinearity was not an issue. Significant interaction effects were further investigated using the Johnson–Neyman Regions of Significance (ROS) test. This allowed for the identification of specific body appreciation levels at which significant associations between body dissatisfaction and attentional maintenance bias scores were observed.

Conversely, body dissatisfaction showed a negative correlation with attentional maintenance bias for the other neutral/control stimuli. These findings indicate that higher body dissatisfaction is linked to increased attention to weight-related cues, but not to average body images or other neutral content.
Body appreciation correlated negatively with attentional bias for thin- and fat-related words and images. Conversely, it showed a positive association with neutral/control stimuli, though no link existed for average-sized women's images. As anticipated, body appreciation and body dissatisfaction were inversely related (r = –.601). These findings suggest higher body appreciation generally reduces attention to weight-related information.

This study investigated how body appreciation affects the link between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases toward weight-related words and images. We focused on a large group of undergraduate women with high body dissatisfaction. Our primary goal was to see if greater body appreciation could moderate the relationship between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-ideal cues. Understanding this interaction is crucial for developing prevention programs that shield women from the negative impact of thin-ideal media.
The study's findings indicate a link between body dissatisfaction and increased attention to weight-related information. Specifically, analyses showed a moderate positive correlation between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-related images and fat-related words. A weaker positive correlation was observed for body dissatisfaction with fat-related images and thin-related words. These results align with prior research, confirming that higher body dissatisfaction is associated with heightened attentional biases towards such stimuli. This is particularly significant given that the study sample comprised only women reporting high levels of body dissatisfaction, which inherently limited the range of dissatisfaction observed. This evidence reinforces the understanding that body dissatisfaction is connected to maladaptive attentional biases, which in turn perpetuate negative body image. The reciprocal relationship between exposure to thin-ideal cues, which fosters body dissatisfaction, and these attentional biases underscores the critical need for effective body dissatisfaction prevention strategies.
This study explored how body appreciation relates to attention toward weight-related information, building on prior research. Findings showed that greater body appreciation correlated with reduced attention to such information. Specifically, a moderate inverse relationship existed between body appreciation and attention to thin-related images, while weaker inverse relationships were observed for thin-related words, fat-related images, and fat-related words. These results align with questionnaire-based studies suggesting body appreciation offers protection against negative impacts from thin-ideal media.
Our study revealed a moderate negative correlation (r = –.601) between body dissatisfaction and body appreciation. This association aligns with prior research demonstrating a similar relationship between body appreciation and body satisfaction. While these constructs are related, body appreciation has been shown to independently predict well-being measures like self-esteem and optimism, even after accounting for other body image factors, including body dissatisfaction. Therefore, the observed correlation in our sample supports the idea that body appreciation and body satisfaction are distinct yet interconnected constructs. This distinction is crucial for understanding their unique roles in body image and mental health.
The study confirmed that body appreciation influences the link between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-related words. Specifically, for women with low body appreciation, increased body dissatisfaction correlated with heightened attention to thin-related words. However, for those with average body appreciation, this relationship disappeared, indicating that moderate body appreciation can mitigate the connection between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases towards thin-ideal cues. This suggests that body appreciation might protect women from negative media effects by reducing their focus on thin-ideal information. This moderating effect was unique to thin-related words. No such influence of body appreciation was observed for fat-related words or images, nor for any neutral stimuli, including neutral words, images of average-sized women, or gardening and household items. This specificity underscores the particular role body appreciation plays in processing thin-ideal verbal information. These findings offer initial support for the idea that body appreciation helps women avoid the negative impact of thin-ideal cues by altering their attentional patterns.
For women exhibiting very high body appreciation (BAS-2 scores ≥ 4.8), a negative correlation emerged between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-related words: as dissatisfaction increased, attention to these words decreased. This suggests that highly appreciative women, even when body-dissatisfied, might actively avoid thin-ideal information. Such avoidance could stem from the perceived threat of thin-ideal cues, which can trigger negative self-schemas related to weight, as posited by cognitive models. A core aspect of high body appreciation involves safeguarding one's body image by rejecting unrealistic appearance standards. Therefore, women with strong body appreciation may deliberately disengage from threatening thin-ideal stimuli. This finding has significant implications for understanding the inconsistencies in existing attentional bias research. Some studies report that body-dissatisfied women avoid thin-related information, while others observe heightened attention to such cues. Our results suggest that body appreciation could be a confounding factor, influencing attentional patterns in body-dissatisfied individuals. It may lead some to avoid thin-ideal stimuli, while others, perhaps with lower body appreciation, engage in preferential processing. Consequently, body appreciation might explain why different studies yield contradictory findings regarding how body-dissatisfied women attend to thin-ideal information. This highlights the need to consider body appreciation when investigating attentional biases in this population.
While body appreciation might shield body-dissatisfied women from preferentially processing thin-ideal words by weakening the link between body dissatisfaction and attention to such words, it remains uncertain if this avoidance of thin-related information, particularly at very high levels of body appreciation, actually reduces body dissatisfaction or prevents future eating disorders. It is conceivable that avoiding threatening stimuli could inadvertently perpetuate body dissatisfaction, especially if such avoidance hinders the effectiveness of treatment interventions, such as those directly targeting body dissatisfaction. Therefore, future research is crucial to longitudinally investigate the long-term effects of both attentional avoidance and attentional maintenance of thin-related words on body dissatisfaction and eating disorder risk factors. This will help identify optimal attentional strategies for prevention programs.
Our prediction that body appreciation would moderate the link between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-related images was not supported. This divergence may stem from how word and image stimuli are processed. Words, potentially reflecting internal thoughts, are often interpreted as self-relevant. Cognitive models suggest that negative weight-related schemas are sustained by selectively processing self-relevant information. Therefore, for individuals with high body dissatisfaction, thin-related words might be more schema-relevant and thus preferentially processed, with body appreciation influencing this relationship. However, this moderation was not observed for thin-related images, indicating a difference in how these visual cues are processed.
The relationship between body dissatisfaction and attentional biases appears more intricate when considering image stimuli compared to words. For women experiencing body dissatisfaction, attentional focus on thin-ideal images might be more significantly influenced by social comparison processes than their attention to thin-ideal words. This is because social comparison is a primary mechanism through which thin-ideal media contributes to increased body dissatisfaction. When body-dissatisfied women encounter images of thin women, upward social comparisons—comparing themselves to more attractive individuals—are likely to be more pronounced. This heightened engagement with social comparison stems from images being richer, more evocative stimuli that closely mirror the thin-ideal messages frequently encountered in advertisements and social media. Conversely, thin-related words often possess multiple meanings, such as "lean." In such instances, an upward social comparison might not be immediately triggered, potentially leading to a negligible effect on attention. This distinction between word and image processing could explain why body appreciation did not moderate the association between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-related images. If increased body appreciation fails to shield women from upward social comparisons when viewing body images, it would account for the lack of a moderating effect. Therefore, the potential role of social comparison in our findings highlights a crucial area for future investigation. Further research should explore how social comparison contributes to the manifestation of attentional biases linked to body dissatisfaction, as current understanding of these processes in this context remains limited.
This study's strengths include a large sample size and a priori power calculation, ensuring robust detection of moderation effects. Eye-tracking technology measured attention to validated image and word stimuli in university women. This allowed confirmation of prior findings on attentional biases in body-dissatisfied women and assessment of a moderating variable's role in body dissatisfaction and thin-ideal attentional biases.
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The study's reliance on a convenience sample of female university students restricts the generalizability of the results, primarily applying to younger, English-speaking women in a university setting. Furthermore, Body Mass Index (BMI) was determined through self-reported weight and height. Although self-reported measurements correlate strongly with actual measurements, previous research indicates that self-reports can lead to an underestimation of weight and an overestimation of height, thereby affecting calculated BMI. The assessments of body dissatisfaction, body appreciation, and attention were cross-sectional. Longitudinal studies are essential to ascertain if body appreciation can prevent the persistence or exacerbation of maladaptive attentional biases. Nevertheless, this study provides a methodological framework for future longitudinal investigations into the impact of body appreciation on attention to weight-related stimuli. Another limitation is the absence of assessment for current or past eating disorders, making it impossible to determine their potential influence on the results. Utilizing a nonclinical sample might have led to an underestimation of the relationships between body dissatisfaction, body appreciation, and attentional bias, as clinical populations with eating disorders have demonstrated more pronounced attentional biases toward weight-related stimuli. Conversely, given that the sample exclusively comprised women with high body dissatisfaction—a recognized risk factor for eating disorders—the observed attentional bias patterns might still reflect those found in clinical samples. Future research should investigate whether body appreciation can mitigate attentional biases in individuals with eating disorders.
This study pioneeringly established body appreciation's moderating influence on the link between body dissatisfaction and attention to thin-related words. Furthermore, it was the first to reveal a correlation between body appreciation and attention to various weight-related stimuli, including thin- and fat-related images and words. Specifically, increased body appreciation corresponded with reduced attention to these weight-related cues. These findings highlight body appreciation as a crucial element in biased information processing concerning body image. Clinically, the results propose that fostering body appreciation could be a valuable strategy in preventive interventions designed to mitigate maladaptive attentional biases linked to body dissatisfaction and eating disorders. Future longitudinal studies are essential to ascertain if high body appreciation indeed safeguards women from developing attentional biases toward weight-related information and if this influence can prevent other eating disorder risk factors.
