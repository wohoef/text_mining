Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a prevalent and aggressive solid tumor globally, frequently linked to hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Effective treatments for advanced HCC are currently insufficient, highlighting an urgent need for improved biomarkers for early detection and therapeutic intervention. To address this, researchers performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on total RNA extracted from matched HBV-derived HCC tumors and adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs). This analysis aimed to identify and validate differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these tissue types. The study identified 166 DEGs. Among these, eight highly ranked and validated DEGs—TK1, CTTN, CEP72, TRIP13, FTH1, FLAD1, CHRM2, and AMBP—were notable for possessing putative OCT4 binding motifs within their promoter regions. Further investigation revealed that TK1, TRIP13, and OCT4 consistently showed elevated expression in HCC tumors compared to APTs. Analysis of mRNA levels for TK1, TRIP13, and OCT4 in 384 HCC samples from the TCGA database demonstrated a negative correlation with patient overall survival, relapse-free survival, and progression-free survival. This finding supports the role of TK1 and TRIP13 as HCC biomarkers and suggests a connection with OCT4. Experimental evidence, both in vitro and in vivo, confirmed that OCT4 proteins directly bind to the promoters of TK1 and TRIP13. Moreover, knocking out OCT4 in HCC cell lines led to a reduction in TK1 and TRIP13 expression and significantly diminished the tumorigenic capacity of these cells. In conclusion, this RNA-seq study successfully identified novel HCC signature genes that hold promise as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. The shared transcriptional regulation of these genes by OCT4 indicates a crucial role for OCT4 in HCC development, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a prevalent solid tumor, ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, and second in less developed nations. Annually, approximately 500,000 new cases are reported in the Asia-Pacific region, with over 60% of these cases originating in China, frequently linked to hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Chronic HBV infections, particularly when accompanied by chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, elevate an individual's risk of developing HCC. HBV-related HCCs differ from those caused by other risk factors, showing increased chromosomal alterations, p53 mutations, heightened WNT/β-catenin pathway activation, and elevated expression of fetal liver/hepatic progenitor genes.
Improving survival for late-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients remains a significant challenge due to the absence of highly effective treatment options. Consequently, early diagnosis and intervention are crucial. While existing diagnostic biomarkers like alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), AFP-L3, and des-gamma-carboxyprothrombin (DCP) have been identified and assessed clinically, their utility is limited by insufficient sensitivity and specificity, particularly in early HCC stages. This has prompted ongoing research into combining these with novel candidate biomarkers. Recent advancements in technologies such as whole genome sequencing (WGS), RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq), and proteomic profiling have revolutionized biomarker discovery, deepening our comprehension of the intricate interplay between proteins, genes, and noncoding RNAs in hepatocarcinogenesis. Despite this progress, many studies have overlooked the distinction between HBV-related HCC and HCC stemming from other etiologies. Furthermore, the analysis of tumor tissues often lacked corresponding adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs) from the same patients. To date, only two genome-wide transcriptome analyses utilizing RNA-seq have specifically focused on identifying HBV-related HCC biomarkers from matched patient samples. Huang et al. pioneered this approach, analyzing 10 matched pairs of cancerous and non-cancerous tissues. Their work identified 1378 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were predominantly enriched in 54 bio-function terms and 41 canonical pathways, offering valuable insights into HCC's molecular mechanisms. Subsequently, Miao et al. performed multi-omics profiling on a comprehensive set of HCC patient samples, identifying four DEGs (SFN, TTK, BUB1, MCM4) linked to varying tumor differentiation patterns. They highlighted TTK, a dual-specificity protein kinase, as a promising prognostic biomarker for HBV-related HCC. Nevertheless, both studies lacked a thorough functional characterization of the identified DEGs and candidate biomarkers.
This study utilized RNA sequencing to analyze the complete transcriptomes of matched tumor and adjacent peritumor tissues from three HBV-related HCC patients, leading to the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Subsequently, the top-ranked DEGs were validated as potential HCC signature genes in a separate cohort of 30 HCC patients using qRT-PCR. Notably, all eight of the highest-ranked and validated DEGs (TK1, CTTN, CEP72, TRIP13, FTH1, FLAD1, CHRM2, AMBP) were found to possess predicted OCT4 binding motifs in their promoter regions. Furthermore, TK1 and TRIP13 were confirmed as direct transcriptional targets of the pluripotency factor OCT4.
Antibodies for OCT4 (monoclonal sc-5279, polyclonal ab19857), TK1 (monoclonal ab-76,495), and TRIP13 (polyclonal ab-204,331) were acquired from Santa Cruz Biotechnology and Abcam. Secondary antibodies (anti-mouse 7076, anti-rabbit 7074), normal Rabbit IgG (2729), and a biotinylated protein ladder (7727) were sourced from Cell Signaling Technology. Additionally, an anti-GAPDH antibody (AG019) was obtained from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology.
This study utilized a cohort of 33 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients, previously detailed in reference [12], for RNA sequencing and subsequent validation. Three male HBV-related HCC patients (T1/P1, T2/P2, T3/P3) provided samples specifically for RNA-seq analysis. During hepatic carcinectomy, both tumor tissues and adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs) were meticulously collected. Tumor tissues were harvested precisely from within the tumor boundaries, while APTs were taken at least 3 cm away from the tumor margins to ensure distinct separation. A single pathologist oversaw all tissue collections, and two independent expert pathologists confirmed all pathological diagnoses as primary hepatocellular carcinoma. The broader 33-patient cohort comprised 27 men and 6 women, with an average age of 56. All patients tested positive for HBV surface antigen, were negative for hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, and shared a common underlying cirrhosis etiology. Retrospective data, including demographic, preoperative laboratory, and pathologic parameters, were extracted from electronic medical records. Comprehensive clinical information for these patients is presented in Table 1.
Huh7 and Hep3B human HCC cell lines were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Cell Bank. They were maintained in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS (HycloneR GE, USA), at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and humidified conditions.
To prepare for RNA sequencing, total RNA was first extracted from matched hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tumor tissues and adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs) obtained from three patients with HBV-related HCC, utilizing Trizol reagent. An Illumina standard kit guided the subsequent RNA-library preparation. This process began with the purification of poly-A containing RNAs, which were then fragmented. These RNA fragments were reverse-transcribed into single-strand cDNA using superscript II reverse transcriptase and random hexa-primers. Following this, DNA polymerase I and E. coli RNase H facilitated the synthesis of the second strand. The resulting double-stranded cDNA fragments underwent end repairing and A-tailing before purification and ligation to Illumina adapters with a Quick ligation™ kit and DNA ligase. The adapter-modified cDNA libraries were then separated on agarose gels, and 200-bp fragments were isolated and amplified through 15 PCR cycles. The quality of these purified cDNA libraries was verified using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Before cluster generation, the cDNA library concentrations were determined and adjusted to 10 nM in Tris-HCl buffer. Finally, cluster formation, primer hybridization, and sequencing reactions were executed on an Illumina® Hiseq2000 apparatus, adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol.
Gene expression levels were quantified following alignment of quality-controlled sequencing reads to the hg19 human reference genome (NCBI build: GRCh37) using TopHat software [13] with standard settings. The mapped read coordinates were then compared against a human gene set from RefSeq (NCBI) to count reads per gene, thereby establishing individual gene expression. To enable comparisons, these expression levels were normalized to Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (RPKM). The Limma package within Bioconductor was employed to analyze differential gene expression between tumor and adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs), utilizing raw read counts. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to calculate the false discovery rate (FDR) for each gene, alongside a mean log2 fold change (RPKM of tumor/RPKM of APTs). Genes were identified as significantly differentially expressed (DEGs) if their FDR was below 0.05 and the fold change exceeded 2 when comparing tumor and APT samples.
To understand the DEGs' roles in HCC development, functional enrichment analyses were conducted. This involved using Gene Ontology (GO), a standard classification system for gene functions, alongside PANTHER and KEGG pathway analyses to identify their potential contributions to liver carcinogenesis.
To confirm the initial RNA-seq findings, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were first validated using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on the same RNA samples. For further validation, an independent cohort of 30 HBV-related HCC patients provided tumor and adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs), from which total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit. RNA quality and concentration were assessed with a Merinton SMA1000. Following cDNA synthesis via Prime ScriptTm RT Master Mix, qRT-PCR was performed in triplicate using SYBR Premix Ex TaqII on an ABI system 7500. GAPDH or B2M served as reference genes, and relative gene expression, presented as fold changes, was calculated using the Delta-Delta Ct method. Primer details for qRT-PCR are provided in Table S1.
To evaluate the prognostic significance of eight identified HCC signature genes and POU5F1, their mRNA expression data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) RNA sequence database. A total of 348 HCC patients were included, selected based on a pathological diagnosis of HCC and the availability of comprehensive clinical and survival data, including overall survival (OS), relapse-free survival (RFS), and progression-free survival (PFS). Survival curves were then generated using the Kaplan-Meier plotter online tool. Patients were categorized into high and low expression groups for each gene based on median mRNA levels. Statistical differences in survival were determined using the log-rank (Mantel-Haenszel) test, with significance set at p < 0.05, and hazard ratios (HR) and p-values were computed online.
To create OCT4-knockout (OCT4-KO) Huh7 and Hep3B cell lines, a CRISPR/Cas9 lentiviral system was employed, following a protocol from Feng Zhang's lab. First, a single guide RNA (sgRNA) specific for OCT4A was designed and inserted into a pLentiCRISPRv2 vector. Lentiviruses for both OCT4-KO and non-targeting control (NTC) were then produced by transfecting 293T cells with the respective pLentiCRISPRv2 constructs, along with psPAX2 and pMD2.G plasmids. After determining viral titers, Huh7 or Hep3B cells were infected at a low multiplicity of infection (0.3–0.5). Puromycin selection (0.5–1 μg/ml) for seven days isolated infected cells, which were then expanded for an additional week for subsequent analyses, including Surveyor assays, qRT-PCR, and other functional tests.
Protein levels in tumor and adjacent peritumor tissues were determined using Western blotting. Samples were first quantified with a BCA Protein Assay, then boiled and subjected to SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and visualized with ECL. GAPDH served as a loading control.
Formalin-fixed tumor tissue sections were analyzed via immunohistochemistry. Anti-TK1, anti-TRIP13 (both 1:100), and anti-OCT4 (1:500) antibodies were used for this examination.
To investigate protein-DNA interactions, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed following the manufacturer's protocol for the LightShift chemiluminescent EMSA kit. Nuclear extracts from cells were prepared using NE-PER™ reagents. Biotin-labeled double-stranded DNA probes for TK1 (three probes) and TRIP13, containing putative OCT4 binding motifs, were synthesized with specific sequences for use in the assay.
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis, following established protocols [17] and utilizing an EZ-ChIP™ kit (Merck Millipore 2,673,061), was employed to investigate DNA-protein interactions. The procedure began with chemically cross-linking 2 × 107 to 5 × 107 cells using a 1% formaldehyde solution for 10 minutes at room temperature, with the reaction subsequently quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM. Cells were then washed twice with cold PBS and collected with a silicon scraper. To shear the cross-linked DNA into fragments averaging 300–800 bp, the cell samples underwent sonication. Immunoprecipitation was performed using 5 μg of rabbit anti-OCT4 antibody (ab 19,857) and 100 μl of protein A/G agarose beads (Pierce 20,421), with normal rabbit IgG serving as a negative control. The resulting DNA fragments were then amplified using specific primers for TK1 (F: 5’-CTGGCAGGGTCTACGGATAT-3′, R: 5′-CCGTCTAGAGCACTCCTGT-3′) and TRIP13 (F: 5′-TCGAGGTCCCTTCTTCCCAA-3′, R: 5′-AGTAGCCCCATCTGGGTCAG-3′). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted on an ABI system 7500 (Life Technologies, USA) using SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (TaKaRa, Japan). The anti-OCT4 precipitated DNA fragments, corresponding to specific genes, were quantified relative to anti-IgG precipitated fragments, with input genomic DNA and extracted DNA fragments serving as templates. Relative quantification was achieved using the Delta-Delta Ct method.
Female NOD/SCID mice, aged 3–4 weeks, were acquired from the Shanghai Experimental Animal Centre, Chinese Academy of Science. These animals were maintained in the central animal facility at the First Affiliated Hospital of the School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, under specific pathogen-free conditions within laminar-flow cabinets, adhering to a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All mouse studies followed the National Institute Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, with the animal protocol receiving approval from the Zhejiang University Committee of the Ethics of Animal Experiments.
To assess the impact of OCT4 knockout on tumor growth, mice were randomly assigned to either an OCT4-KO or a control (NTC) group. Each mouse received a subcutaneous inoculation of 2 x 10^6 Huh7 cells, either OCT4-KO or NTC. Tumor diameters were precisely measured every three days, and volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = [(tumor length) × (tumor width)^2]/2. On day 18, mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0. Continuous data were presented as mean ± S.D. or medians with interquartile ranges. Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test compared continuous variables, while paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for paired groups. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient assessed the relationship between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR measurements.
The quality of the RNA-seq data was confirmed by evaluating sequencing randomness and saturation, with all libraries exhibiting a Q30 value exceeding 80%. These data, derived from three matched pairs of HBV-related HCC tumors and adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs), revealed no significant variation in the number of genes identified per tumor-APT pair. Across these samples, an average of 12,073 genes were upregulated and 8,095 genes were downregulated in tumor tissues compared to their paired APTs. Gene coverage levels for each sample are detailed in Fig. S1, and overall data characteristics are summarized in Table S3 and S4.
Applying a stringent criterion of FDRs < 0.05 and a fold change > 2, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in three pairs of HCC tumor tissues (T1, T2, T3) compared to their adjacent peritumor tissues (P1, P2, P3). Specifically, 947, 1089, and 1054 genes showed increased expression in the respective tumor samples. Conversely, 839, 720, and 873 genes exhibited decreased expression in these same samples (as detailed in Fig. 1a). Across all three paired HCC samples, a consistent set of 166 DEGs was identified, comprising 105 up-regulated and 61 down-regulated genes. A heat map visually represents the relative expression levels of these 166 common DEGs (Fig. 1b).
To understand the initial characteristics of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), functional enrichment analyses were conducted. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis identified 38 significantly over-represented terms (adjusted p-value < 0.05), comprising 23 biological processes, 14 cellular components, and 1 molecular function (Table S5). Furthermore, a PANTHER website classification revealed that upregulated genes in tumor tissues were primarily linked to "transcription factor and signaling molecule" categories, whereas downregulated genes were predominantly associated with "receptor and hydrolase" functions (Fig. 1c). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis also indicated that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in "cell cycle" and "pathway in cancer" pathways.
To confirm the accuracy of our RNA-seq findings, we initially validated 14 of the most significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). These 14 DEGs were chosen from the 166 identified, and their mRNA levels were assessed using the same six amplified RNA samples originally used for RNA-seq. The selected DEGs comprised nine genes that were upregulated (TK1, PITX2, CTTN, CEP72, TRIP13, FTH1, FLAD1, MMP12, ZNF695) and five that were downregulated (CHRM2, AMBP, KCNA1, C14orf180, PRB2). A strong correlation coefficient of 0.82 was observed when comparing the log2 fold changes from qRT-PCR with those from RNA-seq for these genes, affirming the reliability of our RNA-seq approach for quantifying gene expression variations in HCC samples. Subsequently, this validation was expanded to a larger, independent cohort of 30 patients diagnosed with HBV-related HCC. This cohort consisted of 24 males and 6 females, with an average age of 51.69 years. Clinical details for these patients are provided in Table 1. The majority of these patients presented with poorly or moderately differentiated tumors, with 14 having a single tumor and 16 having multiple tumors. Additionally, four patients exhibited portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT).
In a validation cohort, 10 out of 14 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) exhibited consistent expression patterns. Specifically, mRNA levels of TK1, CTTN, CEP72, TRIP13, FTH1, and FLAD1 were significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs). Conversely, CHRM2, AMBP, and KCNA1 showed significant downregulation in tumor tissues. The individual expression levels of these HCC signature genes in paired HCC and APT samples are detailed in Fig. 2c. To further contextualize our RNA-seq findings, we compared them with existing literature. We analyzed supplementary data from Huang et al. [10], who conducted genome-wide RNA-seq on 10 matched HBV-related HCC cancer and non-cancerous tissue pairs. We extracted the RPKM values for our 10 DEGs from their dataset (Table S6) and calculated tumor/peritumor ratios, which were then plotted as fold changes (Fig. S2). PITX2 and KCNA1 were excluded from further analysis due to their negligible RPKM values. Pairwise comparisons of tumor versus peritumor samples for each DEG are presented in Fig. S3. A strong correlation coefficient of 0.882 was observed between our RNA-seq data (Fig. 2b) and the dataset from Huang et al. (Fig. S2), confirming the robustness and reproducibility of both experimental approaches. Based on these findings, we propose that eight DEGs—TK1, CTTN, CEP72, TRIP13, FTH1, FLAD1, CHRM2, and AMBP—represent novel signature genes for HBV-related HCC.
To understand the potential regulatory mechanisms governing the identified HCC signature genes, we analyzed a 4 kb promoter region (from -3 kb to +1 kb relative to the Transcription Start Site, TSS) for each gene using Genomatix software. This analysis aimed to detect potential transcription factor binding sites. While several transcription factors were predicted to associate with the promoters of these eight genes, either uniquely or partially in common, OCT4 stood out. It was the only transcription factor found to possess a canonical octamer binding motif (ATGCA/TAAT) within the promoter regions of all eight HCC signature genes (as illustrated in Fig. 3a). Among these eight genes, TRIP13, CEP72, and CHRM2 each contained a single OCT4 binding motif. The remaining genes, however, exhibited multiple OCT4 binding motifs within their promoter regions. Based on these findings, TK1, which has multiple OCT4 binding motifs, and TRIP13, with its single OCT4 binding motif, were chosen for more detailed validation and characterization. Our investigation further extended to previously reported HCC biomarkers. We discovered that OCT4 binding motifs were also present in the promoter regions of TTK and CDC20 (Fig. S4). These two biomarkers, identified in separate studies, also showed high tumor-to-peritumor (T/P) expression ratios in the Huang et al. [10] study (Table S6). Therefore, a consistent pattern emerged: both the eight HCC signature genes identified in our study and the two established HCC biomarker genes from the literature all contain canonical OCT4 binding motifs in their promoter regions. This observation strongly suggests a common transcriptional regulatory mechanism by OCT4 for these HCC signature genes. To experimentally verify this, we synthesized biotin-labeled DNA probes corresponding to the octamer motifs within the TK1 and TRIP13 promoter regions (Fig. 3b). We then used an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to assess their binding to OCT4. A biotin-labeled NANOG probe, known to contain a well-characterized octamer motif, served as a positive control. As anticipated, recombinant wild-type OCT4 protein bound to the NANOG probe (Fig. S5A and S5B). It also bound to three TK1 probes with similar affinity to the NANOG probe, with TK1 probe 2 exhibiting the strongest binding (Fig. S5A). The TRIP13 probe also bound to OCT4, though with a lower affinity compared to the NANOG probe (Fig. S5B). Having established direct in vitro binding between OCT4 and the TK1 and TRIP13 promoter probes, we proceeded to confirm this binding in vivo using Hep3B cell nuclear extracts, which contain endogenous OCT4 protein. EMSA results showed a band for both the TK1 and TRIP13 probes that migrated similarly to the NANOG probe/recombinant OCT4 protein complex, indicating the formation of TK1 probe/OCT4 and TRIP13 probe/OCT4 complexes (Fig. 3c, arrow). Additionally, slower-migrating multiple bands were observed for both probes (Fig. 3c, arrowheads), potentially representing probes bound to larger protein complexes that include OCT4. The intensity of these slow-migrating bands was greater than that of the monomeric TK1 probe/OCT4 band, and even more so than the TRIP13 probe/OCT4 band, suggesting that OCT4 in complex with other proteins is likely the predominant form binding to the TK1/TRIP13 promoters. Finally, we used a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay to validate the TK1/TRIP13 promoter-OCT4 interactions in Hep3B cells in vivo. We used a rabbit anti-OCT4 antibody to precipitate OCT4-bound DNA fragments from Hep3B cell lysates. The OCT4-binding motif-containing DNA fragments of the TK1 or TRIP13 promoters were then quantified using specific primers for qPCR. Compared to the negative control (rabbit IgG), the anti-OCT4 antibody enriched the TK1 and TRIP13 promoter fragments by 2.5-3 fold (Fig. 3d), confirming a genuine binding between OCT4 and the TK1/TRIP13 promoters within living cells.
Our bioinformatics analysis indicated that the promoter regions of the TK1 and TRIP13 genes contain one or more putative OCT4 binding octamer motifs (Fig. 3a). To investigate this, we synthesized biotin-labeled DNA probes corresponding to these octamer motifs (Fig. 3b) and used an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to assess their binding to OCT4. A biotin-NANOG probe, which has a known octamer motif, served as a positive control. As anticipated, recombinant wild-type OCT4 protein successfully bound to the NANOG probe (Fig. S5A and S5B). We observed comparable binding affinity between OCT4 and three TK1 probes, with TK1 probe 2 showing the strongest interaction (Fig. S5A). The TRIP13 probe also bound to OCT4, though with a lower affinity compared to the NANOG probe (Fig. S5B). After confirming direct OCT4 binding to TK1 and TRIP13 promoter probes *in vitro*, we extended our investigation using Hep3B cell nuclear extracts containing endogenous OCT4 protein. EMSA results showed a band for both the TK1 and TRIP13 probes that migrated similarly to the NANOG probe/recombinant OCT4 complex, indicating the formation of TK1/OCT4 and TRIP13/OCT4 complexes (Fig. 3c, arrow). Additionally, multiple slower-migrating bands were observed for both probes (Fig. 3c, arrowheads), suggesting the presence of larger protein complexes involving OCT4. These slower-migrating bands exhibited stronger intensity than the monomeric TK1/OCT4 and TRIP13/OCT4 bands, implying that OCT4 often binds to the TK1/TRIP13 promoters as part of larger complexes rather than as a monomer. To further validate these interactions *in vivo*, we performed a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay in Hep3B cells. Using a rabbit anti-OCT4 antibody, we specifically precipitated OCT4-bound DNA fragments. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) with specific primers for the TK1 and TRIP13 promoters revealed a 2.5-3 fold enrichment of these promoter fragments when compared to the rabbit IgG negative control (Fig. 3d). This conclusively demonstrated a genuine binding interaction between OCT4 and the TK1/TRIP13 promoters within living cells.
To investigate the relationship between the identified HCC signature genes and OCT4 (encoded by POU5F1) expression in tumor versus adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs), we initially quantified OCT4 mRNA levels. Using qRT-PCR with specific primers for the OCT4A transcript, we observed that OCT4A mRNA was more abundant in HCC tumors compared to their paired APTs (Fig. S6). However, RNA-seq data from a separate study by Huang et al. [10] presented a different picture, showing either no correlation or a slight negative correlation between OCT4 and the HCC signature gene mRNA levels (Table S6). Despite this, our analysis of a cohort of 384 HCC samples from the TCGA database revealed a significant negative correlation between high mRNA levels of TK1, TRIP13, and POU5F1 and patient survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS), relapse-free survival (RFS), and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 4a). This finding reinforces the potential of TK1 and TRIP13 as HCC biomarkers and suggests a link to OCT4. Further examination of the clinicopathological characteristics of these 384 HCC samples (Table S2) indicated that elevated mRNA levels of most HCC signature genes generally corresponded with a high Ragnum Hypoxia Score (≥ 0.5), while low mRNA levels were associated with an Aneuploidy Score of 0. Specifically, high mRNA levels of TK1, TRIP13, and POU5F1 were linked to advanced tumor stages (T3/T4). No clear correlations were found between the mRNA levels of these HCC signature genes and other clinicopathological factors such as mutation count, cancer metastasis stage, publication version type, sex, diagnosis age, or neoplasm histologic grade.
To investigate the protein expression of the identified HCC signature genes, particularly TK1 and TRIP13, and their relationship with OCT4, we employed immunohistochemistry and Western blotting. This was crucial given that mRNA and protein levels don't always align in HCC. Immunohistochemistry revealed that OCT4 was present in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of liver cells within HCC tissues, a finding not observed in adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs) (Fig. 4b). While OCT4's cytoplasmic staining was widespread across most HCC cells, its nuclear presence was more restricted to a subset of cells. Interestingly, TK1 and TRIP13 exhibited a comparable expression pattern in tumor tissues, displaying heterogeneous distribution and primarily cytoplasmic staining, unlike in APTs (Fig. 4b). Further confirmation of elevated protein levels for OCT4, TK1, and TRIP13 in HCC tumor tissues compared to APTs came from Western blot analysis of 12 matched samples (Fig. 4c). Notably, the correlation between OCT4 and TK1 protein levels was stronger than that between OCT4 and TRIP13. This consistent overexpression of OCT4, TK1, and TRIP13 in HCC tumors not only reinforces TK1 and TRIP13's potential as novel HCC biomarkers but also strengthens the hypothesis that OCT4 acts as a shared transcriptional regulator for these two genes in HCC.
To definitively establish OCT4's role as a shared transcription factor for the newly discovered HCC signature genes and its necessity for HCC cell tumorigenesis, we performed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing to knock out (KO) the POU5F1 gene in both Hep3B and Huh7 cell lines, a method previously detailed [14]. The successful knockout of OCT4 was confirmed by Surveyor nuclease assays, which showed clear cleavage of OCT4 PCR products at the Cas9 target site (Fig. 5b). This cleavage indicated a high likelihood of DNA indel formation, signifying effective gene knockout. Subsequently, we investigated the binding of endogenous OCT4 to the promoters of TK1 and TRIP13 in control (NTC) and OCT4-KO Huh7 cells. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) revealed a substantial decrease in monomeric OCT4-bound TK1 promoter fragments (Fig. 5c, arrow) and complexed OCT4-bound TRIP13 promoter fragments (Fig. 5d, arrowheads) within the OCT4-KO cells. These EMSA findings were further supported and corroborated by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) data (Fig. 5e). Consistent with the reduced promoter binding, the transcriptional activity of TK1 was found to be diminished in OCT4-KO Huh7 cells. Furthermore, the transcription of both TK1 and TRIP13 genes was reduced in OCT4-KO Hep3B cells (Fig. 5f). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that OCT4 functions as a common transcription factor for several novel HCC signature genes identified in this study, playing a critical role in their regulation within HCC cells. Figure 5 visually summarizes these results, illustrating how OCT4 knockout leads to decreased promoter occupancy and transcriptional activation of the TK1 and TRIP13 genes by OCT4. Specifically, Figure 5a provides a schematic of the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated OCT4 knockout. Figure 5b displays Surveyor assay results, indicating DNA indel formation and successful KO in the OCT4-KO cell lines. Figures 5c and 5d present EMSA data for biotinylated TK1 and TRIP13 probes, respectively, showing reduced binding in OCT4-KO Huh7 cells compared to NTC cells, with recombinant human OCT4 proteins serving as a positive control for NANOG probes. Figure 5e details the ChIP analysis, quantifying the enrichment of TK1 and TRIP13 promoter fragments precipitated by anti-OCT4 antibody in NTC versus OCT4-KO Huh7 cells. Finally, Figure 5f shows the qRT-PCR quantification of TK1 and TRIP13 transcription in NTC and OCT4-KO Huh7/Hep3B cells, using B2M as an internal control. The statistical significance of these results is indicated by p-values, with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
To investigate OCT4's role in HCC tumorigenesis in a living organism, a study was conducted using nude mice. These mice were subcutaneously injected with either OCT4-knockout (OCT4-KO) Huh7 cells or non-targeting control (NTC) Huh7 cells, ensuring an equal number of cells for each group. Tumor growth was monitored by measuring xenograft volumes every three days. The experiment concluded after 18 days, at which point the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised and weighed. The results clearly demonstrated a significant impact of OCT4 on tumor development. Tumors in the NTC group exhibited rapid and consistent growth. In contrast, the OCT4-KO group displayed markedly slower tumor progression. By day 18, substantial disparities were observed in both the volume and weight of the tumors between the two experimental groups. These findings underscore the critical involvement of OCT4 in driving the tumorigenic process within HCC cells.
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for over 90% of primary liver cancers and poses a significant health challenge due to its poor prognosis. The global incidence of HCC is rising, particularly in less developed areas where HBV infections are more prevalent. Persistent hepatitis B virus (HBV) infections are responsible for more than half of all HCC cases. The development of HBV-related HCC is a complex, multi-stage process influenced by viral elements, host genetics, and environmental factors. Therefore, discovering molecular biomarkers and signature genes active at various stages of HCC development is crucial for improving patient outcomes.
Our study employed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), a modern deep-sequencing technique for comprehensive transcriptome analysis, to identify 164 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tumor tissues compared to adjacent peritumor tissues (APTs). RNA-seq offers superior precision in quantifying gene transcript levels and their variants compared to other methods. The reliability of our RNA-seq findings was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR for 14 selected DEGs, showing a strong correlation between the two measurement techniques. Furthermore, the RNA-seq data for eight key HCC signature genes in our research aligned closely with results from a previous study by Huang et al., even though their DEGs were not further prioritized. This strong agreement underscores the high reliability and consistency of the experimental methodologies used in both investigations.
The newly identified HCC signature genes, while novel in this study, have established links to various human cancers, including HCC itself. For instance, the gene AMBP showed downregulation in both HCC tissues and cell lines, aligning with our current findings. Conversely, several genes exhibited upregulation. Overexpression of CTTN (cortactin) has been strongly correlated with poor HCC prognosis, driven by increased cell motility and metastasis. Similarly, FTH1 (ferritin heavy chain 1) upregulation in HCC cells, induced by TNF-α, was observed to mitigate starvation-induced apoptosis. TK1 (thymidine kinase-1) has been implicated in the early stages of numerous cancers, such as breast, lung, heart, esophageal, and gallbladder cancers. Clinical studies have shown that serum TK1 levels exceeding 2.0 pmol/L could signal an elevated risk for later malignancy development. Furthermore, serum TK1 levels were notably higher in HCC patients compared to those with benign liver conditions, suggesting its utility as an additional diagnostic biomarker for HCC. TRIP13 (thyroid hormone receptor interactor 13) also demonstrated widespread upregulation across 12 human cancers, according to a comprehensive analysis of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. High TRIP13 expression was associated with unfavorable prognoses in patients with liver, breast, gastric, and lung cancers. Its gene copy numbers are elevated in early-stage non-small-cell-lung cancer, and increased transcript and protein levels correlate with prostate cancer progression. Moreover, TRIP13 overexpression has been shown to induce malignant transformation in non-malignant fibroblasts and contribute to aggressive, treatment-resistant tumors and enhanced DNA damage repair in head and neck cancer cells. The roles of the remaining three genes—CEP72, FLAD1, and CHRM2—in liver cancer development still require further investigation.
This study suggests OCT4 acts as a shared transcription factor for the eight HCC signature genes identified. OCT4, encoded by POU5F1, belongs to the POU family and is highly expressed in pluripotent stem cells like embryonic stem cells, playing a vital role in early mammalian embryonic development. The POU domains of the OCT4 protein can bind independently and flexibly to half-sites of the octamer motif (ATGCA/TAAT), enabling OCT4 to recognize enhancer or promoter regions of numerous target genes. This adaptability facilitates OCT4's formation of heterodimers with other transcription factors, such as SOX2, cooperatively activating genes linked to pluripotency and self-renewal while simultaneously suppressing differentiation-promoting genes. While distinct from its established roles in pluripotent stem cells, OCT4 is generally understood to enhance the self-renewal, survival, metastasis, and drug resistance of cancer stem cells.
The microenvironment of hepatitis B virus (HBV)-infected cells is crucial in directing liver cells toward a cancer stem cell (CSC) phenotype and influencing HBV-induced pathways that lead to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) or stemness. During liver regeneration, inflammatory and stromal cells release IL-6, which facilitates the transformation of non-CSCs into CSCs. Research by Chang et al. revealed that HBV-related HCC patients exhibit elevated serum IL-6 levels. This, in turn, boosts the expression of autocrine insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I) and its receptor (IGF-IR), which then promotes OCT4 and NANOG expression via a STAT3-dependent mechanism. Furthermore, inflammation-conditioned media from lipopolysaccharide-stimulated U937 cells significantly increased OCT4/NANOG and IGF-I/IGF-IR expression, and activated IGF-IR/AKT signaling in HBV-active HCC cells. This strong link between IL-6/IGF-1R signaling and HBV-related HCC progression suggests that both HBV and proinflammatory cytokines are necessary and work together to induce pluripotency factors and CSC formation. However, the precise mechanism behind OCT4 re-expression still needs to be fully understood.
The HBV viral component HBx is a multi-functional protein that influences numerous cellular processes, including host cell proliferation, apoptosis, and invasion, by activating various viral and cellular genes and modulating signaling pathways [5]. While HBx does not directly bind DNA, it regulates gene expression through the transactivation of multiple transcription factors. Although a direct interaction between HBx and the OCT4 protein has not been established, several studies indicate that HBx can upregulate OCT4 expression in HCC cells through indirect mechanisms. For instance, overexpression of HBx in HepG2 cells has been linked to increased expression of pluripotency factors like OCT4, NANOG, and KLF4, as well as stemness markers such as EpCAM and β-catenin, as reported by Arzumanyan et al. [46]. Another study suggested that HBV viral components, including HBx, might trigger regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP) of EpCAM in HCC cells. This process leads to the cleavage of the EpCAM/β-catenin complex, which then translocates to the nucleus, activating canonical Wnt signaling and subsequently increasing OCT4 expression [47]. While β-catenin has been shown to directly bind and activate the OCT4 promoter in mouse embryonic stem cells [48], this direct regulatory link has not been confirmed in cancer stem cells (CSCs). More recently, HBx was found to stimulate AFP expression before the upregulation of pluripotency factors like OCT4, contributing to a partial reprogramming of liver cells towards HCC progenitor/stem cells [49]. Additionally, HBx can activate FOXM1 expression in HCC cells via the ERK/CREB pathway [50], and FOXM1 is known to directly bind and activate the OCT4 promoter in P19 embryonal carcinoma cells [51]. Separately, HBV PreS1 has been observed to stimulate the expression of multiple pluripotency factors, including OCT4, and promote the self-renewal of liver CSCs [52]. Despite these findings, a definitive identification of the specific factors or mechanisms directly linking HBx to increased OCT4 expression in HBV-related HCC remains elusive. In a study investigating differentially expressed transcription factors in HCC cell lines, ZIC2 was found to be highly expressed in CSCs [16] and was subsequently identified as a direct transcription factor for OCT4. However, whether this mechanism applies to HBV-related HCC and the role of HBV viral components in this context still need to be determined. Notably, the increase in OCT4 mRNA levels in HCC tumor tissues was significantly lower than the increase in OCT4 protein levels, a finding consistent with the current study.
We identified several HCC signature genes, potentially useful as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for HBV-related HCC. OCT4's shared transcriptional control over these genes highlights its crucial role in HCC development, suggesting it as a promising therapeutic target.
