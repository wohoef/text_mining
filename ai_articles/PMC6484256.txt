This study aimed to assess the diagnostic utility of lipocalin-2 (NGAL) in identifying endometrial cancer, a prevalent gynecological malignancy. Researchers compared NGAL levels in patients with endometrial cancer against those with healthy or mildly pathological endometrium. The investigation involved 123 patients, all with a BMI exceeding 21 kg/m2 and presenting with abnormal bleeding; 52 of these individuals had confirmed endometrial cancer. Serum levels of NGAL, CA125, and HE4 were measured across all participants. The findings indicated that median NGAL serum levels were significantly lower in patients with normal endometrium compared to those with endometrial cancer (p=0.006). As a diagnostic tool for distinguishing endometrial cancer from benign endometrial conditions, NGAL protein demonstrated an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.81 (p<0.00001). NGAL exhibited high sensitivity across all patient groups: 84% overall, 82% in pre-menopausal, and 81% in postmenopausal women, with corresponding specificities of 78%, 80%, and 87%. Furthermore, logistic regression analysis revealed NGAL as a statistically significant independent variable for FIGO staging (p=0.000602), with an odds ratio of 3.66. The grading model suggested that each ng/ml increase in NGAL raised the likelihood of less differentiated cancer by 2.32 times. These initial results suggest that lipocalin-2 holds promise for diagnosing uterine body cancers.

Endometrial cancer is a common malignancy, particularly in developed nations, with obesity being a primary contributing factor. Adipose tissue plays diverse roles, including metabolic, immune, and endocrine functions. In 2007, it was discovered that adipose tissue produces neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), also known as lipocalin-2 or siderocalin. This 178-amino acid glycoprotein, initially identified in active neutrophils bound to human neutrophil gelatinase, is a member of the extracellular matrix protein family. Lipocalin-2 is implicated in oncogenesis and is a risk factor for conditions like insulin resistance, diabetes, and both chronic and acute inflammatory states.
Lipocalin-2's role in tumor progression has been observed across various cancers. For instance, increased levels of lipocalin-2 have been detected in tumor tissues from breast, colon, ovarian, and pancreatic cancers. Specifically in colorectal cancer cell lines, an overexpression of lipocalin-2 led to decreased E-cadherin expression, which in turn enhanced the mobility and invasiveness of these cells. More recent investigations into endometrial cancer indicate that lipocalin-2 plays a part in its advancement. Immunohistochemical analyses have further substantiated this, revealing higher lipocalin-2 expression in endometrial cancer compared to atypical endometrial hyperplasia. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic utility of NGAL (lipocalin-2) in identifying endometrial cancer in patients presenting with abnormal bleeding and ultrasound-detected endometrial abnormalities.
The study enrolled 123 individuals who presented with abnormal genital bleeding and had a BMI exceeding 21 kg/m2. Eligibility criteria included both pre- and post-menopausal women experiencing uterine bleeding irregularities and exhibiting ultrasound abnormalities. All participants underwent transvaginal ultrasonography (USG TV) to assess the uterine endometrium. Ethical approval for the study protocol was granted by the PUM Ethical Committee, and every patient provided informed consent. Four women were excluded due to extremely high baseline lipocalin-2 levels (above 2000 ng/mL), as these individuals also had elevated creatinine and were receiving nephrological care for chronic kidney disease (CKD). Histopathological samples were collected through procedures such as abrasion, hysteroscopy, or radical surgery if endometrial cancer was confirmed. Based on the outcomes of surgical treatment and histopathological analysis, patients were categorized into four distinct groups (Table 1).
The study categorized 123 patients into four groups: 52 with endometrial cancer, 30 with normal endometrium, 20 with endometrial polyps, and 17 with submucosal leiomyomas.
The study included 67 control patients with benign pathologies (30 normal endometrium, 17 submucosal leiomyomas, 20 endometrial polyps) and 52 examined patients with endometrial cancer. The average age was 53 years for controls and 58 years for the cancer group.
Patients in each subgroup were categorized by hormonal status and BMI. Body Mass Index was determined by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters.
Within the endometrial cancer cohort, 47 individuals had endometrioid carcinoma, while 5 presented with non-endometrioid endometrial carcinoma.
The 52 endometrial cancer patients were categorized by tumor grade: 18 were G1, 24 were G2, and 10 were G3. Additionally, clinical staging divided them into two groups: 42 patients had FIGO stages I or II, while 10 had FIGO stages III or IV.
For protein analysis, 5 ml of blood was drawn from each patient during routine preoperative tests, then centrifuged. The resulting serum was immediately frozen and stored at -70°C. Figure 1 illustrates the NGAL-BMI relationship across all patients.
Serum levels of various biomarkers were assessed using specific methodologies. For lipocalin-2 (NGAL), concentrations were measured via fluorescent bead-based immunoassays, specifically the Human Cardiovascular Disease (CVD) Magnetic Bead Panel 2 from Merck Millipore, utilizing Luminex Corporation technology. CA125 levels were determined with the ARCHITECT CA 125 II assay from Abbott Diagnostics, which has an analytical sensitivity of 1.0 U/mL. HE4 serum concentrations were quantified using the Elecsys ECLIA assay from Roche Diagnostics Ltd, performed on a cobas e 601 analyzer, with a detection limit of 15.0 pmol/L. Statistical analysis was conducted using STATISTICA 9.1 PL software. Due to non-normal data distributions, descriptive statistics included median values, alongside minimum, maximum, and mean. Non-parametric tests, such as Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc for three-group comparisons and Mann-Whitney’s U-test for two-group comparisons, were employed to evaluate variable distribution compatibility.

ROC curves were generated for chosen groups, and the area under these curves (AUC) was determined using DeLong's nonparametric method, including 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined by a p-value below 0.05.
A logit model was employed, defining 'p' as the probability of a specific event and 'x' as its chance. Model parameters were estimated using either generalized largest squares or maximum likelihood methods. For interpretation, the odds ratio, representing the likelihood of an event occurring versus not occurring, was utilized. Model accuracy was determined by counting both correctly and incorrectly classified cases. Figure 2 illustrates the differences in NGAL medians across groups.
The study adhered to ethical standards, including the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its subsequent revisions. Approval was granted by the Pomeranian University of Medicine in Szczecin's Bioethics Committee on October 13, 2012, under resolution KB-0012/77/12.
Each patient involved in the study gave conscious, written consent. This consent was also provided by their physician. Before signing, patients carefully reviewed the information and had the opportunity to ask questions.

However, the Kruskal-Wallis test indicated no age distribution differences among the study groups, nor were there significant BMI variations between specific patient groups.

To assess the diagnostic utility of lipocalin-2, HE4, and CA125, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and their respective areas under the curve (AUC) were computed. Lipocalin-2 demonstrated an AUC of 0.81 (p<0.00001) for distinguishing endometrial cancer from other benign endometrial alterations. When specifically differentiating endometrial cancer from normal endometrium, lipocalin-2's AUC was 0.76 (p=0.00003). For distinguishing endometrial cancer from leiomyomas, lipocalin-2 yielded an AUC of 0.74 (p=0.0033). In contrast, HE4 and CA125 showed AUCs of 0.74 and 0.53, respectively, for differentiating endometrial cancer from other benign lesions. For distinguishing endometrial cancer from normal endometrium, their AUCs were 0.80 (p=0.0004) and 0.61 (p=0.05). When comparing endometrial cancer patients to those with leiomyomas, HE4 and CA125 had AUCs of 0.76 (p=0.01) and 0.66 (p=0.04).
Table 4 presents the sensitivity and specificity of lipocalin-2, HE4, and CA125, stratified by hormonal status. Lipocalin-2 exhibited superior sensitivity across all women (84%), and specifically in premenopausal (82%) and postmenopausal (81%) cohorts, surpassing both HE4 (66%, 64%/67%) and CA125 (52%, 63%, 54%). Conversely, HE4 demonstrated higher specificity than lipocalin-2 and CA125 in two study groups: 93% versus 78% versus 54% for all women, and 90% versus 80% versus 59% for premenopausal women. In postmenopausal women, lipocalin-2's specificity was 87%, slightly exceeding HE4's 85% and CA125's 64%.
A statistically significant correlation was observed between serum lipocalin-2 levels and histopathological tumor differentiation (G3 vs. G1, p=0.003). Patients with advanced clinical staging exhibited significantly elevated mean lipocalin-2 levels (240 ng/mL) compared to those with lower staging (170 ng/mL), with a p-value of 0.0027.
A logistic regression model was developed to assess the relationship between lipocalin-2 levels and FIGO staging. This model demonstrated strong statistical significance (p=0.000001) with an AUC of 0.884 (p=0.000003), indicating that elevated lipocalin-2 concentrations are associated with a 3.66 times greater likelihood of advanced disease. Furthermore, a separate grading model, also statistically significant (p=0.00024) with an AUC of 0.764 (p=0.00016), showed that each unit increase in serum lipocalin-2 raises the risk of less differentiated endometrial cancer by 2.32 times.

Obesity or overweight is linked to 80% of endometrial cancer cases. Adipose tissue functions as an endocrine organ, releasing adipokines that regulate various bodily processes. Lipocalin-2 contributes to insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, and chronic inflammation, with this inflammatory process stimulating proinflammatory cytokine production.
Obese and overweight individuals exhibit elevated lipocalin-2 levels, consistent with existing literature. This obesity-related increase disrupts paracrine signaling, causing high adipocytokine concentrations. These interactions impair adipose tissue's immune function and promote increased cell migration and endothelial damage.
The precise roles of lipocalin-2 within the endometrium remain largely unexplored. Research by Lin et al. on endometrial cancer cell lines indicated that after 24 hours of incubation, lipocalin-2 influenced cell proliferation, reduced apoptosis, and stimulated the release of cytokines, specifically interleukins 6 and 8. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that lipocalin-2 secretion is influenced by sex hormones, particularly estrogens, which are crucial for the cyclical proliferation and apoptosis in the endometrium of menstruating women. Elevated lipocalin-2 levels are also associated with increased serum concentrations of glucocorticoids and estrogens.
We observed elevated lipocalin-2 levels in individuals with leiomyomas when contrasted with those having normal endometrium. This increase in lipocalin-2 might be linked to higher estrogen concentrations in leiomyoma patients. Lipocalin-2 functions as an acute phase protein. Endometrial cancer incidence peaks during perimenopause, a period characterized by relative hyperestrogenism, which in turn boosts lipocalin-2 expression. Furthermore, adipose tissue produces lipocalin-2, meaning higher BMI correlates with increased serum lipocalin-2. More fat tissue leads to greater conversion of adrenal androstenedione into estrone, the primary estrogen in postmenopausal women.
Given lipocalin-2's carcinogenic properties and follicular tissue production, we aimed to assess its diagnostic utility as an endometrial cancer marker.
Our research established a serum lipocalin-2 threshold of 160 ng/ml for distinguishing benign endometrial changes from endometrial cancer. This protein demonstrated superior sensitivity compared to both HE4 and CA125 across the entire patient cohort, irrespective of hormonal status. While HE4 exhibited considerably higher sensitivity and specificity than CA125, aligning with findings by Liu et al., who also noted HE4's greater diagnostic utility for early endometrial cancer detection regardless of patient age or hormonal state.
Our research yielded an AUC of 0.53 for CA125 and 0.74 for HE4, aligning with Angioli et al.'s findings of 0.54 and 0.86, respectively. Angioli's review of five studies consistently showed significantly elevated HE4 levels in endometrial cancer patients compared to controls. HE4's ROC and AUC values, ranging from 0.78 to 0.88, consistently met the criteria for an effective diagnostic marker. Furthermore, three separate studies confirmed statistically significant differences in HE4 serum concentrations between control subjects and those with Stage I endometrial cancer.
Angioli and Plotti et al. proposed the development of REM or refined REM B algorithms, utilizing HE4 levels and endometrial thickness, to categorize patients into low or high endometrial cancer risk groups. This recommendation stems from their research, which also highlighted that the CA125 marker's serum levels are elevated in only 10% of patients with early-stage (I and II) endometrial cancer.
While HE4 generally exhibited superior specificity compared to lipocalin-2 in the overall patient cohort and among premenopausal individuals, lipocalin-2 demonstrated greater specificity (87%) than HE4 (85%) specifically in postmenopausal women. Our investigation established a cut-off value of 64.1 pmol/l for HE4 to distinguish between benign endometrial lesions and carcinomas, which is marginally higher than the 58.08 pmol/l reported by Gasiorowska et al. Conversely, the CA125 cut-off in our study was set at 28.6 U/ml, contrasting with the 20 U/ml suggested by Patsner and Yim for early-stage endometrial carcinoma. It's worth noting that Hu et al. highlighted HE4's higher sensitivity over CA125, though they expressed reservations about the overall diagnostic efficacy of both markers.
Elevated lipocalin-2 expression in endometrial cancer is linked to increased tumor aggressiveness and a worse prognosis. This elevated expression is also observed in endometrial cancers when compared to atypical or simple endometrial hypertrophy. While HE4 levels exceeding 70 pmol/l at diagnosis showed 67% sensitivity and 53% specificity for predicting endometrial cancer recurrences, lipocalin-2 concentration was also investigated in relation to tumor size, advancement, and the presence of metastases.
Research by Miyamoto et al. indicated that elevated levels of lipocalin-2 and its receptor SLC22A17 predict a worse outcome for endometrial cancer patients.
Our research indicates a link between elevated lipocalin-2 serum levels and more advanced clinical staging of endometrial cancer. This aligns with findings in other cancer types, where lipocalin-2's role has also been investigated. For instance, increased lipocalin-2 expression has been observed to coincide with disease progression in breast cancer and chronic myeloid leukemia. Furthermore, the growth of tumor mass has been associated with rising lipocalin-2 expression. In pancreatic cancer, lipocalin-2 has even been suggested as a potential marker for early detection. The elevated expression of lipocalin-2 is linked to higher levels of IL-8, which drives increased cell proliferation, hinders apoptosis, and promotes cell migration. While high lipocalin-2 levels generally predict a poorer prognosis in esophageal and stomach cancers, some studies suggest a protective role for lipocalin-2 in thyroid and renal cancers.
Given its high sensitivity and specificity, similar to HE4, lipocalin-2 appears promising for diagnosing endometrial cancer.
Lipocalin-2, a protein with both metabolic and proliferative characteristics, contributes to endometrial cancer development in multiple ways. Its proliferative actions are exerted directly and indirectly, partly through interactions with extracellular matrix metalloproteinases.
Our findings confirm a link between BMI and lipocalin-2 levels, aligning with existing literature. While lipocalin-2's sensitivity for distinguishing benign endometrial changes from cancer might be slightly reduced in obese individuals, it nonetheless maintains its effectiveness as a diagnostic marker.
Further research with a larger patient cohort is needed to definitively determine if serum lipocalin-2 levels can identify individuals at high risk for endometrial cancer, thereby guiding the need for endometrial sampling.
Initial findings suggest lipocalin-2, found at elevated levels in endometrial cancer patients, could be a valuable diagnostic marker for uterine body cancers.
