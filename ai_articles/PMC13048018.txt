For patients requiring endovascular repair of common iliac artery aneurysms (CIAA), preserving internal iliac artery (IIA) blood flow presents a significant challenge. Standard iliac branched devices (IBDs) necessitate a minimum common iliac artery (CIA) length of 5 cm, a requirement often unmet in Asian populations due to shorter anatomical dimensions. This study presents the medium-term outcomes of a novel, tailor-made IBD approach for patients with short CIAs, both with and without concomitant abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA). The methodology involved modifying a standard AAA stent-graft iliac limb. A 6-mm fenestration was created at a specific location along the CIA length from the proximal end. To enhance durability and visibility, the fenestration edge was reinforced with the tip of a radiopaque wire. This modified limb was then reloaded into a delivery sheath, forming the tailor-made IBD. Following insertion via the femoral artery, the device was deployed, allowing cannulation of the ipsilateral IIA through the fenestration. A bridging stent-graft, either balloon-expandable or self-expandable, was then deployed within the IIA. Between March 2013 and March 2017, ten patients underwent this procedure. Technical success was achieved in all cases. One patient experienced a fatal systemic thromboembolism two days post-operation. Over the follow-up period, all bridging stent-grafts remained patent, with the exception of one occlusion at one year. The conclusion is that this tailor-made IBD offers a straightforward and effective alternative for maintaining IIA perfusion in patients with short CIAs, irrespective of the presence of AAA.

Endovascular repair is a treatment for common iliac artery aneurysms (CIAA). While embolizing the internal iliac artery (IIA) can aid distal fixation, it risks complications like pelvic ischemia, buttock claudication, sexual impotence, and colonic ischemia. Preserving IIA flow is therefore desirable.
To extend the distal landing zone and preserve internal iliac artery (IIA) flow, branched iliac stent grafting is an option. However, standard iliac branched devices (IBD) necessitate a common iliac artery (CIA) segment of at least 5 cm for implantation, a length often unsuitable for Asian patients. Since 2013, our institution has modified commercially available endografts to create tailor-made IBDs. This approach has been used to treat patients with common iliac artery aneurysms (CIAA) or type Ib endoleaks following prior endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR), thereby maintaining IIA perfusion.
This study reports medium-term outcomes of a tailor-made iliac branched device for treating common iliac artery aneurysms, with or without abdominal aortic aneurysms.
Between March 2013 and March 2017, a retrospective review was conducted on 10 patients who received tailor-made iliac branched devices (IBDs). These patients had either short common iliac artery aneurysms (CIAA) or type Ib endoleaks. The study, approved by the institutional review board with waived informed consent due to its retrospective nature, included patients with unilateral or bilateral CIAA (with or without abdominal aortic aneurysm), isolated CIAA, or type Ib endoleaks from prior endovascular aneurysm repair.
Patients underwent CT scans with 3-mm slices. DICOM data were analyzed using the 3mensio vascular workstation. If a patient's common iliac artery (CIA) measured less than 50 mm, precluding commercial iliac branched device (IBD) use, a tailor-made IBD was then designed for administration.
To create the tailor-made iliac branched device (IBD), an iliac limb from an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) stent-graft was selected based on appropriate diameter and length. This limb was then modified. Initially, the stent-graft was removed from its delivery system. A 6-mm fenestration was created by cauterization, positioned at a specific length from the proximal end, approximately 5 to 10 mm shorter than the common iliac artery (CIA) length. To reinforce the fenestration's edge, preventing fabric tearing and aiding visualization under fluoroscopy, the soft, radiopaque tip of a 0.014-inch wire was applied. Following this, the modified iliac limb was reloaded into the introducer sheath, facilitated by a silk tape. The tailor-made IBD was then introduced through a chosen femoral artery. Its placement occurred either before or after the main body stent-graft deployment. The device was carefully oriented and partially deployed, ensuring the fenestration was positioned directly above the internal iliac artery (IIA). Next, a 7 French, 90-cm long sheath was inserted into the tailor-made IBD via a left brachial artery access, guided by a stiff wire. Through this long sheath and the fenestration, the IIA was catheterized. A bridging endograft, either a balloon-expandable (e.g., Advanta V12, Lifestream) or self-expandable (e.g., Viabahn) covered stent of suitable dimensions, was then deployed. This bridging stent was positioned to protrude at least 1 cm into the fenestration. Subsequently, the rest of the tailor-made IBD was fully deployed. To prevent downward migration, the proximal section of the bridging covered stent within the IBD was further expanded using a 12-mm balloon. If needed, a contralateral leg stent-graft was employed to connect the main body stent-graft to the tailor-made IBD.
Technical success meant the tailor-made IBD and bridging stent-graft were successfully deployed, preserving IIA flow without immediate endoleak. Primary outcomes included technical success, bridging stent-graft patency, aneurysm-related death, and endoleaks.
Patients were prescribed daily clopidogrel for at least one year if tolerated. Postoperative CT scans were conducted at six and twelve months, followed by annual assessments.

Performing EVAR for AAA with unilateral or bilateral CIAA presents challenges with distal fixation. While some techniques involve occluding the internal iliac artery (IIA), this can lead to complications like buttock claudication and ischemia. Therefore, preserving IIA blood flow is preferred. Various methods exist for this, including IIA bypass, transposition, trifurcated endografts, sandwich techniques, and crossover chimney techniques.
While Iliac Branched Devices (IBDs) offer a promising endovascular approach for preserving internal iliac artery (IIA) flow with good technical success and medium-term results, their application is frequently restricted by anatomical limitations. Specifically, the required common iliac artery (CIA) length for commercially available IBDs, typically 5 cm or sometimes 4 cm, excludes a significant number of patients. Studies indicate that only about 35% of common iliac artery aneurysm repairs would be suitable for these standard devices. This is particularly relevant for Asian populations, where the average CIA length is considerably shorter, often ranging from 25 to 30 mm. Consequently, these anatomical constraints necessitate the development of customized, tailor-made solutions.
The development of our tailor-made iliac branched device (IBD) drew inspiration from existing fenestration endograft designs. This approach offers greater cost-effectiveness compared to other methods, primarily due to reduced requirements for additional devices and preparation time. For all patients in this study, we utilized iliac limbs from the Endurant II stent-graft (Medtronic Cardiovascular, CA, USA), selected for their broad availability in various sizes and lengths. Several technical considerations are important. Thorough preprocedural planning, including CT angiography with centerline of flow analysis to accurately measure the abdominal aorta and iliac arteries, is critical. Understanding the specific anatomical characteristics of the aneurysm and employing appropriate technical strategies are essential. In cases where the iliac artery pathway is not significantly angulated or tortuous, cannulating the internal iliac artery (IIA) and delivering the bridging endograft via contralateral femoral access is achievable. However, brachial access for IIA bridging stent-graft deployment should be avoided in patients with severe thoracic aortic atherosclerosis. Furthermore, when significant mural thrombus is present in the common iliac artery (CIA), the fenestration should be strategically oriented away from the thrombus to facilitate easier wiring and catheterization.
This off-label technique requires further follow-up to confirm its long-term efficacy.
Treating common iliac artery aneurysms, with or without abdominal aortic aneurysms, presents challenges for endovascular aneurysm repair due to difficulties in achieving secure distal fixation. While commercial iliac branched devices offer an attractive option for preserving internal iliac artery flow, their use is often restricted by anatomical limitations. Our custom-made iliac branched device overcomes these length-related device constraints, offering benefits in preparation, delivery, and cost-effectiveness. This method shows promise, though its long-term effectiveness requires further study.
