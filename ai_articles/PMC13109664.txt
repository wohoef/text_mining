This article explores how migrants utilize social media to foster a sense of "co-presence" with distant loved ones. Based on empirical findings, it contributes to ongoing discussions regarding social media's influence on emotional experiences. The research involved short-term ethnographic study of daily social media habits among second-generation Turkish-Dutch individuals. These participants, raised in the Netherlands, later relocated to Istanbul as adults. The article specifically examines transnational family intimacy within this migratory context, serving as a detailed case study. This allows for an understanding of how social media platforms and mobile devices facilitate emotional togetherness despite prolonged geographical separation. The author demonstrates that social media offers not only the ambient, rapid, background communication previously highlighted in literature, but also more direct, immersive, and conversational communication methods. The central argument posits that individuals' deliberate transitions between these varied social media communication modes generate their experiences of transnational emotional intimacy. The concept of "careful co-presence" is introduced, illustrating how social media practices that cultivate intimacy involve both selective discernment and emotional attentiveness. This perspective is grounded in existing scholarship that advocates for practice-based approaches to comprehending the mediation of emotion through digital media.

My research into the transnational family lives of second-generation Turkish-Dutch young adults revealed a profound shift in daily experiences, largely attributable to technological advancements. Interviewees, like Miriyam, offered compelling insights into these changes. She recounted her childhood migration from Turkey to the Netherlands, a journey undertaken by car, laden with possessions. Early family visits back to Turkey similarly involved long car trips, as air travel was then cost-prohibitive. Miriyam vividly described the emotional intensity of these past family interactions: arrivals were communal celebrations, while departures were marked by tears, embraces, and traditional blessings under the Koran. She explained the depth of these emotions by noting the extended periods—months or even years—between visits. However, Miriyam contrasted this with the present, observing that the widespread availability of tools like Facetime and affordable flights has diminished the need for such dramatic emotional displays. Individuals like Miriyam, who grew up within transnational families, were uniquely positioned to articulate how social media applications have redefined emotional closeness and facilitated the maintenance of family bonds across vast distances. This evolution prompts critical questions for scholars of transnational intimacy and care, particularly concerning the transformations and continuities in emotional relationships brought about by the proliferation of social media.
The academic discourse surrounding digital media and transnational mobility increasingly examines how individuals communicate and experience emotions across distances. This growing body of literature, drawing from diverse disciplinary perspectives, explores various migration phenomena. A central theme within this research is how migrants leverage digital media to manage the emotional challenges of separation from loved ones in their home countries. This inquiry often centers on "co-presence," defined as the emotional proximity or feeling of closeness experienced with physically distant individuals. While transnational emotional relationships have historically been mediated through technologies like letter writing, the widespread integration of digital media, particularly Web 2.0, into daily life presents new challenges for understanding its role in fostering intimate togetherness. Scholars such as Boccagni and Baldassar (2015) highlight that "new media are transforming and creating new forms of co-presence and sociality that facilitate the constitution and maintenance of emotional bonds across distance and over time" (p. 75). Longitudinal studies further indicate that the capacity for individuals to feel co-present has expanded with the advent of new technologies enabling synchronous communication (Baldassar, Nedelcu, Merla, & Wilding, 2016). Consequently, web-based communications appear to be reshaping how migrants maintain emotional connections with loved ones abroad. This evolving landscape necessitates a deeper investigation into the specific mechanisms and practices through which digital platforms influence the emotional dimensions of transnational relationships.
Researchers have explored various forms of digitally facilitated co-presence. Madianou (2016) introduced "ambient co-presence" to describe the continuous sense of connection transnational families maintain through pervasive digital connectivity, a state she termed the "'always on' culture of ubiquitous connectivity" (p. 198). Similarly, Nedelcu and Wyss (2016) coined "ordinary co-presence" to characterize how transnationally mediated communication reflects typical family dynamics and cosmopolitan daily life. These and other scholars, including Diminescu (2008) and Wilding (2006), have drawn inspiration from Christian Licoppe's concept of "connected presence." Licoppe (2004) posited that evolving communication technologies, such as mobile calls, texts, and emails, blur the lines between absence and presence (p. 136). Proponents of Licoppe's "connected" communication in the context of transnational migrant intimacy often highlight how digital media, especially social media platforms, enable continuous, fleeting, and readily available communication. For example, Diminescu (2008) argued that these "connected" modes of maintaining relationships have shifted presence from a physical state to an affective experience, thereby "revolutionizing" migration practices and mobility experiences. However, Licoppe's original framework acknowledges the coexistence of both traditional "conversational" communication and newer "connected" media repertoires, despite their distinct characteristics. Moreover, Licoppe (2004) concluded that the brief communicative exchanges inherent in "connected" communication do not inherently cultivate strong intimate relationships. Instead, he suggested that achieving deep intimacy necessitates a deliberate and intensified communication effort, fostering "a common world of signification" (p. 154). In essence, constant digital interaction does not automatically equate to intimacy, just as periods of silence do not invariably signal emotional detachment. This nuanced understanding underscores the complexity of digital mediation in fostering emotional connections across distances.
The emotional nuances of distant family connections, particularly in transnational contexts, are more intricate than often assumed, extending beyond the simple notion that constant communication fosters closeness. Research in migration studies has established that care and intimacy in transnational families endure across geographical divides and human movement (Baldassar & Merla, 2013). However, the specific ways social media influence the emotional aspects of these geographically dispersed relationships remain less understood. For example, Madianou (2016) highlights that intense transnational co-presence can have drawbacks, such as moral surveillance, and notes that digital platforms can deliberately introduce distance into intimate relationships. Expanding on this, Madianou and Miller (2012) observe instances where transnational familial affection is more easily conveyed through digital media than in person, proposing that "mediation can potentially create the 'ideal distance' necessary for a relationship to flourish" (p. 146). Similarly, Nedelcu and Wyss (2016) introduce the concept of "ordinary co-presence" to explore how modern family life inherently involves distance and difference. They explicitly challenge the assumption that communicative gaps or silences necessarily signify emotional detachment. This perspective implies that transnational intimate relationships might, to some degree, depend on periods of silence, omissions, and absence, employing diverse communication strategies and fulfilling various needs. Therefore, the experience of transnational family intimacy is mediated in ways that are more complex than a straightforward focus on how continuous, synchronous communication alone bridges physical distance to create feelings of closeness.
This article examines how transnational migrants experience "co-presence" with loved ones, focusing on the emotional dimensions mediated by social media in long-distance, intimate relationships. The study proposes a concept called "careful co-presence," which describes a deliberate and emotionally formative approach to social media use. This "careful" usage encompasses both the emotional care inherent in transnational family relationships, as explored in existing scholarship (Baldassar & Merla, 2013), and a discerning selection of media practices for maintaining connections, acknowledging the constraints of time and attention in the digital era (Terranova, 2012). The framework of careful co-presence is informed by Bareither's (forthcoming) concept of "emotional affordances," which, from a practice-oriented, ethnographic perspective rooted in the anthropology of emotions, posits that digital media transform emotional experiences by influencing the practices through which emotions are expressed. This article demonstrates how the idea of careful practices for fostering transnational intimacy through social media emerged from empirical data. This study investigates how social media facilitates "co-presence" and emotional closeness among a specific demographic: second-generation Turkish-Dutch migrants. These individuals, having spent their formative years in the Netherlands, chose to relocate to Istanbul, Turkey, as adults. This group forms a key part of a broader research initiative examining digital communication among migrants across Europe. While research on the Turkish-Dutch second generation exists, studies on their emotional experiences, particularly concerning transnational relationships or return migration to Turkey, are scarce. Existing scholarship predominantly focuses on their cultural integration, education, and labor market participation within the Netherlands. This emphasis reflects the historical ties between the two nations, stemming from the 1970s when Turkish migrants constituted the largest foreign labor force in the Netherlands. Notably, this group now exhibits the highest rate of "return" migration from the Netherlands, signifying a reversal in the historical migration flow, with more individuals emigrating to Turkey than immigrating from it. The descendants of these initial migrants, referred to as second- and third-generation Turkish-Dutch individuals, now represent over half of the total Turkish-Dutch population. As they reach adulthood, their experiences offer unique insights into evolving transnational dynamics. This research specifically addresses the gap in understanding how social media shapes emotional connections for this particular group, who navigate complex identities and relationships across geographical and cultural boundaries. By focusing on their use of social media to maintain emotional proximity despite physical distance, the study aims to shed light on the nuanced ways digital platforms mediate feelings of togetherness in a transnational context.
Second-generation Turkish-Dutch individuals have recently garnered considerable attention in the Netherlands from academics, policymakers, and media outlets. This interest stems from their involvement in the economy and education, as well as debates surrounding their perceived allegiances to both the Netherlands and Turkey. Over the last five years, a notable trend has emerged: a significant number of young Turkish-Dutch individuals from this generation have chosen to emigrate from the Netherlands to establish lives in Turkey. While this group represents a minority within the broader second-generation population, they form the largest absolute number and highest proportion of second-generation emigrants from the Netherlands. For the participants in this study, several factors primarily motivated this relocation. These included the pursuit of professional opportunities in Turkey, where their bilingual and bicultural backgrounds were considered advantageous, the appeal of a cosmopolitan urban lifestyle in Istanbul, and/or prior experiences of discrimination within the Dutch job market. However, some who initially moved with optimism about Turkey's economic growth and the potential for an opening of its creative and civic society became increasingly disillusioned. Consequently, at the time of this research, a number of these respondents were contemplating a return to the Netherlands.
This article draws on four months of short-term ethnographic fieldwork conducted in Istanbul during 2017. The research investigated the daily social media and mobile device practices of second-generation Turkish-Dutch migrants. The methodology involved in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 15 respondents residing in Istanbul, some of whom were interviewed multiple times. Additionally, four informal interviews were conducted with second-generation Turkish-Dutch individuals living in Amsterdam, bringing the total interviewee count to 19. Participants were recruited through a combination of snowball sampling, initiated via Turkish-Dutch peer contacts in Istanbul, and theoretical sampling for the remaining respondents. All interviewees were skilled, upwardly mobile individuals from middle-class backgrounds, aged between their twenties and forties, with 11 men and 8 women. These individuals had either frequently visited Istanbul as adults or had previously relocated there before contemplating a return to the Netherlands. The ethnographic approach also included observing respondents in their daily lives, attending social gatherings, conducting home visits, and participating in neighborhood walks within their Istanbul residential areas.
Sinan, a man in his early thirties, decided to relocate to Istanbul, Turkey, after spending his formative years in the Netherlands. His parents, originally from Bodrum, Turkey, had moved to the Netherlands before his birth, and he had grown up there, attending school and making regular summer visits to Bodrum and Istanbul for family and tourism. This move to Istanbul was a significant personal and professional decision for Sinan, mirroring the aspirations of many other respondents in the study. A key motivation was to achieve greater independence from his immediate family and his previous environment in The Hague. Sinan had envisioned a long-term future in Istanbul, having moved there several years prior to the research. He observed that Instagram enjoyed greater popularity in Istanbul compared to the Netherlands, an observation corroborated by the researcher. Despite this, Sinan’s activity on the platform was limited. He explained that his mother’s presence on both Instagram and Facebook, which she had discovered at some point, influenced his sharing habits. His parents held different expectations for his lifestyle, making him reluctant to post content, such as photos of himself with a beer, that might displease them. He noted that if he were to share such images, his parents’ disapproval would be immediate, leading to a lack of suitable content for frequent posting, given his regular visits to bars. Initially, his parents had enjoyed his frequent photo updates. However, a turning point came when they saw a photo featuring girls, which they disliked, as it revealed aspects of his life they disapproved of. This led to a conflict, culminating in Sinan blocking his parents on Facebook. While he hadn't yet taken the same step on Instagram, he consciously reduced his posting frequency there. He attributed his parents’ strong reactions to their more religious background, imagining their commentary: “look, look what he’s doing there. And then he even went and shared it!” This anecdote highlights the complex interplay between social media use, personal autonomy, and familial expectations within transnational contexts. Sinan's careful management of his online presence demonstrates a strategic negotiation of emotional boundaries and cultural norms, aiming to balance his desire for independence with his family's values. His experience underscores how digital platforms become sites for navigating evolving relationships and identity formation for second-generation migrants.
The author's interest was piqued by Sinan's decision to first connect with, then disconnect from, his parents on Facebook, prompting an investigation into his management of social media interactions with them. This was particularly relevant given that other participants typically utilized platforms like Facebook and Instagram for peer communication. Sinan, however, opted to restrict parental contact to WhatsApp, a direct messaging application. This preference for direct, one-to-one communication platforms, such as WhatsApp and Facetime, over feed-scrolling social media, was common among respondents for maintaining intimate, long-distance family relationships. Many participants, in fact, did not even categorize WhatsApp as a social media platform. Sinan's rationale for this choice stemmed from the enhanced control direct messaging offered over shared content, a feature lacking in Instagram or Facebook. This allowed him to carefully curate the information his parents received about his life in Istanbul. An illustrative example of this ongoing negotiation was Sinan's eventual update of his WhatsApp profile picture to one depicting him with a beer and a smartphone. When questioned about this seemingly provocative choice, given past parental disapproval, Sinan stated, "I don’t let it bother me as much as it used to." This shift was interpreted as Sinan's evolving approach to living his desired lifestyle in Istanbul, involving a dynamic process of selectively revealing and concealing aspects of his life from his parents. WhatsApp facilitated this careful boundary management due to its inherent control mechanisms, which contrasted sharply with the asynchronous nature and broader, less defined privacy settings of traditional social media feeds. The platform's ability to offer precise control over communication content and audience proved crucial for Sinan in navigating the complexities of maintaining family ties while asserting personal independence across geographical and social divides. This highlights how individuals strategically leverage different communication tools to shape their emotional proximity and distance within transnational family contexts, adapting their practices over time as their personal circumstances and relationships evolve.
Sinan, who spent 28 years in The Hague’s Schilderswijk, a historically working-class neighborhood known for urban decay and its migrant population, expressed a long-held desire to move to Istanbul. He felt constrained by his parents' expectation that he remain at home until marriage, stating, "I lived with my parents for a long time. I kind of just couldn’t get away." This personal frustration, coupled with a lifelong affection for Istanbul—having visited the city 17 or 18 times prior to his relocation—motivated his move. His decision to leave the Netherlands was driven by a yearning for independence and a dream of living in Istanbul, a city he deeply admired. This move allowed him to escape the confines of his upbringing and pursue a new chapter in a place he loved.
Sinan’s decision to move to Istanbul was influenced by a complex interplay of personal circumstances and a long-standing emotional connection to the city. Growing up in The Hague, he had been involved in a drug-using peer group, and he worried about the implications of moving out independently without severing those ties. Furthermore, his experiences with ethnic profiling by the police in The Hague fostered a sense of unease and caution when navigating his home city. In contrast, Istanbul held a deep emotional significance for Sinan, cultivated through childhood summer visits with his family. This connection was further solidified by his parents' prior purchase of an apartment in the city, which had housed extended family members. When this apartment became available, Sinan seized the opportunity, purchasing a ticket to explore life in this new yet familiar environment. His narrative highlights a negotiation between distinct social spheres: the challenging but familiar neighborhood life in The Hague, the conservative Turkish family environment in the Schilderswijk, and the vibrant Istanbul nightlife he embraced. This transition across diverse social worlds necessitated a deliberate approach to managing emotional connections and disconnections through his social media use, carefully balancing proximity and distance with the people, places, and social dynamics he had left behind.
Traditional migration studies often viewed physical proximity as the ideal for experiencing co-presence in transnational family relationships, considering mediated interactions as inherently less significant (Baldassar et al., 2016). However, this perspective has been challenged. Madianou (2016), for instance, introduced the concept of "ambient co-presence," arguing that digital communications, even when indirect and operating in the background of daily life, are equally meaningful. She posited that ambient co-presence fosters a sense of belonging by immersing individuals in shared emotional and moral environments. The case of Sinan, a migrant, illustrates this evolving understanding. His migration created physical distance from his family, which he intentionally leveraged to establish a new life away from the social pressures of his conservative upbringing and peer group. Initially, his mother's access to his social media, specifically Instagram, created emotional tension, extending pre-existing family dynamics into his new Istanbul life. This highlights that distance itself isn't always the primary cause of strain in long-distance family relationships, a point also acknowledged by Baldassar (2016), referencing Madianou and Miller's (2012) work. Sinan's strategic use of communication platforms, particularly his choice to share specific aspects of his life with his parents via WhatsApp, demonstrates a deliberate management of both physical and emotional distance. His practices involved carefully navigating different platforms to control the flow and nature of information shared. This careful balancing act, involving conscious shifts between communication modes, allowed him to maintain emotional proximity while also asserting a degree of independence. This example underscores how migrants actively shape their emotional experiences within transnational relationships through discerning social media use, moving beyond a simple dichotomy of physical presence versus mediated absence.
While social media is often characterized by its "always-on," ambient, and rapid-paced nature, influencing how emotions are conceptualized in digital communication (Madianou, 2016; Papacharissi, 2016; Wilding, 2006), this study reveals a more nuanced reality. Typically, the emotional affordances of social media are understood as fostering a "lightweight awareness of connection" (Madianou, 2016), diverging from the direct, immersive interaction of traditional phone calls. Participants in this research acknowledged that these pervasive social media features shaped their communication of news and significant life events with family. However, they also reported that these quick, indirect, and brief communication methods often hindered intimacy and proved insufficient for sustaining deep emotional bonds. In an environment saturated with social media and ambient communication, direct communication gained renewed importance for individuals. For instance, Umut, a young professional in Istanbul, exclusively used Facetime and Skype for video calls with his family in the Netherlands. Notably, he even resorted to letter writing to communicate with a former girlfriend in the Netherlands, explaining its personal nature and the opportunity for more thoughtful expression. He emphasized that unlike quick messages, letter writing allowed for deeper reflection, making it feel more romantic and nostalgic. This preference for direct communication, whether through voice calls, video calls, or even letters, appeared to serve as a crucial counterpoint to the fleeting nature of other social media interactions. Most respondents regularly used WhatsApp or similar voice calling applications to connect with parents, often replacing conventional telephony. They utilized WhatsApp for both voice/video calls and texting, underscoring the continued significance of phone calls. Some even assisted their parents in transitioning to internet-based calls via WhatsApp, with long-distance communication often being the primary reason parents adopted these apps. Huseyn, who moved to Istanbul in 2012, noted that his mother acquired a smartphone and downloaded apps like WhatsApp and Facetime specifically to stay in touch with him, highlighting the timely evolution of such technologies in facilitating affordable long-distance contact. This pattern suggests that while feed-scrolling platforms like Facebook and Instagram are prevalent, direct communication channels are strategically employed to control shared content and maintain distinct social boundaries. This aligns with discussions around "context collapse" (Marwick & boyd, 2011), where users actively manage their audience. However, the choice of direct, one-to-one communication, particularly for emotionally intimate relationships, goes beyond merely separating social worlds. It reveals how calling and direct messaging apps augment older communication forms like phone calls and letter writing, emphasizing emotional dynamics over generic platform capabilities or local cultural norms. This highlights the complex interplay between media affordances and the emotional needs of transnational relationships.
The transient nature of many social media interactions highlights the enduring significance of direct communication, exemplified by Umut's nostalgic wish for a girlfriend to whom he could write letters. This preference for directness, whether through voice calls, video calls, or even traditional correspondence, was a recurring theme among respondents. Many maintained contact with their parents in the Netherlands via WhatsApp, which had largely supplanted conventional phone services. They utilized WhatsApp for both voice and video calls, alongside texting, underscoring the continued importance of verbal communication. Some even assisted their parents in transitioning to WhatsApp calls, enabling them to leverage internet telephony. For some parents, these long-distance calls were the sole motivation for adopting such applications. Huseyn, for instance, a young man who relocated from the Netherlands to Istanbul for a banking position, recounted his mother's experience. He noted that social media usage in 2012, when he moved, was less prevalent than it is today. His mother's immediate response to his move was to acquire a smartphone and download communication apps like WhatsApp, FaceTime, and Viber, specifically to stay connected with him. Huseyn considered this timing fortunate, as an earlier move, perhaps in 2005 or 2007, would have resulted in substantial phone bills. The accessibility provided by these applications made maintaining contact considerably easier. This illustrates how the evolution of communication technologies directly influenced the ability of transnational families to sustain close relationships, with direct, often synchronous, communication methods proving particularly vital in bridging geographical distances.
The use of direct communication methods, such as WhatsApp or other voice calling applications, was a prevalent practice among individuals for regular contact with their parents. This contrasted with their use of brief WhatsApp messages for daily interactions with local acquaintances. This preference for direct communication, particularly in the context of feed-scrolling social media platforms like Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter, allowed these young people to manage the content they shared with their parents. As exemplified by Sinan's situation, this capability facilitated the creation of distinct social spheres. This phenomenon connects with scholarly discussions surrounding "context collapse" (Marwick & boyd, 2011), a concept suggesting that social media platforms inherently merge diverse social contexts, which users then mitigate through "strategic audience management" (p. 128). However, Costa's (2018) digital anthropological research on Facebook use in Mardin, a Turkish border city, offers a different perspective. She argues that platform affordances are not intrinsic properties but rather manifest through media usage within specific social and cultural environments. Costa's findings indicate that the concept of context collapse inadequately explains how her Turkish participants utilized Facebook. These users consistently employed intricate privacy settings to uphold local social norms, thereby maintaining separation between various social circles. In this regard, the choice of direct, one-to-one communication, beyond merely separating social worlds according to cultural norms, highlights the capacity of calling and direct messaging applications to foster emotionally intimate relationships. These applications do not replace older communication forms like phone calls and letter writing but rather emulate and enhance them. The existing discourse on context collapse, when discussing affordances, appears to undervalue the emotional complexities involved in managing relationships through social media. Instead, it tends to prioritize either local cultural norms governing social interactions or the general functionalities of platforms. This oversight suggests a need for a more nuanced understanding that integrates the emotional dimensions of communication. The subsequent section will further explore the emotional affordances of these direct communication practices, examining how messaging applications are integrated into daily routines and specific environments. This deeper analysis will illuminate how individuals navigate the emotional landscape of their relationships within the evolving digital communication sphere, demonstrating that the deliberate selection of communication channels is crucial for maintaining and expressing intimacy across distances, particularly within the framework of transnational family ties. The deliberate choice of direct communication methods, therefore, serves as a powerful tool for emotional regulation and relationship management, offering a controlled environment for sharing and connecting that is often absent in the broader, less private realms of feed-based social media.
In Istanbul, respondents’ daily social media use was often characterized by receiving WhatsApp messages from colleagues, even outside of work hours. This constant connectivity, combined with demanding work schedules, lengthy commutes, and active social lives, left little time for maintaining transnational family connections online, as noted by Nedelcu & Wyss (2016). This intense time pressure influenced how individuals managed their app usage to prevent feeling overwhelmed. Many respondents adopted apps that facilitated a fast-paced, urban lifestyle. For instance, the popular Yemeksepeti food delivery app and the Yandex traffic app were frequently used. These locally-oriented applications saved time by helping users navigate Istanbul’s congested routes, underscoring a preference for convenience and efficiency in their digital interactions. To manage information overload, some individuals proactively deleted certain social media applications to limit screen time. Others opted for a more personalized approach, configuring app notifications to alert them only about topics of significant interest. Huseyn, for example, configured his Dutch news app, NOS, to provide updates exclusively on "global finance, economy, Turkish-Dutch relations, that kind of thing," consciously avoiding other notifications. This deliberate selectivity highlights a careful strategy to prevent digital fatigue. Huseyn’s experience even led to physical strain from excessive smartphone use, resulting in him wearing a wrist brace, a condition he referred to as "telephone hand," illustrating the tangible impact of this digital overload. This demonstrates a conscious effort among respondents to control their digital environment and mitigate the demands of constant connectivity within their busy urban lives.
Respondents actively managed their smartphone use, often reducing engagement with certain social media platforms. Sinan, for instance, deleted the Facebook mobile application to limit daily usage. Similarly, Umut deactivated his Facebook account, preferring Instagram due to its less time-consuming nature, noting that Facebook's extensive updates could lead to hours of engagement. Hakan, a design professional in Istanbul, consciously decreased his Facebook activity, maintaining his account primarily for contacts lacking phone numbers and to preserve stored photos, as his Instagram use was already substantial. Many participants perceived Facebook as a significant time sink. This deliberate management of application usage appears to be a necessity for these skilled, early-career professionals navigating the demands of daily life in Istanbul. These local circumstances lend significance to the practice of dedicating time to more prolonged phone calls and direct communication, free from other digital distractions. Considering the middle-class backgrounds of the respondents, this behavior aligns with recent observations regarding Facebook's evolving role in middle-class user environments. This shift suggests a move towards fewer emotional affordances and more utilitarian functions, such as scheduling and archiving, as described by Sujon, Viney, and Toker-Turnalar (2018). Such patterns illustrate how newer media forms can redefine or enhance the functionalities of older ones, as posited by Bolter and Grusin (2000). Alternatively, it highlights how various platforms, when used concurrently, acquire meaning in relation to each other, forming a "polymedia" environment, a concept explored by Madianou and Miller (2012). This selective engagement with social media underscores a strategic approach to digital interaction, where platforms are chosen and utilized based on their perceived efficiency and their capacity to support specific communication needs within a demanding urban lifestyle. The emphasis on direct, focused communication, therefore, becomes a deliberate choice to foster deeper connections amidst widespread digital distractions.
Umut, like other interviewees, cultivated specific routines for maintaining close transnational family ties. His primary method for connecting with his cousins in the Netherlands involved regular Facetime voice calls from his solitary, furnished rental apartment in Istanbul. These calls typically occurred on weekday evenings, with Umut settled on his living room couch, often with his work and personal smartphones charging nearby. This deliberate setting, away from the city's daytime bustle and distractions, fostered the intimate communication that fleeting mobile messages throughout the day could not. Umut viewed these relationships with his Dutch cousins as a vital escape from the demanding pace of his Istanbul life. Despite infrequent in-person contact, their bond had deepened over two decades, allowing him to discuss "everything." Crucially, he intentionally kept these relationships separate from his social life in Istanbul, valuing this distinction as a key aspect of their transnational appeal. He explained that these interactions allowed him to revert to his pre-migration self, stating, "I still talk the same with them like I talked to them when I was there. Nothing’s changed. So, they’re not a part at all of my social life in Istanbul. For me, they’re a kind of escape from my social life in Istanbul. I go back to basics." This practice of engaging in immersive, undisturbed voice calls with his cousins evoked pleasant memories of shared pasts and family life in the Netherlands, providing an emotional release from the pressures of his current Istanbul existence. The physical distance, rather than hindering intimacy, facilitated a unique form of connection that did not merge with his Istanbul social context. Instead, the focused nature of these calls, free from external distractions, became the very mechanism for mediating this specific emotional intimacy. This highlights how, for Umut, the choice of a less immersive communication method would have failed to provide the necessary emotional depth for this "escape." His approach, therefore, demonstrates how separating different social spheres on social media, in this case through direct, immersive communication, can be a consequence of seeking specific emotional closeness or distance, rather than solely a strategic management of social norms.
For Umut, FaceTime calls with his cousins in the Netherlands serve as a crucial emotional outlet, evoking positive memories of family life and a sense of belonging from his pre-migration past. This digital connection offers a respite from the daily pressures of his life in Istanbul, providing a distinct emotional space that doesn't merge with his current social environment. Unlike strategic social media use aimed at maintaining separate social contexts, Umut's choice of immersive voice calls is driven by a need for genuine emotional intimacy that less engaging communication methods cannot provide. This preference for deep, undistracted communication mirrors Sinan's approach to managing his online interactions, where selecting specific communication channels helps regulate emotional proximity and distance with loved ones. Umut's experience highlights how direct, focused conversations with family offer a calm escape, a stark contrast to the anxiety he feels when consuming Turkish news on Twitter, which often details the country's economic and international challenges. This demonstrates a deliberate differentiation in his digital engagement, where certain platforms are reserved for emotional connection and others for information, even if anxiety-inducing. The immersive nature of these calls allows him to mentally revisit a distant time and identity, reinforcing a sense of continuity with his past while navigating the demands of his present. This selective engagement underscores the nuanced ways individuals utilize digital tools to manage their emotional landscapes across transnational divides.
In bustling urban environments, particularly megacities like Istanbul, where constant digital prompts intensely compete for attention, the immersive nature of voice calling gains significant meaning. This phenomenon aligns with the concept of an "attention economy," where social media platforms position user attention as a finite resource. While this idea has been extended to "affective publics" in political contexts, its relevance to personal emotional communication is also notable. Scholars have observed that contemporary understandings of privacy and intimacy are often shaped by contrasting them with the "affective qualities" of platforms like Facebook. This implies that certain social media platforms facilitate intimacy by offering an escape or a distinct alternative to the distracting aspects of other digital media. This growing awareness of the drawbacks of excessive social media use is increasingly recognized as a characteristic of a tech-savvy lifestyle. Therefore, in such demanding digital landscapes, the focused engagement of voice calls stands out, providing a unique space for deeper emotional connection amidst pervasive distractions. This highlights how the perceived value of different communication methods shifts based on the surrounding digital and urban context.
Facebook's role in mediating intimate emotions is limited, as evidenced by its perceived distractiveness in my research. Meyra, a Turkish-Dutch migrant in Istanbul, highlighted this by stating that Facebook is primarily for less intimate connections, unlike WhatsApp, which she uses extensively for closer relationships. This distinction underscores how local conditions, particularly economic anxieties prevalent in Turkey during this period and the demanding urban life fostered by neoliberal structures, shape the emotional dimensions of daily social media use. In such advanced urban societies, the widespread presence of social media can paradoxically hinder intimacy. Thus, the specific media affordances that emerge in intimate communications are deeply intertwined with these broader societal and economic pressures, influencing how individuals navigate their emotional connections online.
Huseyn, who regularly communicates with his mother via calls and texts, acknowledges that his sisters maintain much more frequent contact with her. His two sisters and younger brother reside in Eindhoven, the same city as their parents and Huseyn's childhood home. Having moved out, his sisters are deeply involved in their parents' daily lives. However, Huseyn perceives their closeness as more than just a result of physical proximity. He explains, with a slight smile, "They talk all the time. This highlights a perceived gender difference in communication frequency within the family. In contrast to Huseyn's perspective, Merel, whose mother lives in Amsterdam, openly shared that her mother messages her daily. Merel, of Dutch and Turkish-Dutch heritage, illustrates a different dynamic of consistent, daily communication. Her friend, Meyra, further elaborated on the evolution of her own communication patterns. Upon her initial move to Istanbul, Meyra's busy schedule limited her to WhatsApp messaging for long-distance contact. However, she has since adapted to a routine that includes frequent calls to and from the Netherlands, supplementing her messages. While the interviewed women also lived independently from their families, these examples suggest that young men might face distinct pressures or expectations when asserting independence from their families post-migration, particularly concerning communication frequency and perceived emotional needs. This implies that gendered norms may influence how individuals navigate and express family intimacy across transnational distances.
Faruk’s move to Istanbul, following his Bachelor’s degree to pursue International Business, marked a significant life transition for him, signifying a newfound independence. He described this period as finally "beginning to live," a sentiment echoed by many respondents who viewed their relocation to Istanbul as a fresh start, promising independence and opportunity despite requiring perseverance. Initially, Faruk resided with his aunt and uncle on the Asian side of Istanbul before securing his own apartment on the European side, further solidifying his autonomy while maintaining social connections with his cousins. However, this pursuit of independence introduced complexities in his family relationships. Faruk initially struggled to maintain contact with one of his sisters, with whom he shared a particularly close bond. Her hurt over his silence was eventually conveyed to him through their mother. Upon learning of his sister’s distress, Faruk initiated a phone call to resolve the issue, explaining that he had expected her to reach out to him. This conversation ultimately mended the rift between them. Faruk acknowledged his own stoic nature regarding emotional expression, stating, “I don’t usually let myself get carried away by emotions,” and viewing the distance from his family as an inherent part of life, despite missing them. This stoicism, however, did not negate his responsibility to alleviate his parents’ concerns about his move. He proactively demonstrated his well-being by securing a job, enrolling at Bogazici University, and finding an apartment, thereby gradually convincing his parents to support his decision. This experience highlights a common theme among the migrants: while they readily adapted to the geographical distance from their families, they also took on the role of helping their family members adjust to the separation. This often involved actively managing perceptions and demonstrating stability. The narrative of Faruk, and others, underscores how the pursuit of personal and professional growth in a new city intertwined with the careful navigation of family expectations and emotional dynamics, particularly in maintaining reassurance and connection across transnational distances. The act of moving to Istanbul, for many, was not just a physical relocation but also an emotional journey of self-discovery and renegotiation of familial ties.
Migrants often feel a responsibility to alleviate parental worry from afar, sometimes leading to reduced communication during difficult periods. This tendency was particularly noted among men, such as Umut and Huseyn, who intentionally withheld personal struggles from their parents to prevent distress. Huseyn, for instance, explained that he avoids burdening his mother because the geographical distance limits her ability to offer practical assistance, making it futile to upset her unnecessarily. This contrasts with Umut's interactions with his cousins, where he exhibited greater openness than he did with his parents. The need to project emotional independence as young adults influences these communication choices, especially for those whose migration signifies a new life stage. Consequently, migrants strategically manage information shared with parents, aiming to reassure them of their well-being and stability, even if it means concealing challenges. This careful curation of communication highlights the complex emotional dynamics within transnational families.
The decision to migrate often entails a dual responsibility for young adults: establishing personal independence while simultaneously reassuring parents of their well-being. This dynamic frequently shapes how individuals manage emotional closeness with their families, particularly when navigating the complexities of transnational relationships. For some, maintaining emotional independence from parents necessitates withholding certain personal struggles, as this is perceived as the most effective way to alleviate parental worry from afar. The degree of parental support and the migrants' pursuit of self-sufficiency varied among the respondents. For example, Sinan resided in an apartment his parents had purchased as an investment, indicating a level of continued parental involvement. Similarly, Esra, an editor, brought a dining table inherited from her Istanbul-based grandfather and a coffee machine from her parents' Dutch home, while Meyra received assistance from her parents in furnishing her new house in Istanbul. These instances highlight how parents contributed to their children's settlement in Istanbul, fostering a sense of support. In turn, these young adults strived for largely self-sufficient lives, a combination of reciprocating parental care and reducing their reliance on it. They communicated their success and stability to their parents through social media, effectively managing their emotional reactions while receiving distant emotional care. For some, the physical distance afforded by migration to Istanbul facilitated a more effective management of intense family emotions. Cengiz, for instance, reflected on the emotional culture prevalent in the Turkish families he encountered during his upbringing in the Netherlands in the 1990s. He described a home environment characterized by emotional reactivity, a sentiment echoed by other second-generation Turkish-Dutch individuals. He observed a stark contrast between his experiences at home, marked by "autocratic fathers and pastry-baking mothers," and the more lenient reactions of his Dutch friends' parents to minor mishaps like spilling milk. While Cengiz sometimes associated this emotional intensity with negative aspects of Turkish culture, he also recognized its positive dimensions, such as a greater propensity for "showing affection, touching, sharing." He articulated his bicultural identity by stating, "my way of thinking is Dutch, my way of feeling is Turkish." His move to Istanbul was not driven by a strong sense of Turkish identity, but by an appreciation for the more overt emotional expression he found there. He felt that in the Netherlands, even basic emotions were often perceived as excessive, leaving him feeling "in between" these two cultural norms. This suggests that for some, migration provided an opportunity to engage with a different expression of Turkishness, one that offered a more comfortable space for their emotional lives.

This discourse on Turkish and Dutch national cultures revealed the extent to which individuals felt aligned with or alienated from either identity. Istanbul offered these individuals a different environment to engage with these feelings compared to the Netherlands. Here, they could associate Turkishness with a broader, more diverse, and cosmopolitan urban population, rather than solely with their immediate families, family friends, and neighbors. This contrasted sharply with the more socially conservative, working-class, and provincial backgrounds often associated with the Turkish community in the Netherlands. The Turkish identity discovered in Istanbul was perceived as more urban, diverse, cosmopolitan, and open-minded, values that these young people actively embraced in their lifestyles within specific Istanbul neighborhoods. This article explores how migrants experience emotional closeness with loved ones over long distances, specifically through social media. It contributes to ongoing discussions about social media's role in shaping emotional experiences. The research draws on short-term ethnographic study of second-generation Turkish-Dutch migrants who grew up in the Netherlands and later moved to Istanbul. The focus is on transnational family intimacy within this group, serving as a detailed case study to understand how social media platforms and mobile devices foster feelings of togetherness despite long-term separation. The author argues that social media platforms facilitate not only the ambient, fast-paced, background communications often highlighted in existing literature but also more direct, immersive, and conversational modes. The article posits that individuals' deliberate choices in shifting between these communication modes create their experiences of transnational emotional intimacy. This concept is termed "careful co-presence," emphasizing that social media practices generating intimacy involve both discerning selectivity and emotional care. This perspective builds on scholarship that uses practice-based approaches to understand how digital media mediates emotion. The study investigates how social media mediates "co-presence," or a sense of togetherness and emotional proximity, within the social web era, particularly for intimate lives lived across transnational distances. It demonstrates how intimacy is mediated through specific social media affordances that arise from a deliberate and emotionally constructive use of social media, which the author labels "careful." This "careful" descriptor refers to social media practices that produce experiences of transnational co-presence. These practices are "care-full" in that they involve feelings of care, as explored in research on dynamic care circulation within transnational families. They also entail careful and discerning selection among available media practices for maintaining contact with loved ones, considering the demands on time and attention in the digital age. Conceptualizing the integration of carefully chosen media practices and transnational care practices, "careful co-presence" is inspired by the notion of "emotional affordances." This concept, theorized from a practice-oriented, ethnographic perspective rooted in the anthropology of emotions, argues that digital media alter emotional experiences by shaping the bodily practices through which emotions are enacted. The article subsequently illustrates how the idea of careful practices in producing transnational intimacy through social media emerged from empirical data.
This analysis argues that the specific ways individuals communicate with family via social media, rather than inherent platform features, shape experiences of transnational intimacy. This "careful" communication mode generates feelings of both mediated co-presence and distance within relationships spanning Turkey and the Netherlands. The term "careful" encompasses both attentiveness and discernment, implying a deliberate choice of communication timing and platform, alongside the expression of emotional care for recipients. This careful social media practice, it is contended, both creates and conveys intimate emotional experiences. This perspective draws inspiration from recent scholarship, particularly practice theories of emotion and digital media, which posit that emotional experiences are not only generated by media practices but also expressed and performed through them (Bareither, 2017). Furthermore, the argument incorporates mediation theories, which suggest that media do more than simply serve as tools; they actively cultivate specific sensibilities through user engagement (Grasseni & Gieser, 2019). Building on this understanding of digital media and their "emotional affordances" (Bareither, forthcoming), it becomes crucial to examine how individuals strategically combine, manage, and transition between various social media applications. This selective approach is an intrinsic component of their situated emotional relationships. This framework is particularly vital for comprehending transnational migration contexts, where intimacy is sustained across significant distances and where mobility entails navigating diverse socio-cultural environments in which social media platforms are utilized. Therefore, the focus shifts from the inherent capabilities of social media to the intentional and nuanced practices users employ to cultivate and maintain emotional bonds across geographical and cultural divides.
