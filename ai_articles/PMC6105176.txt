Basal cell carcinoma (BCC), a prevalent skin cancer, commonly arises from an overactive Hedgehog (Hh) pathway. This pathway's regulation hinges on the interplay between two receptors: Patched-1 (Ptch1) and Smoothened (Smo). Smo is an activating receptor, normally kept in check by Ptch1's inhibitory influence. When a ligand binds to Ptch1, this inhibition is lifted, leading to pathway activation. This receptor dynamic is crucial for controlling cell proliferation, and both Ptch1 and Smo are frequently mutated in BCCs. Ptch2, a paralog of Ptch1, shares functional similarities in both normal development and maintaining tissue health. However, its specific role in cancer progression remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to elucidate how Ptch2 contributes to inhibiting BCC growth. To achieve this, we employed various in vitro assays to measure transcriptional and chemotactic Hh signaling in ASZ001 cells, a BCC-derived cell line. Additionally, a new xenograft model was utilized to evaluate BCC tumor growth in vivo. Gene editing with TALEN technology allowed us to differentiate the distinct responses of Ptch2 to its ligand, sonic hedgehog (Shh). Our initial in vitro findings in Ptch1-deficient ASZ001 cells revealed that Ptch2 ligand binding primarily stimulated cell migration, rather than a transcriptional response. Subsequent targeting of Ptch2 effectively eliminated this chemotactic effect. When we investigated Ptch2's role in in vivo tumor growth using a xenograft model, we observed that diminished Ptch function led to accelerated tumor growth. Interestingly, there appeared to be selective pressure against the complete removal of Ptch2. In conclusion, our research indicates that Ptch2, similar to Ptch1, acts as a tumor suppressor in BCC cells. Furthermore, when both paralogs are targeted, ligand-independent activation of the Hh pathway becomes a factor in tumor growth.

The Hedgehog (Hh) pathway plays a vital role in both embryonic development and adult tissue maintenance, but its dysregulation also contributes to tumor initiation and progression [1, 2]. Central to Hh pathway control are the transmembrane proteins Patched-1 (Ptch1) and Smoothened (Smo) [3, 4]. Ptch1 functions as the primary receptor for the activating ligand, sonic hedgehog (Shh) [5]. When Shh is absent, Ptch1 actively suppresses Smo, thereby maintaining pathway inactivity [6]. However, upon Shh binding, Ptch1's inhibitory effect is released through its relocation, allowing Smo to activate downstream signaling elements [7–9]. This intracellular signaling cascade can trigger diverse cellular responses, including the activation of transcription factors and the remodeling of the cytoskeleton to facilitate chemotaxis [10, 11]. Aberrant Hh pathway activation in cancer can arise from two distinct mechanisms [12]. One mechanism involves tumor cells overproducing Shh ligand, which then promotes tumor growth through autocrine or paracrine signaling [13, 14]. The second mechanism involves genetic alterations within Hh pathway components [15–18]. These genetic changes often include inactivating mutations in Ptch1, which compromise its ability to inhibit Smo, or activating mutations in Smo, making it unresponsive to Ptch1's suppression. Such activating mutations lead to cell-autonomous pathway activation, freeing cells from dependence on Shh ligands from their environment. Consequently, Ptch1 is recognized as a tumor suppressor. Basal cell carcinoma (BCC), the most common skin cancer, frequently exhibits mutations in Hh pathway components. Oncogenic mutations in Ptch1 and Smo are well-established drivers of BCC, a fact supported by mouse models demonstrating the malignancy's strong reliance on these mutations [19, 20]. Indeed, a study of 126 BCC mutational profiles found PTCH1 mutations in 73% of cases [21].
While Ptch1 is traditionally considered the primary receptor for Shh in Hedgehog (Hh) pathway regulation, with Cdon, Boc, and Gas1 acting as coreceptors, its paralog, Patched-2 (Ptch2), also plays a role. Although Ptch2 is believed to complement some Ptch1 functions, it doesn't regulate the pathway with the same potency. Evidence for this includes the viability and normal development of Ptch2-deficient embryos, and Ptch2's inability to fully compensate for Ptch1 loss in genetically Ptch1-deficient systems. However, the absence of Ptch2 can worsen skin tumor phenotypes in mice with partial Ptch1 deficiency by disrupting epidermal differentiation. Furthermore, the lack of both Ptch paralogs negatively impacts skin maintenance. Studies on Hh pathway target expression in the epidermis indicate that complete Ptch deficiency leads to uniformly high pathway activation. Interestingly, recent research in embryonic stem cells highlights Ptch2's necessity for ligand perception when Ptch1 is absent. Supporting this, a Ptch1-deficient mouse model of Hh pathway-driven BCC showed tumors preferentially developing near Shh sources. These findings suggest that even without Ptch1, some responsiveness to Shh persists, implying Shh's potential role in mediating Ptch2 activity.
Mutations in PTCH2 are uncommon, with a genetic analysis of 126 BCC cases showing only 14 instances of mutations in both PTCH1 and PTCH2, and just 4 cases having PTCH2 mutations exclusively [21]. This low frequency implies that, when PTCH1 is non-functional, there isn't strong selective pressure acting on PTCH2. Our study aimed to investigate two possibilities: first, if the lack of PTCH1's repressive activity increases PTCH2's role in sensing Shh ligand and activating the pathway, making cells very responsive to Shh; and second, if PTCH2's impact on tumor growth is purely due to its tumor suppressor role by inhibiting Smo. We also sought to determine if the rarity of PTCH2 mutations in patient samples could be explained by selective pressure against the complete removal of both PTCH paralogs. Utilizing in vitro and in vivo models, alongside gene editing, we distinguished how BCC cells respond to Shh ligand, ultimately demonstrating that a lack of both PTCH paralogs speeds up tumor growth.
Routine cell culture procedures were followed for PANC-1 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (Ptch1−/− and Ptch1+/+ MEFs from Dr. Scott, Stanford University [35]), which were grown in high-glucose DMEM with 8% FBS, L-glutamine, penicillin, and streptomycin (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). ASZ001 cells [36, 37] were maintained in 154CF keratinocyte medium (Life Technologies) with 50 μM CaCl, penicillin, streptomycin, and 2% chelex-treated FCS. Mycoplasma screening was conducted monthly via PCR.
Gene expression levels were quantified using quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR). First, RNA was extracted from lysed cells using Trizol, following the manufacturer's instructions. This RNA was then converted into cDNA with Superscript III and random primers. qRT-PCR was conducted on a Lightcycler LC480 II, utilizing SYBR green for detection. Relative expression was determined by the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method, normalized against the Gapdh reference gene. Specific primer sequences were employed for Gapdh, Gli1, Ptch1 (including exon 2), and Ptch2.
Flow cytometry was employed to analyze cells, which were first harvested with trypsin-EDTA (Lonza) and then washed with FACS buffer (PBS with 1% FBS). For antibody incubation, cells were treated for 30 minutes at 4°C with hybridoma supernatants containing either anti-Shh antibody 5E1 or anti-Myc antibody 9E10 (isotype), both diluted 1:5 in FACS buffer. A subsequent wash was followed by incubation with a 1:500 dilution of APC-labeled anti-mouse secondary antibody (BD, 550826). Finally, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer containing 1 µg/ml propidium iodide (PI) (Sigma) for analysis on a FACSCanto II machine (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Data processing was performed using FlowJo 7 software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).
To assess Smo ciliary localization, ASZ001 cells were transfected with Myc-tagged Smo using PEI. After 48 hours, these cells were starved for two days and then stimulated with a 1:4 diluted ShhN supernatant from 293 T cells. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde. Following permeabilization and blocking with 5% goat serum/PBS-T, an acetylated α-tubulin primary antibody (Sigma, 1:2000) was applied for either one hour at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C. An Alexa 488 conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 1:2000) was then added for one hour at room temperature. Finally, coverslips were mounted with ProLong Gold (Invitrogen) and imaged using a Zeiss AxioVert microscope.
To assess GLI transcription factor activity, a GBS-GFP reporter construct was utilized. This construct was created by PCR-amplifying an 8 × 3’GLI binding site sequence from the pδ51 GBS-luciferase reporter and inserting it into a lentiviral pRRL TOP-d2GFP vector, as previously described. MEFs and ASZ001 cells were then transduced with this GBS-GFP reporter. Following transduction, cells were starved in medium containing 0.5% FCS and subsequently stimulated for four days with either ShhN conditioned supernatant from 293 T cells or 200 nM Smo agonist (SAG). Cells expressing GFP were isolated using a BD FACSAria sorter. These GFP-positive cells were then cultured in either 8% FCS (MEFs) or 2% FCS (ASZ001), which led to the anticipated decrease in GFP activity. For later experiments, cells were plated in 24-well plates, subjected to various treatments, harvested, and the proportion of GFP+ cells was quantified via flow cytometry on a FACSCanto II machine.
To assess cell viability, 2000 cells per well were plated in either 0.5% or 2% FCS. After a 3-hour incubation, specified compounds were introduced. Following 4 days, MTT was added, and dye reduction was measured after 4 hours. Control-treated cell values were normalized to 1, with background from H2O2-treated cells subtracted.
To quantify cell migration, a previously established method was employed. Cells were initially stained with 10 μM CellTracker Green (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Subsequently, cells were detached using 5 mM EDTA, then suspended in serum-free medium before being seeded into FluoroBlok Transwell inserts (BD Falcon) at a concentration of approximately 5 × 10^4 cells per insert. Chemoattractants were introduced into the lower chambers of the Transwell plates. Over a period of roughly 3 hours, GFP-spectrum fluorescence in these bottom compartments was monitored every 2 minutes using a Synergy HT plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). To isolate specific migratory activity, background fluorescence, measured from wells containing only medium, was subtracted from all readings. Furthermore, baseline cell movement, determined from a "no attractant" control for each experimental condition, was subtracted from measurements taken in the presence of chemoattractants. This process yielded specific migration values for each attractant.
To facilitate selection, a pair of TALEN constructs was engineered from the pCTIGTALEN expression vector. One construct was designed to co-express GFP, while the other expressed tdTomato, enabling the simultaneous selection of both 5' and 3' targeting constructs via FACS sorting. These constructs were developed through Golden Gate cloning into pCTIG, utilizing specific variable domain architectures: 5’ NN NN HD NG NG HD NN NI NN HD NG NG NI HD NG NG HD and 3’ NG HD NG NN NN NI NG HD HD NG NN HD NI HD HD HD HD (refer to Supplementary Fig. S4a).
To generate monoclonal cultures, ASZ001 cells were initially transfected with paired TALEN constructs via PEI. Following a 3-day incubation, cells expressing both GFP and tdTomato were isolated using flow sorting. These sorted cells were then bulk-seeded into T25 flasks for a 7-day recovery period. Subsequently, individual cells were plated into 2 × 96-well plates to establish monoclonal lines, with 35 out of 192 wells successfully yielding cell growth. The resulting clones were genotyped by sequencing PCR products spanning the TALEN binding sites. Specifically, genomic DNA was screened using PCR with primers flanking the TALEN sites, followed by AccI digestion. Monoclonal lines demonstrating a loss of the restriction site underwent TOPO cloning and Sanger sequencing for further verification.
To achieve overexpression of Hedgehog pathway components, researchers utilized wild-type Smo (SmoWT) and a ciliary localization domain-mutated Smo (SmoCLD) from Dr. Jeremy Reiter, both in pCS107 constructs. Additionally, a wild-type Ptch1 construct in pcDNA3.1 was sourced from Dr. Matthew Scott. A specific Ptch1 variant, mPtch1Δloop2, which lacks the ligand-binding extracellular loop, was also employed for its Smo-repressive activity.
In-house bred NSG mice were used for xenograft experiments. Animals received subcutaneous injections of either 1 × 10^5 or 5 × 10^5 ASZ001 cells suspended in Matrigel. All procedures adhered to ethical guidelines set by the animal experiment committee (LEX237). Tumor progression was tracked weekly, with experiments concluding upon the onset of ulceration, which generally occurred before tumors reached the 1000 mm^3 humane endpoint. Harvested tumors underwent paraffin embedding for histopathological analysis and RNA extraction.
To investigate skin cancer driven by Ptch1 deficiency, the ASZ001 cell line was employed as a model. This cell line originated from an irradiation-induced tumor in a Ptch1+/- mouse, and subsequently underwent Ptch1 loss of heterozygosity, as detailed in reference [36] and Figure 1a. The prevailing understanding of Hedgehog (Hh) pathway regulation posits that the absence of Ptch1 is sufficient to fully activate the Hh pathway, without the need for additional ligand. Indeed, evidence from abundant transcription of the remaining exon on the targeted Ptch1 allele, which itself is a downstream target of the activated pathway, confirmed that the Hh pathway was active in these cells (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, the ASZ001 cells were found not to produce their own ligand capable of cell-autonomous pathway activation (Fig. 1b). For comparison, controls using SHH-expressing pancreatic cancer PANC-1 cells and Shh-transfected ASZ001 cells are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. A crucial prerequisite for Hh ligand responsiveness is the presence of a primary cilium. This was confirmed in nearly all ASZ001 cells examined (Fig. 1c, right panel) [7]. The primary cilium, an antenna-like protrusion extending from the cell membrane, is structured by the microtubule cytoskeleton. Its role in pathway regulation involves the dynamic localization of Hh pathway components into and out of this organelle, and it is essential for proper ligand perception. Therefore, at least a portion of the immediate signaling machinery necessary for Shh response was found to be intact in these cells. The ciliary localization of exogenously overexpressed Smo in response to Shh addition was subsequently evaluated using microscopy. While a high baseline percentage of cells already exhibited Smo localized in the cilium—an expected observation given the Ptch1-deficient system and Smo overexpression—the addition of ligand led to a moderate increase in this number (Fig. 1d). As a control, SmoCLD, a form of Smo unable to localize to the cilium, was utilized [7]. These collective findings suggest that ASZ001 cells, despite lacking Ptch1, are still capable of perceiving the Shh ligand.
While Smoothened (Smo) relocation to the primary cilium typically triggers transcriptional responses in the Hedgehog (Hh) pathway, ASZ001 cells exhibited a different pattern. Despite high baseline Hh pathway activity, these cells showed only minimal transcriptional responses to external pathway activators when assessed using a stably integrated GLI-binding site GFP reporter construct (Fig. 2a,b). This was further supported by qRT-PCR analysis of target genes, which revealed only minor responses in ASZ001 cells compared to Ptch1-proficient Mouse Embryonic Fibroblasts (MEFs) (Fig. 2c,d). In contrast, MEFs displayed robust responses to these activators. The Hh pathway in ASZ001 cells did demonstrate some regulatory capacity. Under conditions of high serum proliferation, a significant decrease in basal reporter activity was observed (only 4% GFP+ cells), suggesting a link to cell cycle progression and subsequent loss of the primary cilium. Reverting to low-serum conditions restored GFP reporter expression to the initial levels. Further investigation into the mitogenic Hh response in ASZ001 cells, specifically whether it could be driven by externally supplied ligand, yielded limited results. Treatment with ShhN or control supernatants showed only marginal proliferative responses to Shh compared to the control (Supplementary Fig. S2a). Additionally, inhibitors targeting Hh pathway components and related signaling molecules proved largely ineffective at curbing ASZ001 cell proliferation, as indicated by their high IC50 values (Supplementary Fig. S2b,c). However, ASZ001 cells did mediate a chemotactic response to Shh (Fig. 2e). Utilizing a modified Boyden chamber assay, a strong migratory response was observed in the presence of both recombinant ShhN (5 nM) and Smoothened Agonist (SAG, 200 nM). This chemotactic activity was dependent on Smo, as it could be blocked by cyclopamine (5 μM), and the use of SAG further confirmed this dependency. Overexpression of Ptch1 led to an increased relative responsiveness of the cells to ligand, evidenced by an enhanced net migratory response to Shh (Fig. 2f). Conversely, a form of Ptch1 unable to bind Shh (Ptch1Δloop2) inhibited Shh-stimulated migration. This suggests that while the migratory response is not strictly reliant on Ptch1, it can be modulated by Ptch1's inhibitory activity. These findings collectively indicate that even in the absence of Ptch1, ASZ001 cells retain the ability to respond chemotactically to Shh, implying the involvement of other receptors, potentially its paralog Ptch2.
We investigated the migratory potential of ASZ001 cells to determine if they could activate a chemotactic response upon Shh perception. Prior research in fibroblasts and neural development indicated that Shh ligand can trigger chemotaxis, even without Hh pathway transcription factors, and this response is largely unaffected by pathway component disruption. Using a modified Boyden chamber assay, we quantitatively measured chemotaxis with pathway activators. Both recombinant ShhN (5 nM) and SAG (200 nM) elicited a strong migratory response (Fig. 2e). This chemotaxis was dependent on Smo, as confirmed by its inhibition with cyclopamine (5 μM) and further emphasized by the use of the Smo agonist, SAG. Interestingly, while not strictly reliant on Ptch1, the migratory response could be influenced by Ptch1's inhibitory action. Overexpression of exogenous Ptch1 enhanced the cells' relative responsiveness to the ligand, leading to a greater net migratory response to Shh (Fig. 2f). Conversely, a Ptch1 variant unable to bind Shh (Ptch1Δloop2) inhibited Shh-stimulated migration. These findings collectively demonstrate that ASZ001 cells, despite lacking Ptch1, maintain their ability to respond chemotactically to Shh. This suggests the involvement of other receptors in mediating this chemotactic response, with Ptch2 being a probable candidate.
Investigating the effects of Ptch loss necessitates highly efficient targeting due to Ptch's potent inhibitory action on Smo, where even a small number of Ptch molecules can suppress Hh pathway activity. Our initial attempts to silence Ptch2 using lentiviral shRNA, employing five distinct sequences and puromycin selection, proved ineffective (Supplementary Fig. S3). Consequently, we adopted transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs) for editing the Ptch2 locus in ASZ001 cells, following the strategy detailed in Supplementary Fig. S4. After transfecting the cells, we bulk FACS-sorted them to ensure the survival of those expressing both TALENs (Supplementary Fig. S5a). Following a recovery period, cells were seeded at a single-cell density into 2 × 96-well plates, from which 35 clones (18%) successfully grew. We then analyzed these single-cell clones for editing events. Five clones (lines 1, 4, 14, 16, 17) exhibited a digest pattern indicative of successful editing (Supplementary Fig. S5b). To confirm editing efficiency, these lines were TOPO cloned, and 20 clones per line underwent Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S5c). Intriguingly, none of the lines were entirely devoid of wild-type Ptch2 sequences. Specifically, cell line 4 displayed one-third mutant sequences, while cell lines 1, 14, 16, and 17 showed two-thirds mutant sequences. Furthermore, the gene editing patterns in lines 1, 14, 16, and 17 were almost identical, suggesting that among the initial 35 clones, only two parental clones had significant Ptch2 editing: clone 4 (designated Ptch2MED) and another parental clone that produced clones 1, 14, 16, and 17 (designated Ptch2LOW), with clone 1 being selected for further study. The inability to generate cell lines with complete Ptch2 gene editing, despite successful transfection, implies the presence of selective pressure against full Ptch deficiency. This observation aligns with our earlier findings from the lentiviral shRNA Ptch2 silencing experiments (Supplementary Fig. S3).
Our investigation into the role of Ptch2 in ASZ001 cells revealed distinct effects on both transcriptional activity and chemotaxis. When Ptch2 was targeted, the cells exhibited no transcriptional response to ShhN ligand, as measured by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3a). Interestingly, gene editing of Ptch2 also led to an unexpected increase in basal transcriptional pathway activity in vitro. This was observed when comparing the parental Ptch2+/+ cells with the gene-edited, yet wild-type, Ptch2+/+ clone 35, suggesting that such gene-edited wild-type cells are appropriate controls for these experiments. Furthermore, our findings supported the hypothesis that Ptch2 is crucial for mediating chemotaxis in response to Hh ligand. Consistent with this, targeting Ptch2 significantly reduced the chemotactic response to ShhN in both Ptch2LOW and Ptch2MED cells when compared to the Ptch2+/+ clone 35 cells (Fig. 3b-c). It is important to note that the baseline chemokinesis, which represents cell movement without an attractant, remained consistent across all genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S6a). The remaining chemotactic activity in the Ptch2-edited ASZ001 cells was sensitive to cyclopamine, confirming that the regulation of Shh chemotaxis is dependent on Smo (Supplementary Fig. S6b). These results collectively indicate that Ptch2 plays a critical role in Shh-induced chemotaxis in these cells.
Our investigation into Shh chemotaxis in ASZ001 cells considered receptors beyond Ptch1 and Ptch2. We explored whether other potential Hh receptors could also mediate this response. By using lentiviral shRNA to target Cdon and Boc receptors, we observed reduced cell migration, even with only moderate knockdown. This suggests that additional Shh receptors, besides Ptch2, might contribute to chemotactic signaling. However, since Ptch2 itself showed a limited transcriptional response to ShhN, we infer that in Ptch1-deficient cells in vitro, Ptch2's ligand-dependent role is primarily restricted to chemotaxis.
To investigate the implications of these signaling outcomes on tumor growth within a living organism, we conducted experiments using an in vivo model. Our previous findings demonstrated that in cells lacking Ptch1, the Hh ligand is exclusively sensed by Ptch2, leading to chemotaxis. Furthermore, we observed that additional manipulation of Ptch2 hinders the cells' capacity to detect the Hh ligand. For the in vivo study, ASZ001 cells were subcutaneously injected into immunodeficient mice, and tumor development was subsequently tracked. Tumors originating from ASZ001 cells where both Ptch paralogs were targeted (specifically, Ptch2LOW and Ptch2MED cells) exhibited more rapid expansion compared to tumors derived from cells with intact Ptch2 (ASZ001 Ptch2+/+ clone 35 cells). Consequently, the mice bearing tumors with targeted Ptch paralogs had to be euthanized earlier due to ulceration (as detailed in Fig. 4a). Further analysis using immunohistochemistry to detect the proliferation marker Ki67 corroborated these observations, revealing an elevated proliferative index in the tumors that had undergone Ptch2 gene editing (quantified in Fig. 4b and 4c). The experimental setup involved grafting 1 × 10^5 or 5 × 10^5 ASZ001 cells of various genotypes in PBS/Matrigel into each of four mice per group, with each mouse receiving two tumors, resulting in a sample size of eight. Tumors were harvested upon signs of ulceration. Post-harvesting, tumors were prepared for Ki67 immunohistochemistry, and positive nuclei were quantified automatically per optical field (n ≥ 7). Statistical significance was determined using the Mann-Whitney U test.
Pathological examination of tumor histology confirmed the cellular origin as cutaneous, yet surprisingly, both genotypes displayed a squamous rather than basal histological pattern. This deviation might be attributed to the extensive in vitro culturing of the cells before grafting. Tumors originating from Ptch2LOW and Ptch2MED cells exhibited a greater accumulation of keratin, visible as eosin-rich pink regions (quantified in Fig. 4e, depicted in Fig. 4d). To investigate if the accelerated in vivo proliferation of these Ptch2-edited cells stemmed from further activation of the Hedgehog (Hh) pathway, beyond the already elevated levels due to Ptch1-deficiency, Hh pathway target gene expression was assessed via qRT-PCR. Indeed, these genes showed increased expression in the Ptch2LOW and Ptch2MED ASZ001 tumors (Fig. 4f), likely accounting for the observed faster tumor growth. We propose that the difference in Hh pathway activation between in vitro and in vivo settings after Ptch2 targeting arises from unique environmental cues or mechanical forces present only in vivo, which influence the signaling cascade downstream of Smo. The heightened baseline pathway activity and subsequent tumor growth observed in vivo after Ptch2 targeting lead us to conclude that Ptch2 functions as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting the pathway in a ligand-independent manner, a role also crucial for cancer cell viability.
The prevailing understanding of Hedgehog (Hh) pathway control posits Patched-1 (Ptch1) as the primary receptor for sonic hedgehog (Shh) and the central regulator of downstream signaling [3, 6]. However, recent research indicates that this view of Ptch1 as the sole key Shh receptor requires re-evaluation. Studies in developmental models have demonstrated that Patched-2 (Ptch2) becomes essential for ligand perception when Ptch1 is absent [27]. Furthermore, Ptch1 and Ptch2 share overlapping roles in skin development and upkeep, both dependent on the Hh pathway [32, 34]. Despite clinical evidence suggesting a tumor suppressor role for Ptch2 in basal cell carcinoma (BCC) [50], its precise contribution to this established cancer type has been ambiguous. The potential for Ptch2's tumor suppressor activity to be distinct from its ligand-binding function is currently unexplored, a complexity arising from the known connection between these functions [45]. Our investigation aimed to clarify these roles by initially characterizing Ptch2's signaling capacities in BCC cells in vitro, followed by an assessment of how Ptch2 disruption impacts BCC tumor growth in vivo. Through migration assays, we determined that in parental ASZ001 cells lacking Ptch1, the sole response to Hh ligand was chemotaxis. Subsequent targeting of Ptch2 using TALEN technology significantly impaired this chemotactic response. Consequently, we infer that the accelerated tumor growth observed after Ptch2 targeting is probably not linked to ligand perception. Instead, Ptch2's tumor-suppressive effect likely stems from its inherent inhibitory action on Smoothened (Smo), a function that only becomes evident in an in vivo context for reasons yet to be understood.
The infrequent occurrence of Ptch2 mutations in Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC) patients, as documented in a large-scale genetic analysis, presents a puzzling contrast to its functional similarities and overlapping roles with Ptch1 in signaling pathways. Our research suggests that Ptch2's tumor-suppressive role is likely overshadowed by the more potent inhibitory action of Ptch1 on the Hedgehog (Hh) pathway, especially since Ptch2 exhibits a limited capacity for ligand perception in BCC. This perspective is reinforced by the high prevalence of TP53 mutations, a significantly stronger tumor suppressor than Ptch2. TP53 is more readily perturbed, requiring only a single allele mutation for an oncogenic event, making it a more advantageous and frequently observed mutation compared to the loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in PTCH2, which may explain the low mutation rate of PTCH2. Furthermore, our inability to achieve complete ablation of the Ptch2 gene, even with selective pressure, indicates that a minimal level of Ptch2 activity is crucial for the viability of cancer cells in vitro. While modest disruptions to Ptch2 are tolerated and lead to sufficient Hh pathway upregulation in vivo to promote tumor growth, as seen in Ptch2MED cells, the precise balance between these opposing activities remains to be elucidated. This balance might stem from Ptch2's diverse signaling functions, such as its role as a Smo antagonist, a chemotaxis receptor, or a dependence receptor, or it could be influenced by the specific cancer context. It's also plausible that in certain rare tumors not currently classified as Hh-driven, mutations in Ptch2 could be critical if it acts as the primary Hh receptor in the tissue of origin. An example of this is the testis-specific expression of Desert Hedgehog (Dhh) and Ptch2, and their involvement in glioblastoma-endothelium crosstalk. Our findings also revealed that Ptch-edited ASZ001 cells retained some chemotactic responsiveness. We hypothesize that this residual response could be mediated by other candidate Shh co-receptors, such as Cdon, Boc, or Gas1, or potentially by Smo itself. Indeed, our data suggest that Cdon and Boc receptors may contribute to chemotactic Shh signaling. However, given that the xenograft experiments' sole variable was the Ptch2 level, we are cautious about drawing definitive conclusions regarding the in vivo signaling contributions of these other receptors.
The direct applicability of our findings from murine cells to human conditions remains an open question. While the ASZ001 cell line is a valuable experimental system due to its clear Hh-dependency and the availability of numerous murine-specific genetic tools for Hh signaling research, further validation using a human-derived model is necessary. Additionally, the tumor-suppressive role of Ptch2 identified in this study needs to be investigated in non-malignant or pre-malignant cultured keratinocytes.
Our prior research indicated that Shh chemotaxis does not necessitate the ciliary relocation of Smo, suggesting that chemotactic and transcriptional responses to Shh might stem from distinct intracellular mechanisms. However, our present findings reveal that these responses are not mutually exclusive. Specifically, Smo's localization to the cilium, even without strong downstream transcriptional signaling (as seen in Ptch1−/−;Ptch2+/+ cells), can still trigger a chemotactic response. The convergence of these two pathways at common points, such as Smo and the primary cilium, implies they are part of a largely conserved signaling cascade. This has positive implications for existing BCC treatments like erivedge/vismodegib. Since vismodegib targets Smo directly, rather than downstream elements or transcription factors, it could effectively counter both chemotactic and transcriptional ligand responses, as well as baseline pathway activity, regardless of the mutational status of other Hh ligand receptors.
