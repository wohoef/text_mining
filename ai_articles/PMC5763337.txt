This article undertakes a comprehensive, long-term analysis of Austrian resource efficiency from 1900 to 2012. It begins by examining various viewpoints on energy consumption and then applies the Ayres and Warr methodology to present findings from an exergy and useful work analysis of the Austrian economy. The study then broadens its scope to assess overall Austrian resource efficiency. This is achieved by contrasting the exergy and useful work data with information on material use, CO2 emissions, and land use, all sourced from statistical records. This novel, extensive comparison allows for an evaluation of Austria's progress against both EU and national policy objectives, particularly in light of the EU's recent emphasis on resource efficiency in environmental and sustainable development initiatives. These EU programs specifically target four key resource categories: materials, energy (including CO2), water, and land.

The concept of industrial or social metabolism, as defined by various researchers (Ayres and Ayres 2002; Ayres and Kneese 1969; Ayres and Simonis 1994; Fischer-Kowalski and Haberl 1993; Fischer-Kowalski and Hüttler 1998), describes the total material and energy resources a society consumes relative to its utilization. This metabolic rate is intrinsically linked to a society's structural characteristics. Historically, human societies have exhibited significant shifts in their material and energy metabolism. Hunter-gatherer societies, for instance, consumed between 10 and 20 gigajoules (GJ) per person. Agrarian societies saw this increase to 40-70 GJ or 3-6 tonnes per capita, while industrialized societies reached much higher levels, ranging from 150 to 400 GJ or 15 to 25 tonnes per capita (Haberl 2006; Krausmann et al. 2008). A pivotal factor in the transition from agrarian to industrial societies was the adoption of fossil fuels, which enabled societies to move beyond the constraints of relying solely on biological resources. This fundamental resource requirement underpins all socioeconomic activities, from basic biological functions to complex industrial processes, transportation, powering electronics, and infrastructure development.
The 20th century saw a dual development: an expansion of energy supplies alongside advancements in energy technologies, which enhanced the efficiency of energy service delivery (Ayres et al. 2003). This technological innovation facilitated a reduction in the cost of obtaining more power from the same quantity of energy. This "technological progress" initiated a positive feedback loop: lower energy costs led to cheaper energy-intensive products, which in turn spurred greater consumption, increased revenues, and overall economic growth. Economies responded by boosting production, leveraging economies of scale. Simultaneously, investment in research and development (R&D) intensified, aiming to create new technologies that would further cut costs and boost productivity. These interconnected activities ultimately resulted in both increased economic growth and improved energy efficiency. To accurately measure these "real" energy efficiencies, specialized energy accounts are necessary. These accounts track the technological conversion of primary energy resources into available exergy—the portion of energy potentially usable by humans—and ultimately into useful work, which represents the actual energy service delivered to societies. Exergy accounts serve this purpose, with studies published for various regions including the United States (Ayres et al. 2003), Sweden (Wall 1987), Japan (Williams et al. 2008), the UK (Hammond and Stapleton 2001; Warr et al. 2008), China (Brockway et al. 2015), and on a global scale (Nakićenović et al. 1996). Comparative analyses using exergy accounts have also been conducted for the United States, Japan, the UK, and Austria (Warr et al. 2010), 15 European countries (Serrenho et al. 2014), and specifically the United States and the UK (Brockway et al. 2014). Researchers, notably Ayres and Warr (2009), have utilized these exergy accounts to model economic growth, asserting that "energy" is a fundamental driver of economic expansion (Ayres et al. 2003; Ayres and Warr 2009; Voudouris et al. 2015; Warr et al. 2008, 2010; Williams et al. 2008; Hamilton 1983, 2003).
The concept of resource efficiency has gained significant policy attention recently. The European Union, for instance, launched its "resource efficient Europe" initiative in 2011, accompanied by a roadmap for its execution (EC 2012). Other international bodies, such as the OECD (2004, 2012) and UNEP (2012), have also released comparable policy frameworks. This focus on resource efficiency expands beyond just energy to encompass a wider range of resources, including materials, water, land, and carbon. The EU advocates for Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) as a primary indicator for material use and efficiency (EC 2012; Fischer-Kowalski et al. 2011), suggesting its supplementation with additional indicators for energy, water, land, and carbon (EC 2012, 21).
This study first employs the exergy accounting method in an Austrian context. It updates the existing database from Warr and colleagues (2010) to encompass energy input data for Austria from 1900 to 2012. This involves categorizing exergy inputs by their final use and determining useful work supply through energy conversion efficiencies. Subsequently, the article compares these exergy and useful work findings with data on material consumption, CO2 emissions, and land use, along with their respective efficiencies. This comprehensive approach allows for an assessment of Austria's resource efficiency in line with the European Commission's framework and evaluates its progress toward policy objectives.
While energy, as defined by the first law of thermodynamics, remains constant through all transformations, the term "energy" in common discourse often refers to exergy. Exergy, also known as "available work" or "available energy," represents the portion of energy that is actually useful and can be consumed to provide services to society. Unlike energy, exergy is not conserved; it is partially depleted during processes. To be utilized, exergy must first be converted into a usable form called useful work, which is then delivered for final consumption. This useful work encompasses categories such as heat, light, mechanical power (for vehicles), and electricity. The amount of useful work obtained is always less than the initial exergy input into a socioeconomic system due to losses during transformation, a principle explained by the second law of thermodynamics. These losses result in the dissipation of a wasted fraction as irreversible entropy, with the extent of exergy loss determined by the efficiency of the transformation process and the specific technology employed.
Exergy analysis is a versatile tool, historically applied to comprehend ecosystem dynamics, as seen in the works of Odum (1973) and Schneider and Kay (1994). Beyond natural systems, it has also proven valuable in engineering, aiding in the design of efficient energy technologies and man-made systems. In more recent times, its application expanded to evaluating shifts in the supply, demand, and technological landscapes of national and regional economies. Early studies in this area often concentrated on a single year, with examples including analyses for the United States (Reistad 1975), Sweden (Wall 1987), Japan (Wall 1990), Canada (Rosen 1992), Italy (Wall et al. 1994), Turkey (Özdoĝan and Arikol 1995; Rosen and Dincer 1997), Norway (Ertesvåg and Mielnik 2000), the UK (Hammond and Stapleton 2001), and Malaysia (Saidur et al. 2007), alongside a global multi-country comparison (Ertesvåg 2001). More contemporary research has delved into the historical progression of resource exergy supply and utilization. This includes long-term studies spanning the twentieth century for the United States (Ayres et al. 2003), Japan (Williams et al. 2008), and the UK (Warr et al. 2008), as well as specific periods like China from 1980–2002 (Chen and Chen 2007). The most recent contributions have provided data for the EU15 countries over the last five decades (Serrenho et al. 2014) and for China (Brockway et al. 2015).
Converting natural resource exergy into useful work involves a two-stage process, each incurring losses. These losses are influenced by the efficiency of the conversion technologies employed. Initially, "transformation losses" happen within the energy sector, for instance, during the generation of electricity from fossil fuels or the refining of crude oil into gasoline. Following this, "conversion losses" take place at the point of final consumption, such as in households. Here, energy inputs are converted by equipment like furnaces, engines, or appliances into energy services like heat or light. By accounting for these losses, we can determine the useful work obtained from the initial exergy input, categorized into five types: heat, light, mechanical drive (for transport), and electricity. Notably, electricity is regarded as "pure" useful work due to its high efficiency in performing mechanical or chemical tasks.
Our compiled database of exergy inputs encompasses coal, crude oil and its products, natural gas, and renewable resources, notably biomass for human and animal consumption. The data spans from 1900 to 2012. For the period 1900-1960, historical Austrian datasets from various sources (e.g., BMWA 1990, Bundesamt für Statistik 1925) were utilized. Subsequent years, from the 1960s onwards, relied on the United Nations International Energy Agency (IEA) database. Comprehensive details regarding all data and their sources are provided in the Journal's Supporting Information. It's important to note that data from the World War periods are excluded due to issues with availability and quality.
The determination of exergy inputs for various energy sources is crucial. For fossil fuels, the primary exergy input is considered synonymous with the domestic energy supply. When addressing other energy sources, the methodology established by Ayres and Warr in earlier research (Ayres et al. 2003; Warr et al. 2010) is adopted for converting them into exergy inputs. Specifically for biomass, the exergy inputs encompass both fuel wood and biomass utilized for human and animal sustenance. These biomass exergy consumption figures are derived from estimates of daily energy intake and the efficiency of food and feed processing systems, as detailed by Wirsenius (2000). Further specifics on these accounting methods can be found in the online Supporting Information, and in works by Ayres and Warr (2009) or Ayres et al. (2003).
Societal services derived from exergy inputs are categorized into five distinct types of useful work. These categories include electricity, considered a versatile form of pure work, and prime movers, encompassing all internal and external combustion engines like steam turbines and jet engines. Heat constitutes another major category, further divided by temperature: high-temperature (over 600°C) for applications such as metal smelting, medium-temperature (100-600°C) for enhancing solubility, and low-temperature (under 100°C) for processes like water heating or drying. Low-temperature heat is even more granularly split into uses up to 20°C (general heating), around 40°C (food/tobacco drying), and 40-100°C (e.g., pulp and paper industries). The remaining categories are exergy utilized for light and muscle work, provided by both animals and humans.
The study categorized final exergy consumption by economic sector, aligning with energy statistics. However, for industrial heat, more specific data than available in standard energy statistics was required. To address this, the researchers adopted a method previously used for the United States (Lovins 1977), where industrial process heat was evenly divided between high-temperature and medium-temperature applications. This division was applied to coal, gas, and furnace oil within the industrial sector, with the exception of the iron and steel industry, which was entirely attributed to high-temperature heat. Exergy directed to residential and commercial sectors was presumed to be predominantly for low-temperature space heating. While acknowledging that this approach overlooks other uses like mechanical drive or electricity generation, the authors believe any resulting bias in their findings is negligible.
This analysis adopts a territorial approach, meaning it excludes exergy losses that occur internationally during the production of imported goods. Conversely, consumption-based accounting provides a broader view by encompassing all global resources utilized to meet a nation's final domestic consumption, thereby factoring in the upstream resource demands of traded products. Developing these consumption-based indicators is complex, typically relying on environmentally extended input-output models, a field that has seen rapid advancements. Studies on various resource types, conducted for individual nations, country clusters, or globally, consistently demonstrate that industrialized countries generally exhibit higher resource consumption when assessed from a consumption-based rather than a territorial perspective. This trend is also evident in Austria. While a consumption-based analysis was outside the scope of this study, it is a crucial area for future investigation. Therefore, it's important to acknowledge that shifts in Austria's efficiency might be partly attributable to evolving trade patterns and the relocation of exergy-intensive production processes abroad.
To offer a complete understanding of Austria's resource efficiency trends, this study incorporates environmental accounting indicators alongside exergy and useful work data. For material use, the primary metric is Domestic Material Consumption (DMC), a key indicator in material flow accounts (Eurostat 2012, 2013; Fischer-Kowalski et al. 2011; Schaffartzik et al. 2014b). DMC is determined by summing all materials extracted within Austria with physical imports, then subtracting exports. These material flows are typically grouped into four categories: biomass, fossil energy carriers, metal ores, and nonmetallic minerals, all quantified in metric tonnes. The European flagship initiative for a resource-efficient Europe (EC 2012, 2011b) also employs DMC as a general proxy for resource consumption. Land use is assessed using Human Appropriation of Net Primary Production (HANPP) (Erb et al. 2009; Haberl et al. 2007, 2012). This aggregate indicator quantifies land use and its intensification by evaluating the socioeconomic impact on ecological energy flows, specifically measuring alterations in net primary production resulting from land conversion and biomass harvesting. Finally, CO2 emissions are included, specifically those originating from the combustion of fossil fuels and cement production, reported in metric tonnes (World Bank 2014).
Austria's primary exergy inputs underwent a significant transformation between 1900 and 2012. In 1900, coal and biomass were the primary sources, contributing 60% (230 PJ) and 38% (140 PJ) respectively to the total supply of 429 PJ. By 2012, the total supply had surged to 1,495 PJ, a 3.5-fold increase, with a dramatic shift in the energy mix. Coal's share plummeted to 4% (56 PJ), and biomass, while increasing in absolute terms to 261 PJ, saw its proportion drop to 18%. Oil emerged as the leading exergy source in 2012, accounting for 30% (444 PJ), closely followed by commercial renewable energies at 30% (446 PJ), and gas at 19% (288 PJ). Hydroelectric power (190 PJ) and biomass were the main contributors within renewable energies, providing electricity and heat, respectively.
The paper categorizes exergy inputs into five types of useful work: electricity, prime movers, heat (high, medium, and low temperature), light, and muscle work, following Ayres and colleagues (2003). An analysis of final exergy consumption by these types over a century, as depicted in Figure 2, reveals significant shifts. Muscle work saw a substantial reduction, decreasing from 33% (143 PJ) of total consumption in 1900 to 17% (261 PJ) in 2012. Conversely, electricity use experienced a dramatic rise, escalating from 10% (45 PJ) in 1900 to 27% (400 PJ) in 2012. Exergy consumed for heating remained relatively stable in its proportion, moving from 38% (162 PJ) in 1900 to 31% (468 PJ) in 2012, though this figure excludes electricity used for heating, estimated at approximately 30% of total electricity consumption.
Since the 1960s, electricity has emerged as Austria's primary energy application. The nation predominantly generates its electricity from hydropower, notably without operating any nuclear power plants. Electricity's useful work output serves a diverse range of purposes: approximately one-third is consumed by the industrial sector, while about half is directed towards commercial, public services, and residential uses. The transport system, primarily railways, accounts for six percent, and the energy sector utilizes eight percent. Electricity facilitates various services, including lighting, cooling (e.g., refrigerators, air conditioning), powering industrial machinery and electronic devices, and enabling mobility (e.g., railways). Its consumption has steadily climbed, reaching 400 PJ by 2012, equating to roughly 47 GJ per person. Although electricity use in households and government sectors isn't exceptionally high, these areas have experienced the most significant growth, with a 900% increase between 1960 and 2012. This rapid expansion in electronic device ownership has offset any product-level efficiency improvements. Following electricity, heat and transport represent the next largest categories of energy use. Transport briefly surpassed heat in 2005 but subsequently reverted to 2003 levels. Both electricity and transport have seen substantial growth since the 1950s, with useful work from electricity increasing tenfold and mechanical work for transport rising by a factor of 24.
Figure 3 illustrates the composition and proportion of useful work provided in Austria between 1900 and 2012. A striking observation is the significantly reduced role of muscle power in the economy, a trend more pronounced here than when examining exergy inputs (as shown in Figure 2). This shift highlights the superior efficiency of machines in converting fuel exergy into useful work compared to human physiological conversion. The electrification of the economy stands out as a primary development, with electrical devices contributing 45% (99 PJ) of the total useful work in 2012. Since 1950, electricity provision has seen a tenfold surge. While useful work for transportation experienced an even more rapid increase, by a factor of 24, its overall share grew less dramatically than electricity, rising from 14% (3 PJ) in 1900 to 37% (81 PJ) in 2012. Conversely, although the total useful work dedicated to heating expanded from 8 PJ to 37 PJ, its share of the total useful work decreased from 40% to 17%. The useful work for lighting remained negligible throughout the period and was therefore omitted from the detailed analysis. Data from the World War periods were excluded due to quality concerns.
Even with Austria's significant reliance on renewable energy, particularly hydropower, fossil fuels remain a crucial component of its energy system. In 2012, fossil fuels constituted approximately 66% of useful work supplies and 53% of exergy inputs. Historically, the reliance on fossil fuels peaked in the early 1970s, accounting for about 70% of exergy inputs. This share then decreased through the late 1980s, stabilizing at around 60% of exergy inputs until 2004, before experiencing another decline from 2005 onwards.
Resource efficiency aims to deliver socioeconomic services using the fewest environmentally damaging inputs. This concept can be viewed from two angles. Firstly, technological efficiency focuses on optimizing physical outputs relative to physical inputs, striving to minimize resource consumption, waste, and emissions while maximizing useful outputs. Secondly, economic efficiency, often termed productivity, evaluates the economic output generated in relation to a physical flow, which can be either resource inputs or physical outputs like emissions.
The overall energy conversion efficiency of Austria's economy, calculated by comparing total useful work to total exergy inputs, showed a significant 3.3-fold increase throughout the century, rising from 4.5% to 15%. This improvement, however, was not uniform. The period between 1945 and 1980 saw the most substantial gains, averaging 0.2% annually. This acceleration was largely due to the modernization of infrastructure after the war, with older equipment being replaced by advanced technologies, and the increased utilization of highly efficient hydropower resources. Post-1980, the rate of improvement decelerated considerably to 0.06% per year. Several factors contributed to this slowdown, including an aging capital stock, a reduced proportion of hydropower in the exergy supply, and a greater reliance on exergy for transportation.
Significant efficiency gains were most evident in industrial processes involving high- and medium-temperature heat. While the exergy efficiency of transportation doubled by 1970, improvements have stalled since, aligning with the adoption of higher compression ratio gasoline engines. Electricity generation, distribution, and utilization also saw improvements until 1980, but have since stagnated or even declined, largely due to a reduced reliance on hydroelectric power compared to fossil fuels. In contrast, low-temperature heat processes experienced a much more gradual increase in exergy efficiency.
CO2 emissions and fossil fuel consumption exhibit parallel trends due to the inherent link between burning fossil fuels and emitting CO2 (as shown in Figure 6a). Variations in these trends can arise from shifts in the types of energy carriers utilized. For instance, in 1960, coal was the primary energy source, contributing 23% of exergy inputs, followed by oil at 19% and natural gas at 15%. Over the subsequent 15 years, coal's contribution steadily fell, reaching a mere 4% of exergy inputs by 2012, while oil (30%) and gas (19%) became the dominant energy sources. Despite these changes in fuel mix, the CO2 emissions generated per unit of exergy input remained relatively stable over five decades. However, advancements in technology that improve the conversion of exergy inputs into useful work outputs have led to a separation between useful work outputs and both exergy inputs and CO2 emissions (refer to Figures 5 and 6b). Between 1960 and 2012, CO2 emissions per unit of exergy input saw only a modest 12% reduction. In contrast, CO2 emissions per unit of useful work output decreased by a more significant 35% during the same period. This indicates a reduction in the environmental impact associated with the energy services consumed by Austrian society.
Austria's economic productivity, measured by the GDP/exergy ratio, consistently rose throughout the 20th century, mirroring the US's 1.2% annual increase with its own 1.1% growth. By 2012, each unit of exergy consumed generated twice the economic output compared to 1900.
While technical efficiency gains have decelerated since the 1980s, the economic efficiency of useful work in Austria has shown a contrasting trend. Figure 7 illustrates that the Austrian economy's economic efficiency, measured by the GDP/useful work ratio, actually improved from 1980 onwards, following a period of decline. Specifically, between 1940 and 1980, the efficiency of useful work decreased. In 1940, producing 1 million 1990 Geary–Khamis dollars (GK$) required 1.1 terajoules (TJ) of useful work. By 1980, this figure had risen to 1.3 TJ for the same economic output. However, after 1980, this trend reversed, with the ratio decreasing by 0.5% annually, returning to 1.1 TJ per 1 million 1990 GK$ by 2012. This rate of improvement was somewhat slower than that observed in the UK, which saw a 1.4% annual decline. The authors propose that this improvement in economic efficiency, despite the slower pace of technical efficiency gains, is attributable to a structural transformation within the Austrian economy. This shift involved a move away from energy-intensive industries with low value-added towards a service-sector dominated economy. Furthermore, changes in trade patterns, potentially involving the outsourcing of energy-intensive processes, could also contribute to this development, although this requires further empirical validation using consumption-based data. The coincidence of this structural shift with the oil crises and energy concerns of the 1970s and 1980s suggests a causal link. It is plausible that the significant increase in energy prices during that period had a lasting impact on the demand for useful work and its relationship with economic activity.
To gain a comprehensive understanding of sustainable development and identify critical problem areas, it's essential to expand beyond a narrow focus on energy use. This analysis will therefore compare energy consumption data with a broader set of resource indicators, aligning with the European Union's "dashboard of indicators" for a resource-efficient Europe. Specifically, we will examine material use, quantified by Domestic Material Consumption (DMC), land-use intensity, measured by Human Appropriation of Net Primary Production (HANPP), and carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, which represent societal physical outputs.
From 1960 to 2012, Austria experienced consistent economic growth, with its GDP increasing by a factor of 4.4, interrupted only briefly by the 2008 and 2009 economic crises. This economic expansion coincided with a general increase across all resource use indicators, signifying a broader consumption of resources within Austria. Useful work outputs demonstrated the most substantial growth, multiplying by 3.4. Exergy inputs and CO2 emissions saw comparable increases, by factors of 2.4 and 2.1 respectively. Material use, measured by DMC, exhibited the slowest growth, increasing by a factor of 1.6. Notably, land-use intensity, as indicated by HANPP, showed minimal change, rising by only 22%, suggesting a relatively stable impact on land resources.
While all resource use indicators show a relative decoupling from economic growth, meaning their growth rates are slower than GDP, none exhibit an absolute decline, which would signify a reduction in environmental pressure. The most substantial productivity gains were seen in the GDP/land-use ratio (3.3-fold increase) and material productivity (2.7-fold increase). Productivity related to CO2 emissions and exergy increased at comparable rates (2.1-fold and 1.8-fold, respectively), with the smallest gains observed for the GDP/useful work output ratio (1.3-fold increase).
Austria's progress towards national and European policy objectives is now assessed. The EU's 2012 Energy Strategy set three goals for 2020, using a 1990 baseline: a 20% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, a 20% increase in renewable energy share, and a 20% improvement in energy efficiency. Austria's performance against these targets shows mixed results. While the country's CO2 emissions actually rose by 13% between 1990 and 2012, failing the first objective, it significantly surpassed the renewable energy target. In 2012, renewable sources, largely hydropower, accounted for 30% of exergy inputs, exceeding the EU's 20% goal. Regarding energy efficiency, Austria achieved a 25% improvement between 1990 and 2020. However, this gain was primarily driven by economic growth rather than an absolute decrease in energy consumption, whether measured by exergy inputs or useful work outputs.
Austria's national resource efficiency targets, outlined in a 2012 action plan by the Federal Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, Environment and Water Management, aimed for a 50% increase in material productivity and a 20% reduction in Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) between 2008 and 2020. However, data indicates these goals were not met, with DMC decreasing by only 2% and material efficiency improving by just 4% during that period. The EU, meanwhile, has yet to finalize specific material use targets.
Modern lifestyles and industrial production fundamentally depend on energy. The 20th century saw a rapid evolution in energy consumption patterns, resulting in a substantial increase in overall energy use and a shift in the underlying socioeconomic energy structure. However, a more precise energy analysis reveals that not all exergy input is effectively utilized. Significant portions are lost during energy transformation processes and due to technological constraints and inefficiencies in the usage phase. The usable portion, which ultimately meets societal demands, is termed "useful work." The ratio of exergy input to useful work serves as an indicator of the economy's overall efficiency in energy transformation and utilization. This ratio also highlights the extent of exergy input wasted during socioeconomic processes. In Austria, for example, only 15% of the available natural resource exergy input was converted into useful work. While this aggregate efficiency showed a remarkable improvement from 5% in 1900 to 15% by 2012, it remains a low figure, signifying that 85% of natural resource exergy input is still lost. This raises critical questions about the potential for further technical progress in efficiency and the inherent limitations.
Electricity stands out as the primary and fastest-growing category when examining exergy inputs by their final use. It's important to recognize that this electricity serves various energy needs, including heating and lighting, thereby supporting other consumption categories. The bulk of electricity consumption occurs in households, commercial, and public services, with the remaining 30% directed towards industrial production. The residential and commercial sectors exhibit the most significant growth, reflecting the expanding electrification of homes and services, enhanced living standards, and the service sector's increasing economic prominence.
Austria's energy landscape is significantly shaped by hydropower, a renewable source highly efficient in transforming exergy into useful work, accounting for over half of all useful work supplied. Following electricity, transport demands represent the second-largest portion of useful work consumption in 2012. Both these demands are escalating, yet the traditional exergy sources supporting them face limitations. The future scarcity of oil is a recognized concern, necessitating the identification of alternative exergy sources for transport. Similarly, Austria's capacity for increasing hydropower generation is limited, as most viable sites have already been developed. This situation raises critical questions regarding the availability of alternative exergy sources, and how Austria's socioeconomic energy system and prevailing technologies can be adapted to guarantee future energy service provision.
Throughout the twentieth century, Austria consistently improved its exergy productivity relative to GDP, demonstrating technological advancements that allowed for greater useful work output per unit of exergy input. However, the useful work productivity of GDP followed a more complex trajectory, exhibiting three distinct periods. Initially, productivity saw a modest increase until the conclusion of World War II. This was succeeded by a sharp decrease that persisted until the late 1960s, after which it stabilized. A significant shift occurred in the early 1970s, marking the beginning of a phase of increasing productivity. This pattern suggests that the Austrian economy experienced a decline in efficiency during the first seven decades of the twentieth century, particularly during the post-WWII reconstruction era. Furthermore, the efficiency improvements observed towards the century's end were not substantial. The turnaround in useful work productivity, which began around the early 1970s—a trend also observed in other nations like the United States, UK, and Japan—is strongly linked to the oil crises and the subsequent surge in energy prices. These events likely spurred a lasting improvement in the efficiency of useful work consumption. It is also probable that ongoing efforts to enhance resource use efficiency, driven by the imperative to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from energy consumption, have solidified this trend of decreasing useful work intensity within the economy.
Examining resource efficiency more broadly, beyond just energy, using indicators like material use, land use, and CO2 emissions, shows that Austria's improvements in energy efficiency are actually surpassed by other resource efficiency metrics. For instance, land-use intensity has remained largely stable in recent times, indicating strong land-use efficiency. Material productivity has seen a moderate rise, primarily due to material consumption stagnating since the early 1970s. It's important to note that these efficiency gains are primarily linked to economic expansion; resource consumption has slowed but not declined, signifying a relative rather than absolute decoupling from GDP growth. While Austria's resource use and efficiency trends align with the European Energy Strategy, they fall short of the national material productivity goal outlined in the Austria Resource Efficiency Action Plan. The influence of evolving trade patterns and the potential outsourcing of resource-intensive activities to other nations on Austria's resource efficiency are factors not addressed in this analysis but warrant future investigation.
