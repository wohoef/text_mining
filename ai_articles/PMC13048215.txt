This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the potential link between proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk. The study was prompted by recent concerns regarding this association, despite PPIs being widely prescribed globally for conditions like peptic ulcers and gastro-esophageal reflux disease. To achieve this, researchers systematically reviewed English-language literature, employing search terms such as "hepatocellular carcinoma," "liver cancer," "hepatic tumor," and "proton pump inhibitor" across major medical databases. A meta-analysis was then conducted on the eligible studies. The analysis initially identified five studies suggesting an association between PPIs and HCC, with an unadjusted risk ratio (RR) of 2.27 (95% CI: 1.44-3.57; p < 0.01). Furthermore, a linear relationship was observed, indicating that higher cumulative PPI doses might elevate HCC risk (p < 0.01). However, a crucial finding emerged when data were adjusted for confounding factors like comorbidities and co-occurring medications: the association between PPI use and HCC became statistically insignificant (adjusted RR = 1.62, 95% CI: 0.89-2.93; p = 0.11). This lack of significance was consistently upheld in sensitivity analyses. In conclusion, the meta-analysis indicates that PPI use does not substantially increase HCC risk once confounding variables are accounted for. Nevertheless, the authors recommend further research to confirm these findings, particularly within specific patient groups, such as those with viral or alcoholic liver diseases.

While proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are commonly prescribed for conditions like peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and *Helicobacter pylori* eradication, and are generally deemed safe and effective, their long-term use has been linked to several adverse effects. These include *Clostridium difficile*-associated diarrhea, vitamin and mineral deficiencies, hip fractures, and community-acquired pneumonia. Recent reports also highlight a trend of PPI overprescription, particularly in chronic liver disease patients, where 30-50% of prescriptions might be inappropriate.
While PPI-induced liver damage is uncommon, concerns exist regarding PPI safety in chronic liver disease patients. Studies indicate PPI use correlates with more severe cirrhosis and a higher risk of complications like ascites, SBP, and HE. Furthermore, PPIs have been linked to increased readmission and mortality rates in individuals with chronic liver conditions.
PPIs are considered a potential carcinogen, with long-term use linked to an elevated risk of various cancers, including colorectal, esophageal, gastric, and pancreatic types. This carcinogenic potential in the gastrointestinal tract is possibly due to PPIs inducing hypergastrinemia and gut microbiome dysbiosis. While animal studies, specifically with omeprazole, have indicated liver tumor promotion, and some clinical research suggests a connection between PPIs and increased HCC risk, other recent studies have presented conflicting findings regarding this association. The question of whether prolonged PPI use elevates HCC risk remains a subject of ongoing investigation.
The impact of PPI use on HCC risk is uncertain. This study, therefore, conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the association between PPIs and hepatocellular carcinoma.
To identify relevant English-language articles on PPI use and HCC, a comprehensive literature search was performed across major medical databases (PubMed, Medline, Embase, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews). The search, covering publications up to January 2019, utilized specific medical subject headings: "hepatocellular carcinoma," "liver cancer," "hepatic tumor," and "proton pump inhibitor." Initial screening involved reviewing titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation of selected articles. Additionally, reference lists of retrieved papers were manually examined to find further pertinent studies. Two independent researchers assessed article eligibility, with any disagreements resolved by consulting coauthors.
For inclusion in this analysis, studies needed to be full-length articles where participants received PPIs such as omeprazole or lansoprazole. Crucially, they also had to provide data comparing patients both with and without PPI exposure, and with and without HCC. Conversely, studies were excluded if they involved duplicate cohorts or lacked complete data for effect measures like odds ratios or risk ratios. The characteristics examined for each included study encompassed the authors, publication year, participant ethnicity, study design (e.g., case-control), and the specific type of PPI used.
To assess publication bias, funnel plots were employed. The association between HCC incidence and PPI use was quantified using risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager version 5.3.5, applying inverse variance as the primary method. Inter-study heterogeneity was identified if I2 statistics were ≥50% or the χ2 test for Cochran Q statistics yielded p < 0.10. In cases of significant heterogeneity, a random effect model was chosen for pooling data.
To evaluate the relationship between PPI dosage and HCC risk, a dose-response meta-analysis was conducted. This analysis utilized the two-stage generalized least squares for trend (GLST) method, which first determined individual study slope lines before calculating an overall average slope. The data points for this analysis included the median PPI dose, reported risk ratios (RRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for effect size, and the number of cases and controls within each quantitative group. When the median PPI dose was not explicitly stated for a group, it was approximated by taking the midpoint of its lower and upper limits. For open-ended highest quantitative groups, the difference between the lowest range and the median was assumed to be consistent with that of the nearest adjacent category. Each relevant RR was then assigned its corresponding cumulative dose. Only two studies provided the necessary RRs and 95% CIs for inclusion in this GLST dose-response analysis.
From an initial search yielding 37 citations, five studies met the criteria for meta-analysis (Fig. 1). These included four case-controlled studies and one prospective study. Geographically, three originated from Asia, one from the United States, and one from Europe. Three of these studies also provided data on cumulative PPI exposure (Table 1).
Unadjusted risk ratios in the meta-analysis revealed a significant link between PPI use and HCC (RR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.44-3.57; p < 0.01).
Li's study examined HCC risk across various cumulative defined daily doses (cDDD) of PPIs, ranging from 30 to over 900. However, the overall impact of PPIs on HCC incidence couldn't be determined from this particular study. Consequently, for the meta-analysis, the 181-540 cDDD range was selected to represent Li's findings, as other dose groups were deemed either excessively high or low.
A dose-response relationship between PPI use and HCC was identified for long-term exposure. This was determined using a linear regression model, which calculated a summary RR of 1.24 (95% CI: 1.22-1.26; p < 0.01) for every 50-unit increase in cumulative PPI dose. Only two studies provided the necessary RRs and 95% CIs for this analysis; Li's study was excluded due to insufficient data on HCC cases and PPI users.
Lai et al., Tran et al., and Shao et al. provided both unadjusted and adjusted risk ratios in their respective studies. These adjustments accounted for various factors, including age, cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), diabetes mellitus, and concomitant medications like statins, metformin, and aspirin. When these adjusted risk ratios were incorporated into the meta-analysis, the link between PPI use and HCC was no longer statistically significant (adjusted RR = 1.62, 95% CI: 0.89-2.93; p = 0.11). A subsequent sensitivity analysis, necessitated by significant heterogeneity (I2 = 97%; p < 0.01), confirmed this lack of significance, except when Lai et al.'s study was excluded.
The potential link between PPI use and HCC risk has been a topic of debate. This study identified a possible association, including a dose-response relationship, with increased HCC risk from PPIs. Nevertheless, this connection disappeared once confounding factors were accounted for in the data analysis.
Long-term PPI use leads to hypergastrinemia due to inhibited hydrogen transport in stomach parietal cells, which reduces gastric acid. Gastrin, a key trophic factor in the GI tract and regulator of acid secretion, along with its receptor (cholecystokinin B receptor), is expressed in HCC and may promote tumor growth.
PPIs may contribute to carcinogenesis by disrupting the gut microbiota. This dysbiosis can lead to intestinal inflammation, and bacteria, along with their byproducts, might then enter the bloodstream through a compromised gut barrier, triggering systemic chronic inflammation. These substances can subsequently reach the liver via the portal vein, activating pro-inflammatory pathways. Such processes are implicated in the development of hepatitis, fibrosis, and cancer. Furthermore, the gut microbiota is crucial for regulating bile acids. Imbalances in bile acid regulation and an increase in fermented products from a dysbiotic gut microbiome could be linked to HCC formation. PPIs are known to cause gut microbiota dysbiosis, fostering the proliferation of potentially harmful bacteria like *Clostridium difficile*, *Enterococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Staphylococcus*, and *Escherichia coli*. A study by Grąt et al. in Poland observed a significant increase in fecal *Escherichia coli* counts in HCC patients compared to those without HCC, although this pattern wasn't seen with other enterobacteria. Further research is necessary to confirm if PPI-induced changes in gut microbiome composition directly cause liver carcinogenesis.
Certain PPIs, specifically omeprazole, lansoprazole, and pantoprazole, induce CYP1A, which can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). AhR is implicated in tumor promotion, notably by dioxin. Animal studies have shown other CYP1A inducers can promote liver tumors and increase oxidative stress. Omeprazole, for instance, promoted hepatocellular tumors in rats by significantly increasing AhR gene expression.
Dultz et al. observed that PPI use correlated with elevated MELD scores and ascites in cirrhotic patients, independently increasing mortality risk beyond MELD, HCC, and hepatic decompensation. Additionally, PPIs might heighten the risk of hepatic encephalopathy (HE) and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) in cirrhotic individuals. A study by Llorente et al. indicated that PPIs could exacerbate alcoholic liver disease through gut microbiota alterations.
The relationship between PPI use and HCC has been explored in several studies, yielding varied conclusions. In 2013, a study by Lai et al. in Taiwan indicated no significant increase in HCC risk with PPI use, even after accounting for factors like age, cirrhosis, alcoholic liver damage, NAFLD, viral hepatitis, diabetes, and other medications. However, 2018 saw the publication of two studies suggesting a potential link. Li et al., from the United States, observed an elevated HCC risk among chronic hepatitis C patients using PPIs. Concurrently, Shao et al. in Taiwan reported a dose-response relationship between PPI use and increased HCC risk in individuals without viral hepatitis or cirrhosis. In contrast, Tran et al. in the United Kingdom, analyzing data from the UK Biobank and PCCIU, found no significant association between PPI use and HCC. Their analysis adjusted for numerous variables, including age, gender, BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking, other medications, and comorbidities like cirrhosis, hepatitis, diabetes, and peptic ulcer disease. Similarly, Kao et al., also from Taiwan, specifically examined patients with chronic hepatitis B and C infections and concluded that PPI use, even long-term, did not correlate with HCC development. A 2018 meta-analysis also concluded no association between PPI use and HCC. However, this meta-analysis included patients with both HCC and cholangiocarcinoma, a factor that could have skewed its findings. The current study, while utilizing some of the same studies, differentiated itself by focusing exclusively on HCC patients.
The current meta-analysis initially identified a notable link between PPI use and HCC when analyzing unadjusted risk ratios. Nevertheless, the diverse characteristics of the study populations introduced significant variability. For instance, Lai et al.'s research encompassed a broad spectrum of patients, including those with viral hepatitis, cirrhosis, alcoholic liver disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). In contrast, Li et al. focused on individuals with Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection undergoing anti-HCV treatment, deliberately excluding patients with prior hepatic decompensation, existing HCC, or co-infection with HIV or Hepatitis B virus (HBV). It's worth noting that in Li et al.'s study, PPI users exhibited higher baseline HCV RNA levels, diminished sustained viral response rates, and a greater prevalence of alcohol abuse or smoking history compared to non-PPI users, factors that could have influenced the study's outcomes. Shao et al. adopted a different approach, excluding patients with viral hepatitis or cirrhosis from their baseline population. Tran et al. utilized two distinct datasets from the UK Biobank and PCCIU for separate analyses. The PCCIU data, however, were not incorporated into the current study due to its inclusion of both HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma patients, which would have complicated the specific assessment of PPI's impact on HCC. The UK Biobank data, which contained a small percentage of patients with cirrhosis (0.1%) and hepatitis (0.5%), indicated no association between PPI use and HCC. Kao et al.'s study specifically examined patients with either chronic HBV or HCV infection, excluding those with dual infections. Their findings revealed no significant connection between PPI use and HCC in either the HBV or HCV groups, even among patients experiencing hepatic decompensation. This suggests that within homogeneous cohorts of HBV or HCV carriers, the association between PPI use and HCC was negligible. A crucial observation was that viral hepatitis, a recognized risk factor for HCC, might overshadow any potential effect of PPIs. Indeed, when the meta-analysis adjusted for coexisting liver diseases and other medications, the link between PPI use and HCC disappeared. Unfortunately, a detailed subgroup analysis for specific coexisting liver diseases, such as viral hepatitis infection, was not feasible due to the limited number of studies and insufficient data regarding viral hepatitis status across all included research.
This study identified a dose-response link between PPIs and HCC. However, only two studies supported this finding, necessitating further research to confirm if extended PPI use elevates HCC risk.
This meta-analysis had several limitations. A key issue was the varied patient populations across the included studies; some concentrated on viral hepatitis patients, while one specifically excluded those with HBV or HCV. Additionally, hepatic function and antiviral treatment details were often poorly defined. Furthermore, most studies were case-controlled, meaning unmeasured confounders like alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, smoking, and family history could have influenced results. Finally, the potential misclassification of over-the-counter PPI users as non-users also presents a limitation.
This meta-analysis concludes that PPI use does not significantly elevate HCC risk. However, the possibility of a dose-response relationship between PPIs and HCC, suggested by a few studies, requires further investigation.
