Soybean DAS-81419-2, developed through Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation, expresses Cry1F and Cry1Ac proteins for resistance to specific lepidopteran species. It also produces the PAT protein, conferring tolerance to glufosinate ammonium-based herbicides, which served as a selectable marker. Molecular characterization of soybean DAS-81419-2 revealed no safety issues. Field assessments of its agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional traits showed no significant differences compared to its conventional counterpart, alleviating concerns for food, feed, and environmental safety. The newly expressed Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins were deemed safe regarding toxicity and allergenicity. The genetic modification did not appear to significantly alter the overall allergenicity of soybean DAS-81419-2. Consequently, its nutritional value is expected to be comparable to non-GM soybean varieties, negating the need for post-market food/feed monitoring. Regarding environmental impact, the likelihood of feral soybean DAS-81419-2 plants establishing and spreading is not increased, except under exposure to glufosinate ammonium-based herbicides or susceptible insect pests. These scenarios would not lead to different environmental impacts than conventional soybean. Interactions with the biotic and abiotic environment were not considered an issue within the application's scope. Theoretical risks of horizontal gene transfer to bacteria were not identified. The post-market environmental monitoring plan and reporting intervals align with the intended uses. The GMO Panel concluded that soybean DAS-81419-2 is as safe and nutritious as its conventional counterpart and tested non-GM reference varieties, within the scope of the application.

The EFSA Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms (GMO Panel) was tasked with evaluating the safety of insect-resistant genetically modified (GM) soybean DAS-81419-2. This evaluation followed an application (EFSA-GMO-NL-2013-116) submitted by Dow AgroSciences LLC under Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. The application's scope covers the import, processing, and food and feed uses of this soybean within the European Union, explicitly excluding cultivation. Soybean DAS-81419-2 incorporates genes for Cry1F and Cry1Ac proteins, providing resistance to lepidopteran pests. Additionally, it expresses the pat gene, which confers tolerance to glufosinate ammonium-based herbicides and served solely as a selectable marker. Consequently, herbicide tolerance was not included in the comparative analyses, aligning with the application's scope.
The GMO Panel assessed soybean DAS‐81419‐2 according to its risk assessment guidelines for GM plants, considering the application's scope. This evaluation encompassed the molecular characterization of the inserted DNA and protein expression. It also included comparative analyses of the plant's composition, agronomic traits, and phenotype. Furthermore, the safety of the novel proteins and the overall food/feed was examined for toxicity, allergenicity, and nutritional value. Finally, the environmental risk assessment and the post-market environmental monitoring plan (PMEM) were reviewed.
Soybean DAS-81419-2 was created through Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation of Maverick soybean seeds. This genetically modified soybean expresses Cry1F and Cry1Ac proteins, providing resistance to lepidopteran pests, and the PAT protein, which confers tolerance to glufosinate ammonium-based herbicides and served as a selectable marker. Molecular characterization confirmed a single insert containing the cry1Fv3, cry1Ac (synpro), and pat expression cassettes, responsible for producing these proteins. No other plasmid components were found in the soybean. Bioinformatic analyses and genetic stability studies revealed no safety concerns. The expression levels of the newly introduced proteins were adequately determined. Furthermore, characterization data demonstrated that the plant-produced Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins are structurally, biochemically, and functionally equivalent to their microbial-derived counterparts.
Field trials of soybean DAS‐81419‐2 revealed no significant agronomic or phenotypic differences compared to its conventional counterpart, with the exception of '100 seed weight'. Furthermore, seed germination tests under controlled conditions showed no relevant variations between the GM soybean and its non-GM counterpart.
No safety concerns were raised regarding the toxicity or allergenicity of the newly expressed Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins in soybean DAS-81419-2. Furthermore, the genetic modification did not appear to significantly alter the overall allergenicity of the soybean when compared to its conventional counterpart and non-GM reference varieties. Consequently, the nutritional value of food and feed from soybean DAS-81419-2 is anticipated to be consistent with non-GM soybean varieties. Due to the lack of identified safety concerns, the GMO Panel deemed post-market monitoring of food and feed unnecessary. The application, which permits import, processing, and food/feed use but excludes cultivation, necessitates an environmental risk assessment focused on accidental seed release during transport or processing, and bacterial exposure to recombinant DNA in animal digestive tracts and related environments.
Soybean's inherent low survival capacity means that the reduced '100 seed weight' observed in DAS-81419-2 is unlikely to impact its fitness, such as survival, reproduction, or competitiveness, nor its invasiveness. Should viable seeds of DAS-81419-2 be accidentally released into the environment, there is no increased probability of feral plants establishing or spreading. This is unless they are exposed to glufosinate ammonium-based herbicides or affected by insect pests susceptible to the Cry1F and Cry1Ac proteins. The GMO Panel considers that such scenarios would not lead to environmental impacts distinct from those of conventional soybean. Given the application's scope, interactions with the biotic and abiotic environment are not deemed significant. While bioinformatic analysis of the inserted DNA revealed sequence identity with bacterial DNA, potentially facilitating the transfer of plant codon-optimized cry and pat genes to a soil bacterium's plasmid, the GMO Panel found no safety concerns regarding horizontal gene transfer. This conclusion is based on the function of the encoded proteins and the widespread presence of natural variants of these genes in environmental bacterial communities. Therefore, considering the introduced traits, comparative analysis results, exposure routes, and limited exposure levels, the GMO Panel concluded that accidental release of viable GM soybean seeds would not pose safety concerns. The proposed post-market environmental monitoring plan and its reporting intervals align with the intended uses of soybean DAS-81419-2 and relevant GMO Panel guidelines.
The GMO Panel based its scientific opinion on application EFSA-GMO-NL-2013-116, supplementary data from the applicant, Member State comments, and pertinent scientific literature. Ultimately, the Panel concluded that the available information on soybean DAS-81419-2 adequately addresses Member State concerns. Therefore, under the specified processing conditions and within the application's scope, soybean DAS-81419-2 is deemed as safe and nutritious as its conventional counterpart and the evaluated non-GM reference varieties concerning potential impacts on human and animal health, as well as the environment.

Soybean DAS-81419-2 was created through Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation. This process involved using the A. tumefaciens strain EHA101, which contained the binary plasmid pDAB9582, to transform cotyledonary nodes of the soybean variety Maverick. The transfer-deoxyribonucleic acid (T-DNA) within plasmid pDAB9582, situated between its right and left border regions, comprised several key elements. These included a partial matrix attachment region (MAR) sequence from the Nicotiana tabacum rb-7-5A gene. For constitutive expression, an AtUbi10 promoter, 5' untranslated region (UTR), and intron from the Arabidopsis thaliana polyubiquitin 10 (UBQ10) gene were utilized. The T-DNA also contained a synthetic cry1Fv3 coding sequence, designed to produce the chimeric Cry1F protein. This sequence was derived from parts of the cry1Fa2 and cry1Ca3 genes of B. thuringiensis subsp. aizawai strain PS811, along with a portion of the cry1Ab1 gene from B. thuringiensis subsp. berliner strain 1715. Transcription termination and polyadenylation were directed by the 3' UTR of open reading frame (ORF) 23 from the A. tumefaciens plasmid pTi15955. Furthermore, a Cassava vein mosaic virus (CsVMV) promoter and 5' UTR were included to drive constitutive expression. A synthetic cry1Ac (synpro) coding sequence was present to produce the chimeric Cry1Ac protein. This sequence was assembled from a part of the cry1Ac1 gene from B. thuringiensis subsp. kurstaki strain HD73, a part of the cry1Ca3 gene from B. thuringiensis subsp. aizawai strain PS811, and a part of the cry1Ab1 gene from B. thuringiensis subsp. berliner strain 1715. Transcription termination and polyadenylation for this gene were also managed by the 3' UTR of AtuORF23 from the A. tumefaciens plasmid pTi15955. Finally, a pat coding sequence from S. viridochromogenes was incorporated, with transcription termination and polyadenylation governed by the 3' UTR of AtuORF1 from the A. tumefaciens plasmid pTi15955.
Outside the T-DNA, the plasmid vector contained additional functional elements not expected to transfer to the soybean genome. These included the ori origin of replication from the RK2 plasmid for bacterial maintenance of pDAB9582, the trfA replication initiator from the RK2 plasmid, and the SpecR bacterial selectable marker gene from E. coli Tn7 transposon for spectinomycin resistance.
Molecular characterization of soybean DAS-81419-2 involved Southern analysis, PCR, and DNA sequencing. These methods determined the insert's copy number, size, and organization, confirming the absence of plasmid backbone sequences. The approach was deemed acceptable for its coverage and sensitivity.
Soybean DAS-81419-2 was found to contain a single insert of T-DNA, matching the configuration in the pDAB9582 vector, as confirmed by Southern analyses. Multiple restriction enzyme and probe combinations covering the T-DNA and flanking regions verified the insert and copy number. PCR analyses corroborated these findings, and Southern analysis using backbone-specific probes demonstrated the absence of vector backbone sequences.
The complete nucleotide sequence of the insert in soybean DAS‐81419‐2, along with its 5′ and 3′ flanking regions (1,297 bp and 1,379 bp respectively), was determined. This 12,496 bp insert is identical to the T‐DNA of the pDAB9582 plasmid, with the exception of deletions at the 5′ and 3′ border regions (23 bp and 408 bp, respectively). During integration, 37 bp of filler DNA and a 98 bp fragment of cry1Ac (synpro) were added at the 5′ junction, and 9 bp of filler DNA at the 3′ junction. Analysis of the pre-insertion locus revealed that 57 bp of soybean genomic DNA were deleted. Bioinformatic evaluation of the pre-insertion locus and flanking genomic sequences did not indicate that the insertion event DAS‐81419‐2 interrupted any known endogenous soybean genes.
Segregation and bioinformatics analyses confirmed the insert is in the nuclear genome.
Bioinformatic analyses of the amino acid sequences of the newly expressed Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins showed no significant similarities to toxins or allergens. Further analysis of new open reading frames within the inserts and at their junctions indicated that the expression of ORFs with significant similarities to toxins or allergens is highly unlikely.
To evaluate the potential for horizontal gene transfer (HGT) via homologous recombination (HR), the applicant analyzed the bacterial DNA sequences present in soybean DAS‐81419‐2. Three regions were found to have sufficient length and similarity to facilitate HR. These included a 709 bp fragment from the *A. tumefaciens* Ti plasmid pTi15955, containing the 3′ untranslated region of AtuORF1, and two fragments (526 bp and 457 bp) from the same plasmid, containing the 3′ untranslated region of AtuORF23. The possibility of a double HR event involving these sequences and the *A. tumefaciens* Ti plasmid was considered. The potential risks and outcomes of gene transfer from plants to bacteria are detailed elsewhere in the report. Soybean DAS‐81419‐2 produces three novel proteins: Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT.
For safety testing, the required protein quantities could not be obtained from plants. Therefore, the proteins were produced in microbes, specifically *Pseudomonas fluorescens* or *E. coli*. Before safety studies, biochemical methods were used to confirm the equivalence of these microbe-derived proteins with those from soybean. Their physicochemical, structural, and functional properties were characterized and compared.
Plant and microbe-derived Cry1F proteins were analyzed using SDS-PAGE and western blot, revealing a consistent molecular weight of approximately 130 kDa and similar immunoreactivity to specific antibodies. Glycosylation analysis confirmed the absence of N-glycosylation in both forms. Mass spectrometry confirmed that the amino acid sequences matched expectations. Insect feeding bioassays demonstrated comparable insecticidal activity, establishing functional equivalence between the plant and microbe-produced Cry1F proteins.
The Cry1Ac protein, whether derived from plants or microbes, was characterized using SDS-PAGE and western blot, revealing a molecular weight of approximately 130 kDa and comparable immunoreactivity to specific antibodies. Glycosylation analysis confirmed the absence of N-glycosylation in both forms. Mass spectrometry confirmed that the amino acid sequences matched expectations. Insect feeding bioassays demonstrated equivalent insecticidal activity between the plant and microbe-derived Cry1Ac proteins.
Plant and microbe-derived PAT proteins were analyzed using SDS-PAGE and western blot, revealing a consistent molecular weight of approximately 20 kDa and comparable immunoreactivity to specific antibodies. Glycosylation analysis confirmed the absence of N-glycosylation in both forms. Mass spectrometry confirmed that the amino acid sequences of both plant and microbe-derived PAT proteins matched their expected sequences. Furthermore, an in vitro biochemical activity assay demonstrated equivalent activity and specificity towards the target herbicide, establishing functional equivalence.
The GMO Panel accepts the use of Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins produced in P. fluorescens or E. coli for safety studies.

Southern analysis of genomic DNA from five generations confirmed the genetic stability of the soybean DAS‐81419‐2 insert. Sufficient restriction enzyme/probe combinations demonstrated that all tested plants retained a single copy of the insert and flanking regions, which were stably inherited across generations.
Segregation analysis, PAT protein expression, and event-specific PCR data confirmed phenotypic stability. These results supported a single insertion that segregated in a Mendelian fashion.
Soybean DAS‐81419‐2 contains a single insert with one copy of the Cry1Fv3, Cry1Ac (synpro), and pat expression cassettes, which produce the Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins. Safety was not compromised by bioinformatic analyses of the newly expressed proteins and ORFs within the insert or at junction sites with genomic DNA. The stability of the inserted DNA and the insect resistance and herbicide tolerance traits was confirmed across multiple generations. Adequate reporting of Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT protein levels was provided. Furthermore, characterization data demonstrated that plant-derived and microbial-derived Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins are equivalent in their structural, biochemical, and functional properties.
Field trials in the USA (2011-2012) provided data on soybean DAS-81419-2's agronomic and phenotypic traits, along with its forage and seed composition.
Field trials assessing the agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional characteristics of soybean DAS‐81419‐2 took place in major US soybean-growing regions, encompassing varied agricultural practices and environmental conditions. Each trial site utilized a randomized complete block design with four replicates, growing soybean DAS‐81419‐2, its conventional counterpart, and three non-GM reference varieties. All plants received necessary pesticides, including conventional herbicides, as per local requirements. A total of nine non-GM soybean reference varieties were included across all trial sites.
Soybean DAS-81419-2 was developed using the non-GM soybean variety Maverick as the recipient. Pedigree documentation confirms that the DAS-81419-2 line used in field trials was not cross-bred with other soybean lines. Maverick, possessing the same genetic background as DAS-81419-2, served as the comparator in these trials. The GMO Panel deemed this non-transgenic line to be the suitable conventional counterpart.
Agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional data from 2011-2012 field trials were statistically analyzed according to GMO Panel recommendations. This involved difference testing between the GM soybean and its conventional counterpart, alongside equivalence testing against non-GM commercial varieties. Equivalence test results were categorized into four outcomes, from equivalence to non-equivalence.
Agronomic and phenotypic characteristics of soybean DAS‐81419‐2 were evaluated across 11 U.S. field trial sites in 2011 and 2012. These included early and final stand counts, seedling vigour, days to 50% flowering and maturity, disease and insect incidence, lodging, plant height, number of pods and seeds, pod shattering, yield, and 100 seed weight. For four endpoints unsuitable for parametric analysis due to their measurement nature, differences between the GM soybean and its conventional counterpart were assessed using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank (WSR) test. Pod shattering was excluded from statistical analysis as all recorded values were zero.
In field trials, statistically significant differences were observed between soybean DAS‐81419‐2 and its conventional counterpart for several agronomic and phenotypic characteristics, including days to 50% flowering, early stand count, plant height, number of pods, number of seeds, '100 seed weight', and yield. With the exception of '100 seed weight', all these endpoints fell within equivalence category I, meaning they were within the established limits set by non-GM reference varieties. '100 seed weight' was categorized as equivalence category II, indicating a higher likelihood of equivalence than non-equivalence. 'Final population' was not categorized for equivalence due to limited variation among non-GM varieties but was not significantly different from the conventional counterpart. Analysis using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank test also revealed statistically significant differences in plant lodging and seedling vigor; however, the mean values for the GM soybean in these instances remained within the range observed for non-GM reference varieties.
To assess seed germination, soybean DAS‐81419‐2 was compared to its near-isogenic non-GM counterpart. Four replicates of 100 seeds for each line were tested under both warm and cold conditions using a randomized design. The warm treatment involved 5 days at 25°C, while the cold treatment consisted of 7 days at 10°C followed by 5 days at 25°C. Results showed no significant difference in germination rates between soybean DAS‐81419‐2 and its conventional counterpart under either temperature regime.
Field trials in the USA during 2011 and 2012 yielded soybean forage and seeds that were analyzed for 87 components, adhering to OECD guidelines. Of these, 9 were in forage and 78 in seed. For 17 seed constituents, over half of the data points were below the quantification limit. Statistical analysis was then performed on the remaining 70 constituents.
Equivalence could not be determined for four seed endpoints due to minimal variation in non-GM varieties. Three of these endpoints showed significant differences between soybean DAS-81419-2 and its conventional counterpart, requiring further consideration.
For 66 endpoints, a combination of difference and equivalence tests was used. Soybean DAS‐81419‐2 showed statistically significant differences from its conventional counterpart in 23 endpoints, including ash in forage and 22 seed endpoints. Most of these fell into equivalence category I, with the exception of linoleic acid (18:2), which was in category IV.
The GMO Panel reviewed compositional differences between soybean DAS‐81419‐2 and its conventional counterpart. Considering the compounds' biological roles and observed changes, no further food or feed safety assessment was deemed necessary.
No further food and feed safety assessment is needed for soybean DAS-81419-2, as identified compositional, agronomic, and phenotypic differences with its conventional counterpart do not require it.
Field trials of soybean DAS‐81419‐2 revealed no significant differences in agronomic and phenotypic characteristics compared to its conventional counterpart, with the exception of '100 seed weight'. Seed germination under controlled conditions also showed no relevant differences. The '100 seed weight' variation was further evaluated for its potential environmental impact.
Processing soybean DAS‐81419‐2 into food and feed products is not expected to yield different results compared to commercial non-GM soybean varieties, based on the comparative assessment.
The GMO Panel previously determined that Cry1F and Cry1Ac proteins lose their insecticidal activity when heated to 75°C or 90°C at pH 7.5 for 30 minutes. For this application, PAT protein was subjected to similar heating conditions (25–95°C for 30 minutes). Results showed that the PAT protein lost 99% of its enzymatic activity at temperatures of 55°C and above, and 91% of its immunoreactivity at temperatures of 37°C and above.
Soybean DAS‐81419‐2 expresses three proteins: Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT. These proteins have been thoroughly characterized, exhibiting expected molecular weights, immunoreactivity to specific antibodies, amino acid sequences, and functional activities. Cry1F and Cry1Ac are delta-endotoxins with targeted insecticidal effects, acting via cellular receptors in insects. Mammalian gastrointestinal tracts, including humans, are reported to lack high-affinity receptors for Cry proteins. The GMO Panel has previously assessed these Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins, finding no safety concerns for humans or animals in prior applications. Recent bioinformatic analyses further support the absence of significant similarities between these newly expressed proteins and known toxins.
Studies on Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins produced in bacterial recombinant systems were provided, and these were deemed equivalent to the plant-produced versions.
The pepsin resistance of bacterial Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins was examined in three separate studies at pH ~ 1.2. Previous assessments by the GMO Panel covered Cry1F and Cry1Ac. For the current application, a study on the PAT protein was provided, analyzing its integrity over time using SDS-PAGE and western blotting. This analysis revealed that the PAT protein was degraded by pepsin within one minute.
Previous assessments by the EFSA GMO Panel (2010c) found no adverse effects in mice gavaged with a mixture of Cry1F and Cry1Ac proteins (375 and 350 mg/kg body weight, respectively), nor with PAT administered at 5,000 mg/kg. For the current application, the applicant provided further acute oral toxicity studies in mice. These studies involved Cry1F at 300 mg/kg body weight, Cry1Ac at 700 mg/kg, and PAT at 2,000 mg/kg. No protein-related effects were observed in these additional studies. The GMO Panel notes that acute toxicity testing of newly expressed proteins offers limited additional value for assessing the long-term risks of consuming food and feed derived from genetically modified plants (EFSA GMO Panel, 2011a).
No compositional differences requiring further assessment were found in soybean DAS-81419-2 compared to its conventional counterpart. Therefore, no additional food and feed safety assessment of components other than the newly expressed proteins is needed.
The applicant did not provide animal studies, such as 90-day toxicity studies in rodents or feeding studies in young animals, for food/feed derived from soybean DAS-81419-2.
Compositional analysis of soybean DAS‐81419‐2 revealed no substantial modifications in food/feed derived from it. No unintended effects requiring further assessment were identified. Consequently, animal studies on food/feed from this soybean variety are deemed unnecessary for its safety assessment.

All available information on the newly expressed protein was considered using a weight-of-evidence approach, as no single method can definitively predict allergenicity.
The genes for Cry1F and Cry1Ac originate from *B. thuringiensis*, and the *pat* gene from *S. viridochromogenes*. These microorganisms are not considered allergenic sources.
Bioinformatic analyses of the Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins' amino acid sequences, employing a 35% identity criterion over an 80-amino acid window, found no significant similarities to known allergens. Further analyses by the applicant, specifically searching for eight contiguous identical amino acid sequences shared with known allergens, corroborated these findings.
Pepsin degradation resistance of Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins was detailed in Section 3.3.1.2.
Previous evaluations by the GMO Panel found no allergenicity concerns with Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins in earlier applications.

Available evidence indicates that Bt proteins, as expressed in soybean DAS‐81419‐2, are unlikely to act as adjuvants. Specifically, they are not expected to enhance immunoglobulin E (IgE) responses or promote allergic reactions.
The GMO Panel found no indications that the Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins, whether alone or together, in soybean DAS‐81419‐2 are allergenic.
Soybeans are recognized as a common food allergen, necessitating an assessment of any alterations in the GM plant's inherent allergenicity compared to its non-GM counterparts. To address this, the applicant conducted in vitro allergenicity studies utilizing human sera. Additionally, a comparative analysis was performed, measuring specific known soybean allergens in extracts from DAS-81419-2, its conventional counterpart, and a selection of non-GM commercial reference soybean varieties.
To assess allergenicity, the applicant initially conducted one-dimensional electrophoresis with Western blot and inhibition ELISA using pooled sera from ten soybean-allergic individuals. Further studies involved two-dimensional electrophoresis, Western blot, and inhibition ELISA with single sera from six soybean-allergic individuals. Following a request from the GMO Panel, a densitometric analysis of the two-dimensional Western blots was performed. The applicant noted minor variations in spot intensities between soybean DAS-81419-2 and its conventional counterpart, which are typical for such analyses.
In response to a request, the applicant provided measurements of specific known soybean allergens using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). This method has been recognized as an acceptable approach for evaluating the endogenous allergenicity of GM plants. The analysis focused on allergens such as Gly m 1, Gly m 3, Gly m 4, Gly m 5, Gly m 6, Gly m Bd 28 K, Gly m Bd 30 K, and Gly m 8 in soybean seeds, selected based on OECD guidelines. A comparative analysis, employing difference and equivalence testing as recommended by the EFSA GMO Panel, was conducted. The results indicated that the genetic modification did not lead to an overexpression of naturally occurring endogenous allergens.
The GMO Panel found no evidence that the genetic modification of soybean DAS-81419-2 significantly alters its overall allergenicity compared to conventional or non-GM varieties.

The environmental risk assessment for soybean DAS‐81419‐2, excluding cultivation, focuses on two main areas. Firstly, it considers the exposure of bacteria to recombinant DNA within animal digestive systems and in environments contaminated by animal waste. Secondly, it addresses the potential for accidental release of viable GM soybean seeds during transport and processing.
Soybean (Glycine max L. Merr.), a highly domesticated crop originating from eastern Asia, belongs to the subgenus Soja within the genus Glycine. Its seeds typically lack dormancy, meaning they can only develop into volunteer plants in the year following cultivation under specific environmental conditions. When volunteer plants do emerge, they generally exhibit poor competition with subsequent crops and are readily managed through mechanical or chemical means. While occasional volunteer soybean plants have been documented in intensively cultivated regions of Italy, soybean seeds generally do not survive winter. This limited survival is attributed to the absence of dormancy, along with factors like herbivory, rotting, germination, and pre-planting agricultural practices. Furthermore, the survival of soybean plants outside of cultivated areas is primarily constrained by their low competitiveness, lack of dormancy, and vulnerability to plant pathogens and cold climates.
Field trials in the USA provided agronomic and phenotypic data for soybean DAS‐81419‐2. A notable difference was observed in a reduced '100 seed weight' for the GM soybean. However, other measured plant characteristics showed no significant differences. Given soybean's limited survival capacity, this '100 seed weight' variation is unlikely to impact the plant's fitness, including its survival, reproduction, or competitiveness, nor its invasiveness.
Given that soybean DAS‐81419‐2's general characteristics are similar to its conventional counterpart, it is highly improbable that it will differ from non-GM soybeans in its survival or establishment as feral plants in Europe. This is particularly true in the event of accidental release of viable seeds during transport or processing.
The GMO Panel has not encountered scientific reports indicating enhanced survival, including overwintering, in existing GM soybean varieties. Consequently, the Panel believes that soybean DAS‐81419‐2 is unlikely to have different environmental effects in Europe compared to non-GM soybean varieties.
Gene transfer requires pathways, including horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of DNA or vertical gene flow via cross-pollination from plants grown from spilled seed. Plant-to-microorganism gene transfer is one such pathway.
Food and feed products derived from soybean contain genomic plant DNA. This DNA is significantly degraded during processing and digestion in humans and animals. Nevertheless, some fragmented ingested DNA, including recombinant DNA, is expected to expose bacteria in the digestive tract of humans and domesticated animals, as well as in other environments where GM plants or plant material are present.
Horizontal gene transfer of non-mobile plant DNA fragments to bacteria is unlikely to occur at detectable frequencies in natural conditions, according to current scientific understanding of bacterial recombination processes.
For horizontal gene transfer (HGT) to be successful, recombinant DNA must stably integrate into a bacterial genome and provide a selective advantage. Homologous recombination is the primary known mechanism for transferring non-mobile, chromosomal DNA fragments into bacterial genomes. This process necessitates similar DNA sequences within the recombining molecules. Furthermore, the insertion of non-homologous DNA sequences is aided by sequence similarity between their flanking regions and the recipient bacterial sequences.
Soybean event DAS-81419-2 incorporates several bacterial genetic elements. These include synthetic versions of the cry1Fv3 gene from *B. thuringiensis* subsp. *aizawai* strain PS811, and the cry1Ac (synpro) gene from *B. thuringiensis* subsp. *kurstaki* strain HD73. Additionally, it contains the synthetic pat gene for phosphinothricin acetyltransferase from *S. viridochromogenes*. The event also includes three untranslated regions (UTRs) from *A. tumefaciens* plasmid pTi15955: two from AtuORF23 and one from AtuORF1.
While the plant-optimized bacterial genes *cry1Fv3*, *cry1Ac (synpro)*, and *pat* in soybean DAS-81419-2 lacked sufficient sequence identity for homologous recombination, bacterial DNA sequences were present. Specifically, the 3' sequence of *AtuORF23*, bordering the *cry1Ac (synpro)* gene, and *AtuORF23* and *AtuORF1*, flanking the *pat* gene, all from the *A. tumefaciens* plasmid pTi15955, showed adequate identity. This could theoretically allow for a double homologous recombination event between the transgenic plant DNA and *A. tumefaciens* plasmid DNA. Such an event might lead to the insertion of the *cry1Ac (synpro)* gene at the *AtuORF23* site and the *pat* gene within the *AtuORF23* and *AtuORF1* region.
*Agrobacterium tumefaciens* is found in soil, water, and plant rhizospheres, making it unlikely to be prevalent in the gastrointestinal tracts of humans or animals. However, recombinant genes could potentially appear in soils outside immediate receiving environments via faecal material. This possibility is considered when assessing horizontal gene transfer risks.
A theoretical scenario involves double homologous recombination, potentially leading to a plasmid in *A. tumefaciens* containing either the codon-optimized *cry1Ac (synpro)* gene or the *pat* gene, both under the control of a plant viral CsVMV promoter. However, expression from this viral promoter is anticipated to be low. Plant-optimized genes are likely to yield less functional proteins in bacteria compared to their natural bacterial counterparts. The acquisition of the *cry1Ac (synpro)* and *pat* genes is unlikely to provide *A. tumefaciens* with a selective advantage in its natural environment, unless the *pat* gene alters sensitivity to glufosinate ammonium. Inserting the *pat* gene onto the Ti plasmid would render it dysfunctional due to the loss of a significant fragment between AtuORF23 and AtuORF1. Recombinant Ti-plasmids theoretically could transfer these genes back to infected plants, but this transfer would be confined to the infected tissue (crown gall tumor) and would not reach the germline. Furthermore, the *pat* gene insertion would compromise the Ti plasmid's ability to induce crown gall formation.
While non-homologous recombination, which doesn't require DNA similarity, is theoretically possible, it has not been detected in studies exposing bacteria to high concentrations of GM plant DNA. Therefore, compared to homologous recombination, this process is not considered a significant contributor to horizontal gene transfer events.
The GMO Panel identified two theoretical scenarios for recombinant gene transfer to soil bacteria via double homologous recombination onto a plasmid. Such transfer would not offer an immediate selective advantage to recipient bacteria, except in the case of glufosinate ammonium. Given the presence of natural variants of *cry* and *pat* genes in environmental bacterial communities, these recombinant genes would not introduce novel properties to the environment. Consequently, the GMO Panel found no concern regarding the theoretical horizontal transfer of recombinant genes from DAS-81419-2 to bacteria. Plant-to-plant gene transfer was also considered.
The potential for feral GM soybean plants from seed spills to transfer recombinant DNA to cross-compatible plants was assessed, considering the application's scope and soybean biology.
The genus *Glycine* is classified into two subgenera: *Glycine* and *Soja*. The *Glycine* subgenus comprises 16 perennial wild species. In contrast, the cultivated soybean (*G. max*), along with its wild and semiwild annual relatives (*G. soja* and *G. gracilis*), belong to the *Soja* subgenus. Due to limited genomic similarity across *Glycine* species, *G. max* can only naturally cross with other members of the *Glycine* subgenus *Soja*. Consequently, these three *Soja* subgenus species can cross-pollinate, producing hybrid seeds that germinate into fertile plants. While *G. soja* and *G. gracilis* are native to various Asian regions, Russia, and Australia, they are not found where cultivated soybean is grown elsewhere. Therefore, plant-to-plant gene transfer in soybean is confined to cultivated areas and sporadic soybean plants that emerge from spilled seeds within the EU.
Soybean is an annual crop that primarily self-pollinates, with cross-pollination occurring in less than 1% of cases. Pollen dispersal is limited as anthers mature within the bud, directly pollinating the flower's own stigma.
While soybean is primarily self-pollinating, cross-pollination rates of up to 6.3% have been observed in closely planted crops, indicating potential gene flow within soybean fields. These rates can vary considerably depending on environmental factors like climate and pollinator availability.
For gene transfer between plants to happen, imported soybean DAS‐81419‐2 seeds must be processed outside import ports, moved to European soybean production areas, spilled during transport, and then germinate and grow near soybean fields. This would also require overlapping flowering periods and conditions favorable for cross-pollination. However, most DAS‐81419‐2 seeds are processed in their countries of origin or at import ports. Consequently, the probability of cross-pollination between occasional feral GM soybean plants and cultivated soybean is very low.
The GMO Panel concluded that even if cross-pollination occurs, the environmental effects from feral GM soybean plants in Europe would not differ from conventional varieties. This assessment aligns with previous findings on environmental impacts.
The GMO Panel considers potential interactions of feral soybean DAS‐81419‐2 plants, arising from seed spills, with target organisms to be irrelevant given the application's scope.
Exposure of non-target organisms to stored or spilled GM seeds, or plants from spilled seeds, is limited. Therefore, potential exposure to Cry1Ac and Cry1F proteins for sensitive non-target organisms is very low and not relevant.
The GMO Panel assessed the potential impact of Cry1Ac and Cry1F proteins on non-target organisms, considering their entry into the environment via animal feces after consumption of GM soybean. It was established that Cry proteins undergo enzymatic degradation within the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in minimal intact protein in feces, a finding supported by studies on Cry1Ab. Subsequent degradation occurs in manure and feces due to microbial proteolytic activity. Further breakdown of Cry proteins in soil further reduces the potential exposure for sensitive non-target organisms. While Cry proteins can bind to soil components, limiting microbial degradation, there is no evidence of their persistence or accumulation in soil from GM crops. The GMO Panel has not encountered data indicating that Cry proteins released from GM plants negatively affect soil microorganisms.
Exposure of non-target organisms to Cry1Ac and Cry1F proteins is expected to be very low and biologically irrelevant, even considering potential synergistic interactions between these Cry proteins. This conclusion is based on the scope of the application.
Due to low environmental exposure, the GMO Panel does not consider interactions between feral soybean DAS-81419-2 plants, resulting from seed spills, and the abiotic environment or biogeochemical cycles to be a relevant issue, given the application's scope.
A Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plan, as outlined in Directive 2001/18/EC, serves two primary objectives. Firstly, it verifies the accuracy of assumptions made during the Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA) concerning the potential adverse effects of the genetically modified organism (GMO) or its use. Secondly, it aims to detect any unforeseen adverse effects on human health or the environment that were not identified in the initial ERA.
While EFSA's mandate does not include the final adoption of post-market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plans, as these are risk management tools, the GMO Panel provides its scientific opinion on the content of the applicant's submitted PMEM plan.
The applicant's Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plan outlines a multi-faceted approach. It involves operators, such as soybean import and processing federations, reporting any observed adverse effects of GMOs on human health and the environment to the applicant through a centralized system. Additionally, EuropaBio has established a coordinating system to gather information from these operators. The plan also incorporates the utilization of existing surveillance networks. The applicant intends to submit PMEM reports annually, with a final report due at the conclusion of the consent period. The GMO Panel found the PMEM plan's scope to be consistent with that of soybean DAS-81419-2. Since the Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA) does not encompass cultivation and identified no potential adverse environmental effects from this soybean, case-specific monitoring is deemed unnecessary. The GMO Panel also approved the reporting intervals proposed by the applicant.
Should viable soybean DAS‐81419‐2 seeds be accidentally released, feral plants are unlikely to establish or spread more readily than conventional soybean. This holds true unless they encounter glufosinate ammonium-based herbicides or susceptible insect pests. The GMO Panel believes such scenarios would not lead to distinct environmental impacts compared to non-GM soybean.
Given the application's scope, soybean DAS-81419-2's interactions with its environment are deemed not relevant. While bioinformatic analysis revealed bacterial DNA sequence identity that could theoretically enable plant codon-optimized cry and pat genes to transfer to a soil bacterium's plasmid, the GMO Panel found no concern. This is because natural variants of these genes are present in environmental bacterial communities.
Considering its traits, comparative analysis, exposure routes, and limited exposure levels, the GMO Panel concludes that accidental release of viable soybean DAS‐81419‐2 seeds poses no safety concerns.
The applicant's Post-Market Environmental Monitoring plan and reporting intervals align with the intended uses of soybean DAS-81419-2 and the GMO Panel's guidelines.
The European Food Safety Authority's (EFSA) Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms (GMO Panel) conducted a scientific assessment of soybean DAS-81419-2 for import, processing, and food/feed uses, as mandated by Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. The molecular characterization of soybean DAS-81419-2 did not reveal any safety concerns. The GMO Panel determined that observed differences in the compositional, agronomic, and phenotypic characteristics of soybean DAS-81419-2 did not necessitate further food and feed safety assessments. Furthermore, no concerns were raised regarding the toxicity or allergenicity of the newly expressed Cry1F, Cry1Ac, and PAT proteins. There was also no evidence to suggest that the genetic modification significantly altered the overall allergenicity of soybean DAS-81419-2. The nutritional value of food and feed derived from soybean DAS-81419-2 is anticipated to be comparable to that of conventional soybean varieties. Consequently, the GMO Panel concluded that soybean DAS-81419-2 is as safe and nutritious as its conventional counterpart and the tested non-GM soybean reference varieties. Regarding environmental safety, the GMO Panel assessed that there is a very low likelihood of environmental effects arising from the accidental release of viable soybean DAS-81419-2 seeds. Given the scope of the application, which focuses on food and feed use, interactions with the biotic and abiotic environment were not deemed a significant issue. Potential risks associated with the theoretically possible, though unlikely, horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from soybean DAS-81419-2 to bacteria were not identified as a concern. The proposed post-market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plan aligns with the intended uses of soybean DAS-81419-2.
The GMO Panel concludes that available information on soybean DAS‐81419‐2 addresses Member State comments. The GM soybean is deemed as safe and nutritious as its conventional counterpart and tested non-GM varieties regarding human/animal health and environmental impacts within the application's scope.
The application for the authorisation of genetically modified (GM) soybean DAS-81419-2, submitted by Dow AgroSciences LLC, was received by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on May 13, 2013. The Competent Authority of the Netherlands forwarded this request for market placement. Subsequently, on June 25, 2013, EFSA contacted the applicant to request additional information as part of a completeness check. The applicant responded with the requested information on November 21, 2013. Further requests for information were issued by EFSA on December 12, 2013, and the applicant provided this information on January 17, 2014. On February 7, 2014, EFSA declared the application valid, confirming compliance with Articles 6(1) and 18(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. Following this, EFSA informed the Member States and the European Commission and made the application summary publicly available.
