The relationship between obesity and illness in older adults, along with the significance and potential drawbacks of treating overweight and obesity in this demographic, remains a subject of ongoing discussion. Our study aimed to determine if a higher Body Mass Index (BMI) correlates with increased cardiovascular risk across all age groups, with a particular focus on older individuals. To achieve this, we conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from 3,926 patients who received diagnostic or therapeutic interventions at a medical outpatient center between January 1995 and July 2010. These patients were categorized into eight distinct age groups, spanning from under 20 years to 80 years and older, with each group representing a decade. Our findings indicated a consistent rise in BMI with advancing age, reaching its highest point in the 61–70 year age bracket, where the average BMI was 26.29 ± 4.42 kg/m². This trend was mirrored by an increase in cardiovascular events and the necessity for ongoing medication, which also peaked in the 61–70 year age group, affecting 22.3% of women and 24.7% of men. Notably, across all age decades up to 80 years, patients experiencing cardiovascular events or requiring medication exhibited higher BMI values compared to those who did not. Furthermore, a multivariable linear regression analysis, which accounted for confounding factors such as blood pressure, fasting glucose, HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, physical activity, and smoking, revealed a robust positive association between BMI and the likelihood of a cardiovascular event or the need for medication. Conversely, fat-free mass (FFM) showed a negative relationship with this probability. In conclusion, our research demonstrates a direct correlation between BMI and the incidence of cardiovascular events across all age groups. Given the societal shift towards an aging population, it is imperative that therapeutic and preventive strategies incorporate weight management approaches for the elderly.

Obesity has significantly increased in recent decades, with a notable prevalence among the elderly. Generally, overweight and obesity are linked to higher risks of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, certain cancers, cognitive impairment, and overall mortality. However, a debate persists regarding whether these associations hold true in old age and their clinical significance for older individuals. Interestingly, in specific situations like heart failure, being overweight or obese has been associated with reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Furthermore, retrospective analyses suggest a U-shaped relationship between Body Mass Index (BMI) and mortality in individuals over 75. This pattern indicates elevated mortality rates for both overweight/obese individuals and those with very low BMIs, a finding supported by the Bergen Clinical Blood Pressure study. It is also crucial to consider that weight loss in the elderly can be either intentional, as part of treatment, or unintentional due to underlying health conditions, both of which can negatively impact prognosis.
Unintentional weight loss in the elderly, often linked to frailty, elevates risks of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality.
The standard definitions of overweight (BMI 25.0-29.9 kg/m²) and obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²) apply irrespective of age. However, in older adults, the relationship between BMI and body fat percentage is altered. This is due to age-related changes such as vertebral compression leading to reduced height, a decrease in muscle mass, and a corresponding increase in fat mass. These physiological shifts can artificially inflate BMI by approximately 1.5 kg/m² in men and 2.5 kg/m² in women. Consequently, an adjusted definition of overweight for the elderly is proposed, setting the threshold at a BMI of ≥27.0 kg/m². This adaptation is supported by research indicating no increased risk of cardiovascular or all-cause mortality in older individuals with BMIs between 25.0 and 27.0 kg/m². While observational data suggest body weight typically rises with age, peaking in the 50s before declining, the impact of obesity on life expectancy in the elderly remains a subject of debate. It is possible that the risks associated with obesity in older populations are underestimated due to factors like competing mortality, shorter remaining life expectancy, and unintentional weight loss. Some younger obese individuals may not reach old age due to premature mortality, while overweight individuals who do survive might possess genetic factors contributing to longevity. Contrary to observational findings, longitudinal studies indicate only minor decreases in body weight and BMI among older adults.
Regarding age-related alterations in body composition, research indicates a significant decline in muscle mass, potentially up to 40% between the ages of 20 and 70. Concurrently, fat mass tends to rise, with a particular increase in abdominal fat accumulation. These shifts contribute to a greater prevalence of metabolic and cardiovascular issues associated with the metabolic syndrome. Consequently, central fat accumulation and a reduction in fat-free mass may emerge as more significant indicators of risk than BMI alone. In older adults, both a high BMI and increased waist circumference, signifying central fat accumulation, have been linked to diminished physical functioning. This association amplifies the risk of developing the metabolic syndrome, as well as cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, glucose levels, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and systolic blood pressure demonstrate a linear correlation with both aging and BMI. Long-term studies suggest that being overweight and having cardiovascular risk factors, particularly during middle age, are crucial determinants of lifelong risk for morbidity and mortality.
To address the ongoing debate about obesity's relevance to morbidity in old age, we conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from a large cohort of patients. These individuals were categorized into eight age decades, ranging from under 20 to over 80 years. Our primary objective was to examine Body Mass Index (BMI) across these age groups and its association with cardiovascular events and the need for continuous medication. This analysis also considered the significance of fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol, and blood pressure as indicators of the metabolic syndrome.
From January 1995 to July 2010, 3926 individuals visited a medical outdoor center for diagnostic or therapeutic interventions. These patients underwent routine clinical checks and laboratory tests. Blood samples for laboratory analysis were collected in the morning after an overnight fast. Detailed records of the type, dosage, and frequency of all prescribed medications were maintained for every patient.
Body mass index (BMI) and fat-free mass (FFM) data were gathered for all participants. FFM was measured using bioimpedance analysis (BIA) with an OMRON BF 302 body fat monitor. This method determines total body water and FFM by assessing conductivity and hydration levels. FFM was subsequently calculated by subtracting fat mass from total body weight.
Blood pressure was measured by a nurse or technician using a standard sphygmomanometer. Patients rested for 5-10 minutes supine. The final blood pressure reading was the average of at least three measurements taken during two separate visits.
Patients in the study maintained euthyroid function and reported no recent lifestyle changes or participation in weight reduction programs. Physical activity was assessed by the number of weekly sports activities. Smoking status was recorded, with 1060 (approximately 27%) of the 3926 patients identified as current smokers, based on weekly cigarette consumption. The remaining participants were either lifelong non-smokers or had quit over a year prior.
Patients with acute or chronic inflammatory conditions, severe kidney or liver failure, or those undergoing chemotherapy were excluded. Inflammatory disease was identified by elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, and leukocyte count. Renal disease was defined by serum creatinine exceeding 1.5 mg/dL, and impaired liver function by a twofold or greater increase in hepatic enzyme levels. Additionally, 509 patients (9.5%) had diagnosed diabetes mellitus.
Patients were categorized into eight age groups, ranging from under 20 years to over 80 years. Within these groups, individuals were further distinguished based on the presence or absence of cardiovascular events and the need for continuous medication. Cardiovascular events encompassed a range of conditions, including angina, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, stroke, and cerebral ischemia. Continuous medication referred to ongoing drug therapies such as antihypertensives, lipid-lowering agents, antidiabetics, diuretics, and antiplatelets. All participants provided informed consent for their inclusion in the study.
Data were presented as means and standard deviations. Student's t-tests were used for both within-group and between-group comparisons, with a p-value less than 0.05 indicating statistical significance. To identify the factors influencing the likelihood of a cardiovascular event or the requirement for ongoing medication, a logistic regression (logit) model was employed. The reported parameter estimates, calculated at the average values of the explanatory variables, represent the individual percentage effect of each variable on the probability of experiencing a cardiovascular event or needing continuous medication.
The study encompassed 3,926 patients, with an average weight of 71.67 ±15.49 kg and a mean Body Mass Index (BMI) of 24.73 ±4.51 kg/m². Fat-free mass (FFM) averaged 51.3 ±11.52 kg. Patients were categorized into eight age groups, ranging from under 20 years to over 80 years. The age decades from 21 to 70 years each represented approximately 15% of the total patient cohort. Younger and older age groups, specifically those under 20 years, between 71-80 years, and over 80 years, constituted smaller proportions of the study population at 3%, 10%, and 7.3% respectively. A total of 1,848 patients (1,114 women and 734 men) experienced a cardiovascular event or required continuous medication. Analysis of these patients by age decade revealed a progressive increase in the event rate. This rate rose from 5.7% in the 21-30 year age group to a peak of 23.3% in the 61-70 year age decade. This peak incidence of cardiovascular events and medication needs was consistent across both female (22.3%) and male (24.7%) subgroups within the 61-70 year age bracket. For patients over 80 years old (275 individuals), the proportion experiencing cardiovascular events or requiring medication was 16.6% for women and 12.3% for men.
Across the entire study population, Body Mass Index (BMI) showed a consistent upward trend with increasing age, reaching its highest point in the 61-70 year age group (26.29 ±4.42 kg/m2). Beyond this peak, BMI values declined in the older age decades of 71-80 years and over 80 years, with a statistically significant difference observed between these two groups. When patients were analyzed based on the presence or absence of cardiovascular events and the need for continuous medication, this pattern of increasing BMI with age, peaking between 61-70 years, was replicated in both subgroups. Notably, for all age decades from 21 to 80 years, individuals experiencing cardiovascular events or requiring medication exhibited higher BMI values compared to those without. This observation held true across all age groups up to 80 years.
Analyzing patients separately based on the presence or absence of cardiovascular events and medication revealed a consistent pattern. Both groups showed a continuous rise in BMI with increasing age, reaching peak values in the 61-70 year decade. Notably, across all age decades from 21 to 80 years, individuals experiencing cardiovascular events had higher BMI values compared to those without. This distinction was statistically significant, with the exception of the 31-40 year age group. However, in patients over 80 years old, no significant difference in BMI was observed between those with and without cardiovascular events or medication.
To assess drug therapy's impact on weight modulation, patients with cardiovascular events were analyzed. This involved comparing a full sample with a restricted sample that excluded individuals on insulin, antidepressive, or hormone replacement therapies. No significant differences were found between these two groups.
To investigate the probability of cardiovascular events or the need for continuous medication, a logistic regression analysis was conducted. This analysis utilized a dependent variable that indicated whether a patient experienced a cardiovascular event and/or required ongoing medication. The probability was determined using a logit model that incorporated several explanatory variables: Body Mass Index (BMI), fat-free mass (FFM), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose levels, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol, patient sex, weekly physical activity, and smoking habits. Two distinct versions of this logit model were developed. Model A included the entire patient population. In contrast, Model B excluded patients who were taking medications that could potentially influence weight, such as insulin, antidepressive drugs, or hormone replacement therapy. The patients were categorized into four age groups: under 20 years, 21-40 years, 41-60 years, and 61-75 years. An additional group was designated for patients over 75 years, representing geriatric patients. For example, in the 61-75 year age group, when patients on weight-modulating drugs were excluded (Model B), a one-unit increase in BMI was associated with an approximate 3.7% rise in the probability of a cardiovascular event or the need for medication. Conversely, in the same group, a one-unit increase in BMI was associated with a 2.8% rise in probability.

Conversely, fat-free mass (FFM) showed a negative relationship with cardiovascular event probability. The impact of other variables on cardiovascular event risk varied.
Our study, involving 3926 patients across age groups from under 20 to over 80 years, demonstrated a consistent rise in BMI with advancing age, up to 80 years. Notably, across all age groups, including the elderly up to 80, individuals experiencing cardiovascular events or requiring ongoing medication exhibited higher BMIs compared to those without. This finding indicates that overweight is associated with increased morbidity not only in younger individuals but also in the elderly population.
In our study, the highest rates of cardiovascular events and elevated BMI were observed in the 61-70 age decade. However, for patients over 80 years old, no significant difference in BMI was found between those with and without cardiovascular events or medication. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting a diminished relevance of overweight in geriatric populations. The concept of a "reverse metabolic syndrome" has been proposed to describe a state characterized by unintended weight loss due to malnutrition or chronic illness, coupled with a low BMI, low diastolic blood pressure, and high insulin sensitivity. This condition is associated with a heightened mortality risk, with poorer outcomes observed at lower body weights. Similarly, frailty, a geriatric syndrome linked to increased mortality, exhibits a U-shaped relationship with BMI and body fat, with the lowest risk occurring within the normal BMI range of 19.9-25.0 kg/m². It is important to note limitations in our study. The older participants were community-dwelling and active, without significant cognitive or functional impairments, precluding an assessment of the BMI and fat-free mass association with frailty. Furthermore, the absence of representative waist circumference measurements prevents us from correlating cardiovascular risk with abdominal obesity. Aging is associated with a shift in body composition, including a decrease in subcutaneous fat and an increase in visceral fat, which elevates the risk of metabolic syndrome. However, current diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome, which include waist circumference, have not been fully adapted to account for age-related changes in body composition, blood pressure, or laboratory values.
To analyze the impact of BMI and cardiovascular risk factors, a logistic regression was conducted. Patients experiencing cardiovascular events or requiring continuous medication were categorized into age groups: under 20, 21-40, 41-60, 61-75, and over 75 (geriatric patients). This stratification ensured sufficient patient numbers for separate analyses of the entire cohort and a subgroup excluding individuals on weight-modifying medications.
Across all age groups over 20 years old, higher BMI was consistently linked to an increased likelihood of cardiovascular events and the need for continuous medication. Conversely, fat-free mass (FFM) showed a stable negative association with this probability. The impact of traditional cardiovascular risk factors on the likelihood of events varied across age groups. This variability can be attributed to factors such as the use of therapeutic interventions like antihypertensive and lipid-lowering drugs, as well as age-related shifts in these risk factors. For instance, HDL-cholesterol tends to decline with age, arterial stiffness contributes to systolic hypertension, and fasting plasma glucose levels rise as individuals age. Notably, in patients over 75 years old, physical activity demonstrated a significant negative association with the probability of cardiovascular events, underscoring the importance of exercise in this demographic. Smoking, however, was positively associated with the probability of cardiovascular events and medication, particularly in the 21-40 year age bracket.
Our findings indicate that overweight poses a risk factor across all age groups, including active elderly individuals. We observed a correlation between higher BMI and body fat percentage with cardiovascular events and the need for continuous medication. Given the demographic shift towards an aging population, it is recommended that therapeutic and preventive strategies incorporate weight management for older adults.
