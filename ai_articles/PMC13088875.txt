The EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) has re-evaluated the safety of agar (E 406) as a food additive. Previous assessments by the Scientific Committee for Food (SCF) in 1989 and the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) in 1974 had concluded that agar did not require a specified Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI). Following the framework for re-evaluating food additives under Commission Regulation (EU) No 257/2010, the Panel focused its safety assessment on the uses and levels reported by industry across seven specific food categories, out of the 70 where agar is authorized. An indicative refined exposure assessment identified a high intake of up to 26 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day for toddlers at the 95th percentile in a non-brand-loyal scenario. The Panel found that agar is unlikely to be absorbed unchanged and is only slightly fermented by intestinal microbiota. Sufficient toxicity data were available, indicating no concerns regarding genotoxicity. Carcinogenicity studies in mice and rats, at doses up to 4,500 mg/kg bw per day and 2,500 mg/kg bw per day respectively, did not reveal any carcinogenic effects. Furthermore, human studies showed that oral intake of agar at 4,500 mg per person (equivalent to 64 mg/kg bw per day) was well-tolerated for 12 weeks without adverse effects. Consequently, the Panel concluded that a numerical ADI for agar is not necessary. Based on the refined exposure assessment for the reported uses of agar as a food additive, the Panel found no safety concerns for the general population.

Agar (E 406) is an authorized food additive in the European Union (EU), governed by Annex II of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008. Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012 outlines its specific purity criteria. Previous evaluations of agar (E 406) in the EU were conducted by the Scientific Committee for Food (SCF) in 1989 and the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) in 1974. The SCF determined that a numerical acceptable daily intake (ADI) was unnecessary, thus assigning a "not specified" ADI. JECFA, with limited data from two animal studies and one human study, concluded that agar had no ADI limit.
Agar (E 406) is a hydrophilic colloidal polysaccharide derived from specific strains of marine algae, primarily red algae from the families Gelidiaceae and Gracilariaceae. Its structure consists mainly of galactose units linked alternately with l- and d-isomeric forms. These hexoses are joined by alpha-1,3 and beta-1,4 bonds. Approximately every tenth d-galactopyranose unit features a hydroxyl group esterified with sulfuric acid, neutralized by various cations.
Agar's polysaccharide structure makes it susceptible to microbiological contamination during storage, as evidenced by recent mycotoxin contamination in gums. The Panel found that discrepancies in microbiological criteria between EU and JECFA specifications for agar were not significant.
In vitro human models have investigated agar's degradation, showing partial fermentation in the large intestine by gut microflora. While the exact hydrolysis rate in the human gastrointestinal tract remains unknown, the Panel concluded that agar is unlikely to be absorbed unchanged. The limited fermentation is expected to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFA). Given the established role of SCFA as end products of dietary fiber fermentation by anaerobic intestinal microflora, their formation from agar is not considered a safety concern. Furthermore, agar exhibits low acute toxicity in mice, rats, and rabbits.
Two subchronic toxicity studies by the NTP (1982) found no adverse effects in mice up to 10,000 mg/kg bw/day and in rats up to 4,500 mg/kg bw/day, the highest doses tested.
No genotoxic activity was observed in the available in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity assays for agar. While the Panel acknowledged the limited scope of these studies, they also noted agar's common use as a substrate in in vitro gene mutation assays. Considering agar's chemical structure and minimal absorption, the Panel concluded there is no genotoxicity concern for E 406. The Panel also identified a lack of chronic toxicity studies according to OECD guidelines.
Carcinogenicity studies by NTP (1982) and Melnick et al. (1983) found no evidence of agar causing increased tumor incidence in mice or rats. The highest doses tested, 4,500 mg/kg bw per day in mice and 2,500 mg/kg bw per day in rats, did not result in carcinogenic effects. The Panel concurred with these findings.
No reproductive toxicity data were available. However, prenatal developmental toxicity studies in rats and hamsters showed no maternal or developmental effects, even at the highest tested doses of 1,140 and 650 mg agar/kg body weight per day, respectively.
Agar has been investigated for its use as an adjuvant in managing diabetes and obesity. A study by Maeda et al. (2005) found no noticeable side effects in participants who consumed 4.5 g of agar daily for 12 weeks as part of their treatment. Additionally, agar is recognized for its mild laxative properties, with no adverse effects reported for single doses of 2.75 to 5.5 g or daily doses of 4 to 16.5 g when taken with adequate fluids. However, intestinal stenosis is noted as a contraindication for its use (Heber, 2007).
Agar (E 406) is authorized for use in numerous food products, yet industry reports indicate its application in only a fraction of these categories (9 out of 70). The Panel anticipates that brand loyalty will not significantly increase general population exposure, with exceptions for specific groups consuming foods from categories 13.2, 13.3, and 13.4. Consequently, the Panel deemed the non-brand-loyal refined scenario the most appropriate for assessing exposure to this additive.
The safety assessment for agar (E 406) as a food additive was limited to seven food categories, out of seventy where it is authorized. Data for these categories were provided by industry. The Panel concluded that identified uncertainties likely lead to an overestimation of exposure within these seven categories.
Analysis of the Mintel Global New Products Database (GNPD) revealed that only about half of food products listing agar (E 406) fell into subcategories with reported use levels. This means these products were included in the exposure assessment. The Panel noted that the GNPD data suggests agar may be used in food categories for which industry data was not provided. If this is the case, the current assessment might underestimate actual exposure levels.
Exposure to agar from its use as a carrier in food additives, food enzymes, nutrients, and flavourings, as outlined in Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, was not included in this exposure assessment.
Refined exposure estimates for agar (E 406) rely on reported usage levels. Any shifts in actual practice would necessitate updating these estimates to maintain their representativeness.

Agar, as defined by Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012, is a hydrophilic colloidal polysaccharide. It primarily comprises galactose units, alternating between l- and d-isomeric forms. These hexoses are linked via alpha-1,3 and beta-1,4 bonds. Approximately every tenth d-galactopyranose unit features a hydroxyl group esterified with sulfuric acid, neutralized by calcium, magnesium, potassium, or sodium. This substance is extracted from specific marine algae, notably from the Gelidiaceae and Gracilariaceae families, as well as relevant red algae belonging to the Rhodophyceae class.
The definition of agar does not specify all families and species used in its production. Reviews by Stanley (2006) and Armisen and Galatas (2000) indicate that agar can be extracted from red algae (class Rhodophyta) beyond the Gracilariaceae and Gelidiaceae families, including Phyllophoraceae, Ceramiaceae, and Ahnfeltiaceae. While most raw material is gathered from wild growth (Stanley, 2006), commercial cultivation has been noted in Chile (OIiviera and Alveal, 1990).
The classification of red algae at higher taxonomic levels has been a subject of debate. While recent systems have been published, they vary and differ from earlier ones. Consequently, this opinion will refer only to the genus and species of these algae. Agar is identified by CAS Registry Number 9002-18-0 and EINECS No. 232-658-1.
Agar is a linear polysaccharide composed of alternating 3-linked β-d-galactopyranose and 4-linked α-l-galactopyranose residues. A significant portion of the α-l-galactose residues can exist as a 3,6-anhydro derivative. This forms the fundamental structure of agar, a repeating disaccharide unit known as 'agarobiose'.
Early research indicated that agar could be divided into two main polysaccharides: agarose and agaropectin, both derived from agarobiose polymers. Agarose, a neutral fraction responsible for gelling, possesses a high molecular weight exceeding 100,000 Da and typically contains less than 0.15% sulfate. In contrast, agaropectin is a sulfated, non-gelling fraction with a molecular weight around 14,000 Da and a sulfate content of 5–8%. However, contemporary studies propose that agar is not simply composed of these two distinct polysaccharides. Instead, it is understood to be a complex blend of various polysaccharides. These range from molecules with negligible charge to charged galactans, some of which are rich in sulfate and others in pyruvate. The precise structure of agar is significantly influenced by the specific seaweed sources utilized and the manufacturing processes employed.
Agar, as defined by Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012, is typically odorless with a faint characteristic scent. In its unground state, it appears as thin, membranous strips, or in cut, flaked, or granulated forms. Its color ranges from light yellowish orange to pale yellow or colorless. While tough when moist, it becomes brittle when dry. Powdered agar is white to yellowish white or pale yellow.
Agar is also known by various names, including agar-agar, gelose, Japan agar, Bengal, Ceylon, Chinese or Japanese isinglass, and Layor Carang.

The JECFA specifications include a 'Foreign insoluble matter test' absent in Commission specifications. Pharmaceutical use specifications are found in the 8.4th European Pharmacopeia (2015).

Agar, a food additive, is sourced from various red algae species, known as agarophytes. These primarily originate from the genera Gelidium and Gracilaria.
Globally, 32 species are reported for commercial agar production, with 12 holding primary commercial importance. These key species, along with their producer countries, include Gelidium acerosa (Japan, India), Gelidium amansii (Japan), Gelidium cartilagineum (USA, Mexico, South Africa), Gelidium corneum (South Africa, Portugal, Spain, Morocco), Gelidium liatulum (Japan), Gelidium lingulatum (Chile), Gelidium pacificum (Japan), Gelidium pristoides (South Africa), Gelidium sesquidale (Portugal, Morocco), and Gracilaria confervoides (South Africa). Despite the numerous Gelidium species cited, Gracilaria algae remain the predominant worldwide source for agar.
Industrial agar production involves diverse extraction methods refined over time. The process begins with harvesting algae and transporting them for initial drying. Subsequent steps include bleaching, processing plant transfer, extraction (often alkaline for Gracilaria spp.), washing, soaking, filtration, gelling, freezing/thawing, drying, milling, and finally, packing the agar into powder form.
Ensuring the quality of seaweed from harvest to agar manufacturing is crucial. Initial preservation involves dehydration to prevent fermentation, which degrades both agar and the seaweed. Natural or artificial drying reduces moisture content to approximately 20%. This process also mitigates risks of post-harvest microbial contamination, fungal growth, and subsequent mycotoxin production. Modern industrial agar production employs sodium hypochlorite or hydrogen peroxide for bleaching, with photobleaching using sunlight also documented.
Preventing moisture during transport and storage is crucial for agar. Agar derived from Gelidium species exhibits greater stability compared to Gracilaria agar, allowing for indefinite preservation of Gelidium agar in seaweed form if properly treated. In contrast, Gracilaria agar is susceptible to hydrolysis from endogenous enzymes and the proliferation of Bacillus cereus. While formaldehyde preservation methods for Gracilaria algae during storage have been documented, their use is discouraged due to formaldehyde's high toxicity and polluting nature, as noted by Freile-Pelegrin (2000). No other references to formaldehyde in agar production were found. The Panel observed a lack of information regarding formaldehyde use in response to the data call.
The extraction of agar can be performed using either acidic or basic conditions, with the choice depending on the specific agarophyte and the desired product quality. For instance, extraction from Gelidium species involves treatment under pressure at temperatures between 105–110°C for 2–4 hours. In contrast, Gracilaria species are typically processed with water at 95–100°C for a similar duration. Following these initial steps, the subsequent stages of the process remain consistent for both raw material types. Acidic extraction methods are known to increase the yield of agar. Conversely, basic treatment alters the structure of the carbohydrate chain, thereby improving its gelling properties. Alkali treatment is a mandatory step in the extraction of Gracilaria spp., as agar derived from these species is generally of lower quality due to high natural sulfation. However, treatment with alkaline solutions enhances quality, partly by converting l-galactose-6-sulfate to 3,6-anhydro-l-galactose, which boosts gel-forming capabilities. A combined approach, involving initial basic treatment to improve gel strength followed by acidic extraction, may also be employed. In the alkali treatment process, seaweed is heated in a 2–5% sodium hydroxide solution at 85–90°C for one hour. The precise strength of the alkali is adjusted based on the specific seaweed species and determined through small-scale testing. After the alkali is removed, the seaweed is rinsed with water, and occasionally with a very dilute acid to neutralize any remaining alkali. Regardless of the extraction method, agar is isolated from the gelled extract through a process of freezing and thawing, which effectively separates water and dissolved salts. Factors such as the extraction procedure and the timing of seaweed harvest can influence both the yield and the gel strength of the final product. A more recent extraction technique, known as steam explosion, involves heating the agarophyte in an autoclave to 140–190°C before rapid decompression. The resulting exploded algae are then extracted using a phosphate buffer.
Glueck and Thier (1980) detailed an analytical method for detecting agar and other polysaccharides like locust bean gum, guar, and carrageenan in dairy products. This process begins with extracting polysaccharides from the foodstuff, followed by the removal of fats, starches, milk proteins, and carbohydrates through extraction or degradation. The isolated polysaccharide fraction is then analyzed using gas chromatography. This involves hydrolysis with trifluoroacetic acid and derivatization of the resulting monosaccharides into aldonitrilacetate derivatives using hydroxylaminhydrochloride and acetic acid anhydride. Agar can be identified by its unique monosaccharide pattern, as galactose is its sole hydrolysis product. Recoveries for these polysaccharides ranged from 80-90% when added to skim milk or ice cream and pudding mixtures. Earlier work by Mergenthaler and Scherz (1976) described a similar analytical method that did not require prior extraction from the foodstuff, and Schmolck and Mergenthaler (1973) reported an alternative derivatization technique.
Preuss and Thier (1983) enhanced the analytical method previously detailed by Glueck and Thier (1980). These improvements in separating fats, proteins, and starch enabled the quantitative analysis of agar in foods such as blancmange powder, glaze, fruit ice, and cream cheese. The revised method achieved recovery rates of 60–85% for most thickeners and gums, with a coefficient of variation between 2–8%.
Both methods fail to differentiate agar from carrageenan, as both are composed solely of galactose.
An analytical method by Mergenthaler and Scherz (1980) distinguishes agar from carrageenan and other polysaccharides. Following fat and protein extraction, polysaccharides are passed through a DEAE-cellulose column, separating agar. Further analysis of the resulting fractions can be done using thin-layer chromatography or gas chromatography after derivatization.
Pechanek et al. (1982) published an electrophoretic method for analyzing gelling agents in various foods. This method involves removing fat, starch, and proteins, followed by polysaccharide precipitation. The separated polysaccharides are then quantified using a TL-scanner after electrophoresis on polyacrylamide gel, agarose gel, or cellulose acetate membrane.
The Panel lacked data on the shelf-life stability of agar (E 406). However, a study by Freile-Pelegrin (2000) indicated that agar quality and yield from Gracilaria cornea stored for 18 months under specific temperature and humidity conditions remained within food-grade standards.
While dry agar is not susceptible to microbial contamination, its solutions and gels can readily support bacterial and mold growth. Therefore, precautions are necessary to prevent microbial proliferation.
Agar, a polysaccharide, can undergo hydrolysis, leading to a reduced molecular weight and loss of its gelling ability. Acid hydrolysis occurs at pH levels below 5.5, particularly with extensive heating. Alkaline hydrolysis is significant at pH above 8. Enzymatic hydrolysis by agarases, found in certain marine bacteria and microorganisms not typically present in food products, is considered irrelevant in this context.
Agar's gelling power increases when combined with sugar in products like jams and jellies. Humectants such as glycerol or sorbitol can be added to agar gels to reduce drying when exposed to air. However, tannic acid, found in fruits like squash, apples, and prunes, can inhibit the gelling process.
Salts can increase the melting point of agar gels. Alkaline treatment may enhance gel strength by removing bound protein, leading to a more regular polymer structure.
Agar exhibits significant swelling in water, increasing its volume over fivefold within four hours, though its water-binding capacity is less than that of guar or karaya gum. It remains insoluble in cold water and alcohol. However, agar dissolves in hot water above its melting point of 85°C, forming sols. These sols can then be cooled to much lower temperatures before they set into gels. Melting the set gel requires reheating to a higher temperature, a phenomenon termed "gelation hysteresis." The viscosity of the sol is notably influenced by the specific seaweed species and extraction methods used. Agar can form gels at very low concentrations, with gelation typically occurring around 0.2%.
Agar's gelation is exclusive to its neutral agarose fraction, forming "physical gels" solely through hydrogen bonds between polymer molecules. This structure allows the gels to retain significant amounts of water. A key characteristic of these physical gels is their reversibility: they melt upon heating and re-gel upon cooling. The gelling temperature is approximately 38°C, while the melting temperature is around 85°C.
Agar is frequently blended with other gelling agents in food products to enhance gel strength, texture, and elasticity. Combinations with locust bean gum minimize liquid loss from the gel during handling and storage. When mixed with konjac flour or glucomannan, agar exhibits synergistic properties, resulting in a more elastic gel. These blends are utilized to modify the physical characteristics of food gels.

Consequently, only seven food categories were ultimately considered for the agar (E 406) exposure assessment across all scenarios.
Chronic exposure to agar (E 406) was estimated for infants, toddlers, children, adolescents, adults, and the elderly. This was achieved by combining food category concentrations of agar (E 406) with individual consumption data from the Comprehensive Database, expressed per kilogram of body weight. The calculated exposure for each food category was summed to determine an individual's total daily intake. These estimates were then averaged across survey days to represent the average daily exposure for the survey period. Surveys with only one day of data per subject were excluded due to their inadequacy for assessing repeated exposure.
Distributions of individual exposure were generated for each survey and population group, reflecting the data from all participants. Based on these distributions, the mean and 95th percentile of exposure were determined for each survey and population group. Calculation of the 95th percentile was restricted to population groups with adequate sample sizes, as per EFSA guidelines. Consequently, the 95th percentile of exposure for infants from Italy and toddlers from Belgium, Italy, and Spain could not be included in this assessment.
The ANS Panel conducted the exposure assessment for agar (E 406) using two scenarios. The first, the maximum level exposure assessment scenario, utilized data on maximum levels provided to EFSA. The second, the refined exposure assessment scenario, was based on reported use levels submitted by industry. Both scenarios are elaborated upon below.
Exposure to agar (E 406) from its use as a food additive in food additives, enzymes, and nutrients, as per Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, was not included in the exposure assessment scenarios due to a lack of available data.
The Panel did not consider a specific scenario for food supplements. Agar is authorized at quantum satis in Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, and industry provided no usage data.
The maximum level exposure assessment scenario adheres to the MPLs established in Annex II of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008. Given agar's (E 406) authorization under QS across most food categories, this scenario was developed using the highest reported industry use levels, as outlined in the EFSA Conceptual Framework. Appendix C details the concentration data employed in this assessment.
This scenario's exposure estimates are the most conservative. They assume long-term consumption of agar (E 406) in food at the highest reported use levels.
The refined exposure assessment relies on industry-reported use levels, limited to food categories for which this data was available to the Panel.
The Panel calculated two refined exposure estimates using available data. These estimates were based on different model populations and are summarized in Appendix C, detailing the concentration levels of agar (E 406) used in the refined exposure assessment scenario.
In the brand-loyal consumer scenario, it was assumed that an individual would be exposed to agar (E 406) at the highest reported use level within a single food category over an extended period. This was calculated by combining individual food consumption data with the maximum reported use levels for the primary contributing food category, while utilizing the average reported use levels for all other food categories. Conversely, the non-brand-loyal consumer scenario posited long-term exposure to agar (E 406) at its average reported use level across all foods. This exposure estimate was derived by averaging the typical reported use levels across all food categories.
Estimated exposure to agar (E 406) as a food additive across six population groups and various exposure scenarios is detailed in Table 4. Comprehensive results, broken down by population group and survey, can be found in Appendix D.
Under the maximum level exposure scenario, mean daily intake of agar (E 406) as a food additive varied from 0.01 mg/kg body weight (bw) in infants to 17.0 mg/kg bw in toddlers. The 95th percentile exposure ranged from less than 0.01 mg/kg bw in infants to 48.7 mg/kg bw in toddlers.
In the brand-loyal scenario for refined exposure, mean intake of agar (E 406) as a food additive spanned from 0.01 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day in infants to 14.8 mg/kg bw per day in toddlers. High exposure levels in this scenario varied from less than 0.01 mg/kg bw per day for infants to 44.5 mg/kg bw per day for toddlers. For the non-brand-loyal scenario, mean exposure was between 0.01 mg/kg bw per day (infants) and 8.4 mg/kg bw per day (toddlers). The 95th percentile exposure in this scenario ranged from below 0.01 mg/kg bw per day in infants to 26.2 mg/kg bw per day in toddlers.
Sources of uncertainty in the agar (E 406) exposure assessment were previously discussed. Following EFSA guidance on dietary exposure uncertainties, these sources have been considered and are summarized in Table 8.
Agar (E 406) is permitted as a Group I food additive across 66 food categories, with specific uses in four others. Given its general authorization, its use is not guaranteed in all these categories, which likely explains why reported usage data were only provided for nine categories. The Panel's current estimates, based on seven food categories, represent between 12% and 16% by weight of the foods permitted to contain agar under Annex II, varying by population group.
The Panel observed that Mintel's GNPD data revealed approximately 15 of the 72 food subcategories, classified by Mintel nomenclature, that listed agar (E 406) were incorporated into the current exposure estimates. These 15 subcategories accounted for roughly 50% of all food products labeled with agar (E 406) in the database. In the remaining 57 subcategories, where agar (E 406) was also labeled but not included in the exposure assessment, its use was authorized in 50 of them.
The Panel concluded that identified uncertainties likely lead to an overestimation of agar (E 406) exposure in Europe for both the maximum level and refined scenarios. This refined scenario only considered data from 10% of authorized uses.
The Panel observed that only about half of the food products labeled with agar (E 406), according to the Mintel GNPD, fell into subcategories with reported use levels included in the exposure assessment. This suggests that agar may be used in food categories for which no industry data was submitted. If this assumption holds true, the current assessment might underestimate actual exposure levels.
The exposure assessment did not include agar (E 406) that may be present due to carry-over in food additives, enzymes, and nutrients. Additionally, uses of agar as an ingredient in slimming products and other foods were not considered in this opinion.
Agar is used in slimming products and for supportive treatment of diabetes and/or obesity, acting as a satiating agent by swelling to induce fullness.
Agar, also known as kanten, is used in slimming products and diets, such as the 'kanten plan' in Japan and gaining popularity in the USA. It functions as a satiating material by swelling in the stomach, increasing bulk and promoting a feeling of fullness, which can lead to reduced food intake.
Agar is used in homemade foods and as a pharmaceutical excipient.
Agar serves as an excipient in pharmaceuticals, functioning as a tablet disintegrant or emulsifier across solid, semi-solid, and liquid dosage forms.
Agar's historical medicinal applications are documented in various textbooks and pharmacopoeias, including the British Pharmaceutical Codex (BPC) and the United States Pharmacopeia (U.S.P.). Doses typically ranged from 4 to 16 grams daily, with an average single dose of 4 grams, though some sources mention daily intakes up to 20 grams. Its primary medicinal action is as a mild laxative, attributed to its swelling properties which increase intestinal bulk and stimulate muscle activity to promote peristalsis. Adverse effects were not generally reported. A common dosage recommendation was one to two teaspoons (approximately 2.75 grams each) three times a day, taken with sufficient fluids. Agar also found use in combination products for constipation, where its role was often as an emulsion stabilizer due to the small quantities present.
Information on the current medicinal use of agar, including its role as an excipient in pharmaceuticals, was obtained from the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Within the EU, few authorized medicinal products list agar as an active ingredient, typically in combination products. These formulations, used as an adjuvant for obesity treatment, claim a daily dosage of up to 900 mg, administered as 300 mg up to three times daily. Agar's use in children is not anticipated.
High molecular weight dietary polysaccharides can be broken down in the human large intestine. This colonic anaerobic digestion yields short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, which are absorbed. Limited in vitro data exist on the microbial fermentation of agar polysaccharides.
Human faeces from three adult donors were used as inoculum in an in vitro study to ferment low molecular weight agar polysaccharides, prepared via acid hydrolysis or hydrogen peroxide degradation (Ramnani et al., 2012). Fermentation assays measured organic matter disappearance and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production over 5, 10, and 24 hours of anaerobic incubation. After 24 hours, acetate and propionate production from Gracilaria-derived agar powder (48.7 mM) was 3.4 times higher than from Gelidium-derived agar powder (14.4 mM). The Panel observed that agar powders fermented less than inulin (61.5 mM) but more than cellulose (2.0 mM). In vivo data was also considered.
An in vivo study in five albino rats examined agar's digestibility over seven days, with a daily intake of 750 mg agar/rat. The calculated digestibility was 21%, based on a specific definition by the authors relating test material intake to faecal weight increase. However, the Panel deemed these digestibility data, as defined, unsuitable for evaluating agar absorption.
An in vitro human model was used to study the degradation of agar. This research indicated that agar undergoes partial fermentation in the large intestine due to the activity of intestinal microflora. While the exact rate of hydrolysis within the human gastrointestinal tract remains unknown, the Panel concluded that agar is unlikely to be absorbed unaltered. The limited fermentation of agar is expected to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFA). Considering the established role of SCFA as end products of dietary fiber fermentation by anaerobic intestinal microflora, the Panel determined that their potential generation from agar does not pose a safety concern.
Agar's acute oral toxicity is low, with reported LD50 values of 11,400 mg/kg bw in rats and 15,700 mg/kg bw in mice.
In an acute oral toxicity study, F344 rats and B6C3F1 mice received single gavage doses of agar (630, 1,750, or 2,500 mg/kg bw) in distilled water. Five males and five females of each species were tested. Necropsy revealed no chemical-related effects in either rats or mice.
In a one-week study, 30 female F344 rats received a diet containing 3% agar, either freeze-dried or gelled, equating to 3,600 mg/kg body weight daily. No post-observation period was conducted. The dosing did not induce illness, nor did it significantly alter final body weights or feed conversion ratios. Further testing was not performed.
A 14-day oral study involved five F344 rats and five B6C3F1 mice of each sex. They were administered diets containing 6,300 to 100,000 mg/kg of agar. Equivalent daily doses for rats ranged from 756 to 12,000 mg/kg body weight, and for mice, from 1,260 to 20,000 mg/kg body weight. No significant differences in weight or mortality were observed. Necropsies on day 15 revealed no substance-related effects. Further study details were not provided.
A short-term toxicity study, described by JECFA but not available for review, involved male rats fed a diet with 25% agar for four weeks. During the third week, growth retardation was observed. Additionally, the dry weight of the stomach and caecum was moderately increased, and the colon and rectum weighed more than double that of control groups.
A 6-week feeding study was conducted by Glauert et al. (1983) using Sprague-Dawley rats. Ten male rats were divided into two groups: one received a control diet, and the other received a diet supplemented with 8% agar, equivalent to 9,600 mg/kg body weight per day. No significant differences were observed in diet intake or weight gain between the groups. Rats on the control diet exhibited a flat colon mucosa with regularly spaced crypt openings. In contrast, rats fed the agar-supplemented diet developed surface irregularities in their colon lining, characterized by deep, coarse folds. The researchers suggested these disruptions could potentially increase toxin absorption and stimulate cell proliferation in the colon crypts. The Panel noted that the high agar concentration in the diet might have caused damage to the colon epithelium. Furthermore, these effects were only apparent after 6 weeks of treatment, and similar effects were not observed in other rat strains subjected to longer-term, higher-concentration subchronic studies.
A 62-day study by Booth et al. (1963) dosed six male weanling rats with 15% agar in their diet, equivalent to 1,350 mg/kg body weight daily.
While dosed animals showed comparable weight gain to controls, their food efficiency was significantly reduced. The Panel noted that the high dietary concentrations of agar may have led to nutritional imbalance, as other parameters were not investigated.
A subchronic toxicity study, described by JECFA in 1974 but unavailable to the Panel, involved feeding diets with 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30% agar to groups of six rats for 10 weeks. Rats fed the 10% agar diet showed approximately 20% greater weight gain compared to controls. However, those consuming diets with 20% and 30% agar required significantly more feed and water per gram of weight gain than the control group.
A 13-week oral dose range-finding study involved groups of 10 male and 10 female F344 rats and B6C3F1 mice. These animals were fed diets containing 0, 3,100, 6,300, 12,500, 25,000, or 50,000 mg/kg of diet. This equated to daily doses ranging from 0 to 4,500 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day for rats and 0 to 10,000 mg/kg bw per day for mice. Throughout the 91-day study, animals were observed twice daily and weighed weekly. At the study's conclusion, all surviving animals underwent necropsy. Two deaths occurred, one in a male control rat and one in a male control mouse. The administered doses did not result in any changes to body weights or any substance-related gross or histopathological effects. The Panel observed that these NTP studies lacked haematology, urinalysis, and clinical chemistry data.
Agar's mutagenicity was evaluated by Litton Bionetics using Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA 1530 and G-46 in a reverse mutation assay, and in a mitotic gene conversion assay with Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain D3. No genotoxicity was observed in these tests. However, the Panel noted limitations in the bacteria gene mutation assay, specifically an insufficient number of S. Typhimurium strains and a lack of information regarding metabolic activation and test substance concentrations. Furthermore, the gene conversion assay with S. cerevisiae is no longer considered a validated method for risk assessment.
In a study assessing agar's potential to induce chromosomal aberrations, human embryonic lung cells (WI-38) were exposed to doses of 10, 100, and 1,000 μg/mL without S9 mix. No cytogenetic effects were observed. The Panel acknowledged the assay's scientific soundness but noted its lack of further validation.
A host-mediated assay was conducted using groups of five male IBR mice. Agar was administered orally via gavage at doses of 7.15, 71.5, and 715.0 mg/kg. This administration was performed either as a single acute dose or over five consecutive days, with 24-hour intervals between doses. Control groups, both negative and positive, were also included. The highest dose administered, 715 mg/kg, was equivalent to the calculated LD5. The study utilized specific indicator organisms: two histidine auxotrophs (his G-46, TA 1530) of *S. Typhimurium* to assess reverse mutation induction, and a diploid strain (D-3) of *S. cerevisiae* to evaluate mitotic gene conversion. For both acute and subacute study phases, within 30 minutes of the final dose, all mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 2 mL of the indicator organism. Each mL contained 3.0 × 10⁸ cells for *Salmonella* and 5.0 × 10⁸ cells for *Saccharomyces*. Three hours post-injection, the mice were sacrificed. The host cells were then aseptically collected from the peritoneal cavity of each animal and plated under standard conditions. A supplementary experiment was conducted under identical conditions, but with a single agar dose of 5,000 mg/kg. The findings from both experiments indicated that agar exhibited no genotoxic activity across all tested indicator organisms and dose levels. Despite these results, the Panel noted that the host-mediated assay has not undergone further validation and is consequently no longer utilized in risk assessments.
A study investigated agar's potential to induce chromosomal aberrations in rat bone marrow cells through an in vivo cytogenetic assay. Male albino rats, five per group, received oral gavage doses of 7.15, 71.5, or 715 mg/kg body weight (bw) acutely. A subacute treatment involved administering these same doses daily for five consecutive days, with sampling 24 hours apart. Control groups, both negative and positive, were also included. For acute treatment, bone marrow cells were sampled at 6, 24, and 48 hours post-administration. In the subacute study, sampling occurred only at 6 hours after the final dose. An additional experiment used a single dose of 5,000 mg/kg under identical conditions. The findings revealed that agar did not increase chromosomal aberrations in bone marrow cells following either acute or subacute administration at any tested dose level. The Panel observed that this study generally adhered to OECD Guideline 475 requirements, despite being conducted in 1974.
A dominant lethal assay was conducted to assess the genotoxicity of agar. Male albino rats were administered agar via oral gavage either acutely at doses of 7.15, 71.5, or 715 mg/kg body weight (bw), or subacutely over five consecutive days at the same dose levels. Control groups, both negative and positive, were included. Following treatment, the male rats were mated sequentially with two females per week for eight weeks (seven weeks for the subacute study) and housed individually until sacrifice. A separate experiment replicated these conditions but used a single dose of 5,000 mg/kg bw for both acute and subacute treatments. The study evaluated total implants (including live fetuses and fetal deaths), dead implants, the ratio of dead implants to total implants, and preimplantation loss. The results showed some increases in average implantations and average resorptions in the low-dose group during week 8 of the acute treatment, compared to the negative control. Differences were also observed across various parameters during the subacute treatment. However, these effects did not demonstrate a clear dose-response relationship or a time trend. No adverse effects were noted at the 5,000 mg/kg bw dose in either the acute or subacute phases. Based on these findings, the study author concluded that agar does not induce dominant lethal mutations under the tested conditions. The Panel concurred with this conclusion, while also noting the limited number of animals used in the study.
Despite limited in vitro and in vivo studies, no genotoxic activity has been observed for agar. Its routine use as a substrate in bacterial and mammalian cell mutation assays further supports this. Considering agar's chemical structure and minimal absorption, the Panel concluded there is no genotoxicity concern for agar (E 406).
The Panel identified no chronic toxicity studies according to OECD guidelines or equivalent.
A carcinogenicity study was conducted by the National Toxicology Program (NTP) in 1982, with findings published by Melnick et al. in 1983. This study involved groups of 50 B6C3F1 mice of each sex. Agar was administered through their diet, without any solvent, at concentrations of 0, 25,000, or 50,000 mg per kilogram of diet. This equated to daily doses of 0, 2,250, or 4,500 mg of agar per kilogram of body weight for the mice. The exposure period lasted for 103 weeks, followed by two weeks on a basal diet before the study concluded. Throughout the study, animals were observed twice daily. Individual weights and signs of toxicity were documented monthly. Animals that were moribund or survived until the end of the study were euthanized. Comprehensive gross and microscopic examinations were performed on major organs and tissues. However, parameters such as hematology, clinical chemistry, and urinalysis were not assessed. Female mice in the low and high-dose groups showed reduced mean body weights (20% and 16% lower than controls, respectively) from week 20 onwards, a trend that persisted throughout the study. Despite these weight changes, the treatment did not result in adverse effects related to clinical signs of toxicity, survival rates, feed consumption, or non-neoplastic changes. Crucially, there was no indication of a substance-related increase in tumor incidence in either the mice or the rats studied.
In a 1982 carcinogenicity study by the National Toxicology Program (NTP), published by Melnick et al. in 1983, groups of 50 F344 rats of each sex were fed diets containing 0, 25,000, or 50,000 mg of agar per kilogram of diet for 103 weeks. This dosage is equivalent to 0, 1,250, or 2,500 mg of agar per kilogram of body weight per day for rats. The study followed the same design as a parallel study in mice. No adverse effects on clinical signs of toxicity, survival rates, feed consumption, or non-neoplastic changes were observed. Crucially, the study found no indication of a substance-related increase in tumor incidence in either rats or mice. Based on these findings, the Panel concluded that agar showed no carcinogenic potential and that no adverse effects were noted at the highest tested dose of 2,500 mg/kg body weight per day. Initiation-promotion studies were also considered but deemed not relevant for the risk assessment of agar as a food additive.
The Panel reviewed a study on agar's potential to enhance cancer development in animals treated with an initiating agent. However, this study was deemed irrelevant for the risk assessment of agar as a food additive. No other relevant studies were found.
Studies by the Food and Drug Research Laboratories involved administering varying doses of agar, suspended in anhydrous corn oil, via gavage to pregnant animals. Control groups received only the vehicle. Throughout gestation, body weights were monitored, and daily observations of appearance and behavior were conducted. Following caesarean sections, implantation sites, resorptions, and the number and weight of live fetuses were recorded. Fetuses underwent gross examination for sex distribution and external abnormalities, with a subset receiving detailed visceral examination and skeletal defect analysis. These studies included mice, rats, hamsters, and rabbits.
In a prenatal developmental toxicity study, CD-1 mice were administered agar orally via gavage daily from gestation day (GD) 6 to 15. Doses ranged from 0 to 1,570 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day, with varying numbers of mated females per dose group. While pregnancies in lower dose groups survived to term, the highest dose group (1,570 mg/kg bw per day) experienced increased dam mortality (9 out of 48 animals) and a reduced pregnancy rate (42%) among survivors. At GD 17, surviving dams in the highest dose group showed significantly increased mortality and a decrease in pregnancy rate. In contrast, doses up to 329 mg agar/kg bw per day showed no adverse effects on implantation, maternal or fetal survival, fetal numbers, or fetal weights. However, at the highest dose of 1,570 mg/kg bw per day, a notable increase in resorptions and significant growth retardation of live fetuses, including decreased fetal weights and incomplete ossification, were observed. Fetal sex distribution remained unaffected by the treatment. Examination of soft tissues and skeletons revealed no differences in abnormalities between agar-treated groups and the vehicle-treated control group. Consequently, the Panel determined the no-observed-adverse-effect-level (NOAEL) for both maternal and developmental toxicity in this study to be 329 mg agar/kg bw per day.
In a study involving pregnant Wistar rats, daily oral gavage administrations of agar in corn oil were conducted from gestation day 6 to 15. Doses ranged from 0 to 1,140 mg/kg body weight per day, with 25 animals per group. Upon necropsy on gestation day 20, dams receiving doses up to the highest tested level of 1,140 mg/kg body weight per day exhibited no adverse effects on implantation, maternal survival, or fetal survival. Fetal outcomes, including the number of live or dead fetuses, resorptions, average implantations, and fetal weights, were comparable across all groups. Furthermore, the sex distribution of fetuses and the incidence of abnormalities in soft tissues or skeletons did not differ between the agar-treated groups and the vehicle-treated control group. Consequently, the Panel identified the no-observed-adverse-effect-level (NOAEL) for both maternal and developmental toxicity as 1,140 mg agar/kg body weight per day, which was the highest dose administered.
In a study using golden hamsters, pregnant animals (25-30 per group) received oral doses of agar ranging from 0 to 650 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day, administered via gavage from gestation day 6 to 10. Necropsies performed on gestation day 14 revealed that dams treated with doses up to 650 mg/kg bw per day showed no adverse effects. This included normal implantation rates, maternal and fetal survival, and no significant differences in fetal numbers (live or dead), resorptions, implant sites, or fetal weights compared to the control group. The sex distribution of fetuses was also unaffected. Fetal examinations for abnormalities in soft tissues or skeletons did not reveal any differences between the agar-treated groups and the control group. Consequently, the Panel determined that the no-observed-adverse-effect-level (NOAEL) for both maternal and developmental toxicity in this study was 650 mg agar/kg bw per day, the highest dose administered.
In a study involving Dutch-belted rabbits, pregnant females were administered agar daily via oral gavage from gestation day 6 to 18. Doses ranged from 0 to 400 mg/kg body weight per day, with varying numbers of mated females per group. At gestation day 29, dam mortality was observed across the different dose groups, with the highest dose group (400 mg/kg bw per day) showing evident maternal lethality. Surviving dams appeared normal, and no effects on implantation were noted. Caesarean sections revealed normal fetuses. The Panel identified the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) for maternal lethality/toxicity as 118.5 mg agar/kg body weight per day. The NOAEL for developmental toxicity was determined to be 400 mg agar/kg body weight per day, which was the highest dose tested.
Maternal mortality observed in female mice at 1,570 mg/kg bw per day and in female rabbits at 400 mg/kg bw per day was inconsistent with acute toxicity data. This effect was not seen in other species. The Panel attributed the mortality to challenges in administering the viscous solution to mice and rabbits. Consequently, maternal toxicity in these two species was deemed not relevant for the risk assessment.
No fertility data were available. Prenatal developmental toxicity studies in mice and rabbits were deemed unsuitable for risk assessment due to significant maternal mortality. However, studies in rats and hamsters showed no maternal or developmental effects, even at the highest doses tested (1,140 and 650 mg agar/kg bw per day, respectively).
A study investigated the impact of polysaccharides, including agar, on mineral absorption, weight gain, and fecal dry matter excretion in weanling male rats. When included at 10% in a semisynthetic diet (equivalent to 1,200 mg/kg body weight per day) for 8 days, agar significantly reduced the absorption of all tested minerals: calcium, iron, zinc, copper, chromium, and cobalt. Continuous feeding of agar at the same dose for 21 weeks revealed that interference with iron and copper absorption was most pronounced during early rapid growth. Zinc, chromium, and cobalt absorption remained significantly reduced throughout the entire 21-week study. Despite increased fecal mineral losses due to agar ingestion, the total mineral content of the carcasses at the study's conclusion was not affected.
A woman with a gastric bypass experienced nausea, vomiting, and dysphagia after consuming an agar-based dessert. A gastric agar bezoar was later detected and removed.
A 70-year-old Japanese woman presented with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting after consuming a large amount of highly concentrated agar dissolved in boiling water 48 hours prior. Bezoars, believed to have formed from the agar, were surgically removed from her distal jejunum. This individual had a history of type-2 diabetes and occasionally consumed dietary agar. Two other instances of bezoar formation following the ingestion of agar solutions have also been documented.
The Panel deemed these cases irrelevant for assessing agar's safety as a food additive for the general population. The consumption was either for dietary reasons in large quantities or occurred in patients with specific medical conditions, such as gastric bypass. Clinical studies are discussed next.
In a study by Maeda et al. (2005), the effectiveness of an agar-inclusive diet, alongside traditional Japanese food, was assessed in individuals with impaired glucose tolerance and type-2 diabetes who were also obese. Following a four-week period where participants maintained their usual eating habits, 76 individuals were randomly allocated to either a conventional diet or a conventional diet supplemented with agar. Those in the agar-diet group consumed a daily 180g jelly-like preparation containing 4.5g of agar, seasoned with condiments, 15 minutes prior to their evening meal. Both dietary groups followed their assigned regimens for 12 weeks. Before and after this period, various health indicators were measured, including body weight, body mass index (BMI), glycemic control, blood pressure, insulin resistance, total body fat, fat distribution, and lipid levels. The agar-diet group demonstrated significantly greater mean changes in body weight, BMI, and total cholesterol compared to the conventional diet group after 12 weeks. This diet led to substantial weight loss, attributed to sustained reduced calorie intake and improved metabolic parameters. The authors reported that the 38 participants in the agar-diet group finished the 12-week trial without experiencing any notable adverse effects. Agar is also utilized in pharmaceutical applications.
Agar acts as a mild laxative due to its swelling properties. It is believed to increase intestinal bulk, stimulating muscles and aiding peristalsis. No adverse effects are noted with a typical dosage of 1-2 teaspoons (2.75g each) taken three times daily with liquids, potentially reaching up to 16.5g per day (Heber, 2007).
Information regarding the current medicinal use of agar and its role as an excipient in pharmaceuticals was obtained from the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Agar is authorized as an active ingredient in only a few medicinal products within the EU, typically in combination formulations. These products may contain up to 900 mg of agar daily (administered as 300 mg up to three times a day) as an adjuvant for obesity treatment. Contraindications include intestinal stenosis, and caution is advised for patients with megacolon due to altered colic motility, as there is a risk of faecalome. Meteorism, particularly at the start of treatment, is noted as a potential side effect. Agar may be used during pregnancy and lactation if medically necessary, as it is believed to be unabsorbed. Its use in children is not anticipated.
Bowel obstruction and abnormal oesophageal or intestinal narrowing are contraindications for agar used as a mild laxative.
The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has established specific labeling requirements for over-the-counter (OTC) drug products containing agar or similar water-soluble gums as an active ingredient. These requirements apply when the product is in a dry or incompletely hydrated form, such as capsules, granules, powders, tablets, or wafers. The aim is to prevent esophageal obstruction or asphyxiation. The mandated warning states: "Taking this product without adequate fluid may cause it to swell and block your throat or esophagus and may cause choking. Do not take this product if you have difficulty in swallowing. If you experience chest pain, vomiting, or difficulty in swallowing or breathing after taking this product, seek immediate medical attention" (FDA, 2016).
No published case reports of oesophageal obstruction or asphyxiation were found in the literature concerning the intake of solid or dehydrated agar.
Agar (CAS 9002-18-0, EINECS 232-658-1) is a polysaccharide derived from red algae, specifically from the Gelidiaceae and Gracilariaceae families, as defined by Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012.
Agar (E 406) is a hydrophilic colloidal polysaccharide. It primarily comprises galactose units, with a regular alternation of l- and d-isomeric forms. These hexoses are linked alternately via alpha-1,3 and beta-1,4 bonds. Approximately every tenth d-galactopyranose unit has a hydroxyl group esterified with sulfuric acid, which is then neutralized by calcium, magnesium, potassium, or sodium.
Agar's degradation has been studied using an in vitro human model, which showed partial fermentation in the large intestine by gut microflora. While the exact rate of hydrolysis in the human gastrointestinal tract remains unknown, the Panel concluded that agar is unlikely to be absorbed unchanged. The limited fermentation is expected to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFA). Given the established role of SCFA as end products of dietary fiber fermentation by anaerobic intestinal microflora, their potential formation from agar is not a safety concern. Furthermore, agar exhibits low acute toxicity in mice, rats, and rabbits.
In 1982, the National Toxicology Program (NTP) conducted two dose range-finding subchronic toxicity studies on agar. These studies involved mice and rats, with dosages ranging up to 10,000 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day for mice and 4,500 mg/kg bw per day for rats, representing the highest doses tested. No adverse effects were observed at these levels. However, the Panel noted that these NTP studies did not include assessments of haematology, urinalysis, or clinical chemistry.
Agar (E 406) showed no genotoxic activity in available in vitro and in vivo assays. While the study package was limited, agar is commonly used as a substrate in in vitro gene mutation tests. Considering its chemical structure and minimal absorption, the Panel found no genotoxicity concerns for agar.
The Panel identified no chronic toxicity studies meeting OECD guidelines or equivalent standards.
Carcinogenicity studies by NTP and Melnick et al. (1983) found no evidence of agar causing increased tumor incidence in mice or rats. At the highest tested doses of 4,500 mg/kg bw per day in mice and 2,500 mg/kg bw per day in rats, agar was not found to be carcinogenic. The Panel concurred with these findings.
No fertility data were available. However, prenatal developmental toxicity studies in rats and hamsters showed no maternal or developmental effects, even at the highest tested doses of 1,140 and 650 mg agar/kg body weight per day, respectively.
Based on the conceptual framework for re-evaluating food additives under Commission Regulation (EU) No 257/2010, the Panel concluded that sufficient toxicity studies demonstrated no adverse effects even at the highest tested doses. Consequently, they determined that a numerical Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) for agar (E 406) is unnecessary.
Agar has been used as an adjuvant in diabetes and obesity treatment. A study found that participants taking 4.5 g of agar daily for 12 weeks experienced no noticeable side effects.
Agar, used as a mild laxative in doses up to 16.5g daily with sufficient fluids, has no described adverse effects. However, intestinal stenosis is a contraindication.
Agar (E 406) is authorized for use in numerous food types, though industry reports its application in only a fraction of these categories (9 out of 70). The Panel anticipates that brand loyalty will not significantly increase general population exposure, with exceptions for consumers of specific food categories (13.2, 13.3, and 13.4). Consequently, the non-brand-loyal refined scenario was deemed the most appropriate for assessing exposure to this additive.
The safety assessment was limited to seven food categories, out of 70 where agar is authorized, for which industry provided data. The Panel concluded that identified uncertainties likely led to an overestimation of exposure within these seven categories.
The Panel noted that approximately half of food products labeled with agar (E 406), according to Mintel GNPD data, fell into subcategories with reported use levels included in the exposure assessment. This suggests agar may be used in categories for which industry data was not provided. If this is the case, the current assessment might underestimate actual exposure levels.
Exposure to agar from its use as a carrier in food additives, food enzymes, nutrients, and flavourings, as per Annex III, was not included in the exposure assessment.
Refined exposure estimates for agar (E 406) rely on reported usage levels. Should actual practices change, these estimates may become outdated and require revision.
Considering the conceptual framework, agar is unlikely to be absorbed unchanged, has sufficient toxicity data, and poses no genotoxicity or carcinogenic concerns.
The safety assessment for agar (E 406) is confined to its usage and levels within seven specific food categories, out of the seventy where it is authorized. A refined exposure assessment indicated a potential intake of up to 26 mg/kg body weight per day for toddlers at the 95th percentile, assuming a non-brand-loyal consumption pattern. Agar is not expected to be absorbed unchanged and undergoes minor fermentation by gut microbiota. The available toxicity data were deemed sufficient. No genotoxic effects were observed, and carcinogenicity studies in mice and rats did not reveal any substance-related tumor incidence at the highest doses tested (4,500 mg/kg bw/day and 2,500 mg/kg bw/day, respectively). Furthermore, human studies showed that oral intake of agar (4,500 mg/person, equivalent to 64 mg/kg bw/day) was well-tolerated for 12 weeks without adverse effects.
The Panel concluded that a numerical ADI for agar is unnecessary. Furthermore, based on the refined exposure assessment for its reported uses as a food additive, there is no safety concern for the general population.
To prevent agar (E 406) from becoming a significant source of toxic elements, particularly for infants and children, the Panel recommended revising the EU specification's maximum limits for lead, mercury, cadmium, and arsenic impurities.
Information on formaldehyde use was requested, but not provided following the data call. The Panel recommended this information be supplied.
The Panel noted discrepancies between industry-reported data and the Mintel database, which shows agar labeled on more products than the food categories for which industry provided data. Consequently, the Panel recommended collecting usage and use level data for agar (E 406) to enable a more realistic exposure assessment.
The safety re-evaluation of agar (E 406) as a food additive drew upon a comprehensive review of available scientific data. This included communications from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in May and June 2015, requesting information on certain food additive substances. Significant data submissions were also received from industry stakeholders. Hispanagar and Marinalg provided key documents in 2010, including responses to EFSA's call for data on emulsifiers, stabilizers, and gelling agents. These submissions detailed agar's chemistry, specifications, manufacturing processes, current usage, and other relevant studies. Earlier, in 1973, the Federation of American Societies for Experimental Biology (FASEB) had prepared an evaluation of agar-agar's health aspects for the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Similarly, Food and Drug Research Laboratories (FDRL) Inc. conducted a teratologic evaluation of agar-agar in four species (rats, mice, hamsters, and rabbits) in 1973, also submitted by Hispanagar and Marinalg. Informatics Inc. had previously assessed agar-agar as a GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) food ingredient for the FDA in 1972. Further toxicological data came from Litton Bionetic Inc. in 1974, which performed a mutagenic evaluation of powdered agar across three systems: in vitro and in vivo host-mediated assays, in vitro and in vivo cytogenetics, and an in vivo dominant lethal assay. Marinalg International also submitted comprehensive data in 2010, covering ADME, subchronic toxicity, genotoxicity, chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, and reproduction and developmental toxicity. The National Toxicology Program (NTP) provided its 1982 carcinogenesis bioassay of agar in rats and mice. A pre-evaluation document from Fraunhofer ITEM, dated October 2012, also contributed to the review. In 2014, EFSA received usage level data for agar (E 406) in response to its call for data on food additives in food and beverages. This included submissions from Babbi confectionery industry, Biovegan GmbH, FoodDrinkEurope (FDE), the International Chewing Gum Association (ICGA), and an unnamed interested party. These industry submissions provided crucial information on the actual levels of agar used in various food products across the EU.

Agar (E 406) is authorized in numerous food categories, with specific use levels reported by industry for nine out of seventy categories. For instance, in flavored fermented milk products (category 01.4), typical usage is 340 mg/kg, reaching up to 560 mg/kg. Edible ices (category 03) show a wider range, from 0.0 mg/kg to 150.0 mg/kg, with a specific note for a semi-finished product where 100g is added per kg of finished product. Fruit and vegetable preparations, excluding compote (category 04.2.4.1), have reported levels of 187.5 mg/kg, specifically for pastry fillings. Cocoa and chocolate products (category 05.1), restricted to energy-reduced or no-added-sugar varieties, show typical use of 141.0 mg/kg and a maximum of 288.1 mg/kg. Chewing gum (category 05.3) utilizes agar at 800.0 mg/kg typically, up to 1800.0 mg/kg. Decorations, coatings, and fillings (category 05.4) exhibit varied usage, with some levels reaching 20,000.0 mg/kg, and specific applications like sweet sauces for desserts or glazes showing levels of 0.3 mg/kg. Meat preparations (category 08.3.1) have reported levels of 140.0 mg/kg, with a note that actual usage in sausages is much lower. Desserts (category 16), excluding certain types, show typical usage of 79.1 mg/kg and a maximum of 4055.1 mg/kg. Finally, in category 18, which covers processed foods not elsewhere classified, agar is used at 0.5 mg/kg, described as pure agar or a gelling agent for products like aspic and confectionery.
Analysis of the Mintel Global New Products Database revealed that agar (E 406) is labeled on over 2,300 products across various categories. Chilled desserts showed a high prevalence, with 208 products out of 5,582 containing agar. Similarly, non-individually wrapped chocolate pieces and caramel & cream spreads also featured agar prominently. Confectionery items like pastilles, gums, jellies, and chews, along with other frozen and shelf-stable desserts, frequently listed agar as an ingredient. While cakes, pastries, and sweet goods had the highest total number of products (11,854), the percentage labeled with agar was lower at 1.1%. Overall, agar was found on approximately 0.5% of the total food products sampled in the database.

For many food categories, agar is authorized at quantum satis (QS), meaning it can be used without a specific quantitative limit, as long as good manufacturing practices are followed. In these QS categories, concentration data were not provided by industry, and therefore, these categories were excluded from the exposure assessment. Specifically, for unflavoured fermented milk products (01.3), unflavoured live fermented cream products (01.6.2), other creams (01.6.3), unripened cheese (01.7.1), processed cheese (01.7.5), other cheese products (01.7.6), and dairy analogues (01.8), no concentration data were available, leading to their exclusion. Similarly, other fat and oil emulsions (02.2.2) and vegetable oil pan spray (02.3) were not included due to a lack of concentration data and, in the case of vegetable oil pan spray, a missing FoodEx code. Dried fruit and vegetables (04.2.1), fruit and vegetables in vinegar, oil, or brine (04.2.2), and processed potato products (04.2.6) also lacked concentration data. Fruit and vegetable preparations (04.2.4.1) were excluded because there was no specific FoodEx code for 'fruit filling'. Jam, jellies, marmalades, and sweetened chestnut puree (04.2.5.2) and other similar fruit or vegetable spreads (04.2.5.3) have an MPL of 10,000 mg/kg, but no concentration data were provided. Nut butters and nut spreads (04.2.5.4) were excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Other confectionery, including breath freshening microsweets (05.2), has a restriction stating that E 406 may not be used in jelly mini-cups. This category was excluded due to no concentration data. Decorations, coatings, and fillings (05.4), excluding fruit-based fillings, were not included as there was no corresponding FoodEx code. Starches (06.2.2), breakfast cereals (06.3), dry pasta (06.4.2) (with exceptions for gluten-free and hypoproteic diets), potato gnocchi (06.4.4) (with exceptions for fresh refrigerated), fillings of stuffed pasta (06.4.5), noodles (06.5), batters (06.6), and pre-cooked or processed cereals (06.7) were all excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Bread and rolls (07.1) (with exceptions) and fine bakery wares (07.2) were also excluded due to no concentration data. For meat preparations (08.3.1), an MPL of 40 mg/kg was noted, with reported use levels provided. However, these levels were initially provided for seasoning or extracts, and a factor of 10 was applied to reflect their use in finished products. Heat-treated meat products (08.3.2), with exceptions for foie gras and similar products, were also excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Casings, coatings, and decorations for meat (08.3.3) were excluded due to no concentration data and a missing FoodEx code. Processed fish and fishery products (09.2), processed fish roe (09.3), and processed egg products (10.2) were excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Other sugars and syrups (11.2), salt substitutes (12.1.2), seasonings and condiments (12.2.2), vinegars (12.3), mustard (12.4), soups and broths (12.5), sauces (12.6), salads and savoury based sandwich spreads (12.7), yeast and yeast products (12.8), and protein products (12.9) were all excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Dietary foods for special medical purposes (13.2) (only in tablet and coated tablet format), dietary foods for weight control diets (13.3), and foods suitable for people intolerant to gluten (13.4) (including dry pasta) were excluded due to no concentration data. Fruit juices (14.1.2) (only vegetable juices) and fruit nectars (14.1.3) (only vegetable nectars) were excluded due to no concentration data. Flavoured drinks (14.1.4) and other beverages (14.1.5.2) (excluding unflavoured leaf tea, including flavoured instant coffee) were also excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Cider and perry (14.2.3), fruit wine and made wine (14.2.4), mead (14.2.5), spirit drinks (14.2.6) (with exceptions), aromatised wines (14.2.7.1), aromatised wine-based drinks (14.2.7.2), aromatised wine-product cocktails (14.2.7.3), and other alcoholic drinks (14.2.8) were all excluded due to no concentration data. Potato-, cereal-, flour-, or starch-based snacks (15.1) and processed nuts (15.2) were excluded due to a lack of concentration data. Desserts (16), excluding products in categories 1, 3, and 4, have an MPL of 20,794 mg/kg and reported use levels were available, with mean and maximum concentrations noted. Food supplements in solid form (17.1), liquid form (17.2), and syrup-type or chewable form (17.3) were excluded due to no concentration data. Processed foods not covered by categories 1-17 (18), excluding foods for infants and young children, were excluded due to no usable concentration data. In summary, only a limited number of food categories had reported use levels for agar (E 406) that were included in the refined exposure assessment. These included Flavoured fermented milk products (01.4), Edible ices (03), Cocoa and chocolate products (05.1) (with specific restrictions), Chewing gum (05.3), Non-heat-treated meat products (08.3.1), and Desserts (16). For many other categories, agar is authorized but its usage levels were not provided, leading to their exclusion from the exposure calculations.

The Maximum Permitted Level (MPL) scenario shows mean exposures ranging from 0.01 mg/kg bw/day in infants to 17.0 mg/kg bw/day in toddlers, with 95th percentile values reaching up to 48.7 mg/kg bw/day in toddlers. The refined exposure assessment, considering reported use levels, yielded lower estimates. In the brand-loyal scenario, mean exposures ranged from 0.01 mg/kg bw/day in infants to 14.8 mg/kg bw/day in toddlers, with 95th percentile values up to 44.5 mg/kg bw/day. The non-brand-loyal scenario, deemed most relevant, indicated mean exposures from 0.01 mg/kg bw/day in infants to 8.4 mg/kg bw/day in toddlers. The 95th percentile for this scenario was up to 26.2 mg/kg bw/day in toddlers. Certain population groups had missing 95th percentile data due to insufficient sample sizes.
