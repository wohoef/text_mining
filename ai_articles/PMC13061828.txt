Households are significant contributors to global environmental impacts, driving a substantial portion of economic activity. To effectively reduce these impacts, it's crucial to understand and quantify the diverse consumption patterns and their environmental consequences at a local level. This paper introduces and evaluates a detailed, regionalized bottom-up model designed to generate realistic environmental profiles for individual households within a specific area. The model integrates three key components: a physically-based building energy model, data from an agent-based transport simulation, and a data-driven household consumption model. These components are connected through a novel probability-based classification framework and have been applied to Switzerland. This integrated approach allows the model to forecast demands across approximately 400 consumption categories for every Swiss household, taking into account their unique situations. This process yields a realistic representation of the variations in household environmental footprints. Analysis of the model's output at the municipal level highlights several factors influencing carbon footprints, including per-capita income, population density, the age of buildings, and household structure. Municipalities with higher emissions are typically rural and feature a greater proportion of older buildings. Conversely, communities with lower emissions often have more families and are generally situated in densely populated areas. The presence of conflicting influences among these variables underscores the necessity of a model capable of discerning regional differences. Ultimately, this comprehensive model provides policymakers with a robust information base to comprehend local consumption trends and formulate environmental strategies tailored to their specific populations.

Households are significant drivers of economic activity, leading to substantial environmental impacts through the consumption of goods and services. These activities, occurring throughout supply chains, necessitate resource utilization and result in emissions. Research by Ivanova et al. (2016) indicates that households contribute an estimated 65% of global greenhouse gas emissions and between 50% and 80% of the total land, material, and water consumption. Given that human activities are surpassing the Earth's carrying capacity (Steffen et al., 2015), a pressing need exists for fundamental shifts in current consumer behaviors (e.g., Hertwich, 2005; Minx et al., 2009; Schanes, Giljum, & Hertwich, 2016; Tukker, Cohen, Hubacek, & Mont, 2010). Nevertheless, individual households face considerable challenges in altering their lifestyles and minimizing their environmental footprints, largely due to economic limitations, deeply ingrained socio-cultural norms, and the complexity of supply chains (O'Rourke & Lollo, 2015).
Policymakers are crucial in fostering sustainable consumption, regardless of ongoing discussions about responsibility. They can achieve this by implementing financial incentives, regulations, best-practice guidance, and improved infrastructure. Local authorities, including municipal, regional, and city-level initiatives, are increasingly recognized for their significant role in this effort. Their proximity to local needs and desires makes them particularly effective in driving these changes.
To effectively develop targeted policies and interventions, quantitative data regarding local household consumption patterns and their environmental impacts is crucial (Horta & Keirstead, 2017). Many current environmental evaluations utilize top-down methodologies, concentrating on national average households (e.g., Bin & Dowlatabadi, 2005; Hertwich, 2011; Hertwich & Peters, 2009; Ivanova et al., 2016; Tukker, Eder, & Suh, 2006) or examining distinct household segments within a nation (e.g., Girod & De Haan, 2009, 2010; Jones & Kammen, 2011; Weber & Matthews, 2008). While these studies are valuable for international comparisons and identifying national consumption hotspots and trends, they lack the specificity needed for regional policy development (Horta & Keirstead, 2017; Moran et al., 2018). Given the intricate nature of household consumption and diverse lifestyles, generic solutions are often ineffective. Therefore, a higher spatial resolution and more detailed information are necessary for impactful policymaking (Jones & Kammen, 2011; Lenzen et al., 2006; Minx et al., 2009).
Gathering comprehensive consumption data for specific regions is often too demanding for local authorities. To overcome this challenge and facilitate action, models offer a valuable solution by adapting information from existing databases to local circumstances, thereby avoiding extensive data collection.
Existing regionalized models that disaggregate environmental footprints to sub-scales (e.g., Baiocchi et al., 2010; Druckman & Jackson, 2008; Jones & Kammen, 2014; Minx et al., 2013; Moran et al., 2018) often focus on average households within smaller areas. While these models offer some spatial variation, they still operate at aggregated levels. To create truly effective, localized interventions, a deeper understanding of individual households and their lifestyle impacts is essential. This necessitates capturing the diverse consumption patterns within a region. Such detailed insight is especially crucial for "soft" policy tools like awareness campaigns or personalized messages, which benefit from incorporating behavioral economics and psychology (Frederiks et al., 2015; O'Rourke & Lollo, 2015). Given that purchasing decisions occur at the household level, a bottom-up modeling approach, centered on individual households, would be ideal for explicitly representing the variability of household behavior within a study area.
A household-centric modeling approach offers the advantage of generating highly localized data, which can then be aggregated to inform policymakers at various spatial scales, such as municipal, district, or national levels. Previous household-centered models by Saner et al. (2013, 2016) provided foundational inspiration for this study. However, these earlier models were limited in scope, as they did not encompass all consumption categories and failed to maintain the holistic context of household consumption, treating mobility, food, and housing as separate entities. Additionally, these models had not been implemented on a large scale.
The rise of "Big Data" has brought with it an abundance of information (Xu, Cai, & Liang, 2015). For policymakers, the value lies not just in the volume of data, but in the innovative methods used to analyze it. Techniques like data mining and machine learning have proven effective in uncovering patterns and predicting variables (Kuhn & Johnson, 2013; North, 2012; Xu et al., 2015). While the potential of machine learning in industrial ecology is acknowledged (Xu et al., 2015), its application to household environmental impacts remains largely unexplored in existing research. Given the intricate and multifaceted nature of household consumption behavior, these sophisticated techniques, adept at handling complexity and non-linearity (Kuhn & Johnson, 2013), offer significant promise for advancing this area of study.
This article aims to develop and assess a spatially detailed, bottom-up model of household consumption. The primary objective is to generate realistic environmental profiles for every household within a given region, thereby establishing a comprehensive information base that quantifies the diverse environmental impacts stemming from individual households. While existing spatially resolved models often utilize micro-data, account for various household types, or incorporate household characteristics, they typically do not adopt a truly household-centric approach. Consequently, these models fall short of providing comprehensive estimates of total consumption and its associated impacts for individual households. This limitation is highlighted in a recent review of consumption-based carbon footprint studies (Ottelin et al., 2019), which, despite organizing research by spatial dimension (international to sub-city), explicitly omits the household level due to its perceived lack of geographic reference. Our work, therefore, represents a novel effort to estimate consumption behavior and environmental impacts for individual households on a large scale. This provides quantitative data that can inform analyses ranging from the individual household level up to national assessments. We will leverage machine learning techniques to effectively utilize existing databases and maintain the holistic context of total household consumption. This unprecedented level of detailed information is expected to significantly assist policymakers in identifying specific consumer groups and formulating effective strategies to mitigate household-driven environmental impacts. Beyond data provision, the model is also designed to evaluate potential policy scenarios.
This article primarily introduces a novel, highly detailed method for modeling individual households. Beyond this core objective, the study also aims to demonstrate the utility of machine learning in household consumption research. It applies the developed framework to Switzerland, proving its scalability for large-scale operations. Furthermore, the paper includes a municipal-level analysis of household carbon footprints and explores the relationship between population density and greenhouse gas emissions at a hectare scale. These latter analyses serve to illustrate the model's potential, with in-depth scenario computations reserved for future work.
The modeling platform integrates three distinct sub-models to achieve its objectives: a physically based building energy model, a data-driven consumption model, and a mobility model derived from an agent-based traffic simulation. A key feature shared by all these sub-models is their "bottom-up" construction, meaning they focus on individual entities like households or buildings. They also consistently draw their input data from publicly available sources, such as Swiss national registers. This approach ensures transparency, facilitates regular updates, and promotes consistent modeling, leading to comparable results across different regions. The generalizability of these modeling concepts is further enhanced by the presence of similar databases in other countries, making the framework relevant beyond Switzerland. The interlinking of these sub-models is crucial, with the outputs from the building energy and mobility models informing the probability calculations for assigning consumption archetypes to households.
The model's practicality is showcased through its application to Switzerland. The scope of this study encompasses all private households, specifically the permanent residents of Switzerland, excluding groups like seasonal workers, incarcerated individuals, or those in retirement homes. For the life cycle assessment (LCA), the functional unit is defined as one year of household consumption, with all calculations primarily referencing the year 2013. Crucially, the analysis focuses solely on environmental impacts directly attributable to individual household behaviors, deliberately excluding government-induced impacts from redistribution to households. The foundational dataset for households is STATPOP 2013 (Statistics of the Population and Households of Switzerland). This database offers comprehensive socio-demographic details for every household and individual in Switzerland, including gender, age, marital status, and residential coordinates. In total, it provides 110 attributes for roughly 8 million people and 62 attributes for 3.5 million private households.
To determine household residential energy requirements, we incorporated the physically-based building energy model developed by Buffat, Froemelt, Heeren, Raubal, and Hellweg (2017). This model calculates the hot water and space heating demands for every residential building within a given area. It evolved from an earlier model by Saner et al. (2013), which Froemelt and Hellweg (2017) thoroughly analyzed and found suitable for extensive building stock studies. Buffat and colleagues enhanced this model by incorporating detailed geographical data, specifically 3D building geometries obtained from laser-scanning. This advancement allowed for the inclusion of site-specific shading, accounting for the influence of nearby buildings and terrain. The resulting model computes simplified energy balances for each Swiss residential building, considering factors like time, climate data, building features, statistics, surrounding topography, and 3D geometries. Its selection was largely due to its foundation in physical principles and its bottom-up, component-based design. This structure makes the model adept at evaluating detailed renovation scenarios, such as assessing the impact of physical interventions like insulating specific building components (e.g., walls, windows, or roofs).
The building model's integrated Monte Carlo simulation provided median estimates for each building's space heating and hot water needs. This energy demand was directly linked to individual households using STATPOP data, which specifies each household's building. For buildings with multiple dwellings, the total energy demand was distributed among households according to their apartment area, as documented in the Swiss Building and Dwellings Statistics (BDS).
Household mobility demands were determined using an agent-based simulation, mirroring the rationale for employing a physically based building energy model. This approach, utilizing the MATSim (Multi-Agent Transport Simulation) framework, was applied to Switzerland to model the population's travel patterns. MATSim, as detailed by Horni, Nagel, & Axhausen (2016) and Bauer et al. (2016), generates geographically and temporally detailed data on traffic modes and routes for individual agents. This capability makes it ideal for disaggregating mobility demands geographically. The mobility patterns derived from these statistical agents were then linked to specific STATPOP household members through a partially randomized optimization process, considering spatial data and personal attributes (further details in Supporting Information S1). This integration allows for the future evaluation of various mobility policy scenarios.
To quantify household purchases across categories like food, consumables, and other goods and services, the study utilized demand profiles derived from consumption archetypes developed by Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, and Hellweg (2018). This involved an extensive application of machine learning and data mining techniques to the Swiss Household Budget Survey (HBS) (BFS, 2013a). The HBS is a rich data source, offering detailed information on the characteristics, socio-economic status, spending, and quantities purchased for each surveyed Swiss household, with approximately 500 attributes per household (refer to Supporting Information S1). A two-stage clustering method was employed, which integrated both consumption data and socio-economic parameters. This process identified 28 distinct consumption-based archetypes, each representing typical household behavior patterns by grouping households with similar living conditions and behaviors. Unlike approaches in other research (e.g., Bin & Dowlatabadi, 2005; Druckman & Jackson, 2008; Jones & Kammen, 2011; Lenzen et al., 2006; Weber & Matthews, 2008) that often rely on predefined socio-economic segments, this method generated its own household clusters. These clusters are specifically designed to capture the variability in behavior observed even within socio-economic groups (Girod & De Haan, 2009). The archetypes provide comprehensive data, including estimates for expenditures, purchased quantities, income, and ownership of durable goods. This makes them ideal for examining diverse consumption behaviors within the broader context of total household consumption. Research by Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, and Hellweg (2018) highlighted that households sharing similar socio-economic backgrounds can still exhibit different consumption patterns, leading to variations in both the overall environmental impact and its composition.
The consumption sub-model's archetypes, which detail demands across population groups, serve as an ideal foundation for connecting the three sub-models (Figure 1).
The STATPOP dataset, while comprehensive in socio-demographic details, lacked crucial variables necessary for directly assigning consumption archetypes to households. This deficiency meant that a straightforward classification of archetypes to individual households would not yield accurate or meaningful results. To address this, we devised a novel probabilistic classification method designed to accurately reflect the diverse consumption patterns of local households. Our approach utilized a Random Forest Classifier, chosen for its proven robust performance, to bridge the information gap between STATPOP (representing actual households) and HBS (containing archetype data). This classifier was trained using overlapping data from both sources, primarily geographic information and household characteristics. Crucially, we augmented the classifier's training data by incorporating outputs from the building energy and mobility sub-models. Since the consumption archetypes themselves include estimates for residential energy and mobility, integrating these pre-allocated sub-model results as additional household attributes during training allowed for a more holistic classification. Following calibration, the classifier determined the probability of each STATPOP household belonging to each of the 28 consumption archetypes. Subsequently, through a random sampling process guided by these individual probabilities, a specific archetype was assigned to every STATPOP household. This probabilistic assignment, by incorporating the building energy and mobility sub-model results, effectively interconnected all three sub-models. This ensured that the broader context of total household consumption was maintained, preserving the intricate relationships between various consumption areas. The resulting model provides a highly detailed and spatially resolved picture, generating individual consumption and income profiles across approximately 400 categories in both physical and monetary terms for all 3.5 million Swiss households.
The Random Forest Classifier's technical specifications are detailed in Supporting Information S1. During the tuning phase, various performance metrics were evaluated using a 10-fold cross-validation on 90% of the training data. The remaining 10% served as a test set for probability calibration. To address class imbalance and account for archetype frequency, strategies like stratified splitting in cross-validation or prevalence weighting were implemented within the classifiers.
The final stage of the modeling process involved evaluating the environmental impacts resulting from the consumption patterns identified for STATPOP households. This assessment primarily leveraged the comprehensive hybrid Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework previously developed by Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, and Hellweg (2018) for analyzing the environmental effects of consumption archetypes. This framework is termed "hybrid" because it integrates environmental data from two distinct yet complementary methodologies: environmentally extended input-output models (specifically EXIOBASE, as referenced by EXIOBASE Consortium, 2014, and Wood et al., 2015) and process-based life cycle inventory databases (including ecoinvent, from Ecoinvent Centre, 2016, and Wernet et al., 2016, and Agribalyse, by Koch & Salou, 2015). For the complete model, the existing LCA framework for archetypes was expanded to incorporate estimates for household transport and residential energy, which were derived from their respective sub-models. While Froemelt et al.'s original LCA framework had already been adapted to Swiss consumption conditions, further refinements were made to tailor the modeling to the specific circumstances of individual STATPOP households. Examples of these refinements include obtaining information on energy carriers for heating and hot water from individual building data (BDS, BFS, 2014b), developing a car process model based on the car fleet composition of each household's canton (BFS, 2014d), and selecting a wastewater treatment plant activity from the ecoinvent database that corresponded to the size of the plant in the household's municipality (Bundesamt für Umwelt, 2012).
The LCA framework offers granular detail, categorizing environmental footprints into over 200 areas. Beyond carbon footprints, it can also quantify various other environmental indicators.
Before utilizing the model's findings, its validity and that of its sub-models must be examined. The framework aims to realistically depict the variability in individual household environmental footprints within a given region, rather than providing exact figures. This objective serves as the benchmark for evaluating the model.
The validity of the overall model and its sub-components was assessed through various evaluations. The building energy model, for instance, proved suitable for large-scale building stock analysis after thorough comparison with empirical data (Buffat et al., 2017; Froemelt & Hellweg, 2017). Similarly, the MATSim framework's application in Switzerland has been successfully validated (Bauer et al., 2016; Meister et al., 2010). While the direct extrapolation of MATSim agents to STATPOP households within the broader model hadn't been previously examined, our comparison of aggregated person-kilometers with the Swiss Mobility Microcensus 2010 demonstrated its reasonable contribution, with deviations generally under 20%, and often less than 10% (see Supporting Information S1). Furthermore, the consumption archetypes, derived from the representative Swiss Household Budget Survey (HBS) (BFS, 2013b), underwent multi-step internal evaluations (Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, & Hellweg, 2018) and were also validated against independent national statistics.
While the individual sub-models demonstrate plausible results, a key question is whether combining these models and applying the consumption archetypes to real households accurately reflects household consumption patterns. We address this concern with four main points. Firstly, the classifier used for assigning archetypes underwent rigorous internal evaluation. This involved tuning through a 10-fold cross-validation process, where a portion of the training data was repeatedly set aside for validation. Additionally, the refined classifier was tested against a separate 10% held-out sample to confirm its performance (further details are in Supporting Information S1). Secondly, to mitigate issues arising from class imbalance, specific measures were implemented as detailed in Section 2.5. These efforts proved effective, resulting in strong Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients of 0.87 for the prevalence of archetypes between the Household Budget Survey (HBS) and the overall model. This indicates that the model successfully replicates the frequency distribution of archetypes found in the original HBS data. Thirdly, although direct household-level comparison with external data is not feasible due to data unavailability, we assessed the model's disaggregation plausibility by comparing aggregated expenditure and revenue data from the model with the original HBS. This comparison showed good agreement, aligning with the successful management of class imbalance. Finally, the model's ability to reproduce national statistics for energy, water, waste, and an independent income dataset further supports its realism and validity.
To validate the model's findings, the estimated life cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions were benchmarked against existing research. The model calculated Switzerland's total consumption-based carbon footprint at 9.3 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per person per year (tCO2-eq/person/year). This figure falls between estimates from other Swiss studies: Girod and De Haan (2010) reported 8.6 tCO2-eq/person/year from a study solely based on the Household Budget Survey (HBS), while Jungbluth, Nathani, Stucki, and Leuenberger (2011) arrived at 11.0 tCO2-eq/person/year using a top-down assessment. Across all three methodologies, housing, mobility, and food consistently emerged as the primary contributors to overall footprints. This aligns with numerous international studies (e.g., Hertwich & Peters, 2009; Ivanova et al., 2016; Tukker & Jansen, 2006). It's important to note that the two comparative Swiss studies utilized different reference years. For a more accurate comparison, Jungbluth et al.'s original average of 12.8 tCO2-eq/person/year was adjusted. This adjustment accounts for the present model's scope, which, as detailed in Section 2.6 and Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, & Hellweg (2018), specifically focuses on "environmental impacts directly associated with a certain household's behavior" and therefore underestimates impacts from healthcare, governmental activities, and educational services. The bottom-up model's average footprint remains somewhat lower than Jungbluth's top-down approach. This discrepancy might be attributed to the use of consumer expenditure surveys like the HBS, which are known to potentially underreport certain expenditures (Ivanova et al., 2017; Steen-Olsen, Wood, & Hertwich, 2016). The most significant difference in emissions between the models was observed in the food consumption category (2.1 tCO2-eq/person/year in Jungbluth's study versus 1.4 tCO2-eq/person/year in the current model). Additionally, the model's coverage of building construction is incomplete. While it includes maintenance, some refurbishments, and certain building extensions based on HBS expenditure data, it does not account for investments in entirely new building construction.
The model's internal evaluations and its success in replicating national statistical characteristics confirm its ability to realistically and plausibly assess individual household consumption footprints, indicating it is on the right track.
To showcase the model's capabilities, we conducted a status quo analysis focusing on municipal carbon footprints. This analysis aggregates the life cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions generated by private households within each municipality, excluding government-induced impacts, consistent with the functional unit outlined in Section 2.6. Our primary audience for this municipal-level perspective is local authorities. We opted against analyzing individual households at this stage, as that is reserved for future research and would currently yield findings similar to those in Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, and Hellweg (2018). While the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework supports various environmental indicators from its databases (Section 2.6), for clarity and comparison with other research, we present only life cycle GHG emissions within the primary consumption categories.
Initially, we categorized all 2352 Swiss municipalities based on their average per-capita carbon footprint. This involved ranking them and then forming four distinct clusters using the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles as boundaries. The cluster designated LO includes the 10% of municipalities with the lowest emissions. Municipalities with low-to-medium emissions fall into cluster LM (10–50th percentiles), while those with medium-to-high emissions are in cluster MH (50–90th percentiles). Finally, cluster HI encompasses the 10% of municipalities exhibiting the highest emissions. Figure 2 illustrates the average carbon footprints for residents within each of these municipal clusters, juxtaposed against the overall Swiss average.
Following the initial grouping of municipalities, we conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis of their characteristics. This involved examining both continuous and categorical variables. For continuous factors, we looked at estimated environmental impacts, modeled incomes, household demographics (size and age), public transport availability, per-capita living space, and building stock data. To quantitatively assess the distinctiveness of the four municipal clusters based on these continuous variables, we employed one-way ANOVA tests, utilizing the resulting F-values as indicators. Beyond continuous variables, we also explored categorical classifications. These included the European Union's DEGURBA classification, which categorizes municipalities as "thinly populated," "intermediate density," or "densely populated," as well as classifications from the BFS (2014a) distinguishing between rural, urban core, and agglomeration communities, and major Swiss regions. All detailed statistics for these attributes are provided in Supporting Information S1. It's important to note that while environmental impacts and income were derived from our model, all other variables were sourced from official statistics. Recognizing that income is a significant determinant of environmental footprints, as established by numerous previous studies, and given the absence of external municipal-level income statistics, we placed particular emphasis on evaluating our model's income distribution and integrated these modeled incomes into our analysis.
When focusing solely on environmental impacts, the four municipal clusters primarily diverge in the environmental consequences stemming from housing, transportation, and recreation. These three categories are among the top four contributors to the overall carbon footprint, making this distinction somewhat expected. While food also ranks as a significant emitter, its impact is less defining for the municipal clusters. As a fundamental necessity, variations in food-related emissions among households appear less pronounced compared to other consumption categories. Nevertheless, a considerable range exists within each of the four clusters across all consumption areas; for example, some municipalities with low per-capita housing emissions are still categorized in the highest impact cluster.
An examination of continuous variables revealed several key factors distinguishing the four municipal clusters. Firstly, average gross income per inhabitant emerged as a highly differentiating variable, with higher per-capita incomes generally correlating with increased municipal carbon footprints. Secondly, the age of buildings within a municipality also played a significant role. Municipalities with a larger proportion of structures built before 1919 tended to have higher carbon footprints, as these older buildings typically exhibit poorer insulation compared to newer constructions (Wallbaum et al., 2010). Conversely, a higher percentage of new buildings appeared to reduce municipal footprints. Thirdly, household composition significantly influenced municipal carbon footprints. A greater share of "families" (households with individuals aged under 25 and between 25-64 years) in a municipality was associated with a lower municipal footprint. This aligns with previous research (Froemelt, Dürrenmatt, & Hellweg, 2018; Underwood & Zahran, 2015; Weber & Matthews, 2008) suggesting that families, excluding very-high-income ones, often have the lowest per-capita greenhouse gas emissions, likely due to economies of scale. The presence of households with persons aged 65 and older also showed some relevance, but with an opposing effect on the municipal footprint. Fourthly, and related to the previous point, the proportion of 2-person households compared to households with three or more individuals was another important determinant. Municipalities in the HI (highest impact) and MH (medium-to-high impact) clusters displayed noticeably higher percentages of 1- and 2-person households and lower percentages of 4-person or larger households, further highlighting the impact of household size. It's worth noting that a greater number of household members can lead to both efficiency gains and, potentially, lower per-capita incomes for larger households, thus creating interconnections between these findings on income and household structure. Finally, the availability of public transport was a factor; a higher proportion of residents in a municipality with poor public transport services was linked to an increased municipal carbon footprint.
The analysis of municipal classifications highlights two key attributes: the DEGURBA classification and the categorization into rural, urban core, and agglomeration areas (refer to Figure 2 and Supporting Information S1). A strong correlation emerged, showing that nearly all residents in "densely populated" areas reside in municipalities with the lowest or low-to-medium environmental impact (LO or LM clusters). Conversely, municipalities with the highest impact (HI cluster) predominantly comprise individuals living in "thinly populated" regions. This observation aligns with prior research indicating that households in densely urbanized environments generally exhibit reduced environmental footprints, particularly in transportation (due to shorter distances and improved public transport) and housing (smaller living spaces).
Figure 3 visually represents the municipal clusters alongside five key variables. A detailed examination of these maps, complemented by diagrams in Supporting Information S1, reveals that municipal carbon footprints are shaped by a complex interplay of variables, some of which reinforce each other while others have opposing effects. Consider Lausanne, Switzerland's fourth-largest city with 128,070 inhabitants, which falls into the "LO" (lowest impact) cluster. This classification aligns with its "densely populated" DEGURBA status and excellent public transport accessibility. Zurich, the largest city with 368,351 inhabitants, also shares these characteristics of high population density and good public transport. However, Zurich is categorized as "LM" (low-to-medium impact). This difference is likely attributable to Zurich's significantly higher proportion of one- and two-person households and a lower percentage of families. Both cities demonstrate high per-capita incomes; Lausanne's income surpasses 94% of other Swiss municipalities, while Zurich's is higher than 98%. This highlights that while per-capita income is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of a municipality's carbon footprint. The analysis highlights how different factors contribute to a municipality's carbon footprint, as seen in the contrasting cases of Arosa and Courtepin. Arosa, a small Alpine tourist village with 3,030 residents, exhibits characteristics leading to its classification in the high-impact (HI) cluster. It is sparsely populated, suffers from inadequate public transport, and consequently has high emissions from mobility. Furthermore, Arosa has a low proportion of families but a significant number of older households, which correlates with a higher prevalence of one- and two-person households. Its average per-capita income is also notably high, ranking at the 94th percentile. In contrast, Courtepin, a small municipality in the Swiss midlands with 3,501 inhabitants, is categorized in the low-impact (LO) cluster despite also being a thinly populated, rural area with poor public transport and relatively high transport emissions. This is attributed to a very high percentage of families, a comparatively low average income per inhabitant (8th percentile), and a very small proportion of buildings constructed before 1919. These examples underscore that a municipality's environmental impact is a complex interplay of various determinants, necessitating a highly detailed, spatially resolved modeling approach to capture local nuances. While these are simplified illustrations, they demonstrate the importance of considering multiple, sometimes conflicting, variables when assessing environmental footprints.
Given the established importance of population density, as highlighted by the DEGURBA classification, in determining municipal carbon footprints, a more in-depth investigation into its environmental effects was undertaken. The model's high spatial resolution allowed for a detailed analysis of the correlation between persons per hectare (100 × 100 m) and per-capita greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, as illustrated in Figure 4. This granular, household-centric approach facilitated a comprehensive understanding of environmental impacts across different density levels. To categorize the environmental impact based on population density, hectares were grouped into quartiles according to the number of residents per hectare: LOD (lowest 25% density), ILD (low-to-intermediate density, 25–50%), IHD (intermediate-to-high density, 50–75%), and HID (highest 25% density). The analysis revealed a clear inverse relationship: as density increased, environmental impacts generally decreased. However, this reduction in impact appeared to plateau beyond a certain density threshold. Interestingly, some hectares with relatively low population density also exhibited the lowest GHG emissions, suggesting that density is not the sole determinant of environmental impact. This observation underscores the need for future research to explore additional density indicators, such as access to various services, beyond just the number of persons per hectare. Further examination of the footprint compositions across the four density groups (Figure 4) indicated that emission levels were largely consistent across most consumption areas. The notable exceptions were housing and mobility, where emission differences were more pronounced. This aligns with expectations, as both housing and mobility patterns are intrinsically linked to population density; lower density areas typically entail longer travel distances, less accessible public transport, and larger per-capita living spaces.
Developing a comprehensive modeling framework inevitably introduces various limitations and uncertainties, many of which have been addressed in prior publications concerning the individual sub-models. Our focus here is on the assumptions made when connecting these sub-models and assigning archetypes to households. While linking models through shared outputs like person-kilometers and heating demand is a significant yet justifiable assumption, we implemented several strategies to mitigate potential negative consequences. These strategies involved utilizing quantiles and probabilities instead of absolute values or direct assignments. Such approaches also help bridge information gaps between the STATPOP and HBS datasets, enabling realistic estimations for each household. Our evaluation confirms that these measures are effective, leading to plausible and sound results from the modeling framework.
The model's scope is limited by its chosen system boundaries, which only track environmental impacts directly attributable to private household consumption, excluding governmental consumption. Additionally, the final environmental impact calculations may be influenced by inherent assumptions and simplifications within the Life Cycle Assessment methodology.
Given the identified limitations and the absence of comprehensive external data for thorough model validation, a crucial next step involves developing an uncertainty analysis framework for our model. It's important to note that the building energy model already incorporates a detailed Monte Carlo simulation. The process of assigning consumption archetypes to households and allocating MATSim agents to household members essentially represents a single instance of a Monte Carlo simulation. While a similar uncertainty framework could be directly applied, computational constraints currently pose a challenge. Future research will also address uncertainties stemming from life cycle inventories and the archetype methodology.
Local authorities can readily refine our model with specific local data if they apply it to a particular municipality.
Our research successfully generated highly detailed, localized consumption data by meticulously applying data mining and machine learning techniques. This was achieved by integrating several sub-models, each drawing from distinct modeling fields, and leveraging existing national databases. The use of machine learning extends beyond this specific article; we previously employed various tools from this domain in developing the sub-models. These applications included deriving consumption archetypes and managing extensive GIS data within the building energy model, utilizing methods such as self-organizing maps, clustering algorithms, LASSO Regression, and Random Forest Regression. We aim for this work to inspire other industrial ecology researchers to explore data mining tools.
The model's household-centric, bottom-up design provides policymakers with information at various administrative levels. This is crucial because some environmental policies necessitate engagement from national or cantonal authorities, rather than being solely implementable by municipalities.
This article establishes the model's viability by applying it to Switzerland and confirming the realism of its outcomes. It also illustrates the model's utility through an examination of municipal carbon footprints and the connection between population density and environmental impacts. This analysis highlighted per-capita gross income, population density, building age, and household composition as potential factors influencing municipal carbon footprints. While understanding these broad trends is valuable, the model's capabilities extend further. The case studies presented demonstrate that general trends don't always reflect the realities of individual households or municipalities. With detailed information on local consumption pattern variations, specific household groups can be identified, and targeted interventions can be developed. Integrating principles from behavioral economics and psychology with the study's detailed household profiles could lead to innovative policy tools, such as personalized messages, to boost awareness and encourage more sustainable consumption habits.
Beyond developing an uncertainty framework, future work will involve collaborating with municipalities to assess the model's practical utility and inform the creation of policy instruments. The model's design intentionally prioritized the capacity for evaluating policy scenarios. For instance, the building energy model, with its physical and component-based structure, can simulate detailed refurbishment scenarios, analyzing the impact of physical interventions on specific buildings, components (like roofs or walls), geographic areas, or even particular homeowner groups identified by their consumption patterns. Similarly, the integration with MATSim allows for exploring future mobility scenarios, such as the adoption of electric vehicles or autonomous transport systems. When evaluating these scenarios, efforts will also be made to account for rebound effects stemming from different policy implementations. This could be achieved by integrating the model with macroeconomic models or by refining the archetype approach through sub-clustering and reassigning archetypes.
Future research should expand beyond the current consumption-focused model to include a production perspective. While households are crucial for reducing environmental impacts, they lack complete control over their supply chains. Therefore, local policymakers need tools to assess the environmental impacts of local industries and trade, enabling them to develop targeted measures for businesses within their jurisdiction.
Despite potential for further refinement and future research, this model currently offers a robust foundation for policymakers. It aids in comprehending local consumption patterns and serves as a virtual tool for assessing policy scenarios aimed at reducing household environmental impacts.
