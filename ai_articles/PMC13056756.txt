International spillover effects, where demand in one country drives production in others, are increasingly significant, particularly in environmental impact assessments. To account for these, global multiregional input-output (MRIO) tables have been created. While MRIOs offer a comprehensive view of the global economy, they sometimes diverge from official national data. A specific phenomenon, "feedback effects," occurs when these international spillovers also stimulate domestic production in the demanding country. The development of coupled models, which integrate official national input-output tables (IOTs) with global MRIOs, introduces a challenge: these models often fail to incorporate the precise national data when analyzing international feedback loops. This raises a crucial question regarding the magnitude of these feedback loops across various environmental impacts. Our research focuses on quantifying the domestic production embedded in a region's imports due to these feedback mechanisms. We observe that for emissions, these feedback loops are generally minor, typically less than 2% of the total import footprint, although they can reach over 6% for certain countries, years, and stressors. These results imply that employing Leontief multipliers from existing MRIOs could be a suitable approach for modeling imports within national IOTs for environmentally extended MRIO analyses.

Consumption-based accounts, also known as environmental footprints, are gaining traction in both academic research and policy-making. They help in understanding developmental and trade challenges, particularly concerning embodied environmental impacts. These impacts include greenhouse gas emissions, land use, biodiversity, and resource decoupling. Global multiregional input-output (MRIO) tables are the primary data source for measuring these consumption footprints. MRIO tables detail economic exchanges across various sectors and countries, along with their environmental consequences (stressors), offering a comprehensive view of international supply chains.
MRIO tables offer the significant benefit of capturing both direct and indirect international spillover effects. For instance, the production of steel in China driven by German vehicle manufacturing exemplifies a direct spillover. An indirect spillover, on the other hand, could involve Australian coal mining providing inputs for that Chinese steel production. The increasing complexity of global production chains means these indirect supply networks are becoming increasingly common, as highlighted by Wood et al. (2018). A key advantage of MRIOs is their ability to model these supply chains by incorporating the distinct production technologies of each participating region. However, a limitation of MRIO data is the necessity for harmonizing and reconciling data from various countries (Wood et al. 2014). This harmonization process can lead to discrepancies between a country's official statistical data and its representation within an MRIO database. Numerous studies have explored these divergences between national input-output tables and existing MRIO tables (e.g., Wilting 2012; Hoekstra et al. 2014). Consequently, the international spillover effects depicted by an MRIO model might not align with expectations derived from nationally reported import and export figures. This issue isn't unique to MRIO data, as it's also evident in the inconsistencies of global trade statistics (The Economist 2011). Such discrepancies with a country's statistical data can complicate national policymaking, a point emphasized by Edens and colleagues (2015).
When conducting national-level policy and analysis, integrating a high-quality national Input-Output Table (IOT) with global Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) data for international spillovers is often beneficial. This approach allows for the preservation of specific sectoral details within the national table, maintains the integrity of official national data, or enables the inclusion of national tables not explicitly present in an MRIO. Achieving this integration involves several procedural steps, including the insertion of the new regional table and subsequent system rebalancing.
Recent advancements in constructing coupled models are worth noting. For instance, Edens and colleagues (2015) developed the "Single Country National Accounts Consistent" (SNAC) method. This approach creates a multicountry MRIO that precisely aligns with a specific national Input-Output Table (IOT), ensuring that the MRIO's values match those in the national table. Their method involves replacing the MRIO's corresponding table with the new foreground data, then rebalancing the off-diagonal trade blocks while preserving the integrity of the newly integrated data. Similarly, the AISHA MRIO-building engine, which was instrumental in creating the Eora MRIO (Geschke 2012; Geschke et al. 2014), achieves a comparable outcome. It integrates canonical national data for a particular country and assigns zero uncertainty to these values. This guarantees that the data are fully maintained during the conflict resolution and balancing phases. Furthermore, Bachmann and colleagues (2014) and Wenz and colleagues (2014) have addressed the challenge of integrating a subnational MRIO into a global MRIO. This body of work also extends to the problem of incorporating a new country into a global MRIO, particularly when that country was initially part of a "Rest of the World" aggregate. More recently, Christis and colleagues (2016) investigated the extent of concordance, aggregation, and time errors in coupled models, offering insights into balancing modeling investments with result reliability.
A straightforward method for incorporating international spillover effects into national statistical models for consumption-based accounting involves utilizing multipliers derived from a global Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) model and applying them to a country's import vector. This technique is widely adopted in various fields, such as life cycle assessment (Benini et al. 2014) and material flow accounting (Schoer et al. 2012, 2013). Similarly, approaches focusing on environmental impacts embodied in bilateral trade (EEBT), as developed by Peters and colleagues (Peters 2008; Peters et al. 2011), also employ this strategy, though they specifically exclude indirect international spillovers, only accounting for direct ones. The primary benefit of these methods lies in their simplicity of execution. If a background MRIO model can effectively quantify the overall environmental impact intensity of imported goods, this information can then be readily connected to the established import levels within a national table. However, a notable drawback arises with EEBT approaches, where indirect international spillovers are deliberately omitted to prevent double-counting. Conversely, life cycle approaches fully integrate these indirect spillovers through the global MRIO. This distinction becomes particularly significant when "feedback effects" play a substantial role. For instance, an international feedback effect could be the coal mining output in Australia driven by Chinese steel production, which in turn is stimulated by Australian vehicle demand. Such feedback loops can be crucial for accurately tracking embodied environmental impacts. The extent of these feedback effects is not uniform across all product categories, meaning the actual advantage of using background MRIO multipliers hinges on the observed size of these effects.
The concept of feedback effects has been extensively explored in the literature concerning subnational multiregional input-output (MRIO) models. Early research by Miller (1966, 1969) indicated that these effects were insignificant, implying that developing complex multiregional models offered limited additional benefit. Despite the increased integration of regional and national economies today, this topic has been re-examined frequently from both theoretical (e.g., Round 1978, 1985; Miller 1986) and empirical perspectives (e.g., studies on Europe, the United States, Indonesia, and Asia). However, a review by Round (2001) suggests that Miller's initial finding regarding the negligible nature of feedback effects largely still holds true.
Despite growing interest in environmental impacts embedded in trade, a comprehensive evaluation of feedback effect magnitudes within global Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) frameworks remains largely unexplored. Previous research, such as Kanemoto and colleagues (2012), has primarily addressed these impacts conceptually. While studies like Su and Ang (2011), and earlier works on Environmentally Extended Bilateral Trade (EEBT) by Peters et al. (2011), have examined indirect international spillovers, these investigations broadly encompass emissions across all third-party nations stemming from a country's demand. However, this differs from the typical understanding of feedback effects, which specifically refer to emissions generated domestically, then exported, processed abroad, and subsequently reimported as embodied flows (as defined by Miller and Blair 2009). Although these studies offer valuable insights into how disruptions in international supply chains affect consumption's life cycle impacts (Peters 2008), they do not clarify the data discrepancies or footprint variations that arise when integrating national data with global MRIO databases. Consequently, there's a lack of empirical evidence to confirm the reliability of using background MRIO multipliers when applied to national import data.
This paper empirically examines the magnitude and temporal evolution of second-order flows using the EXIOBASE3 global MRIO account. We investigate how significant these effects are and their changes over time.
Environmental footprints are determined by combining the environmental impact within a country's borders with the impacts embedded in its net imports. This calculation is represented by the equation D = F + M - E, where D is the total footprint, F is the territorial impact, M signifies impacts from imports, and E denotes impacts from exports. Our focus is specifically on feedback emissions, which are domestic environmental impacts that are exported and subsequently reimported as embodied flows. These can be measured against either the total footprint (D) or total imports (M). Analyzing these feedback loops helps assess how closely domestic MRIO data aligns with national statistical data regarding embodied import impacts.
To determine the proportion of a region's final demand that originates from domestic emissions, a specific calculation is employed within a multiregional framework comprising 'n' regions and 'm' sectors. The process begins by defining 's' as a row vector representing emission intensities (e.g., carbon emissions per unit of total sectoral output) and 'sd' as the corresponding domestic-only emissions. Economic output is denoted by 'x', and 'L' signifies the Leontief inverse matrix, where each entry Lij quantifies the total output in sector 'i' stimulated by one unit of demand in sector 'j'. A vector 'ym' represents the total imports for a specific target country 'r'. The total global economic output driven by country 'r's imports is calculated as x = Lym. Subsequently, the overall global environmental footprint (e.g., carbon footprint) associated with these imports is given by 'sx'. The domestic emissions component of this footprint is represented by 'sdx'. To ascertain the significance of domestic emissions within the total import footprint, the ratio of domestic emissions to the total environmental footprint of imports is computed, expressed as (sdx) / (sx). This ratio quantifies the feedback emissions. It's important to acknowledge that utilizing a fully populated 'L' matrix can lead to double-counting intermediate imports. While methods like the EEBT (Emissions Embodied in Bilateral Trade) approach have been proposed to circumvent this, they are not suitable here because they isolate all indirect international spillovers, not exclusively feedback effects. As noted in prior literature, such as Schoer and colleagues (2012), which discusses applying multipliers to imports and exports, any double-counting of intermediate imports within the production chain occurs consistently in both the numerator and denominator of the feedback emissions ratio.
This study's computations relied exclusively on the EXIOBASE3 global MRIO model, which features 200 product categories. This database was selected due to its extensive sectoral detail, encompassing 200 sectors per country, and its broad coverage of significant economies, including 41 countries representing over 95% of global GDP, alongside five additional regions. EXIOBASE3 offers a comprehensive intercountry Input-Output Table (IOT) spanning from 1995 to 2011, with all reported results, except for the time-series analysis in Figure 3, specifically pertaining to the year 2011 (using current-year prices without deflation). While now-casted data up to 2015 were available, they were excluded to maintain data confidence. In contrast, the Eora MRIO, though covering more nations, presents challenges for cross-country sector comparisons due to its lower resolution and varied classifications.
Our findings reveal that feedback effects are generally minor. For most nations, these loops account for less than 2% of their total consumption-based CO2 emissions. However, Russia and the United States show slightly higher contributions at 2.7% and 4.1%, respectively. China is a notable exception, with 6% to 8% of its consumption-based emissions stemming from reimported embodied emissions through feedback loops.
It's tempting to explore potential connections between a country's trade activity, its economic specialization, and its GDP, and how these might influence the scale of feedback effects. One might hypothesize that larger or more developed nations would experience fewer feedback loops, assuming their exports are thoroughly processed abroad and not re-imported. Conversely, these same countries could also exhibit more feedback loops due to their involvement in intricate global supply chains. Similarly, predicting whether countries with higher trade exposure would see more or fewer feedback loops is not straightforward. However, this paper refrains from further investigation into these relationships. This decision stems from the consistent finding that feedback effects are generally minor. Even in the rare cases where they are substantial, a deeper economic analysis wouldn't significantly aid in developing improved coupled models.
An analysis of feedback effects was also conducted at the sectoral level. Figure 1 illustrates the proportion of CO2 feedback emissions within the total footprint for each sector of final consumption, averaged across all countries. The findings indicate that, on average, all sectors exhibit a mean feedback emission fraction below 2.5%, with a standard deviation under 10%. The figure displays 200 distinct final consumption goods from EXIOBASE3, ordered by their average feedback effect along the horizontal axis. Notably, 17 of these products are classified as purely intermediate goods, meaning they have no final consumption and are positioned at the far right of the horizontal axis. The overall distribution suggests that feedback loops are broadly dispersed across sectors, with no single sector demonstrating a disproportionately high impact. In Figure 1, individual data points represent sector-specific results for all 41 countries, while solid blue lines denote the mean for each sector, and light gray lines show the standard deviation within each sector.
Beyond CO2, our analysis extended to other environmental impact flows, revealing largely consistent findings (Figure 2). While feedback loops constituted a somewhat larger portion of the import footprint for these alternative stressors compared to CO2, their overall magnitude remained modest. Specifically, we examined methane, virtual water, and embodied cropland using the environmental extensions within the EXIOBASE3 database. Although these other embodied resource flows exhibited slightly more pronounced feedback effects, they generally stayed below 5% in each sector on average. The specific mix of products influences feedback effects, leading to variations across different stressors. Nevertheless, for most sectors and stressors considered, these effects were consistently small, typically less than 5% of imports.
The temporal evolution of feedback loop magnitudes, influenced by shifts in economic and trade structures, warrants examination. An analysis spanning 1995–2011, depicted in Figure 3, illustrates the proportion of total CO2 footprint attributable to feedback loops across various nations. For the majority of countries, this share remains consistently low and stable over time. However, certain trends are noteworthy: China experienced a significant increase, rising from approximately 5–6% to 8% around 2007, while the United States and Russia saw a gradual decrease in feedback effects. Conversely, the Rest of World–Middle East and Rest of World–Africa regions exhibited a slow but steady growth in these effects. China's elevated feedback share is understandable given its status as a major manufacturing hub and one of the world's largest economies, undergoing substantial changes in its value chain during this period. This involves a growing volume of emissions from primary production within China, which are then exported, processed into secondary and tertiary goods and services internationally, and subsequently re-imported for consumption by Chinese end-users. Countries with feedback shares below 1% are excluded from this visualization.
This paper investigated the scale of environmental feedback effects within a global MRIO framework. Our findings indicate that for the majority of nations, these feedback effects constitute under 2% of total embodied emissions, a figure significantly lower than the inherent variability and uncertainty observed in current MRIO outcomes.
This study's reliance on a single MRIO model presents a potential limitation, prompting the question of whether using a different MRIO would alter the findings. However, previous inter-MRIO comparisons (Moran and Wood 2014) suggest that our core conclusion—that feedback loops are not significant—would likely remain consistent. Furthermore, our investigation focused exclusively on interindustry indirect effects, leaving out induced effects that arise from additional spending generated by household income (Miyazawa 1968), capital turnover (Lenzen and Treloar 2004), or secondary flows within the social accounting matrix (Pyatt and Round 1985). It's conceivable that for certain countries, the most substantial feedback effects could stem from incorporating consumption-income loops, particularly if interindustry connections are less influential than income-consumption dynamics. This area warrants further exploration in future research, given its growing importance in today's interconnected global economy.
This paper's specific empirical question emerged from the wider challenge of integrating official national input-output tables into multiregional models.
Constructing a coupled system that combines global and national multipliers offers an appealingly straightforward solution, avoiding many difficulties associated with incorporating new official national input-output tables into a multiregional framework. While a drawback is that this model doesn't utilize the official national data for second- and higher-order feedback effects, our analysis, specifically for embodied GHG emissions using the EXIOBASE3 model, indicates this limitation is not substantial.
Considering the Netherlands as an example, Edens and colleagues (2015) demonstrated that incorporating higher-quality national data to replace standard MRIO values led to a 20% alteration in the overall carbon footprint, encompassing all impact levels. Our analysis reveals that feedback emissions constitute a mere 0.36% of the Netherlands' total embodied emissions in imports and only 0.1% of its entire CO2 footprint. Consequently, for the Netherlands, the critical factor is accurately depicting overall trade flows, particularly distinguishing re-exports and transit trade from imports that undergo substantial domestic processing (information found in a canonical IOT). This is more important than how emission multipliers are applied to imports and exports. Therefore, a coupled integration approach offers a practical alternative to the more intricate SNAC method.
When choosing a model coupling approach, numerous factors beyond just feedback loops require consideration. These include the aggregation of sectors and regions, and the process of balancing to resolve inconsistencies (as discussed by Steen-Olsen et al. 2014; Rodrigues 2014; Lenzen et al. 2009). Such elements pertain to the practicalities of integration, rather than its significance. The specific way a model is linked, particularly how aggregation and disaggregation are managed, will influence the extent of feedback effects captured. Nevertheless, our study's primary finding—that feedback effects are generally minor—is expected to hold true regardless of these integration details.
