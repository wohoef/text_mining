Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is often treated effectively with surgical resection, though this approach is frequently complicated by a high rate of tumor recurrence. Clusterin, a well-conserved glycoprotein, has been shown to promote cell aggregation in laboratory settings. Its elevated expression is observed in various cancers, including breast, ovarian, colon, prostate, kidney, and HCC, and is linked to tumor metastasis. Our study aimed to investigate the clinical relevance of clusterin expression in both the blood and surgically removed tissues of HCC patients. We collected serum, tumor tissue, and adjacent non-tumor tissue from 140 HCC patients undergoing liver resection. Serum clusterin concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, while tissue expression was assessed via immunohistochemistry. Patients were followed for a median of 57.8 months. The average serum clusterin level was 130.0 ± 58.7 µg/mL (ranging from 10.1 to 366.6 µg/mL). These serum levels did not correlate with tumor stage or the degree of liver function impairment. Furthermore, patient survival did not significantly differ between those with high (>130.0 ± 58.7 µg/mL) versus low (≤130.0 ± 58.7 µg/mL) serum clusterin levels. Clusterin was detected in the HCC tissues of 76 patients (54.3%) and in the non-tumor liver tissues of 53 patients (37.9%). No significant survival difference was found based on clusterin expression within the HCC tissues themselves. However, patients with clusterin expression in non-tumor tissues exhibited a significantly lower postoperative disease-free survival rate (p = 0.001) compared to those without. Multivariate analysis identified macrovascular/microvascular invasion and clusterin expression in non-tumor tissues as independent prognostic factors after hepatic resection. In conclusion, clusterin expression in the non-tumor tissue of HCC patients indicates a poorer prognosis following liver resection, suggesting its potential as a prognostic marker for post-resection HCC.

Despite surgical resection being an effective treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a significant issue is the high rate of tumor recurrence after surgery. While vascular invasion is recognized as a primary driver of early recurrence, the liver's normal function has also been implicated in HCC recurrence.
A highly conserved glycoprotein, clusterin, was first identified in rat testes and is known to promote cell aggregation in laboratory settings. Its diverse functions include roles in tissue remodeling, cell adhesion, apoptosis, proliferation, senescence, membrane recycling, lipid transport, complement inhibition, and sperm maturation. Clusterin's involvement in tumor development and progression is well-documented, with its expression being elevated in various cancers like breast, ovarian, colon, prostate, kidney, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Conversely, clusterin levels are reduced in nonmelanoma skin cancer, esophageal cell carcinoma, and prostatic carcinoma. These findings collectively indicate that clusterin's expression, whether increased or decreased, is significant in tumorigenesis.
We aimed to examine clusterin expression levels in serum and resected tissues from 140 HCC patients.
Between May 1998 and February 2000, 140 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients undergoing curative hepatic resection at Taipei Veterans General Hospital provided serum, resected tumor tissue, and adjacent nontumor liver tissue for this study. Patients who had received any prior treatments, such as chemotherapy, ethanol injection, or transarterial chemoembolization, were excluded. The institutional Committee for the Conduct of Human Research approved the study, and all participants provided informed consent. HCC diagnoses were histologically confirmed from the resected specimens. Curative resection was defined as the complete macroscopic removal of the tumor with clear surgical margins, as verified by histological examination. Blood samples were collected from fasting patients before surgery, with serum immediately isolated and stored at –20°C, ensuring only one freeze-thaw cycle per sample. Tumor specimens were procured immediately post-resection, and liver tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at –70°C. Preoperative clinical and laboratory data, along with postoperative survival information and histological analyses of the resected tissues, were collected prospectively. Comprehensive clinicopathological details were documented, including routine liver biochemistry, hepatitis markers, serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels, tumor size and number, and the presence of vascular invasion. Histological evaluations relied on reviewing hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections. HCC tissues were staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)/Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) 7th edition tumor, node, and metastasis (TNM) system. The study aimed to correlate clusterin expression in both serum and HCC specimens with various key clinicopathological factors. These included patient age and sex, anti-hepatitis C virus (HCV) and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) status, serum AFP, tumor dimensions and multiplicity, indocyanine green retention rate at 15 minutes (ICG-15), serum albumin and alanine aminotransferase levels, macroscopic/microscopic vascular invasion, and the presence of liver cirrhosis.
To measure serum clusterin levels, a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit from BioVendor Laboratory Medicine Inc. (Modrice, Czech Republic) was employed, adhering to the manufacturer's protocol. Each sample and standard was analyzed in duplicate, and the average values were computed. The process involved first pipetting samples and reference standard dilutions into 96-well plates. Following a two-hour incubation at room temperature, the plates underwent washing before a clusterin conjugate was added. A subsequent two-hour incubation at room temperature was performed, followed by another wash. Substrate solution was then introduced to each well for a 30-minute incubation at room temperature. The reaction was halted with a stop solution, and absorbance was read at 450 nm using a Spectra Max 250 ELISA plate reader (Molecular Devices, Hampton, New Hampshire, USA). Clusterin concentrations, expressed in µg/mL, were determined by comparison with reference standards.
For immunohistochemical analysis, tissue samples underwent a preparation process beginning with overnight fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C. An automated embedding machine was then used to embed these fixed samples in wax. Prior to embedding, samples were dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), followed by a clearing step with chloroform, and finally embedded in paraffin. Five-micron sections were subsequently cut from these prepared blocks. Immunostaining was conducted using the Universal DAKO LSAB2 system (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA, USA), adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines. The procedure involved rehydrating the tissue sections, treating them with 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6) in a microwave, and then incubating them with 3.0% H2O2 for 10 minutes. A blocking solution was applied for an additional 10 minutes. Following this, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C in a moist chamber with a monoclonal antibody against clusterin (clone B-5, 1:200 dilution; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline, the slides were incubated for 10 minutes with a biotin-labeled secondary antibody, then treated with streptavidin horseradish peroxidase conjugate for another 10 minutes. Staining was developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate for 10 minutes, and counterstained with Mayer's hematoxylin (MUTO Chemicals CO, Tokyo, Japan) for 10 minutes. Mounted slides were examined microscopically. Negative controls, omitting the primary antibody, were also included. Clusterin expression was categorized: negative (–) for weak or absent staining, positive (+) for expression in less than 10% of tumor cells, and moderate (2+) to strong (3+) for overexpression in over 10% of tumor cells.
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 for Windows). Values are presented as mean ± SD, unless specified otherwise. To assess differences between treatment group means, either one-way ANOVA or Student's t-test was employed. Disease-free survival, defined as the period from surgery until local recurrence, metastasis, or death, was evaluated. Kaplan-Meier method was used to determine survival rates, with comparisons made via the log-rank test. Independent survival factors were identified through multivariate analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model, with results reported as risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals. A p-value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
The study population comprised 140 patients, predominantly male (80.7%, n=113), with an average age of 62.3 ± 13.6 years. A significant portion of these patients had a history of liver disease, with 52.1% (n=73) testing positive for HBsAg and 24.3% (n=34) for anti-HCV. Additionally, 15.7% (n=22) reported a family history of HCC. Regarding tumor characteristics, the average size was 5.3 ± 3.7 cm, ranging from 0.8 to 20.0 cm. Solitary HCC was diagnosed in 62.9% (n=88) of patients. Vascular invasion was also prevalent, with macrovascular invasion observed in 20.7% (n=29) and microvascular invasion in 62.9% (n=88). Patients were categorized into three TNM stages (I, II, and III) according to the AJCC/UICC 7th edition, with 48, 47, and 45 patients in each stage, respectively. Furthermore, 41.1% (n=58) of patients exhibited cirrhosis, and the majority presented with chronic liver disease and impaired liver function, indicated by a mean serum ICG-15 level of 14.5 ± 10.8%.
The average follow-up period for patients was 57.8 months, ranging from 1.4 to 272 months. Preoperative serum clusterin levels averaged 130.0 ± 58.7 µg/mL, with a range of 10.1 to 366.6 µg/mL. These serum clusterin concentrations showed no correlation with the tumor stage or the extent of liver function impairment. Furthermore, univariate analysis indicated no significant difference in survival between patients exhibiting high (≥130.0 ± 58.7 µg/mL, n = 76) versus low (<130.0 ± 58.7 µg/mL, n = 64) preoperative serum clusterin levels (p = 0.324).
Clusterin expression was identified in the resected tissues of HCC patients, specifically in 76 (54.3%) of the HCC tumor samples and 53 (37.9%) of the adjacent nontumor liver tissues (Fig. 3A–D). The distribution of clusterin expression across different TNM stages of HCC (stage I: 58.3% in 48 patients; stage II: 57.4% in 47 patients; stage III: 46.7% in 45 patients) did not show significant variation (p = 0.459). While clusterin expression within the HCC tumor tissue itself did not correlate with patient survival (p = 0.767) (Fig. 4), a notable finding emerged regarding its presence in nontumor tissue. Patients exhibiting positive clusterin expression in their nontumor liver tissue experienced a significantly lower postoperative disease-free survival rate (p = 0.001) (Fig. 5). This observation was further supported by an increased tumor recurrence rate (77.4%) in patients with positive nontumor clusterin expression, compared to those with negative expression (60.9%) (p = 0.045). In contrast, tumor recurrence rates did not differ significantly based on clusterin expression in the HCC tissue (positive: 71.1%; negative: 62.5%; p = 0.283). To further investigate the interplay between clusterin expression in tumor and nontumor tissues, patients were categorized into four groups: group 1 (clusterin+/nontumor+, n=40), group 2 (tumor+/nontumor-, n=36), group 3 (tumor–/nontumor+, n=13), and group 4 (tumor–/nontumor-, n=51). Survival analysis revealed that patients in group 2 and group 4 demonstrated better survival outcomes compared to those in group 1 (p=0.047 and p=0.043, respectively) and group 3 (p=0.002 and p=0.001, respectively). No significant survival differences were found between group 1 and group 3, or between group 2 and group 4.

Factors significantly impacting survival were identified through univariate analysis, including serum albumin levels below 4.0 g/dL, the presence of multiple tumors, macrovascular/microvascular invasion, TNM stage, and clusterin expression in non-tumor tissue. A Cox proportional hazards model further showed that macrovascular/microvascular invasion and clusterin expression in non-tumor liver sections were independent prognostic indicators.
We investigated clusterin levels in both serum and resected tissues (HCC and adjacent non-tumor) from HCC patients prior to tumor removal. Our findings indicated that clusterin protein expression within HCC tissues and preoperative serum clusterin concentrations did not correlate with patient outcomes following HCC resection. Conversely, a poorer prognosis was observed in patients exhibiting positive clusterin expression in non-tumor tissue when compared to those with negative expression.
Previous research by Nafee et al. (16) indicated elevated serum clusterin levels in HCC patients. While clusterin's overexpression in HCC has been documented (18), its significance within non-tumor liver tissue sections remained unexplored. In rat hepatocytes, in situ hybridization revealed low basal clusterin expression, which subsequently increased during hepatic growth and regression following hepatomitogen induction (17, 18).
Our findings align with prior research, demonstrating clusterin expression in nonneoplastic liver tissue. Immunohistochemical analysis in human neoplasms, including prostate carcinoma and its stromal components, has previously revealed cytoplasmic clusterin overexpression. In HCC tissues, we observed a similar cytoplasmic and canalicular-secreted staining pattern within tumor cells.
Our investigation revealed that clusterin was present in 54% of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues and 38% of adjacent non-tumor liver tissues. This study is the first to report a connection between clusterin expression in non-tumor tissue and a poorer prognosis. We propose that this observed relationship might represent a secondary pathway contributing to liver carcinogenesis. When we statistically analyzed clusterin overexpression in non-tumor tissue against various clinical and pathological characteristics of HCC patients, we found no significant differences linked to tumor size, the number of tumor nodules, or the presence of vascular invasion. Furthermore, there was no correlation between clusterin expression in non-tumor liver tissue and the extent of liver function impairment. These findings suggest that elevated clusterin expression may be involved in the process of carcinogenesis itself, rather than being a consequence of cirrhosis or fibrosis. Therefore, our results indicate that clusterin could play a crucial role in the development of human liver tumors, though the precise mechanisms warrant further research.
Several limitations affected this study. It was a retrospective investigation conducted at a single institution, and the sample size was comparatively small. Furthermore, the patient cohort primarily consisted of individuals with HCC and prevalent hepatitis B virus infection in Taiwan. Future research should include populations with predominant HCV infection or a history of alcohol abuse, necessitating a larger, prospective study for broader validation.
The study concludes that clusterin expression in non-cancerous liver tissue independently predicts a worse prognosis for HCC patients after surgery, specifically impacting disease-free survival. This suggests that elevated clusterin in these tissues contributes significantly to tumor development and could be a therapeutic target for post-resection HCC treatment.
