Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) is a distinct, typically low-grade form of osteosarcoma. A key characteristic of POS is the specific amplification of two oncogenes: MDM2 and CDK4. While POS usually exhibits an indolent clinical course, it can occasionally transform into a high-grade, potentially fatal malignancy. This malignant transformation, marked by high-grade differentiation, is the strongest predictor of the tumor's aggressiveness and metastatic potential. This study aimed to investigate the link between gene amplification and tumor grading in POS. To achieve this, a retrospective analysis was conducted on 14 patients with recurrent POS. Tumor specimens were examined for MDM2 and CDK4 expression and amplification using immunohistochemistry, multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (MQPCR), and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). The study included forty tumor specimens from these 14 patients. Twenty-seven tumors were classified as low-grade, while thirteen were high-grade. All POS samples demonstrated elevated MDM2 and CDK4 protein expression, a finding not observed in conventional osteosarcoma. Furthermore, MQPCR revealed a significant increase in MDM2 and CDK4 gene copies in most POS specimens, which was corroborated by MDM2 FISH analysis. Notably, the degree of gene amplification increased as tumors progressed, with high-grade POS consistently showing higher amplification levels than conventional osteosarcomas. In conclusion, both FISH and MQPCR are valuable methods for assessing oncogene amplification in bone tumors. The amplification levels of MDM2 and CDK4 are demonstrably related to tumor grading and progression. Determining the gene amplification status at a molecular level offers a reliable approach for predicting the clinical behavior of POS, even from small biopsy samples.

Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS), a distinctive but less frequent osteosarcoma subtype (4%-7%), typically affects young adults, predominantly females, in long bones like the distal femur and proximal tibia. It originates on the bone surface, presenting a broad, cauliflower-like growth. Initially a low-grade bone sarcoma, POS often has an indolent clinical course, allowing patients to live for years with minimal symptoms, even without surgery. Metastasis is rare unless POS progresses to high-grade dedifferentiated sarcoma (HDS), which carries a prognosis similar to conventional osteosarcoma (COS).
Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) pathologically presents with hypocellular, mildly atypical fibroblast-like cells within a desmoplastic stroma, interspersed with bland woven bone. Areas of cellular matrix, osteoblast-rimmed osteoid, and atypical chondroid cells are frequently seen. High-grade transformation and dedifferentiated sarcoma (HDS), similar to conventional osteosarcoma (COS) or undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, can occasionally be found. Differentiating HDS from COS morphologically is challenging, primarily when low-grade POS components are absent in HDS. POS accounts for approximately 4% of all osteosarcomas, with HDS being less common.
Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) exhibits a distinct genetic profile, characterized by consistent amplification of chromosome 12q13–15. This chromosomal region, rarely amplified in other bone tumors, harbors two key genes: cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) and murine double-minute type 2 (MDM2). Previous research indicates that both CDK4 and MDM2 protein expression are significantly elevated in POS, with reported increases in 87%–89% and 70%–89% of cases, respectively. Consequently, antibodies targeting these proteins are now standard diagnostic tools for POS. To further investigate the link between gene amplification and tumor grading, this study retrospectively analyzed archived POS specimens collected between 2004 and 2014 from Taipei Veterans General Hospital. The analysis incorporated clinical and follow-up data, focusing on recurrence and metastasis. The archived materials were examined using immunohistochemistry, multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (MQPCR), and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to explore the relationship between oncogene amplification and tumor grade.
This study analyzed formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks from parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) and six conventional osteosarcoma (COS) cases. Clinical data, including tumor size, stage, outcome, and follow-up, were gathered from orthopedic and pediatric surgeons. All bone tumors were graded using a four-grade system and staged per the AJCC TNM system, following review at surgical-pathologic-clinical conferences.
The institutional review board at Taipei Veterans General Hospital approved this study (IRB #: 2013-04-009A). All specimens were analyzed anonymously.
MDM2 and CDK4 protein expression was assessed on FFPE tissue sections using an automated Bond-Max system. Staining intensity was scored from 0 to 3, with scores 2 and 3 considered positive. Each section underwent at least two staining procedures.
Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on FFPE sections using an MDM2/CEP12 probe kit from Vysis. The MDM2 amplification ratio was determined by dividing the number of red dots by the number of green dots within at least 20 tumor nuclei per slide. Due to the presence of tiny, nested, or overlapping signals in high-grade tumors, exact red dot counts were challenging. Tumor cells with a few separate red dots or a small cluster (<10) were categorized as "+", while those with multiple nests of amplified red dots (>10 total) were designated as "++".
For MQPCR, tumor tissue exceeding 3 mm in diameter was deparaffinized, dried, and extracted using the Arcturus PicoPure DNA extraction kit. DNA concentration was measured using a Thermo NanoDrop 2000, with approximately 100 ng of template DNA extract per reaction. This quantity was chosen to ensure successful PCR amplification, indicated by a Ct value between 20 and 30 cycles.
Multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (MQPCR) was conducted using an ABI StepOnePlus real-time PCR system. Target gene primers and TaqMan fluorescent probes, featuring a black hole quencher, were designed with Beacon Designer 7.01. Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2× QIAGEN Multiplex PCR master mix, 1 μL of template DNA, 2 μL of 2.5 μmol/L primer-probe mixtures, and 7 μL of distilled, deionized water. The thermal cycling protocol involved an initial denaturation step of 15 minutes at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 seconds at 94°C and 45 seconds at 60°C. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, with additional runs for any equivocal amplification results.
To quantify gene amplification, fold changes in target genes were determined using MQPCR. The relative copy ratio (RCR) for CDK4 and MDM2, relative to ASNS, was calculated as 2<sup>-ΔΔCt</sup>. Control RCRs for MDM2 and CDK4 in non-tumor tissue were established. Amplification was defined as ratios exceeding the 97.5th percentile of control values, while ratios below the 2.5th percentile were deemed unsatisfactory. MDM2 and CDK4 RCRs were then categorized into four levels: unsatisfactory PCR (<0.5), no amplification (0.5–1.5), low amplification (1.5–10), and high amplification (>10). The cycle threshold (Ct) was selected during the log phase of amplification.
Bar graphs were generated using Prism 5 software. Standard deviation and probability values were determined through diagnostic tests. The Mann-Whitney test was employed for unpaired, non-parametric comparisons of immunostaining results, while the t-test was used for unpaired, parametric comparisons of MQPCR data.
This study included fourteen patients diagnosed with parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) in their extremities, with follow-up data spanning 34 to 216 months (average 124.4 months) from Taipei-Veterans General Hospital. The cohort consisted of nine women and five men, with ages ranging from 13 to 40 years (average 27.9 years). The most common primary sites were the femur (eight cases), followed by the tibia (four cases) and humerus (two cases). Tumor sizes varied, with a median maximal axis of 7.1 cm. A significant proportion of patients experienced recurrence (twelve), with eight of these progressing to a high-grade state. Three patients developed lung metastasis, and four ultimately died from the disease.
Histological grading was performed on forty tumor blocks from the parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) group and six blocks from the conventional osteosarcoma (COS) group. Two pathologists reviewed the H&E slides, reaching a consensus. All six COS tumors were high-grade, exhibiting osteoblastic and/or chondroblastic differentiation. Within the POS group, 27 tumors were classified as low-grade (Grade 1–2), while 13 displayed high-grade components (Grade 3–4), referred to as high-grade dedifferentiated sarcoma (HDS), with or without residual low-grade areas. Among the HDS samples, seven primarily showed a fibrosarcoma-like fascicular arrangement of neoplastic spindle cells. Three samples presented as sheets of giant cell-rich, pleomorphic sarcoma, and three exhibited both chondroblastic and osteoblastic differentiation, resembling chondroblastic osteosarcoma.
In parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) specimens, MDM2 and CDK4 protein expression was consistently elevated, with at least one marker showing positive staining (score 1 or higher) in all cases. A dual positive staining for both MDM2 and CDK4 was observed in the majority of POS cases (25 out of 27). High-grade dedifferentiated sarcoma (HDS) components within POS exhibited even more intense staining for both MDM2 and CDK4 (score 2 or higher) compared to low-grade POS. Specifically, the mean staining intensities for MDM2 and CDK4 were 1.3±0.8 and 1.8±0.9, respectively, in the POS group, while in the HDS group, these values rose to 2.1±0.8 and 2.5±0.7. These differences were statistically significant (p = 0.015 for MDM2 and p = 0.036 for CDK4). Conventional osteosarcoma (COS) samples generally lacked strong reactivity for MDM2 or CDK4, with only two showing focal weak CDK4 reactivity. While non-specific MDM2 staining was noted in benign histiocytes and osteoclasts, the MDM2 and CDK4 antibodies were confirmed as specific diagnostic markers for POS, and particularly for HDS. This observation mirrors findings in dedifferentiated liposarcoma, where high oncogene amplification levels are linked to poorer outcomes, suggesting a potential underestimation of the clinical significance of oncogene amplification in HDS.
Performing FISH analysis on bone tumors presented challenges, with many specimens exhibiting low or undetectable fluorescent signals. This issue likely stemmed from the detrimental effects of acid decalcification, specifically using 15% hydrochloric acid, on genomic DNA. Consequently, only a limited number of specimens, specifically eight from POS, five from HDS, and three from COS, retained sufficient fluorescent signals in their nuclei for analysis. Across the analyzed samples, all POS and HDS specimens demonstrated MDM2 amplification, with average amplification ratios of 4.6±3.2 and 14.8±6.7, respectively. In contrast, COS samples did not show MDM2 amplification, with an average ratio of 1.17±0.6. POS samples typically displayed a modest increase in tiny red dots, indicating extra MDM2 copies, alongside the normal green centromere control dots. HDS samples, however, exhibited larger clusters of red dots, suggesting amplified MDM2 genes in close proximity, potentially linked to ring or supernumerary chromosomes observed in prior cytogenetic studies.
The decalcification process negatively impacted PCR analysis due to genomic disruption and reduced template DNA yield. Consequently, consistent PCR amplification was achieved in only 70% (30) of FFPE tumor specimens that retained sufficient DNA integrity. Specimens with less than 50% tumor volume were excluded. Of the analyzed samples, 15 were POS, 10 were HDS, and 3 were COS. Conventional osteosarcoma (COS) samples did not exhibit MDM2 or CDK4 amplification, with relative copy ratios (RCRs) of 1.05±0.5 and 1.17±0.6, respectively. In contrast, POS samples showed average MDM2 RCRs of 5.1±2.8 and CDK4 RCRs of 7.2±3.5. High-grade dedifferentiated sarcoma (HDS) samples displayed significantly higher amplification levels, with average MDM2 RCRs of 16.8±2.3 and CDK4 RCRs of 22.7±8.3. These findings, with p-values less than 0.001, demonstrate a strong correlation between genetic amplification levels and tumor grading and differentiation, with an optimal cutoff value around 10 for both genes.
A longitudinal analysis was conducted on 14 patients diagnosed with Parosteal Osteosarcoma (POS) and/or High-Grade Dedifferentiated Sarcoma (HDS), examining recurrent and/or metastatic tumor specimens using MQPCR. Of these, 12 patients had analyzable samples. Four patients provided two additional specimens, while five patients contributed three more. In these recurrent or metastatic tumors, the amplification levels of MDM2 and CDK4 were generally higher than or comparable to, but never lower than, those observed in the primary tumors. This trend suggests a progression of amplification during recurrence, malignant transformation, or metastasis. Specifically, for four patients who succumbed to the disease, the average relative copy ratios (RCRs) for MDM2 and CDK4 in their initial biopsy tumors were 5.1±3.0 and 7.3±3.6, respectively. In stark contrast, the RCRs for MDM2 and CDK4 in their final recurrent or metastatic tumors were significantly higher at 16.2±2.1 and 24.3±5.5, respectively (p < 0.001 for both). This finding indicates that these amplified oncogenes undergo further amplification as tumors progress uncontrollably, metastasize, and ultimately lead to death.
Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) is recognized as a distinct bone tumor, characterized by its subtle morphology and indolent clinical course, in addition to its recently identified genetic amplification. While its clinical behavior is known to correlate with its morphological grade, the precise connections between genetic amplification, morphology, and clinical outcomes remain unclear. Variability in reported gene amplification levels (Table 2) can be attributed to several factors: the fragility of genomic DNA due to differing decalcification and preservation methods, the non-comparability of various amplification measurement techniques, and a lack of correlation between reported amplification status and tumor grading. In contrast, our findings demonstrate a clear relationship between varying levels of MDM2 and CDK4 amplification and tumor grading, clinical progression, and metastasis. Furthermore, these amplification statuses align with protein expression levels.
The success rates for FISH (32.5%) and MQPCR (70%) were limited, leaving a substantial amount of data non-informative. Previous research suggests that employing agents like EDTA or weak acids, along with modified incubation conditions (e.g., increased temperature, microwave use), can improve genomic DNA preservation during decalcification. Therefore, further investigation into these decalcification methods is essential to enhance the success rates of FISH and MQPCR analyses.
Malignant transformation and dedifferentiation are observed in various tumors, including liposarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and parosteal osteosarcoma (POS). Pathologically, these processes involve a loss of normal histological structure (dedifferentiation) coupled with increased nuclear grade and pleomorphism (malignant transformation). While often occurring concurrently, these features may not always be synchronized. For instance, a low-grade myxofibrosarcoma-like pattern has been suggested as an early indicator of malignant dedifferentiation in retroperitoneal liposarcoma. However, specific morphological characteristics of low-grade dedifferentiation in POS have not been well-defined, possibly due to the rarity of dedifferentiated POS and its unclear clinical significance. This study utilized semi-quantitative or quantitative measurement of oncogene amplification to address the morphological ambiguity in transformed POS. The findings indicate that both low-grade and high-grade dedifferentiation in POS can be linked to the amplification and expression status of MDM2 and CDK4. This suggests that these genes are crucial not only for the initiation of low-grade tumors at lower copy numbers but also for driving tumor progression and dedifferentiation through additional gene copies. Similar oncogene amplifications, such as MYC and HER2, are recognized as prognostic indicators in other cancers like neuroblastoma, breast, and gastric carcinoma.
Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) shares molecular similarities with other distinct sarcomas, such as liposarcoma and intimal sarcoma. These include common cytogenetic abnormalities like supernumerary ring chromosomes, giant marker chromosomes, and amplified segments of chromosome 12q13-15, which harbor extra copies of the MDM2 and CDK4 genes. Research indicates that elevated levels of CDK4 and MDM2 amplification are associated with poorer outcomes in liposarcoma patients, mirroring findings in this study. Notably, a previous report identified similar cutoff values (≥ 10) for MDM2 and CDK4 amplification in soft tissue tumors as used in this bone tumor study, despite differences in methodologies and tumor types. This suggests a shared genetic basis for tumor initiation and progression between these histologically distinct sarcomas. Given that POS is resistant to conventional chemotherapy and radiation, therapeutic strategies targeting MDM2 and CDK4, which are being explored in clinical trials for liposarcoma, could potentially benefit patients with recurrent and difficult-to-treat bone tumors.
In conclusion, this study established immunohistochemistry, FISH, and MQPCR as reliable and specific diagnostic assays for parosteal osteosarcoma (POS). These methods, while having limitations, are practical and efficient for pathology laboratories. We found a correlation between the histological grades of POS and the amplification levels of MDM2 and CDK4. Consequently, the amplification status of MDM2 and CDK4 may serve as a predictive marker for tumor behavior and aid in treatment stratification for patients receiving targeted or aggressive therapies. Future research, employing enhanced decalcification techniques and a larger cohort of POS patients with clinical follow-up, is necessary to fully understand the interplay between oncogene anomalies, nuclear grades, and survival outcomes in POS.
