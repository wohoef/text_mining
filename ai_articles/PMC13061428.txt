Existing federal guidelines advise limiting added sugars to under ten percent of daily calories. However, research on how food preparation behaviors affect sugar intake among low-income Mexican-American women remains limited. This study aimed to explore the cultural context surrounding how these women procure, prepare, and serve added sugars within their families' diets. Thirteen overweight or obese Mexican-American women, aged 27-40, participated in five focus groups to discuss their sugar consumption habits. Key themes emerged, including shifts in food purchasing to include high-sugar items and sugar-sweetened beverages after migrating to the U.S. Children's preferences significantly impacted household food choices. Additionally, the influence of relatives preparing meals, family traditions, large social gatherings, and traditional holiday foods on sugar consumption were identified. Culturally specific factors influencing sugar intake were uncovered. The findings highlight strategies for public health interventions aimed at reducing sugar consumption in this demographic. These interventions must recognize the importance of social connections, the influence of family members on dietary choices, and the value of preserving traditional foods and cultural celebrations.

Growing evidence links high sugar consumption to increased risks of overweight and obesity, which are significant risk factors for cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. Beyond weight gain, sugar intake has been associated with elevated blood pressure and blood lipids. Mexican-American women, in particular, exhibit disproportionately higher rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to non-Hispanic white women, with a rapid recent increase in obesity and metabolic disease prevalence. This population also shows high insulin resistance and poor glycemic control. These factors suggest that sugar consumption plays a crucial role in determining obesity and metabolic disease risk within this rapidly growing ethnic group, making it a key target for dietary interventions.
To combat lifestyle-related chronic diseases in the U.S., federal dietary guidelines now advise limiting added sugars to under ten percent of daily caloric intake (U.S. Department of Health & Human Services & U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2015). Effective interventions to reduce added sugar consumption necessitate understanding the specific circumstances of Mexican-American women, particularly how migration to the U.S. impacts their eating habits. Existing research indicates that Mexican-American immigrants often experience dietary shifts upon arriving in the U.S. These changes include less time for food preparation, increased reliance on fast food and snacks, and a greater consumption of processed foods, meats, sweets, and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). Conversely, their intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, beans, and grains tends to decrease. These dietary alterations are likely influenced by factors such as restricted access to quality food, the prevalence of fast-food establishments, social isolation, and shifts in food preparation and eating customs (Ayala, Baquero, & Klinger, 2008; Duffey, Gordon-Larsen, Ayala, & Popkin, 2008; Guarnaccia, Vivar, Bellows, & Alcaraz, 2012; Himmelgreen, Daza, Cooper, & Martinez, 2007). Additionally, Hispanics in the U.S. generally have lower incomes than non-Hispanic whites (DeNavas-Walt & Proctor, 2015), a factor linked to higher consumption of SSBs and fruit drinks (Ervin & Ogden, 2013; Han & Powell, 2013; Thompson et al., 2009). Recent findings highlight the significant role of culture, tradition, family, and attitudes toward healthy eating in shaping food preparation behaviors among Mexican-Americans (Smith, Dunton, Pinard, & Yaroch, 2016). These insights are crucial for developing dietary interventions, as the food preparation practices of Mexican-American mothers can profoundly influence their families' dietary patterns. However, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding the specific culture- and tradition-related food preparation behaviors that contribute to added sugar consumption among this demographic.
To develop effective interventions for reducing added sugar intake among Mexican-American women, a deeper understanding of the cultural influences on their consumption behaviors is crucial. This study addressed this gap by conducting focus groups with young Mexican-American mothers. The research aimed to explore how their cultural background, traditions, and eating habits shape their consumption of added sugars. Specifically, the study describes the cultural context surrounding how these women procure, prepare, and present foods containing added sugars within their families' diets.
Thirteen low-income Mexican-American women, aged 27–40 with BMIs ranging from 25 to 40 kg/m², participated in this study. They were recruited from the "Madres para la Salud" (Madres) participant pool, having previously consented to be re-contacted for future research. Madres was a 12-month randomized controlled trial completed in 2010, which examined the impact of a culturally tailored, social-support-based physical activity intervention on body weight, body fat, and metabolic risk factors in 139 postpartum, sedentary, low-income Hispanic women. The focus group sessions, totaling five and involving all 13 participants, took place between January and May 2015. Eligibility for participation required having been involved in Madres as a participant, a friend of a participant, or a promotora, along with being between 23–40 years old and having a BMI between 25 and 40 kg/m². Each study visit lasted approximately 2.5 hours and included obtaining informed consent, completing an interviewer-administered demographics questionnaire, and participating in a 1.5-hour focus group. A bilingual and bicultural moderator, trained in qualitative interviewing, led the sessions. To ensure accuracy and comprehension, all study materials underwent translation and back-translation by bilingual research team members. The Arizona State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the focus group study.
After consenting, participants completed a demographics questionnaire covering age, marital status, education, work, household size, preferred language, and U.S. residency duration.
To address knowledge gaps regarding sugar consumption among Mexican-American women, a focus group script was created. This script explored food procurement, preparation, and presentation, along with how Hispanic family traditions influence sugar intake. Specifically, the research aimed to understand the role of traditional foods, holidays, and family dynamics in sugar consumption. Focus groups were conducted in both Spanish and English, accommodating participant preferences, with four sessions in Spanish and one in English. All discussions were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed. These transcripts were then translated into English, and the translated versions were cross-referenced with the original audio recordings to ensure translation accuracy.
To understand the cultural and contextual factors contributing to added sugar consumption among Mexican-American women, a qualitative content analysis approach was employed. This method involved identifying data codes and categories through an iterative process of constant comparison. The analysis proceeded in two stages. Initially, focus group statements were reviewed to grasp the data within its context. Subsequently, data from all transcripts were coded using constant comparison to group similar concepts into categories. Researchers independently read the transcripts and developed initial coding schemes. These identified categories were then shared and discussed among the team. Through classification and synthesis, distinct categories and overarching themes were formulated, with ongoing discussion and analysis continuing until consensus was reached. The resulting contextual categories were then organized into broader themes of food procurement, preparation, and presentation, all examined in relation to the norms and values held by Mexican-American women.
Thirteen Mexican/Mexican-American women, aged 27-40, participated in the study. The group comprised six overweight and seven obese individuals, with an average BMI of 29.7 kg/m². Their annual household incomes were below $50,000, with five earning less than $20,000. Most participants, numbering twelve, were responsible for grocery shopping, and all thirteen handled the majority of household food preparation. Only one woman was U.S.-born; the rest had migrated from Mexico and had resided in the U.S. for 8 to 27 years.
Focus group themes revealed three main categories: food procurement, preparation, and presentation.
Migration to the U.S. significantly altered food procurement habits for the participating Mexican-American women. Before immigrating, they prioritized fresh produce, a practice instilled by their families in Mexico, where such items were affordable despite lower incomes. Sugary foods and beverages were generally too expensive. Upon arrival in the U.S., however, these high-sugar items became more readily available and convenient, leading to increased consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages and sweets. This shift in purchasing behavior was a frequently discussed theme.
Shopping habits shifted significantly after migrating to the U.S. Previously, participants shopped multiple times weekly in Mexico, prioritizing fresh items. In contrast, weekly shopping trips in the U.S. necessitated purchasing more calorie-dense, processed foods high in sugars, with fewer fresh options.
Children significantly influenced household food choices, with many mothers reporting increased purchases of sugary items like sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), sweets, and candies when their children accompanied them grocery shopping. One mother explained her strategy of avoiding shopping with her children to prevent them from requesting forbidden high-sugar items. Other participants acknowledged their children's persistent desire for unhealthy foods and admitted to yielding to these demands to please them. These commonly requested items included sugary drinks such as SSBs, fruit drinks, and Kool-Aid. The children's perception of what constitutes "food" was also a factor, as one mother noted that her children considered the absence of cookies, pancakes, and pre-packaged snacks as a lack of food in the house.
Despite understanding the link between nutrition and disease, participants' food procurement was hindered by a lack of nutrition knowledge and misinformation. For instance, some women were unaware of what processed foods entailed or their health implications. Others misinterpreted food label information, believing values applied to the entire package instead of a single serving. Confusion also existed regarding starches versus sugars, with one participant noting, "we eat baked potatoes, but there then there you go again with your sugar intake." Furthermore, participants struggled to differentiate sugar types, mistakenly viewing some, like brown sugar, as healthier options.
The influence of others on sugar consumption was a significant theme within food preparation. Participants described how their typical household eating habits shifted when other family members, such as spouses, in-laws, or children, were responsible for preparing or buying food. This often led to a greater intake of sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), convenient processed items, or traditional dishes laden with added sugars. For instance, one woman noted her husband's tendency to add substantial amounts of sugar to his cooking to satisfy the children. Another shared how her husband's cooking was a source of delight for their children, with him adding ingredients like marmalade and cream to pancakes.
In-laws' extended visits significantly impacted participants' eating habits. One woman shared that her mother-in-law's presence dictated their meals, leading to increased sugar consumption. During a six-month visit, the mother-in-law assumed cooking duties, altering household shopping and diets. This influence was so strong that the mother-in-law would claim they were running out of sugar, even shortly after the participant had bought large quantities. This highlights how extended family presence can directly shape dietary patterns and sugar intake within a household.
Witnessing others consume high-sugar foods also impacted participants' own intake. One woman added sugar to her coffee when her mother-in-law visited, a habit she didn't normally have. Participants also noted their children adopting habits from their own actions, which in turn motivated them to adopt healthier eating patterns.
Children significantly impacted food preparation. Participants noted their children's increased exposure to processed foods at school, leading to preferences for items like pizza and cookies. These children also frequently baked sweets at home, using online recipes, which made high-sugar foods readily accessible. This created an environment where such items were consistently available.
Generational traditions significantly influenced participants' sugar consumption. Many learned to prepare traditional foods and beverages from female relatives like mothers, sisters, or grandmothers. For example, participants recalled learning to make aguas frescas, a common drink in Mexico, from their mothers.
Food consumption settings significantly impacted how much food and sugar was eaten. For the Mexican-American women in this study, traditions and ingrained habits contributed to the sugar content in how food was presented. They identified traditional Mexican items like Piloncillo and aguas frescas as primary sources of sugar in their diets. Many participants noted that sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) were specifically consumed with particular meals. While they often drank water throughout the day, SSBs were part of certain meals. For instance, one woman reported drinking three cans of soda with a restaurant meal, despite drinking only water otherwise. Another participant detailed when her family typically consumes soda.
The frequent presence of sugary foods, especially sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), in participants' homes led to increased consumption. Many women reported keeping SSBs readily available and drinking them regularly due to their accessibility. Some attempted to reduce SSB intake by not stocking them at home, but found it challenging when other family members purchased them. One participant explicitly communicated to her husband her desire to limit soda consumption by requesting he refrain from buying it, even for guests.
Holidays significantly impacted the participants' sugar intake. Celebrations like Christmas and birthdays, characterized by social gatherings and traditional Mexican foods, were focal points for increased sugar consumption. Participants noted that traditional holiday items such as Piloncillo, punches, atole, and hot chocolate are rich in sugar. These events, often involving extensive social engagements, led to a greater consumption of sugary foods and beverages. The women described attending multiple gatherings where they felt obligated to eat, further contributing to their elevated sugar intake during these festive periods.
Increased sugar consumption during holidays stems from attending numerous social gatherings, where declining food is considered impolite. These occasions also unite families to prepare and savor traditional holiday dishes. As one participant noted, the expectation to partake in food at each gathering contributes to higher sugar intake.
During holidays, participants reported consuming a wide array of calorie-dense, high-sugar foods. This included desserts, tamales, and pozole, with a notable absence of fruit. Soda was also frequently consumed during these celebratory periods.
This study highlights key factors influencing sugar consumption among low-income Mexican-American women. These include significant shifts in food procurement between Mexico and the U.S., the pervasive influence of family values on food choices and preparation, the role of family relationships and gatherings in shaping eating habits, and the deep-rooted reliance on traditions in how food is presented.
Following migration to the U.S., participants reported an increase in purchasing sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) and high-sugar foods compared to their home country, aligning with existing literature on post-migration nutritional shifts. This shift is partly attributed to the convenience of SSBs, as one participant noted the lack of access to fresh fruits and the time constraints for preparing natural juices. The greater availability and affordability of processed foods and SSBs in the U.S. may also contribute to their increased consumption. For some, these "American" foods carry a sense of prestige and higher economic status, as items once exclusive to special occasions in their native countries are now readily accessible. This consumption can foster a feeling of belonging and integration into American culture, enhancing the appeal of these foods. Furthermore, reduced access to fresh produce and farming in the U.S., contrasted with rural Mexico, coupled with a higher prevalence of convenience stores, also influences these purchasing behaviors.
The study participants indicated that the affordability of unhealthy options, such as junk foods and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), in the U.S. significantly influenced their food purchasing and preparation choices. This contrasts with findings from Smith et al. (2016), where food cost was not a primary driver and healthy foods were readily available. The researchers in that study noted their participants had a higher socioeconomic status compared to the broader Mexican-American population in the U.S. This difference highlights the necessity of recognizing the varied experiences within the Latino community and incorporating socioeconomic considerations into the design of future interventions.
The study's findings align with existing research highlighting the link between the rapid pace of life in the U.S., reduced time for food preparation, and a rise in processed food and sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption among Latino immigrants. A key difference observed was the time dedicated to food procurement in the U.S. compared to Mexico. Participants indicated that the demanding lifestyle and extended work hours in the U.S. left them with insufficient time for the food purchasing and preparation habits they maintained in their home country. Consequently, due to time limitations, the need to balance work and family responsibilities, participants reported opting for more calorie-dense and convenient food options. This tendency to "grab something on the way home" is a recurring theme in the literature, reflecting the practical challenges faced by this population.
Family members significantly impact participants' decisions regarding healthy lifestyle choices, acting as either supportive or unsupportive influences, a finding consistent with Jay et al. (2014). Children, in particular, exert a considerable influence on dietary choices and shopping selections, as highlighted by Ayala et al. (2008). Despite possessing some knowledge of healthy nutrition, the Mexican-American mothers in this study frequently yielded to their children's desires for sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), and fast foods. This tendency to purchase such items may stem from a desire to please family members and facilitate their integration into American lifestyles. Furthermore, sugary foods are often employed as rewards or treats, serving as a means for parents to express affection, as observed by Heuman et al. (2013). The focus groups also revealed that male spouses likewise prepared and purchased foods with the intention of pleasing others.
In contrast to the U.S., where food rituals often involve solitary eating or snacking, Mexican food rituals are characterized by communal meals served attractively, with gatherings potentially lasting several hours. Research from the 2011-2013 American Time Use Survey indicated that while full-time working Latinas dedicated less time to food preparation, they maintained their commitment to family dinners, underscoring the cultural significance of shared meals. The structure of mealtimes is recognized as influential in shaping children's lifelong eating habits. Furthermore, the nature of social interaction during meals impacts adult consumption patterns, affecting both the quantity and types of food consumed.
In the focus groups, participants described food preparation as a collaborative effort involving numerous family members. This aligns with findings from Lindberg and Stevens (2011), where Mexican-American women also viewed food preparation as a family undertaking, with multiple relatives contributing and learning traditional recipes from mothers, sisters, or mothers-in-law. Similar patterns emerged in our study, particularly during holidays, where the family's diet shifted when husbands, mothers, or in-laws took charge of cooking. This often meant the woman lost control over her family's meals, as exemplified by a mother-in-law who consistently added excessive sugar to all dishes. Tensions also arose when younger family members attempted to prepare traditional foods in healthier ways, as noted by Smith et al. (2016). One participant in their study struggled to maintain a healthy diet due to her mother's persistent use of lard in cooking. Adding to these conflicts, children often preferred "American" foods like hamburgers, hot dogs, and soda over traditional Mexican dishes. This situation mirrors findings from Pyatak et al. (2014), which documented family conflicts in Latino households stemming from differing parental opinions on feeding children. These observations underscore the necessity of involving all family members in future dietary and nutrition interventions for Mexican-American women. Recognizing the crucial role of women, understanding family dynamics, and teaching collaborative food preparation and negotiation strategies are essential.
Mexican-American women in this study, mirroring findings by Smith et al. (2016), frequently attend social events like weddings and quinceañeras. These celebrations feature extensive homemade meals and sugary beverages such as punches, cocktails, atole, and hot chocolate. Echoing Lindberg and Stevens (2011), the participants confirmed the central role of food in Mexican-American culture, extending beyond daily life to holidays and special occasions. Maintaining a healthy diet during these food-centric celebrations proved challenging. The women often attended multiple social gatherings in a single day, feeling obligated to eat at each to avoid appearing impolite, a sentiment also noted by Lindberg and Stevens (2011) who described social and familial pressures to participate in these events.
Despite strong cultural ties, participants also celebrated U.S. holidays like Thanksgiving. The extended holiday seasons, including birthdays and quinceañeras, present a prime opportunity for interventions focused on promoting healthy eating and reducing sugar intake.
The study participants shared instances of how food consumption habits and routines quickly became ingrained. For instance, one mother recounted how her ten-year-old daughter, after being praised for her attractively presented cakes and cookies, began baking sweets multiple times a week. This ongoing positive reinforcement, though unintentional, can foster long-term unhealthy eating patterns. However, the paper suggests that similar behavioral strategies could be employed to cultivate healthy dietary habits, such as teaching children to prepare traditional foods like tortillas. Furthermore, the participants revealed a lack of nutrition knowledge and misinformation regarding the foods they bought and prepared. They often cited time constraints as a barrier to reading supermarket labels, with one participant noting that the price was the only number they typically considered, echoing findings from Lindberg and Stevens (2011). Despite this knowledge gap, the women expressed a strong desire to learn more about food's nutritional value, how to interpret food labels, and participate in cooking demonstrations. These demonstrations would offer a platform to share traditional Mexican recipes and discover healthier methods for preparing them.
The study's findings, derived from a small group of low-income Mexican-American women of childbearing age who have resided in the U.S. for over eight years, may not be broadly generalizable to all Latina adults. This limitation particularly applies to individuals who have recently migrated, are older, or belong to higher socioeconomic strata. The participants, all within a similar age range, reported annual household incomes below $51,200 for a family of four, aligning with the low-income threshold for the Phoenix Metropolitan Area in 2015. Despite the limited sample size, this research offers valuable insights into sugar consumption, a significant and modifiable risk factor, within a major U.S. ethnic subgroup, addressing a gap in existing literature. The study involved 13 participants, with 12 responsible for household food purchasing and all overseeing meal preparation. Traditionally, Hispanic women play a primary role in food shopping and preparation, exerting considerable influence over the home food environment. Understanding the sugar consumption behaviors of women of childbearing age is vital for early childhood obesity prevention, as evidence suggests that the intake of sweets and sugar-sweetened beverages by low-income Hispanic women strongly influences infant weight. The study successfully identified culturally relevant strategies that can be incorporated into behavioral interventions aimed at reducing sugar consumption among low-income Mexican-American mothers. Addressing these cultural and contextual dietary influences is crucial for mitigating sugar intake and associated health issues in this underserved population.
To effectively promote healthy eating and reduce sugar consumption among Mexican-American women, future interventions should recognize the significance of social connections and the substantial influence of family and extended family on dietary choices. The importance of preserving traditional foods and cultural celebrations must also be a key consideration. Drawing from the study's findings, several strategies can be implemented. Firstly, interventions should support the continuation of food traditions and rituals, such as purchasing fresh fruits as done in Mexico and opting for water over sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). This can involve introducing healthier adaptations to traditional practices, like flavoring aguas frescas with herbs instead of sugar, combining fruit with vegetable juices, or modifying recipes to reduce sugar content. Alternatively, new, healthier traditions could be established. Secondly, participants need to be equipped with strategies for efficient and economical food procurement, preparation, and presentation. This includes guidance on selecting affordable low-sugar options, understanding and interpreting sugar content on food labels, determining appropriate portion sizes, and choosing seasonal or frozen fruits. For those utilizing programs like SNAP, education on using benefits for fresh produce at farmers' markets is also beneficial. Thirdly, interventions should emphasize the role of Latina mothers as positive role models for their children, demonstrating healthy dietary behaviors through personal example, such as avoiding SSBs and processed foods. Fourthly, strategies for navigating healthy eating discussions with family members are crucial, addressing situations like in-laws preparing high-sugar foods or spouses bringing home SSBs and fast food. Finally, a family-centered approach is recommended, where all household members collaborate towards shared nutrition goals and address challenges related to food consumption and sugar intake.
