This study investigated how attentional biases related to body dissatisfaction could be altered. Researchers used eye-gaze tracking to measure participants' fixations on images of "thin" models, "fat" models, and average women. These measurements were taken for 8 seconds, both before and after participants completed a priming task designed to induce either body satisfaction or body dissatisfaction. The study included 108 women, categorized into high (n=65) and low (n=43) trait body dissatisfaction groups based on their Body Shape Questionnaire scores. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two priming conditions. Both priming tasks successfully modified participants' temporary (state) body satisfaction levels. Initially, women with high body dissatisfaction showed an attentional bias towards both thin and fat model images, consistent with prior research. However, body dissatisfaction priming unexpectedly increased attention to body images in both high and low body dissatisfaction groups. Conversely, body satisfaction priming did not influence attention in either group. These results highlight the susceptibility of all women, regardless of their baseline body dissatisfaction, to the effects of body dissatisfaction priming.

Body dissatisfaction is a widespread concern among women, with roughly 70% reporting dissatisfaction with their body shape, weight, or appearance. This dissatisfaction is associated with several adverse outcomes, including substance abuse, diminished self-esteem, and problematic peer relationships. Furthermore, it is a significant risk factor for the development and persistence of eating disorders and depression. To effectively address these negative consequences through treatment and prevention programs, a comprehensive understanding of the cognitive mechanisms that underpin and sustain body dissatisfaction in women is essential. Research into these cognitive processes, such as how body-related information is perceived and attended to, is crucial for developing targeted interventions.
Cognitive theories of body dissatisfaction suggest that mental frameworks concerning body shape and weight impact how body-related information is processed. These theories posit that stimuli related to the body preferentially capture attention and are processed more readily than other stimuli (Vitousek & Hollon, 1990; Williamson et al., 2004). This cognitive perspective offers a way to understand body dissatisfaction. For instance, Rodgers and DuBois (2016) observed that women with high body dissatisfaction exhibited an attentional maintenance bias, fixating on body-related stimuli more often and for longer durations compared to non-body stimuli. Such attentional biases may heighten women's susceptibility to persistent body dissatisfaction, especially given the pervasive presence of body and appearance-focused content in media (Gao et al., 2014; Joseph et al., 2016; Rodgers & DuBois, 2016).
Gao et al. (2014) investigated the link between body satisfaction and visual attention using an eye-tracking method. Their study compared how women with high or low body dissatisfaction viewed images of "thin" and "fat" models. During a 15-second free-viewing task, participants' eye movements were recorded as they observed pairs of images. These image sets included garden items, household objects, women performing everyday activities, and either a thin or fat model. The researchers observed that women with higher body dissatisfaction fixated longer on both thin and fat models compared to those with lower body dissatisfaction. They theorized that this might stem from body-dissatisfied women making upward appearance comparisons with thin models and downward comparisons with fat models, associating their own bodies with perceived negative traits. However, contrary to their initial predictions, exposure to these model images did not elevate participants' reported state body dissatisfaction. This suggested that merely observing these images during the eye-tracking task was insufficient to trigger negative body-related cognitive patterns.
Tobin et al. (2018) similarly investigated attention using an eye-tracking paradigm, presenting women with high or low body dissatisfaction with displays of four words: one fat-related, one thin-related, and two neutral. Each display was shown for 8 seconds, during which participants' eye gaze was monitored. The researchers hypothesized that women with higher body dissatisfaction would focus more on fat-related words and less on thin-related words, compared to women with lower body dissatisfaction. However, their findings contradicted this prediction. Instead, women experiencing greater body dissatisfaction allocated more attention to both fat-related and thin-related words than their body-satisfied counterparts. This outcome suggests a broader attentional bias in body-dissatisfied individuals towards body-related terminology, regardless of its specific association with thinness or fatness.
Tobin et al. (2018) investigated how priming negative body-related schemas influenced attention to body-related words. Their study involved participants viewing a slideshow of 25 thin models and rating each image against their ideal female body perception. This priming procedure successfully elevated state body dissatisfaction, but exclusively among women with high baseline body dissatisfaction. However, this priming did not alter attentional maintenance biases in either body-dissatisfied or body-satisfied women. This result aligns with certain studies employing thin model exposure as a prime (Cassin et al., 2008; Smith & Rieger, 2010), yet it contrasts with others that observed an effect on attentional maintenance (Johansson et al., 2005; Labarge et al., 1998; Lane et al., 2017; Markis & McLennan, 2011). The inconsistency in findings might stem from the insufficient potency of thin model exposure in some studies to effectively prime cognitive biases in body-dissatisfied women. Consequently, a goal of the current research was to explore whether a more comprehensive priming method could impact attentional biases in body-dissatisfied women.
Research on reducing attentional biases linked to body dissatisfaction is limited. For instance, Glashouwer et al. (2016) observed no change in attention to "ugly" body parts in highly body-dissatisfied women following five weeks of positive mirror exposure training, despite improved body satisfaction. Similarly, Loughnan et al. (2015) found that an attention bias modification procedure, which aimed to create a bias towards neutral words in a dot-probe task, did not affect participants' body satisfaction or their attention to words related to appearance versus neutral words. Crucially, neither of these studies investigated whether laboratory-based priming, intended to boost body satisfaction, could diminish attention to body-related stimuli. Our study, therefore, explored the adaptability of attentional biases in body-dissatisfied women by investigating if increased state body satisfaction could alter these biases.
This study employed an eye-tracking paradigm to examine how attentional biases in women with varying levels of body dissatisfaction were affected by body satisfaction and body dissatisfaction priming. We aimed to determine if these biases could be modified. Participants, categorized by high or low body dissatisfaction, viewed images of "thin" models, "fat" models, average-sized women, and neutral objects (household and garden items). This approach mirrored Gao et al.'s (2014) methodology but utilized a distinct set of images. Our priming procedures were intentionally more intensive than those in prior research (e.g., Cassin et al., 2008; Smith & Rieger, 2010; Tobin et al., 2018). Each priming task involved both video viewing and a reflective writing activity, designed to either enhance or diminish body satisfaction. The investigation focused on two primary hypotheses: first, the effectiveness of these priming tasks in altering body satisfaction, and second, their subsequent influence on participants' attention to body images.
The study hypothesized that priming tasks would alter self-reported body satisfaction and mood. Specifically, it was predicted that body satisfaction priming would elevate participants' state body satisfaction across all levels of body dissatisfaction. Conversely, body dissatisfaction priming was expected to reduce state body satisfaction, but only in women with high body dissatisfaction, consistent with previous research by Tobin et al. (2018). Regarding mood, the researchers anticipated that body satisfaction priming would enhance self-rated mood, while body dissatisfaction priming would diminish it. These mood changes were measured to provide further evidence of the priming tasks' effectiveness, building on prior work by Bessenoff (2006), Brown and Tiggemann (2016), and Tiggemann, Polivy, and Hargreaves (2009).
This study aimed to understand how shifts in body satisfaction, induced by priming, influence attentional patterns. We hypothesized three main outcomes. Firstly, before any priming, women with high body dissatisfaction were expected to exhibit greater attention to thin and fat model images compared to those with low body dissatisfaction. Secondly, we predicted that body satisfaction priming would reduce attention to body images in women with high body dissatisfaction, relative to their pre-priming levels, while having no impact on women with low body dissatisfaction. Finally, we anticipated that body dissatisfaction priming would heighten attention to body images in women with high body dissatisfaction, again relative to their baseline, but would not affect women with low body dissatisfaction. These predictions guided our investigation into the interplay between body satisfaction, priming, and visual attention.
Participants were first identified through an online survey. Then, eligible participants visited the lab to provide eye-tracking data, which comprised the study's two phases.
The Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) measures body shape concerns over the past four weeks. This 34-question instrument uses a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from "never" (1) to "always" (6). Higher total scores on the BSQ indicate greater trait body dissatisfaction, with scores below 80 suggesting an absence of body concerns. The BSQ demonstrates strong internal consistency, with an alpha of .97 in the current sample, consistent with previous research.
The Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2), developed by Tylka and Wood-Barcalow in 2015, measures an individual's level of body acceptance and respect. It comprises 10 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from "never" (1) to "always" (5). A higher average score across these items signifies greater body appreciation. Research indicates the BAS-2 demonstrates strong internal consistency among women. Furthermore, it exhibits a robust negative correlation with body dissatisfaction, as reported by Tylka and Wood-Barcalow (2015). The scale's utility extends to predicting unique variance in several psychological constructs. For both men and women, BAS-2 scores are predictive of intuitive eating, self-esteem, and proactive coping. Specifically for women, these scores also predict unique variance in eating disorder symptomatology. In the present study's sample, the BAS-2 and Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) scores showed a significant negative correlation of -.73 (N = 108, p < .001). This finding aligns with observations by Tylka and Wood-Barcalow, suggesting a strong relationship between body appreciation and body dissatisfaction, with 57% shared variance. However, this also indicates that while related, these constructs are distinct. The internal consistency for the BAS-2 in this sample was high, with an alpha coefficient of .92.
A visual analog scale (VAS) assessed participants' current body satisfaction. This 100-mm horizontal line ranged from "very dissatisfied" on the left to "very satisfied" on the right. Participants indicated their satisfaction by marking the line, with scores from -50 to +50 derived from the mark's distance in millimeters from the midpoint. This method, previously employed by Tobin et al. (2018) to gauge shifts in state body satisfaction following thin model priming, and by other studies on priming effects (e.g., Gao et al., 2014; Glashouwer et al., 2016), proved effective here. To mitigate demand characteristics linked to the priming, participants completed the body satisfaction VAS before and after each task, then sealed their responses in an envelope for privacy.
Mood was evaluated using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) throughout the study. This 100-mm horizontal line ranged from "very negative" on the left to "very positive" on the right. Participants indicated their current mood by marking a point on this line. Scores were then assigned from -50 to +50, with negative values signifying a negative mood and positive values indicating a positive mood, based on the mark's distance from the midpoint. This method, consistent with prior research (e.g., Frayn, Sears, & von Ranson, 2016; Scherrer & Dobson, 2015), allowed for quantitative assessment of mood changes. Participants completed this mood VAS at two key intervals: before and after each experimental task.
Participants' body mass index (BMI), an indicator of body fat, was calculated from their self-reported height and weight. This method is reliable, as self-reported measurements for height and weight show strong correlations with actual measurements (r = .98 and r = .99, respectively).
Participants were randomly assigned to either the body dissatisfaction or body satisfaction priming task, ensuring an even distribution across conditions. Both priming tasks were designed to be comparable in duration and content, each lasting approximately 10 minutes and incorporating both video viewing and a writing exercise. For the body dissatisfaction prime, the initial 5 minutes involved watching a video of a Sports Illustrated swimsuit model photoshoot (Sports Illustrated Swimsuit, 2016, 2017). This video was selected due to its resemblance to standard body dissatisfaction priming methods that typically utilize slideshows of thin models. Following the video, participants moved to a private room to complete a 5-minute writing activity. The instructions for this task directed participants to: "For the next five minutes, imagine that you are standing in a room surrounded by mirrors, viewing your own nude body. With this image in mind, write about the parts of your body that you dislike or wish you could change, and why. Next, imagine that another person is standing in the room, evaluating your body, and describe the specific aspects of your body you think this person would evaluate negatively, and why. Be as specific as you can. You can choose to keep your writing or have it confidentially shredded. The researcher will not read anything you have written." Conversely, the body satisfaction prime began with participants watching three short videos, totaling about 5 minutes, that promoted body acceptance (BuzzFeedYellow, 2015; I Heart My Body, 2016; Upworthy, 2016). These videos showcased various positive body image perspectives, including celebrating body diversity, appreciating bodily functions, and fostering self-love. Subsequently, participants spent 5 minutes in a private room on a writing activity. The instructions provided were: "For the next five minutes, think about the aspects of your body that you are most proud of. For example, you might take pride in certain features of your body or physical appearance, or you might be proud of things your body has allowed you to do. Write down as many specific aspects as you can think of that make you feel pride or respect for your body, and why. Be as specific as you can. You can choose to keep your writing or have it confidentially shredded. The researcher will not read anything you have written." To adhere to ethical guidelines, participants initially assigned to the body dissatisfaction priming task also completed the body satisfaction priming task before concluding their laboratory visit.
Eye-tracking displays featured five image categories: thin models, fat models, average women, household objects, and gardening objects. These internet-sourced images, while similar to those in Gao et al. (2014), comprised a distinct set. This allowed for a comprehensive assessment of attentional biases towards various body types and neutral stimuli.
An online survey, conducted via Qualtrics, was utilized to select an optimal set of images for the study. One hundred female undergraduate students, none of whom participated in the subsequent eye-tracking phase, provided ratings in exchange for course credit. The survey presented a total of 320 images, with each participant rating a randomly assigned subset of approximately one-third of these (106-107 images). The image pool comprised diverse categories. Specifically, it included 45 images of "fat" models, depicting women with larger bodies in underwear or bathing suits, and 45 images of "thin" models, showing women with smaller bodies similarly attired. Additionally, there were 90 images of average-sized women engaged in everyday activities like reading, talking, walking, or working on a computer. These "average women" images served as control stimuli, as they featured women without emphasizing body shape or weight. The survey also incorporated 70 gardening-related images (e.g., flowers, tools) and 70 household-related images (e.g., furniture, kitchenware). Participants rated all images for valence on a scale ranging from -3 ("extremely negative") to +3 ("extremely positive"). For the three categories featuring women (fat models, thin models, and average women), additional ratings were collected. Body size was assessed on a scale from -3 ("extremely thin") to +3 ("extremely fat"), with 0 representing "neutral." Attractiveness was also rated on a scale from -3 ("extremely unattractive") to +3 ("extremely attractive"), with 0 as "neutral." This comprehensive rating process allowed for the selection of images that met specific criteria for the main study.
From the initial pool, 32 thin model images were chosen based on the lowest average body size ratings, indicating they were perceived as "extremely thin." Conversely, 32 fat model images were selected for their highest average body size ratings, signifying an "extremely fat" perception. For average women, 64 images with ratings nearest to "neutral" (0) were included. Additionally, 64 images each of gardening and household objects were randomly chosen to serve as neutral stimuli. This selection process ensured distinct categories for the study.
An EyeLink 1000 eye-tracking system, employing infrared video technology, recorded participants' eye movements. This system boasts a 1,000-Hz sampling rate, 2 ms temporal resolution, and under 0.5° visual angle gaze error. To ensure accurate tracking, participants viewed stimuli on a 24-inch LCD monitor, positioned 60 cm away, while using a chin rest to minimize head motion. This setup facilitated precise measurement of attention during image viewing.
An initial online survey, conducted in a computer lab via Qualtrics, identified potential participants. A total of 525 female undergraduate students completed this survey in small groups, receiving course credit for their involvement. The survey gathered demographic information, including age, ethnicity, and education, alongside self-reported height and weight for BMI calculation. It also administered the Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ; Cooper et al., 1987) and the Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2; Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015). Following data cleaning to remove incomplete responses and those uninterested in a lab visit, 457 eligible participants remained. These individuals were categorized into three groups based on their total BSQ scores, utilizing cutoffs established by Tobin et al. (2018). The top tertile, representing high trait body dissatisfaction, comprised 192 participants with BSQ scores exceeding 104. The middle tertile included 156 participants with scores between 73 and 103, while the bottom tertile, indicative of low trait body dissatisfaction, consisted of 109 participants with scores below 72. Only women from the top and bottom tertiles were subsequently invited to participate in the laboratory phase of the study.
Laboratory visits were scheduled for eligible participants via email, with psychology course bonus credit offered as an incentive. Participation rates were comparable between the high body dissatisfaction group (top tertile, 47.7%) and the low body dissatisfaction group (bottom tertile, 50.5%). Crucially, no significant selection bias was detected. Non-participating women did not differ from participants in key demographic or psychological measures. Specifically, their average age (M = 20.40 years vs. M = 20.98, t(240) = 0.92, p = .35) and BMI (M = 23.29 kg/m2 vs. M = 22.68 kg/m2, t(242) = 0.26, p = .79) were statistically indistinguishable. Furthermore, chi-squared analyses revealed no differences in ethnicity or education level (ps > .10). T-tests also confirmed no significant differences in Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) or Body Appreciation Scale (BAS) scores (ps > .10). This consistency across groups suggests that the participant recruitment process did not introduce systematic biases that could have influenced the study's outcomes.
The study involved 108 women, categorized into two groups based on their Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) scores: 65 exhibited high trait body dissatisfaction (top tertile), while 43 showed low trait body dissatisfaction (bottom tertile). These groups were comparable in age and ethnicity. As anticipated, women with high body dissatisfaction scored significantly higher on the BSQ (M = 129.00) compared to those with low body dissatisfaction (M = 60.01). This difference was reflected in their pre-prime body satisfaction Visual Analog Scale (VAS) ratings, where the high dissatisfaction group reported considerably lower satisfaction (M = 1.15) than the low dissatisfaction group (M = 27.33). Furthermore, women with high body dissatisfaction also had lower Body Appreciation Scale (BAS) scores (M = 3.00) than women with low body dissatisfaction (M = 4.11). However, no significant difference was observed between the groups in their pre-prime mood VAS ratings.
Upon arrival, participants received verbal and written study instructions and provided informed consent. Baseline mood and state body satisfaction were then assessed using visual analog scales. Eye-tracking data collection occurred in a dedicated room. The eye-tracking system underwent a 5-minute calibration for each participant, with data collection commencing immediately after successful calibration.
Participants’ eye movements were recorded during an 8-second presentation of four images per trial. Before each trial, a central black dot ensured accurate gaze measurement. Each display comprised one image from four categories: household objects, gardening objects, average women, and either a thin or a fat model. To prevent bias, image placement within the display's four quadrants was randomized, ensuring each image type appeared equally across positions. All images were standardized in size, and efforts were made to match their color, brightness, and complexity. Participants were instructed to view the images freely, simulating a slideshow experience. Eye gaze was continuously tracked throughout the 8-second interval. The experimental phase involved 32 trials, preceded by two practice trials. The entire image viewing process lasted approximately five minutes. This methodology allowed for the assessment of attentional patterns towards various body types and neutral stimuli.
Following the initial image viewing, participants completed body satisfaction and mood assessments using Visual Analog Scales (VAS). Subsequently, they engaged in either a body satisfaction or body dissatisfaction priming task, after which the VAS measures were immediately repeated. To gather post-priming eye-tracking data, participants then viewed a second set of images. These displays mirrored the first, each containing a household item, a gardening item, an average woman, and either a thin or fat model image. The sequence of these two image sets was randomized for each participant. This second viewing phase involved two practice trials and 32 experimental trials. After completing this second eye-tracking task, participants again completed the body satisfaction and mood VAS measures. Finally, they completed the Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) and a last set of body satisfaction and mood VAS measures before being debriefed.
This study analyzed body satisfaction and mood ratings to evaluate the efficacy of two priming tasks. Eye-tracking data was then examined to identify group differences in attention to thin and fat model images, both before and after these priming procedures. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25).
A mixed-model Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess the impact of priming tasks on participants' state body satisfaction, as indicated by Visual Analog Scale (VAS) ratings. This analysis aimed to determine if the priming effect varied based on pre-existing trait body dissatisfaction levels. The ANOVA incorporated two key factors: "Body Dissatisfaction Group" (comprising women with either low or high body dissatisfaction) and "Time of Measurement" (including baseline, pre-prime, and three post-prime assessments). The initial post-prime measurement (post-prime 1) was taken immediately after the body satisfaction or dissatisfaction priming task. The second post-prime measurement (post-prime 2) followed the second eye-tracking procedure, while the final measurement (post-prime 3) occurred at the conclusion of the testing session. Separate analyses were conducted for the body satisfaction and body dissatisfaction priming tasks, reflecting distinct hypotheses for each. This methodical approach allowed for a detailed examination of how different priming interventions influenced state body satisfaction across groups with varying levels of trait body dissatisfaction.

The body dissatisfaction priming task significantly lowered state body satisfaction ratings for both groups of women, regardless of their initial level of body dissatisfaction. For women with high body dissatisfaction, ratings dropped from 2.80 to -17.66 (t(31) = 5.99, p < .001). Similarly, women with low body dissatisfaction experienced a decrease from 28.05 to 12.09 (t(21) = 5.34, p < .001). This outcome contradicted the initial hypothesis (1b), which predicted effects only for the high body dissatisfaction group. While the overall pattern of post-prime ratings showed some group differences, the general trend of decreased satisfaction was consistent. Notably, by the study's conclusion (post-prime 3), state body satisfaction ratings for both groups had recovered significantly from their immediate post-prime levels. These findings collectively demonstrate that both body satisfaction and body dissatisfaction priming tasks successfully altered participants' state body satisfaction, increasing it with satisfaction priming and decreasing it with dissatisfaction priming across both high and low body dissatisfaction groups.

Eye-tracking data underwent processing with EyeLink Data Viewer software, employing default settings to remove artifacts like blinks and missing information. For inclusion in subsequent analyses, individual fixations needed to be at least 100 milliseconds long; shorter, consecutive fixations were combined. The primary measure of attention was the total fixation time on an image during its 8-second display. This metric, representing the cumulative duration of all fixations, indicated the level of attention directed towards an image. This total fixation time was determined for each image category—thin models, fat models, and average women—within every trial. These individual trial values were then averaged across all 32 trials. To streamline analysis, fixation times for gardening and household images were combined and designated as "neutral images." Longer fixation times were interpreted as increased attentional engagement with the image.

This study investigated how priming tasks, designed to alter state body satisfaction or dissatisfaction, impacted attentional biases toward body images. Researchers randomly assigned women, categorized by high or low trait body dissatisfaction via the BSQ, to either a body satisfaction or body dissatisfaction priming task. Eye-tracking data, collected both before and after the priming, measured attention. The research aimed to enhance understanding of attentional biases in women with body dissatisfaction by assessing their susceptibility to these priming manipulations.
The study confirmed that women with high trait body dissatisfaction experienced altered self-reported state body satisfaction and mood following both body satisfaction and body dissatisfaction priming tasks, with increases or decreases observed post-priming, respectively. Unexpectedly, women with low trait body dissatisfaction, who were initially more content with their bodies, also showed similar shifts in state body satisfaction due to these priming tasks. This finding contrasts with Tobin et al. (2018), whose body dissatisfaction priming did not impact state body satisfaction in women with low body dissatisfaction. Tobin et al. attributed their results to the brevity of their priming method, which involved a short slideshow of thin model images. Our study, however, employed a more extensive and immersive priming procedure. For instance, the body dissatisfaction prime included a video of a swimsuit model photoshoot followed by a writing exercise where participants detailed disliked body aspects. This more potent approach likely explains why even women with low trait body dissatisfaction were susceptible to the priming effects. This suggests that individuals with lower levels of body dissatisfaction are not immune to environmental cues that promote body dissatisfaction. Furthermore, these women also responded positively to the body satisfaction priming task, which incorporated positive body image messaging. These results highlight the vulnerability of women across the spectrum of body dissatisfaction to both negative and positive body image influences, particularly when exposed to more intensive priming interventions.
Before any priming, women with high body dissatisfaction showed a greater attentional bias towards images of both thin and fat models compared to women with low body dissatisfaction. This observation aligns with previous research by Gao et al. (2014) and others (Rodgers & DuBois, 2016; Tobin et al., 2018; Vitousek & Hollon, 1990; Williamson et al., 2004), which indicates that individuals with negative self-perceptions regarding weight often exhibit such attentional patterns. Following the body dissatisfaction priming, women with high body dissatisfaction intensified their focus on thin and fat model images. This outcome, while consistent with our predictions, diverges from the findings of Gao et al. (2014) and Tobin et al. (2018). Their studies, despite employing body dissatisfaction priming, did not observe a shift in attentional biases among women, regardless of their initial body dissatisfaction levels. We attribute this discrepancy to the more potent and effective body dissatisfaction priming procedure utilized in our study. This suggests that the intensity of the priming task plays a crucial role in its ability to alter attentional biases.
The study revealed that body dissatisfaction priming heightened attention to thin and fat model images even in women with low body dissatisfaction. This finding was unexpected, demonstrating that these women are not impervious to negative body image messages. The priming's effect on attention was remarkably similar for both high and low body dissatisfaction groups, increasing their focus on these body types. This consistent impact across groups suggests that the study's more elaborate priming procedure, compared to previous research, likely contributed to this observed vulnerability.
Body satisfaction priming, unlike body dissatisfaction priming, did not alter women's attention to body-related images, regardless of their pre-existing levels of body dissatisfaction. This outcome occurred despite the body satisfaction priming successfully elevating participants' self-reported state body satisfaction, as indicated by the Body Satisfaction Visual Analog Scale (VAS). This suggests that body satisfaction priming may have a limited capacity to influence attentional biases, or that the specific priming method employed lacked sufficient potency to induce such changes. It is important to consider that the body satisfaction priming task inherently directed participants' focus toward their own bodies. This was due to the writing activity, which required them to reflect on and describe aspects of their bodies they appreciated or felt proud of. This intense self-focus on their bodies might have inadvertently heightened their awareness of perceived flaws, thereby diminishing the intended positive effects of the priming procedure. Further analysis revealed a disparity in the effectiveness of the two priming tasks. The body satisfaction priming task demonstrated a smaller percentage change in state body satisfaction from pre- to post-priming compared to the body dissatisfaction priming task. Specifically, the average increase in state body satisfaction VAS ratings across both groups for the body satisfaction priming was 55% (from 15.41 to 23.90). In contrast, the body dissatisfaction priming led to a more substantial 122% decrease in state body satisfaction VAS ratings (from 12.62 to -2.78). Therefore, the observed difference in the two priming tasks' ability to influence attention might be partly or entirely attributable to this discrepancy in their overall efficacy. It is conceivable that an alternative approach to body satisfaction priming, one that perhaps does not emphasize direct focus on the body, could yield changes in women's attentional biases. This presents a crucial avenue for exploration in future research, as understanding how to effectively modify attentional biases through body satisfaction interventions remains a significant goal.
The generalizability of this study's findings is limited, as it exclusively involved nonclinical undergraduate women. Future research should broaden the participant pool to include clinical populations, men, individuals from diverse ethnic backgrounds, and various age groups. This expansion is crucial because different demographics may encounter distinct body-ideal messaging or react to such messages in varied ways. Another limitation stems from the participation rate: only about half of the recruited women attended the lab visit. While non-participants did not differ significantly from participants in terms of ethnicity, age, BMI, body satisfaction, or body appreciation, unmeasured factors could have introduced selection bias. For instance, differences in socioeconomic status, personality traits, or social engagement might have influenced who chose to participate. Such unmeasured distinctions could have subtly shaped the characteristics of the high and low body dissatisfaction groups, potentially impacting the study's outcomes. Therefore, the observed effects might not be universally applicable across all women.
The priming procedure's specific components warrant further investigation. We did not measure state body satisfaction between the video and writing portions of each priming task, making it difficult to ascertain the individual contribution of each to the overall priming effect. It is conceivable that the writing tasks, where participants articulated their feelings about their bodies, generated a more potent priming effect than the less intensive methods (e.g., viewing fashion model images) employed in prior research. Conversely, the private nature of the written statements means some participants might not have fully engaged, implying that any observed priming effect would then be solely attributable to the videos. Future research on priming should consider these nuances. Exploring variations of our priming tasks, such as using different videos, writing prompts, or instructions, could help isolate the impact of each component. Despite this, the priming tasks, when considered holistically, demonstrably achieved their intended effect. Our findings strongly advocate for the adoption of similarly intensive priming methodologies in subsequent studies on body dissatisfaction, moving beyond mere exposure to thin models. This comprehensive approach is crucial for a more precise understanding of how different elements within a priming intervention contribute to changes in body dissatisfaction.
The ineffectiveness of the body satisfaction priming task in reducing attentional biases among women with high body dissatisfaction carries significant implications for interventions aimed at mitigating body dissatisfaction and preventing eating disorders in young women. Our findings suggest that programs might achieve greater success by de-emphasizing body appearance and fostering body acceptance, thereby reducing an individual's tendency to fixate on their physical form. It appears that inducing body satisfaction through priming is inherently more challenging than inducing body dissatisfaction. This difficulty may stem from a fundamental human tendency to prioritize and react more intensely to negative stimuli than positive ones, a phenomenon often termed "bad is stronger than good" (Baumeister et al., 2001). Consequently, women, particularly those already experiencing elevated body dissatisfaction, may be more susceptible to negative body-related messaging than positive affirmations. If this interpretation holds true, it would advocate for interventions that employ indirect strategies to modify body image attitudes and attentional patterns.
This study investigated how priming tasks, designed to induce either body satisfaction or dissatisfaction, influenced women's attention to body images. We tracked eye gaze before and after these priming tasks. As hypothesized, women with high body dissatisfaction showed increased attention to images of both thin and fat models following the body dissatisfaction prime. Unexpectedly, women with low body dissatisfaction also exhibited this same increase in attention after the body dissatisfaction prime, indicating their susceptibility to negative body image messaging. Conversely, the body satisfaction priming task did not alter attention to thin or fat model images for any participants, regardless of their initial level of body dissatisfaction. These results highlight the importance of further research into attentional biases linked to body dissatisfaction and their potential for modification. Specifically, more work is needed to develop effective body image interventions that can counteract these biases and mitigate negative consequences for women with high body dissatisfaction.
