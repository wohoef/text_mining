This research aimed to improve body dissatisfaction reduction programs by understanding attentional biases. The study utilized eye-gaze tracking to measure fixations on images of "thin" models, "fat" models, and average women over an 8-second period, both before and after participants underwent a body satisfaction or body dissatisfaction priming task. Participants, categorized by high (n=65) or low (n=43) trait body dissatisfaction using the Body Shape Questionnaire, were randomly assigned to one of these priming conditions. The results demonstrated that both priming tasks successfully altered participants' immediate body satisfaction levels. Consistent with prior research, women with high body dissatisfaction initially showed an attentional bias towards thin and fat model images. Unexpectedly, body dissatisfaction priming led to increased attention to body images in both high and low body dissatisfaction groups. In contrast, body satisfaction priming had no impact on attention for either group. These findings highlight the susceptibility of women, regardless of their baseline body dissatisfaction, to the effects of body dissatisfaction priming.

A significant majority of women, around 70%, experience some level of body dissatisfaction concerning their shape, weight, or overall appearance (Fiske et al., 2014). This widespread issue is not merely cosmetic; it's a critical factor in the onset and persistence of eating disorders (Stice et al., 2017; Stice et al., 2011) and depression (Farrer et al., 2016). Furthermore, dissatisfaction with one's body has been connected to substance abuse (Schwinn et al., 2016), as well as diminished self-esteem and problematic peer relationships (Davison & McCabe, 2006). To effectively develop interventions and prevention strategies for these adverse consequences, a comprehensive grasp of the cognitive mechanisms driving and sustaining body dissatisfaction in women is essential (Glashouwer et al., 2016).
Cognitive theories suggest that body dissatisfaction is rooted in how individuals process body-related information. These theories posit that mental frameworks concerning body shape and weight cause body-related stimuli to preferentially capture and hold attention compared to other types of stimuli (Vitousek & Hollon, 1990; Williamson et al., 2004). This perspective is supported by research, such as that by Rodgers and DuBois (2016), who observed that women with significant body dissatisfaction displayed an attentional maintenance bias, fixating on body-related content more often and for longer durations than non-body-related content. Such attentional biases could exacerbate ongoing body dissatisfaction, especially given the pervasive presence of body and appearance-focused media (Gao et al., 2014; Joseph et al., 2016; Rodgers & DuBois, 2016).
Gao et al. (2014) explored the link between body satisfaction and attention using an eye-tracking method. Their study involved women with either high or low body dissatisfaction, observing their gaze patterns when presented with images of "thin" and "fat" models. In a free-viewing task, participants saw image pairs for 15 seconds, which included diverse items like garden and household objects, women performing activities, and a model (either thin or fat). The researchers discovered that women experiencing body dissatisfaction focused longer on both thin and fat models compared to women who were more body-satisfied. They theorized that this behavior might stem from body-dissatisfied women making upward appearance comparisons with thin models and downward comparisons with fat models, associating their own bodies with perceived flaws in the fat models. However, an unexpected outcome was that simply viewing these model images during the eye-tracking task did not elevate participants' immediate body dissatisfaction, suggesting this passive exposure wasn't enough to trigger negative body-related thoughts.
Tobin et al. (2018) conducted a free-viewing eye-tracking study, presenting displays of four words (one fat-related, one thin-related, two neutral) for 8 seconds to women with varying levels of body dissatisfaction. Their aim was to observe attentional patterns. Contrary to their hypothesis that body-dissatisfied women would focus more on fat-related words and less on thin-related words compared to body-satisfied women, the study revealed that body-dissatisfied women actually paid more attention to both fat- and thin-related words than their body-satisfied counterparts. This finding aligns with other similar research.
A study by Tobin et al. (2018) explored how priming negative body-related thoughts influenced attention to body-related words. Their method involved participants viewing a slideshow of 25 thin models and then evaluating how well each image aligned with their ideal female body perception. This priming successfully elevated state body dissatisfaction, but exclusively among women who already experienced high levels of body dissatisfaction. Interestingly, this priming technique did not alter attentional maintenance biases in either body-dissatisfied or body-satisfied women. This result aligns with some prior research where thin model exposure as a prime did not impact attentional biases in body-dissatisfied women (e.g., Cassin et al., 2008; Smith & Rieger, 2010). However, other studies have indeed observed an effect of thin model exposure on attentional maintenance (e.g., Johansson et al., 2005; Labarge et al., 1998; Lane et al., 2017; Markis & McLennan, 2011). The inconsistency in these findings might stem from the possibility that the thin model exposure used in some studies wasn't sufficiently powerful to activate cognitive biases in body-dissatisfied women. Consequently, a goal of the current study was to investigate whether a more comprehensive priming procedure could influence the attentional biases of body-dissatisfied women.
Research into mitigating attentional biases linked to body dissatisfaction is limited. For instance, Glashouwer et al. (2016) investigated whether a 5-week positive mirror exposure training could alter attentional biases towards "ugly" versus "beautiful" body parts. Despite an increase in state body satisfaction, women with high body dissatisfaction showed no change in their attention to "ugly" body parts. Similarly, Loughnan et al. (2015) implemented an attention bias modification procedure, aiming to shift attention towards neutral words in a dot-probe task. This intervention, however, failed to impact participants' state body satisfaction or their attention to neutral versus appearance-related words. Crucially, neither of these studies explored whether laboratory-based priming, specifically designed to enhance state body satisfaction, could diminish attention to body-related stimuli. Our current study sought to address this gap, exploring the flexibility of attentional biases in women with body dissatisfaction by investigating if elevating state body satisfaction could modify these biases.
This research aimed to determine if attentional biases in women experiencing body dissatisfaction could be altered through specific priming tasks. The study employed an eye-tracking methodology to quantify how women with varying levels of body dissatisfaction (high versus low) focused their attention on different body images. Similar to Gao et al. (2014), the visual stimuli included "thin" models, "fat" models, average-sized women, and neutral objects like household and garden items. To achieve a stronger impact than prior studies (e.g., Cassin et al., 2008; Smith & Rieger, 2010; Tobin et al., 2018), the priming procedures were intentionally made more intensive. These involved both video viewing and reflective writing exercises, designed to either enhance or diminish body satisfaction. The investigation focused on two primary hypotheses: the effectiveness of these priming tasks and their subsequent influence on attention.
The study anticipated that the priming tasks would successfully alter participants' self-reported body satisfaction and mood. Specifically, it was hypothesized that the body satisfaction prime would elevate state body satisfaction across all participants, irrespective of their baseline body dissatisfaction levels. Conversely, the body dissatisfaction prime was expected to reduce state body satisfaction, but only in women already experiencing high body dissatisfaction, aligning with previous research by Tobin et al. (2018). Regarding mood, the researchers predicted an improvement following the body satisfaction prime and a decline after the body dissatisfaction prime. These mood changes were considered supplementary indicators of the priming tasks' overall effectiveness.
This study primarily aimed to investigate how alterations in body satisfaction, induced by specific priming tasks, influenced attentional patterns. We hypothesized several outcomes regarding attention. Firstly, before any priming, women with high body dissatisfaction were expected to exhibit greater attention to images of thin and fat models compared to those with low body dissatisfaction. Secondly, we predicted that body satisfaction priming would reduce attention to body images in highly dissatisfied women, but would not impact attention in women with low body dissatisfaction. Lastly, our hypothesis for body dissatisfaction priming was an increase in attention to body images for highly dissatisfied women, while having no effect on women with low body dissatisfaction.
The study comprised two stages: initially, an online survey identified eligible participants for eye-tracking; subsequently, these individuals attended the laboratory for data collection.
The Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ), developed by Cooper, Taylor, and Fairburn in 1987, measures body shape concerns over the preceding four weeks. It comprises 34 items, each assessed on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = "never" to 6 = "always"). A higher total score signifies increased trait body dissatisfaction, while scores below 80 suggest an absence of body concerns. The BSQ demonstrates strong internal consistency, with an alpha of .97 in this study's sample.
The Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2), developed by Tylka & Wood-Barcalow in 2015, measures an individual's level of body acceptance and respect. It comprises 10 items, such as "I feel that my body has at least some good qualities," which are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = "never," 5 = "always"). A higher average score across these items signifies greater body appreciation. Research indicates the BAS-2 demonstrates strong internal consistency in women and exhibits a robust negative correlation with body dissatisfaction. Beyond this, BAS-2 scores uniquely predict variations in intuitive eating, self-esteem, and proactive coping in both men and women. For women specifically, it also accounts for unique variance in eating disorder symptoms. In the present study's sample, the BAS-2 and BSQ scores showed a strong inverse relationship (r = -.73, N = 108, p < .001), suggesting these constructs, while sharing 57% variance, are distinct. The internal consistency (Alpha) for the current sample was .92.
To gauge participants' immediate body satisfaction, a visual analog scale (VAS) was employed. This VAS was a 100-mm horizontal line, anchored by "very dissatisfied" on the left and "very satisfied" on the right. Individuals indicated their current satisfaction by marking a point on this line. Scores, ranging from -50 to +50, were derived by measuring the mark's distance from the center. This method, previously validated by Tobin et al. (2018) for assessing shifts in state body satisfaction following thin model priming, and by other studies on priming effects (e.g., Gao et al., 2014; Glashouwer et al., 2016), was administered before and after each task. To ensure privacy and mitigate demand characteristics related to the priming, participants sealed their completed scales in an envelope.
To gauge participants' emotional state, a visual analog scale (VAS) for mood was employed. This scale was a 100-mm horizontal line, anchored by "very negative" on the left and "very positive" on the right. Individuals indicated their current mood by marking a point on this line. A score, ranging from -50 to +50, was then derived by measuring the mark's distance from the line's center, with negative values signifying negative mood and positive values indicating positive mood. This mood VAS was administered both before and after each task, consistent with its use in various prior studies (e.g., Frayn, Sears, & von Ranson, 2016; Scherrer & Dobson, 2015).
Participants' body mass index (BMI), an indicator of body fat, was calculated from their self-reported height and weight. This method is reliable, as self-reported measurements for height and weight show strong correlations with actual measured values.
Participants were randomly assigned to either a body dissatisfaction or body satisfaction priming task. Both priming tasks were designed to be comparable in duration and content, each lasting approximately 10 minutes and incorporating both video viewing and a writing exercise. For the body dissatisfaction prime, participants initially viewed a five-minute video featuring a Sports Illustrated swimsuit model photoshoot. This video was selected because it mirrored common body dissatisfaction priming methods that typically involve viewing slideshows of thin models. Following the video, participants moved to a private room to complete a writing activity. They received instructions to spend five minutes imagining themselves nude in a mirrored room and to write about body parts they disliked or wished to change, explaining their reasons. They were then asked to imagine another person evaluating their body and to describe specific aspects they believed this person would judge negatively, again with explanations. Participants were assured their writing would remain confidential and unread by the researcher, with the option to keep it or have it shredded. Conversely, for the body satisfaction prime, participants watched three short videos, totaling about five minutes, which promoted body acceptance. These videos showcased diverse positive body image attitudes, such as celebrating body diversity, appreciating bodily functions, and fostering self-love. After the videos, participants engaged in a five-minute writing activity in a private room. Their instructions were to reflect on and write about aspects of their body they were most proud of, including specific features or physical achievements, and to explain why these aspects evoked pride or respect. Similar to the other priming task, participants were given the choice to keep their writing or have it confidentially shredded, with the assurance that the researcher would not read it. As an ethical consideration, participants initially assigned to the body dissatisfaction priming task also completed the body satisfaction priming task before concluding their laboratory session.
The eye-tracking displays utilized five distinct image categories, all sourced online: thin models, fat models, average women, household items, and gardening items. While mirroring categories from Gao et al. (2014), this study employed a unique set of images.
To select the most suitable images for the study, an online survey administered via Qualtrics gathered ratings for a broad collection of visuals. One hundred female undergraduate students, distinct from those in the eye-tracking portion of the study, provided these ratings for psychology course bonus credit. The survey presented a total of 320 images across several categories. These included 45 images of fat models and 45 of thin models, both depicting women in underwear or bathing suits. Additionally, 90 images featured average-sized women engaged in everyday activities like reading or walking, where body shape and weight were less prominent; these served as control stimuli. The collection also comprised 70 gardening-related images and 70 household-related images. Each student rated approximately one-third of the total images (106 to 107, randomly assigned). Participants evaluated all images for valence on a scale from -3 ("extremely negative") to +3 ("extremely positive"). For the images portraying women (fat models, thin models, and average women), additional ratings were collected. Body size was assessed on a scale from -3 ("extremely thin") to +3 ("extremely fat"), with 0 indicating "neutral." Attractiveness was also rated, ranging from -3 ("extremely unattractive") to +3 ("extremely attractive"), with a "neutral" midpoint of 0.
From the body size ratings, 32 thin model images were chosen, representing those with the lowest average scores, indicating they were "extremely thin." Conversely, 32 fat model images were selected based on having the highest average body size ratings, signifying "extremely fat." For average women, 64 images with ratings closest to a "neutral" zero were picked. Additionally, 64 gardening-related and 64 household-related images were included through random selection.
An EyeLink 1000 system, manufactured by SR Research Ltd in Ottawa, Ontario, Canada, was employed for recording eye movements. This infrared video-based eye-tracking technology boasts a 1,000-Hz sampling rate and a 2 ms temporal resolution, with gaze errors typically under 0.5 degrees of visual angle. To ensure precise tracking, participants rested their chins to limit head motion while viewing stimuli on a 24-inch LCD monitor, placed roughly 60 cm away.
An initial screening process identified potential participants through an online survey, administered via Qualtrics in a computer lab. Five hundred twenty-five female undergraduate students participated in small groups, earning bonus credit for a psychology course. This survey gathered demographic data (age, ethnicity, education), self-reported height and weight for BMI calculation, and included the BSQ and BAS-2 questionnaires. After removing incomplete responses and those uninterested in a lab visit, 457 participants were deemed eligible. These individuals were then categorized into three groups—top, middle, and bottom tertiles—based on their total BSQ scores, using established cutoffs from Tobin et al. (2018). Specifically, 192 participants formed the top tertile (BSQ > 104), 156 comprised the middle tertile (BSQ 73-103), and 109 were in the bottom tertile (BSQ < 72). Only women from the top (high trait body dissatisfaction) and bottom (low trait body dissatisfaction) tertiles were subsequently invited for the laboratory phase of the study.
Following an initial screening, eligible participants were contacted by email and offered extra credit in a psychology course for attending a laboratory session. The participation rates were comparable across groups, with 47.7% of women from the top tertile and 50.5% from the bottom tertile agreeing to participate. Importantly, an analysis of those who declined to participate revealed no significant differences from those who did, across several key demographic and psychological measures. Specifically, there were no notable discrepancies in age (M = 20.40 vs. 20.98), BMI (M = 23.29 vs. 22.68 kg/m2), ethnicity, education level, or scores on the BSQ or BAS. This lack of significant variation between participants and non-participants suggests that the study's results are unlikely to be skewed by a selection bias.
The study involved 108 women whose eye movements were tracked. Of these, 65 exhibited high trait body dissatisfaction, falling into the top tertile of BSQ scores, while 43 showed low trait body dissatisfaction, belonging to the bottom tertile. As anticipated, the high body dissatisfaction group displayed significantly elevated BSQ scores (M = 129.00) compared to the low body dissatisfaction group (M = 60.01). Correspondingly, the high body dissatisfaction group reported markedly lower pre-prime body satisfaction (M = 1.15) than the low body dissatisfaction group (M = 27.33), and also had lower BAS scores (M = 3.00 vs. M = 4.11). Importantly, both groups were comparable in age, ethnicity, and pre-prime mood VAS ratings. Descriptive statistics for these groups are detailed in Table 1.
Upon arrival at the lab, participants received both verbal and written instructions detailing the study. Following informed consent, initial mood and state body satisfaction were measured using VAS scales. Eye-tracking data collection occurred in a specialized room, commencing after a 5-minute calibration of the system. This calibration was essential before any data could be gathered.
During each trial, participants' eye movements were recorded for 8 seconds as they freely viewed a display of four images, presented simultaneously. To begin, a central black dot was shown, which participants fixed their gaze upon to calibrate eye-tracking accuracy. Each display was carefully constructed to include one image from four categories: a household item, a gardening item, an average woman, and either a thin or a fat model. The images were standardized in size, color, brightness, and complexity. To prevent bias, the position of each image type within the four quadrants of the display was systematically varied. The session comprised two practice trials followed by 32 experimental trials, taking approximately 5 minutes in total.
Following the initial image viewing, participants completed body satisfaction and mood Visual Analog Scale (VAS) measures. Subsequently, they engaged in either a body satisfaction or body dissatisfaction priming task. Immediately after this priming, the body satisfaction and mood VAS measures were administered again. To gather post-priming eye-tracking data, participants then viewed a second set of images. These displays mirrored the first, each featuring a household item, a gardening item, an average woman, and either a thin or fat model image. The sequence of presenting these two image sets was randomized among participants. This second viewing phase included two practice trials and 32 experimental trials. After completing the second eye-tracking task, participants once more filled out the body satisfaction and mood VAS scales. Finally, they completed the Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) and a last set of body satisfaction and mood VAS measures before being debriefed.
The study's analytical approach began by evaluating the effectiveness of both priming tasks through body satisfaction VAS ratings. Subsequently, participants' mood ratings were analyzed. Finally, eye-tracking data were examined to pinpoint differences in attention to thin and fat model images among groups, both pre- and post-priming. All statistical computations utilized SPSS, version 25.
To assess the impact of priming on participants' immediate body satisfaction, a mixed-model ANOVA was employed. This analysis examined state body satisfaction VAS ratings, collected at multiple points: baseline, immediately before priming, immediately after priming (post-prime 1), after the second eye-tracking session (post-prime 2), and at the study's conclusion (post-prime 3). The factors considered were Body Dissatisfaction Group (high vs. low trait body dissatisfaction) and Time of Measurement. Separate analyses were conducted for the body satisfaction and body dissatisfaction priming tasks, reflecting distinct hypotheses for each. The goal was to determine if priming influenced state body dissatisfaction and if this influence varied based on an individual's pre-existing level of body dissatisfaction.

Eye-tracking data underwent initial processing with EyeLink Data Viewer software, employing default settings to remove blinks, missing data, and other recording anomalies. For analytical purposes, only fixations lasting at least 100 milliseconds were retained; shorter, consecutive fixations were combined into a single event. The primary measure of attention was defined as the cumulative fixation time an individual spent on an image during its 8-second display. A higher cumulative fixation time indicated increased attention. This metric was computed for each image category—thin models, fat models, and average women—within every trial, and then averaged across all 32 trials. To streamline subsequent analyses, fixation times for gardening and household items were combined and labeled as "neutral images."

This research investigated if manipulating body satisfaction or dissatisfaction through priming tasks could alter attentional biases towards body images in women. Participants, categorized by high or low trait body dissatisfaction using the BSQ, were randomly assigned to either a body satisfaction or body dissatisfaction priming condition. Eye-tracking data were gathered both before and after these priming interventions. The study aimed to advance the understanding of how attentional biases in women experiencing body dissatisfaction might be influenced by such experimental manipulations.
The study's findings confirmed that women with high trait body dissatisfaction experienced changes in self-reported state body satisfaction and mood following both body satisfaction and body dissatisfaction priming tasks, with increases or decreases observed as expected. However, an unexpected result emerged: women with low trait body dissatisfaction, who were initially more content with their bodies, also showed similar shifts in state body satisfaction due to these priming tasks. This contrasts with Tobin et al. (2018), whose body dissatisfaction priming did not impact body satisfaction in women with low dissatisfaction. They attributed this to their brief thin model slideshow lacking sufficient potency. Our study's more extensive and immersive priming method, which included a video of a swimsuit model photoshoot followed by a writing exercise about disliked body parts, likely explains this difference. The susceptibility of women with low trait body dissatisfaction to our priming suggests their vulnerability to environmental cues that promote body dissatisfaction. Furthermore, these women also responded positively to the body satisfaction priming's positive body image messages.
Before any priming, women with high body dissatisfaction showed a greater attentional focus on images of both thin and fat models compared to women with low body dissatisfaction. This observation aligns with our hypothesis and corroborates prior research, including that by Gao et al. (2014), which indicates that individuals with negative self-perceptions regarding weight often display attentional biases towards body-related stimuli. Furthermore, our study found that after the body dissatisfaction priming, women with high body dissatisfaction intensified their attention to these thin and fat model images, a result that also matched our predictions. This particular finding diverges from the outcomes reported by Gao et al. (2014) and Tobin et al. (2018), whose body dissatisfaction priming procedures did not alter attentional biases in either high or low body dissatisfaction groups. We attribute this discrepancy to the more potent and effective nature of the body dissatisfaction priming task employed in our study.
The study revealed an unanticipated outcome: even women with low body dissatisfaction exhibited heightened attention to images of thin and fat models subsequent to the body dissatisfaction priming. This suggests that individuals with lower levels of body dissatisfaction are not impervious to the adverse impacts of such priming. In fact, the priming's influence on attention was remarkably similar across both high and low body dissatisfaction groups, leading to increased focus on thin and fat model images. This divergence from previous research is likely attributable to the more intensive body dissatisfaction priming method employed in this study.
Unlike the body dissatisfaction priming, the body satisfaction priming task did not influence how women, regardless of their pre-existing levels of body dissatisfaction, attended to body-related images. This was observed despite the fact that the body satisfaction priming successfully elevated self-reported state body satisfaction, as indicated by the body satisfaction Visual Analog Scale (VAS). One interpretation of this outcome is that body satisfaction priming has a limited capacity to alter attentional biases in women, or perhaps the specific body satisfaction priming method employed lacked sufficient potency to induce such changes. It is important to consider that the body satisfaction priming activity, which involved participants writing about positive aspects of their own bodies, inherently directed their attention to their physical selves. This self-focused attention might have inadvertently led to an increased awareness of perceived flaws, thereby potentially diminishing the intended positive impact of the priming. Furthermore, the body satisfaction priming task demonstrated less overall effectiveness compared to the body dissatisfaction priming task when assessed by the body satisfaction VAS ratings. The average increase in state body satisfaction for the body satisfaction priming was 55% (from 15.41 to 23.90), whereas the body dissatisfaction priming resulted in a more substantial 122% decrease (from 12.62 to -2.78). This disparity in efficacy between the two priming methods could partially or entirely explain their differing effects on attention. Consequently, future research should explore alternative body satisfaction priming approaches, such as those that do not primarily focus on the individual's body, to potentially achieve changes in attentional biases.
When interpreting the study's results, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the use of nonclinical undergraduate women limits the generalizability of these findings to clinical populations. Future studies should broaden their scope to include diverse groups, such as men, individuals from various ethnic backgrounds, and different age cohorts, as their exposure to and responses to body-ideal messages might vary significantly. Secondly, only about half of the women invited to the lab participated. While no significant differences were found between participants and non-participants in terms of ethnicity, age, BMI, body satisfaction, or body appreciation, unmeasured factors like socioeconomic status, personality, or social engagement could have influenced participation. Such unmeasured differences might have impacted the characteristics of the high and low body dissatisfaction groups, potentially affecting the study's outcomes.
A limitation of this study's priming procedure was the inability to gauge the individual impact of its video and writing components on the overall priming effect. Since no visual analog measure of state body satisfaction was taken between these two activities, their relative contributions remain unclear. It's possible that the writing tasks, which involved participants articulating their feelings about their bodies, generated a more potent priming effect compared to methods in prior research, such as simply viewing fashion model images. However, the private nature of these written statements means some participants might not have fully engaged, implying that the videos alone could have been responsible for any observed priming. Future research on priming should consider these nuances, perhaps by experimenting with different videos, writing prompts, or instructions to isolate the effects of each element. Despite this, the priming tasks collectively proved effective. Our findings strongly advocate for future studies on body dissatisfaction priming to employ similarly intensive procedures, moving beyond mere exposure to thin models.
Interventions aimed at decreasing body dissatisfaction and preventing eating disorders in young women should consider the study's finding that body satisfaction priming did not diminish attentional biases in highly body-dissatisfied women. This suggests that programs might be more effective by focusing on strategies that lessen the importance of physical appearance in women's lives and encourage body acceptance, rather than solely trying to boost body satisfaction. It appears that inducing body satisfaction is harder than inducing body dissatisfaction, potentially necessitating more complex methods. This difficulty could stem from a general human tendency to prioritize and react more intensely to negative information, a concept known as "bad is stronger than good." Consequently, women, particularly those already experiencing high body dissatisfaction, may be more receptive to negative body-related messages than positive ones. If this holds true, it supports implementing interventions that use indirect methods to alter body image attitudes and attention.
The research investigated how attentional biases towards body images in women were affected by priming tasks designed to induce either body satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Before the priming, women with high body dissatisfaction showed increased attention to thin and fat model images, as anticipated. Following the body dissatisfaction priming, attention to these body types also increased for women with high body dissatisfaction, aligning with predictions. Unexpectedly, this same increase in attention was observed in women with low body dissatisfaction after the dissatisfaction prime, indicating their susceptibility to negative body image influences. Conversely, the body satisfaction priming had no impact on attention to thin or fat model images for any women, regardless of their initial body dissatisfaction levels. These results highlight the need for further study into attentional biases and their potential for modification, particularly for developing effective interventions against body dissatisfaction.
