This study investigated the prognostic value of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow (BM) and the tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) in primary colorectal cancer (CRC) patients undergoing surgical resection. Current TNM staging inadequately identifies high-risk CRC patients, necessitating the exploration of additional predictive markers. A cohort of 125 primary CRC patients, treated between 2001 and 2007, were included. Prior to surgery, all patients provided BM samples for DTC detection using cytokeratin immunocytochemistry (CK-ICC). The TSR was assessed from H&E stained sections of the primary tumors. DTCs were identified in the BM of 18% (23/125) of patients. However, the presence of DTCs in the BM showed no significant association with overall survival (HR 0.97, p=0.93), with similar 5-year survival rates for BM-positive (72%) and BM-negative (68%) patients. Furthermore, no relationship was observed between DTC presence and TSR. In contrast, the TSR emerged as a significant prognostic factor. Patients with a high TSR exhibited a worse overall survival (HR 2.16, p=0.04), with 5-year survival rates of 62% for stroma-high patients compared to 84% for stroma-low patients. The findings suggest that DTCs in the bone marrow of CRC patients do not appear to be a reliable indicator of disease outcome. Conversely, the tumor microenvironment, as reflected by the TSR, plays a crucial role in CRC behavior and prognosis, with a high stroma content being associated with poorer survival.

Predicting recurrence after curative colorectal cancer (CRC) resection is crucial. Early identification of recurrence could allow for potentially curative reoperations and targeted adjuvant treatment for high-risk patients.
Current colorectal cancer (CRC) prognosis and adjuvant therapy decisions primarily rely on TNM staging. While crucial for classification, this histopathological method offers limited additional markers for refining risk assessment in stage II patients, who are typically considered standard risk. Furthermore, some stage III CRC patients might not benefit from postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, potentially sparing them its toxicity. This highlights a deficiency in current staging, which fails to adequately identify all high-risk individuals. Notably, approximately 25% of lymph node-negative cases, presumed to have localized disease, experience recurrence, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification.
Disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) are recognized as potential biomarkers for prognostication in colorectal cancer and other malignancies. Carcinomas from various organs frequently establish reservoirs of DTCs in the bone marrow (BM), which can subsequently lead to distant metastases. For instance, in breast cancer, DTCs are established as an independent predictor of poorer survival. Various detection methods exist for DTCs, with immunocytochemical staining for cytokeratin (CK-ICC) being the most common and standardized. However, the clinical significance of detecting DTCs in the BM of colorectal cancer patients remains a subject of ongoing discussion.
Tumor cells must first detach from their primary microenvironment to become disseminated tumor cells (DTCs). The tumor microenvironment, particularly its stromal component, is increasingly recognized for its crucial role in tumorigenesis. This stroma supports the survival and growth of neoplastic cells, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and facilitating both local and distant spread. The composition and quantity of stroma vary significantly between tumors. Consequently, the tumor stroma has become a focus in clinical oncology, offering insights into patient prognosis and potential therapeutic responses. Specifically, the tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) has emerged in colon cancer research as a significant predictor of both disease-free and overall survival.
This study investigated the clinical significance of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow (BM) of primary colorectal cancer (CRC) patients using cytokeratin immunocytochemistry (CK-ICC). We compared CK-ICC findings with tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) data and explored potential relationships between TSR and DTCs.
Between May 2001 and November 2007, 125 consecutive patients with primary colorectal cancer (CRC) staged I-III were enrolled. These patients were scheduled for curative resection at participating hospitals. Individuals who died within two months post-surgery were excluded. All participants underwent routine post-operative assessments including clinical examination, liver ultrasonography, and CEA testing. A subset of 42 patients with stage III or IV disease received systemic adjuvant chemotherapy. Patient follow-up extended until June 2014. The study received approval from local Medical Ethical Committees, and all patients provided informed written consent.
Prior to surgery, 5–10 ml of bone marrow (BM) was collected from both anterior iliac crests under general anesthesia. To avoid skin contamination, an incision was made before needle insertion. Mononuclear cells were then isolated from the BM using ficoll gradient centrifugation and prepared as cytospin-slides for immunocytochemistry.
Cytospin slides were prepared and stained. Primary antibodies targeting cytokeratins 8, 18, and 19 were used, alongside isotype antibodies as a negative control. A previously published protocol for cytokeratin immunocytochemical (CK-ICC) staining was followed, which produces a red precipitate in the cytoplasm of positive cells. Hematoxylin was used for counterstaining to visualize cell nuclei. Automated microscopy (ARIOL SL-50) facilitated slide analysis. Each patient had one cytokeratin-stained slide and one negative control slide examined. Further details of this methodology have been previously documented.
Cytokeratin-positive cells, identified through the combination of CK-ICC and automated microscopy, underwent confirmation by an independent pathologist. These cells were then classified into categories such as tumor cells, candidate tumor cells, apoptotic cells, or hematopoietic cells, adhering to morphological criteria established by the European ISHAGE Working Group. Cells not fully meeting the criteria for a positive tumor cell, yet not definitively normal, were labeled as candidate or apoptotic tumor cells. A patient was deemed positive for disseminated tumor cells if even a single tumor cell, candidate tumor cell, or apoptotic cell was detected.
To validate the CK-ICC technique, bone marrow from 20 breast cancer patients and 29 individuals who underwent surgery for benign conditions without malignancy evidence was analyzed.
The tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) was determined through routine microscopic analysis of 5 μm H&E stained sections of the primary tumor, as previously described. Sections were chosen from the most invasive regions, typically those used for T-stage determination, and examined conventionally. If pathology information was unavailable, all tumor slides were gathered for analysis. To assess TSR, areas with abundant stroma were initially identified using 2.5× or 5× magnification. Subsequently, using a 10× objective, regions containing both tumor and stromal tissue were selected for the final TSR scoring. It was crucial that tumor cells were visible at all edges of the selected image fields. Two observers, GvP and WM, independently estimated the TSR without knowledge of patient outcomes. A third pathologist resolved any disagreements or inconclusive scores. TSR was quantified in increments of 10% (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30%) per image field. Due to the influence of preoperative radiotherapy on intra-tumor stroma in rectal cancer patients, TSR analysis was exclusively performed on colon cancer tissues.
Statistical analysis involved describing frequencies using mean ± standard deviation or median (range) for non-normally distributed data. Patients with synchronous metastases, identified during surgery or within three months, were excluded from disease-free survival calculations. Recurrence rates between patients with positive and negative CK-ICC scores were compared using Cox regression, adjusted for sex and age. Overall and disease-free survival were assessed using Cox regression, yielding hazard ratios (HRs). Overall survival was measured from primary tumor surgery until death or the last follow-up. Disease-free survival was calculated from surgery until recurrence, death, or the last follow-up. Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrated the associations between bone marrow (BM) or tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) status and survival. A TSR of >50% stroma was defined as stroma-high, and ≤50% as stroma-low. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows (version 23.0), with a significance level set at p < 0.05.
A total of 125 patients, comprising 65 females and 60 males, constituted the study population. Bone marrow aspiration was performed without any reported complications. The median follow-up period after the primary tumor diagnosis extended to 6.5 years, with a range of 0 to 12 years. During this follow-up, 38 of the 125 patients experienced disease recurrence. These recurrences manifested at single sites, including the liver (20 patients), lung (5 patients), peritoneum/lymph node (3 patients), and bone (1 patient), or at multiple sites in 9 patients. Overall survival (OS) did not differ significantly between patients with negative and positive CK-ICC bone marrow (BM) results, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.97 (95% CI 0.45–2.09, p = 0.93). The 5-year survival rates for these groups were 68% and 72%, respectively. Similarly, disease-free survival (DFS) showed no significant difference between BM-positive and BM-negative patients (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.35–1.82, p = 0.59). For patients with lymph node-negative colorectal cancer (stages I-II), the presence or absence of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow (BM) did not significantly impact overall survival (OS) or disease-free survival (DFS). Hazard ratios for OS and DFS were 0.85 and 1.83, respectively, with p-values of 0.80 and 0.25. This lack of difference extended to the subgroup of elderly patients over 70 years old, where BM status also showed no significant association with OS or DFS. Even after adjusting for factors such as sex, age, tumor location, and chemotherapy, these survival analyses remained unchanged. These findings suggest that for these specific patient groups, BM DTC detection, as measured by CK-ICC, did not serve as a prognostic indicator.
Histological analysis for the tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) was performed on 97 patients, as material from 5 patients was unavailable and 6 had poor quality slides. Of these, 57 (59%) were categorized as stroma-low and 40 (41%) as stroma-high. Initially, univariate analysis indicated a trend towards poorer overall survival (OS) in patients with a high stroma percentage, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.84 (95% CI 0.89–3.82, p=0.10). The 5-year survival rates were 84% for stroma-low and 62% for stroma-high patients. However, after adjusting for sex, age, and chemotherapy, TSR emerged as a significant prognostic factor for worse OS in patients with a high TSR (HR 2.16, 95% CI 1.02–4.57, p=0.04). No significant difference was observed in disease-free survival (DFS) between the stroma-low and stroma-high groups, with 5-year DFS rates of 76% and 71%, respectively.
Disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) were frequently identified in the bone marrow (BM) of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, even in early stages. However, these DTCs, detected via CK-ICC, did not significantly influence the prognosis of patients who had undergone primary tumor resection. Our study did not support the notion that a substantial stroma in the primary tumor correlates with the presence of DTCs. Conversely, we observed that a high stroma percentage within the primary tumor was linked to poorer overall survival in CRC patients.
Previous research on the prognostic significance of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in primary colorectal cancer (CRC) has yielded inconsistent findings. Studies have reported varying percentages of DTCs in bone marrow (BM), ranging from 24% to 64%. These discrepancies may be attributed to the different methodologies employed, particularly in cytokeratin staining. For instance, Flatmark et al. observed minimal overlap between two distinct detection techniques (immunomagnetic selection and CK-ICC). Despite this, both methods in their study were associated with disease outcomes in specific CRC prognostic subgroups. Ultimately, their research suggested that the presence of DTCs in BM could serve as a prognostic marker for CRC patients undergoing curative resection.
Research findings on the prognostic significance of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow (BM) for colorectal cancer (CRC) patients have been inconsistent. While some studies have linked the presence of DTCs to reduced disease-free survival, others have found no prognostic value for DTCs in the BM. For instance, Steinert et al. observed a consistent number of cytokeratin-immunocytochemistry (CK-ICC) positive cells across all TNM stages of CRC. Similarly, Lindemann et al., despite using immunocytochemistry for BM analysis, did not find a significant difference between lymph node-positive and negative patients, although they did suggest BM tumor cells could independently predict relapse. Leinung et al. noted a higher proportion of CK-ICC positive cells in the BM of rectal cancer patients compared to colon cancer patients. A comprehensive meta-analysis by Rahbari et al. in 2010, which included many of these studies, indicated that while circulating tumor cells in peripheral blood had a significant prognostic effect, DTCs in the BM did not, aligning with the current study's conclusions.
Research on breast cancer has demonstrated that the presence of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow (BM) can predict the future development of distant metastases and serves as an independent prognostic factor for survival. This contrasts with colorectal cancer (CRC), where bone is a less common site for metastasis, affecting fewer than half of patients during their disease course. In this study, three out of 125 CRC patients developed skeletal metastases, and two of these were BM-positive. However, the detection of DTCs in 18% of CRC patients' BM did not appear to correlate with the incidence of skeletal metastases. Our findings in CRC are different from those in breast cancer. For instance, in our control group of 20 breast cancer patients, DTCs were found in the BM of 45% of cases, aligning with existing literature reporting DTC occurrences in 13% to 50% of early-stage breast cancer patients, and up to 70% in those with metastatic disease. Notably, we identified CK-ICC positive DTCs in the BM of 2 out of 29 non-carcinoma control cases. This observation is not unique, as CK-positive cells in BM have been reported in up to 5.5% of individuals without known malignancy, depending on the antibody used. This raises the question of whether all disseminated epithelial cells found in BM are genuinely malignant. While early characterization of these cells has been challenging, a malignant phenotype has been suggested. Therefore, it is plausible that these cells are indeed tumor cells, but they may not all possess the capacity for further metastasis. Furthermore, recent experimental models involving colon cancer cells in mice have indicated that disseminated epithelial-like cells might comprise two distinct populations: one originating from the cancer (cytokeratin-positive and Y chromosome-positive) and another derived from resident bone marrow cells.
The clinical utility of detecting disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) at a molecular level is limited by inherent tumor cell diversity and methodological challenges. Single-cell genomic studies reveal that DTCs identified by cytokeratin antibodies often display varied tumor-specific genetic alterations, especially in patients without evident metastases. While tumors are known to enter prolonged dormant phases, the processes that reactivate these dormant cells and trigger metastasis remain poorly understood. Factors influencing DTCs and their microenvironment can disrupt this dormant state. In colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, DTCs residing in the bone marrow (BM) might be maintained in dormancy due to an unfavorable environment. Further comprehensive genomic analysis of DTCs holds promise for elucidating their clinically significant biology.
Historically, tumor stroma formation, or desmoplasia, was viewed as a passive element in cancer development and progression. However, recent decades have seen a significant shift in focus towards the tumor microenvironment, with a growing understanding that the tumor stroma actively contributes to cancer initiation and advancement. This stroma engages with both malignant and nonmalignant cells throughout tumorigenesis, from initial development through invasion and metastasis. The tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) stands out as a valuable characteristic due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness. Its prognostic significance has been consistently demonstrated not only in colon cancer but also in various other solid tumors. To further integrate TSR into the TNM classification system, additional research utilizing larger prospective cohorts is warranted.
In this study, disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) were frequently found in the bone marrow (BM) of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. However, their presence, as determined by CK-ICC, did not correlate with a poorer clinical outcome. Future research focusing on more detailed molecular and functional characterization of BM DTCs could clarify their prognostic significance. Meanwhile, pooling data from multiple institutions studying BM DTCs in CRC patients might offer more robust insights. Conversely, the tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) was found to be linked to a worse overall survival in colon cancer patients. Given its ease of use and accessibility in standard pathology, TSR holds potential as a novel prognostic histological biomarker, pending confirmation of its value in larger prospective studies.
