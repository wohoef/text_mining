Circadian rhythms, endogenous biological oscillations with approximately 24-hour periods, govern numerous molecular processes in mammals. The disruption of these rhythms is linked to various health issues, including diabetes, mental health conditions, and cancer. While the fundamental circadian genes are well-understood, emerging research suggests the existence of novel peripheral regulators. This study aimed to conduct a comprehensive computational analysis to identify these potential novel circadian transcriptional regulators. Utilizing a Boolean function network (BFN) method, we analyzed publicly available microarray time-course datasets from mouse and rat livers. The BFN approach allowed us to infer direct pairwise gene relationships. These inferred relationships were subsequently examined using functional annotation tools. This integrated methodology yielded a list of transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors that were significantly associated with enriched circadian gene ontology (GO) categories. Our analysis identified 93 transcriptional circadian regulators in mouse and 95 in rat. A significant portion of these, 19 in mice and 21 in rats, were previously known, while the remainder represented novel discoveries. To validate these novel circadian TFs, we cross-referenced them with bioinformatics databases, large-scale circadian studies, and smaller-scale investigations. Further support came from ChIP-Seq experiment predictions, which indicated that 40 of our candidate regulators directly target circadian genes. Additionally, we annotated these candidates with disorders frequently associated with circadian rhythm disruption in existing literature. In conclusion, our computational findings, bolstered by extensive literature-based verification, offer valuable contributions to translational research, bridging endocrinology and cancer studies, and paving the way for future investigations into circadian regulation.

Circadian and diurnal rhythms, characterized by 24- or 12-hour oscillations, govern a multitude of mammalian processes, including the transcription of specific genes. While these endogenous rhythms are primarily orchestrated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus, they remain susceptible to external cues like light, temperature, and glucose levels. The specific genes involved, along with their oscillation amplitudes and phases, exhibit tissue-specific variations. Notably, the liver displays the highest proportion of rhythmically expressed genes, underscoring a profound connection between metabolism and the sleep-wake cycle. Disruptions to these circadian rhythms have been linked to various health issues. For instance, imbalances in circadian timing among shift workers have been associated with diabetes mellitus. Severe circadian dysregulation has also been implicated in schizophrenia, and night shift work has been linked to an increased risk of breast cancer. These observations highlight the critical need for deeper exploration into the molecular underpinnings of the circadian clock. Extensive research has been dedicated to identifying key circadian genes and their interrelationships. Significant contributions have come from various high-throughput studies. Notable among these are microarray transcriptome data from Almon et al. on rat liver and skeletal muscle, Atwood et al. on mouse liver, Bailey et al. on the rat pineal gland, and Zhang et al. examining 12 mouse organs. More recently, Vollmers et al. employed deep sequencing to analyze the mouse liver transcriptome and epigenome, identifying circadian oscillating genes. Yoshitane et al. utilized high-throughput ChIP-Seq data to map Clock-controlled E-boxes across the genome. In a recent integrative study, Wang et al. combined multiple experimental techniques on mouse liver transcriptome and proteome to compile a list of transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors involved in circadian regulation. Westermark and Herzel also integrated mouse liver expression data with TF binding information to identify TFs exhibiting 12-hour oscillations. Biomarkers for human circadian phase were proposed by Laing et al. based on blood transcriptome analysis. Furthermore, Korenčič et al. revealed a surprisingly limited overlap in Clock-controlled genes between the mouse liver and adrenal gland. Relógio et al. identified circadian oscillators associated with circadian clock deregulation in skin and colorectal cancer. These existing high-throughput studies served as valuable references for validating the results generated in this work. Our approach employed the Boolean function network (BFN) method, applied to publicly available transcriptome datasets from mouse and rat livers. The BFN is a two-step methodology that integrates hidden Markov models, likelihood ratio tests, and Boolean functions to infer direct pairwise gene relationships from time-course expression data. Our prior successful application of BFN to yeast time-course data demonstrated its advantages in identifying direct pairwise regulatory relationships, including higher accuracy in gene regulatory network reconstruction, differentiation of direct from indirect interactions, computational efficiency on large datasets, and the assignment of direction, Boolean function, and time delay to regulatory links. The process of BFN inference is visually depicted in Figure 1.
Publicly available datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus were utilized. For rat liver, dataset GSE8988 provided 24-hour expression data across 18 time points with three replicates and 15,923 probes. Mouse liver gene expression data was sourced from GSE11923, encompassing 48 hours, 48 time points, 45,101 probes, and pooled replicates.
Prior to analysis, gene expression data underwent a four-step preprocessing pipeline. This involved removing probes lacking gene IDs, filtering out low-variance and low-value probes, identifying transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors as potential regulators, and finally, applying empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) discretization.
Affymetrix probes were mapped to standard gene names using the DAVID conversion tool. This process removed 1760 probes from the rat dataset and 5697 from the mouse dataset.
To refine the gene expression data, the rat liver dataset underwent an analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a 0.05 significance level, resulting in 2164 genes. For the mouse liver dataset, a two-step filtering process was employed: the bottom 25% of probes with the lowest absolute values were removed, followed by the removal of the bottom 25% with the lowest variance, leaving 25,252 genes. These filtering criteria, specifically the ANOVA significance level for rats and quartiles for mice, were selected to ensure that established core circadian genes were retained. The presence of probes for known circadian genes, including Clock, Arntl, Per1, Per2, Per3, Cry1, Cry2, Nr1d1, Nr1d2, and Rora, was confirmed in the processed datasets.
For preprocessing, transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors were sourced from AnimalTFDB 2.0. This yielded 1223 Ensemble IDs for rats and 1882 for mice. These corresponded to 183 rat probes and 2374 mouse probes within the filtered datasets.
Biological replicates in the rat dataset were averaged before discretization. This process transformed gene expression values into continuous values between 0 and 1, a prerequisite for input into Test 1 and Test 2.
Following data preprocessing, we implemented the Boolean Function Network (BFN) procedures. These procedures were designed to first identify direct pairwise relationships between genes (Test 1) and subsequently to distinguish these direct links from indirect ones (Test 2). Both tests operated by comparing two competing models. In Test 1, the models assessed whether genes were linked or not linked, while in Test 2, they evaluated whether the links were direct or indirect. The underlying assumption for these models was that the true, unobserved gene expressions at any given time point—unaffected by other genes or measurement errors—were unknown. These theoretical expressions are represented as x1 and x2 in Figure 1 for Test 1. However, we observed their realized values, y1 and y2, which were the discretized expression values. For each test, we computed the likelihoods of these concurrent models across all available time points. By inferring Boolean functions and time delays that maximized the difference in likelihoods, we could then determine, based on likelihood ratios, whether gene pairs were connected (Test 1) or whether their connections were direct or indirect (Test 2). More comprehensive details regarding the BFN procedures are available in our prior publication. The outcomes of both tests were contingent upon three specific parameters: the significance level for Test 1, the significance level for Test 2, and the maximum permissible time delay between genes. For the rat dataset, we configured the BFN with a time delay range of 1 to 5, setting the significance level for Test 1 (p1) to 0.05 and for Test 2 (p2) to 0.05. For the mouse dataset, the parameters were adjusted to a time delay range of 1 to 8, with p1 set to 0.0001 and p2 to 0.05. The selection of maximum allowed time delays was guided by the total number of time points within each dataset. Limiting the time delay range helps to prevent computationally intensive analyses, as the probability of a relationship between genes generally diminishes with increasing time delays, due to fewer data points available for comparison. The thresholds for the p-values in Test 1 were chosen strategically to manage the number of gene links retained for subsequent functional analysis. For instance, a p1 threshold of 0.0001 for the mouse dataset yielded 276,767 links, whereas a stricter threshold of p1 = 0.00005 reduced this to only 2,944 links. This illustrates that overly stringent thresholds could result in the omission of many potentially significant relationships.
The Boolean function network (BFN) method generated gene pairs with identified regulatory relationships, including specific Boolean functions and time delays. Each transcription factor (TF) or cofactor was associated with a set of target genes exhibiting these shared regulatory attributes, which were then grouped together. These aggregated gene sets were subsequently analyzed using the DAVID functional annotation tool. TFs whose associated target gene groups showed significantly enriched circadian gene ontology (GO) categories were designated as candidate circadian TFs. These candidates were then ranked according to their literature and database support. Ultimately, this analysis of 557 gene groups from rat and 531 from mouse datasets led to the identification of 158 rat and 159 mouse gene sets with significant "circadian rhythm" annotations. These gene sets correspond to 93 unique TFs in mice and 95 in rats.
The Boolean Function Network (BFN) source code, test results, and functional annotation data for both mouse and rat datasets are available in our GitHub repository: https://github.com/BooleanFunctionNetwork/CIRCADIAN.

This process allowed us to compile a list of mammalian circadian regulators, ordered by their known biological significance. Table 1's columns 2 through 4 detail whether our identified candidate circadian transcription factors (TFs) are annotated in specific databases. These include the Rat Genome Database (RGD), which provides information for both rat and mouse species (42), the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) database for mouse-specific data (43), and the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, which covers human, mouse, and rat (44). Because RGD, MGI, and NCBI databases draw from multiple information sources, the specific origin of their evidence is noted in column 7. Further validation of our candidate TFs is presented in columns 8 and 9, utilizing the Circadian Gene Database (CGDB) (45). This database compiles both potentially oscillating and experimentally confirmed oscillatory transcripts across various species, identified through methods like real-time polymerase chain reaction, Northern blot, and in situ hybridization. Our analysis focused exclusively on experimentally validated transcripts, noting the species in which they exhibited oscillation. Columns 10 and 11 present validation data from the Circadian Expression Profiles Database (CircaDB) (46), which catalogs circadian transcriptional rhythms from 13 human and 12 mouse organ systems. A comparison of annotation discriminative power revealed that RGD, MGI, and NCBI provided more specific circadian gene annotations than CGDB or CircaDB. Specifically, only 23.6% of our candidate TFs received circadian annotation from RGD, MGI, or NCBI. In contrast, CGDB confirmed 93.3% of our candidates, including orthologs (i.e., rat-derived candidates experimentally validated in mice). Similarly, CircaDB Human and CircaDB Mouse validated 58.8% and 80% of our findings, respectively. This difference in validation breadth was the basis for prioritizing TFs confirmed by MGI, RGD, or NCBI at the top of our table. Column 12 of Table 1 quantifies the overlap between our candidate circadian regulators and previously published high-throughput circadian or diurnal studies, as referenced in the introduction (1, 5–15). Detailed results of the intersection between our candidate list and the regulators from each of these 12 prior studies are available in Supplementary Table 6 for mouse and Table 7 for rat. Furthermore, Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 provide the original lists of circadian genes from each study for mouse and rat, respectively. These lists were adjusted to focus on TFs, using information from AnimalTFDB 2.0, as most studies (excluding those by Wang et al. and Westermark and Herzel) encompassed all rhythmic or circadian-controlled genes, not solely TFs. Column 13 indicates the dataset from which each result was derived, with TFs identified in both mouse and rat datasets highlighted in bold. Finally, column 14 cites small-scale studies that elucidate the specific mechanisms by which each gene participates in circadian regulation, where such information is available.
Figure 2 summarizes our findings on candidate circadian TFs and cofactors, categorized into seven groups. The top group includes 31 genes confirmed as circadian across all databases, with 11 identified in mice, 12 in rats, and nine common to both. This study specifically highlights novel circadian TFs.
Nine genes identified in Group 2 were annotated as circadian by the NCBI for at least one species: human, mouse, or rat. These genes are presented at the forefront of our novel candidate circadian regulators and are detailed in Table 1. While these genes are highly likely to be circadian, their annotations showed discrepancies across different databases. The Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) and Rat Genome Database (RGD) do not classify these genes as circadian, as they only provide relevant Gene Ontology (GO) categories. In contrast, the NCBI incorporates annotations from pathway databases. Beyond the NCBI, all nine genes from Group 2 received validation from the CircaDB and Circadian Gene Database (CGDB) databases, at least when considering orthologs. However, some transcription factors (TFs) within this group exhibited stronger support from existing literature. For example, Kruppel-like factor 15 (Klf15) was not only identified in high-throughput studies by Almon et al. and Yoshitane et al., but also plays a role in the circadian regulation of bile acid synthesis in mice and transcriptomic oscillations in the mouse heart, according to our literature review. Another gene identified in both rat and mouse datasets was the RNA-binding protein with multiple splicing (Rbpms). This protein is specifically expressed in retinal ganglion cells, which are known to innervate the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and mediate circadian responses across various species, including rats. Similarly, BTG antiproliferation factor 1 (Btg1) has recently been shown to be a component of the circadian immune system in rats experiencing burn trauma. Our references indicate that the sumoylation of BMAL1 by Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier 3 (Sumo3) is a crucial posttranslational modification for the circadian cycle in numerous species, including humans, mice, and rats. Although no direct supporting studies for the mammalian circadian mechanism of Transducin Beta-like 1 X-linked Receptor 1 (Tbl1xr1) were found, it has been implicated in the circadian mechanisms of ectotherms. Furthermore, Wada et al. uncovered a functional interaction between circadian ROR-alpha and the liver X receptor (Nr1h3) in regulating lipid homeostasis in mice. Our findings suggest that Nr1h3 also exhibits circadian activity in rats.
Transcription factors (TFs) were categorized into distinct groups based on validation evidence. Groups 3, 4, and 5 encompassed TFs supported by at least one of the CGDB, CircaDB Human, or CircaDB Mouse databases, with literature scores of 3, 2, and 1, respectively. Group 6 consisted of TFs verified solely by these databases, lacking support from prior high-throughput circadian studies. Group 7 included genes with no validation from either databases or the reference high-throughput studies. This grouping generally indicates the confidence level in a TF's circadian role. Notably, certain TFs within Group 3 demonstrated significant individual study support. These included Estrogen receptor 1 (Esr1), lipopolysaccharide-induced tumor necrosis factor factor (Litaf), Nuclear receptor subfamily 3 Group C member 2 (Nr3c2), TSC22 Domain Family Member 3 (Tsc22d3), Foxo3, Nr2f2, proliferator-activated receptor delta (Ppard), SMAD family member 4 (Smad4), and Catenin Beta 1 (Ctnnb1).
Estrogen Receptor 1 (Esr1) garnered the highest literature score among the identified circadian regulators. Its circadian role has been established across multiple high-throughput studies, including those by Zhang et al., Yoshitane et al., Wang et al., Westermark and Herzel, Laing et al., and Korenčič et al. Specifically, Zhang et al. identified Esr1 as a potential circadian drug target. Yoshitane et al. demonstrated its direct regulation by the CLOCK-BMAL1 complex. Wang et al. recognized Esr1 as a transcription factor (TF) that governs the diurnal rhythm of gene expression in mouse liver. Westermark and Herzel proposed that Esr1, in conjunction with other genes, can contribute to 12-hour oscillations in target genes. Laing et al. noted its rhythmic expression in phase with melatonin during sleep. Korenčič et al. observed Esr1 oscillation in both liver and adrenal glands, albeit with different phases. Furthermore, smaller-scale studies have shown Esr1's modulation of circadian rhythms in adult female mice and its involvement in circadian systemic circulation in male rats. Its role in the circadian regulation of breast cancer proliferation in humans has also been documented. Litaf, another gene with substantial literature support and identified in both rat and mouse datasets, functions as a key mediator of the inflammatory cytokine response to lipopolysaccharides. Litaf is also recognized as a biomarker for sleep deprivation, suggesting a potential link between immunity and circadian activity. Nr3c2 encodes the mineralocorticoid receptor, which plays a role in regulating blood pressure and cardiac function through its influence on sodium transport in renal tissue. Gumz et al. provided evidence for the circadian nature of this process. The gene Tsc22d3, a universal oscillator, is translated into the GILZ protein and has been implicated in various circadian processes across different tissues, including kidney homeostasis, adipose tissue metabolism, and age-related memory formation in the hippocampus. Chaves et al. demonstrated that PI3K-FOXO3 signaling is crucial for hepatic circadian rhythmicity, mediated by Clock gene regulation. Nuclear receptor (NR) Nr2f2, encoding the COUP-TF II protein, exhibits a reduction in Clock-mutant mice, leading to adipose tissue accumulation and an overweight phenotype. Ppard influences the circadian utilization of lipids by muscles and has been observed to be altered in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus. Smad4, a component of the Smad2/3:Smad4 complex, is involved in the interplay between the circadian clock and TGF-β signaling in zebrafish larvae. The gene Ctnnb1 encodes β-catenin, which has potential oncogenic activity in colorectal cancer and is under Clock control. The collective evidence from this and previous high-throughput circadian studies supporting Ctnnb1 warrants further investigation into its circadian regulatory functions. These genes, along with Pnrc1 and Klf13, are included in the top 20 circadian candidates detailed in Table 1. Pnrc1 and Klf13 were selected due to their significant literature scores and their presence in findings from both mouse and rat datasets, even in the absence of detailed small-scale studies elucidating their precise circadian mechanisms.
Genes Mitf, Dnmt3b, Hes6, and Rarb, highlighted in Group 4 of Figure 2, demonstrate circadian associations. Literature reviews link Mitf to melanin synthesis, Dnmt3b to liver DNA methylation regulated by feeding, Hes6 to cholesterol homeostasis, and Rarb to vitamin A-influenced hippocampal rhythms.
Genes Elk3, Gtf2ird1, Mef2a, and Ppargc1b from Group 5 were highlighted due to evidence of circadian activity in small-scale studies. These studies linked circadian rhythms to psychosis and schizophrenia (Elk3), decreased locomotor activity and Williams syndrome (Gtf2ird1), Leydig cell homeostasis (Mef2a), and mitochondrial energy metabolism (Ppargc1b). Group 6 included Nlk, Nfkbia, and Pdlim1, associated with circadian processes such as phosphorylating core circadian genes in drosophila, inflammatory response to weight loss, and cardiac hypertrophy, respectively.
Group 7 genes lacked support from databases and reference studies. However, Dlx5 warrants attention. A genome-wide association study linked it to self-reported "morningness," and altered DNA methylation in shift workers has been associated with increased cancer risk.
Across the identified groups, certain categories of transcription factors (TFs) and TF cofactors appeared frequently in our findings. The KLF family of TFs, including Klf1, Klf9, Klf10, Klf13, Klf15, and Klf16, was notably overrepresented. Among these, Klf10, Klf9, and Klf15 are already known to play established roles in mammalian circadian regulation. While Klf1, Klf13, and Klf16 have been less extensively studied, they exhibit potential in circadian processes due to the presence of E-boxes in their promoter regions. These E-boxes serve as binding sites for the BMAL1-CLOCK circadian complex. Another significant group of TFs identified was the Nuclear Receptors (NRs), encompassing Nr1d1, Nr1d2, Nr2f6, Nrip1, Nr2f2, Nr3c2, Nr1i2, Nr1h3, and Nr4a2. These are hormone-sensitive transcriptional regulators involved in diverse biological functions such as development, energy metabolism, reproduction, inflammation, and tissue homeostasis. Existing research increasingly demonstrates that NRs are influenced by clock genes and, in turn, modulate circadian activity. Our study identified several novel circadian regulators within the NR family, specifically Nr3c2, Nr2f2, Nr1i2, Nr1h3, and Nr4a2. Furthermore, our results included numerous transcriptional regulators and co-regulators associated with metabolism, such as Klf15, Nr1h3, Ppard, Nr2f2, Tsc22d3, Pparg1b, and Hes6. These may function as crucial links connecting metabolic signals from peripheral oscillators to the central molecular clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). It is also noteworthy that several TFs were identified in both the mouse and rat datasets. While some of these overlapping TFs, including Pnrc1, Arl2bp, Irf6, Eno1, Pdlim1, Nfkbia, Tdp2, Carhsp1, and Sfmbt1, do not have a clearly defined role in circadian processes, their rhythmic nature has been confirmed by the CGDB and CircaDB databases, as well as by high-throughput circadian studies. Consequently, these warrant further in-depth investigation.
To investigate direct regulatory relationships, we utilized ChIP-Seq data, a technique that identifies potential binding sites for genes. Numerous databases compile ChIP-Seq information, including ENCODE, ChIPBase, GTRD, and ChIP-Atlas. We selected ChIP-Atlas for our study due to its extensive collection of over 96,000 experiments and a broad range of transcription factors (TFs), encompassing more than 700 human and 500 mouse TFs. Our analysis involved searching the comprehensive list of 165 candidate circadian regulators, identified via BFN analysis of microarray data, for potential binding targets. A target gene was considered valid if the peak-call intervals of a protein overlapped with a transcription start site within a 5 kb range. Detailed information on the circadian targets for specific TFs is available in Supplementary Table 10. The results of our ChIP-Atlas search are summarized in Table 2. This table categorizes TFs by group number, based on prior literature evidence. Gene names presented in capital letters in the second column signify that their circadian targets were identified exclusively in humans. Otherwise, targets were found in either mouse or both mouse and human species. Our search was confined to mouse and human data because ChIP-Atlas currently offers limited information for the rat species.
Table 2 identifies 40 transcription factors (TFs) with stronger evidence for direct circadian regulation. However, other candidates were not excluded, as future experimental data may emerge. Yoshitane et al. highlighted the significance of indirect regulators, noting that many rhythmic genes lack CLOCK-binding sites. Consequently, our novel candidates, including Klf13, Ppard, and Litaf, which are directly regulated by Clock, may function as indirect regulators for other genes. Furthermore, TFs such as Nr3c2, Esr1, and Klf15 were found to be both directly controlled by Clock and to possess direct core circadian targets, placing them in both categories.

Certain genes showed overrepresentation in specific disease clusters, with Ppard linked to metabolic disorders and Nr3c2 to cardiovascular diseases. In contrast, Esr1 exhibited associations across nearly all disease categories.
This study presents a systematic methodology for identifying potential circadian transcriptional regulators. Our approach involved applying the Boolean Function Network (BFN) method to transcriptome time-course datasets from mouse and rat livers. This allowed us to infer direct pairwise gene relationships, including their associated Boolean functions and time delays. To mitigate the identification of false positive links, we incorporated existing knowledge about transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors in these species. The BFN algorithm yielded target genes for each transcriptional regulator, linked by optimal Boolean functions and time delays. These target genes were then grouped based on these attributes, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed. A TF was classified as a candidate circadian regulator if its associated target gene groups exhibited significant GO annotations related to circadian processes. Our analysis identified 93 transcriptional circadian regulators in mice and 95 in rats, with 23 regulators common to both datasets. While our computational approach differed from conventional analyses of gene expression rhythmicity, the results aligned with previous research, bolstering confidence in the discovered candidate regulators. A key advantage of the BFN method over correlation or mutual information-based gene regulatory network reverse engineering techniques is its ability to assign Boolean functions and time delays to each relationship. This facilitated the definition of more precise groups of related target genes, providing a stronger foundation for biological investigation through functional annotation. Further strengthening our findings, we conducted extensive validation using 12 prior circadian studies, three integrative bioinformatics databases, two circadian databases, and relevant small-scale studies. Additionally, integration with available ChIP-Seq data revealed that 40 of our identified circadian TF candidates possess potential binding sites near core circadian genes. A limitation of this research is its focus solely on transcriptional regulators, excluding proteomic-level interactions. Future studies could benefit from analyzing additional time-course datasets from diverse species and tissues. Nevertheless, we have highlighted several potent candidate circadian regulators and co-regulators with potential applications in molecular medicine.
