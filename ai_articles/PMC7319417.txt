Input-output analysis (IOA) is a cornerstone of industrial ecology, yet the literature on environmental extensions (EEs) – specifically, the scope and sectoral allocation of physical flows within monetary input-output tables (MIOTs) – is fragmented. This study examines the conceptual and empirical consequences of using two common EE designs, focusing on energy accounting. One design represents energy supply, while the other represents energy use within the economy. Both energy extensions were derived from Austria's official energy supply-use data and applied to the same single-region input-output (SRIO) model. This approach isolates the impact of the EE design choice. The SRIO findings were further validated by comparing them with energy footprints from the global multi-regional input-output (GMRIO) dataset, EXIOBASE. The research demonstrates that the ranking of final demand categories, such as household consumption and exports, is significantly influenced by the EE design. Furthermore, product-level results can exhibit variations spanning several orders of magnitude. The GMRIO comparison also indicated that for certain countries, like Australia and Norway, the supply-extension footprint can be double the size of the use-extension footprint. To enhance transparency, a graph-based approach is proposed for a generalized framework to describe EE designs. Conceptual differences between the two designs are explored using analogies to hybrid life-cycle assessment. The study concludes that these findings are critical for monitoring energy efficiency and emission reduction targets, as well as for corporate footprint accounting.

Industrial ecology examines the biophysical foundations of human societies, focusing on how socioeconomic systems manage natural resources from extraction to disposal to sustain their structures. A key concern for industrial ecologists is comprehending the drivers behind these processes, such as household consumption and international trade. Input-output analysis (IOA) serves as a valuable framework for this research. Environmentally extended monetary IOA (EE-MIOT) is now widely employed to compute various footprint indicators, including consumption-based and income-based measures.
The construction specifics of input-output (IO) tables are a primary driver of variations in their results. These tables fall into three main categories: environmentally extended monetary IO tables (EE-MIOTs), hybrid IO tables (HIOTs) that combine monetary and physical units, and purely physical IO tables. Each type represents different analytical depths, incorporating either physical flows, monetary flows, or both, and consequently makes distinct assumptions regarding prices and system boundaries. Further diversification arises from geographical resolution and sector aggregation levels. Additionally, the method of constructing tables from supply-use tables (SUTs) introduces variations through different technology assumptions. Different IO models, such as supply-driven and demand-driven variants, can be derived from a single IO table by selecting various exogenous drivers, with further distinctions in how capital formation is treated. Consequently, the comparison of results and the exploration of conceptual similarities and differences remain a persistent topic in the field.
This research centers on Environmentally Extended Monetary Input-Output tables (EE-MIOTs), specifically addressing a less-examined aspect in current Input-Output (IO) model comparisons: the design of the environmental extension (EE). An EE-MIOT is a composite dataset comprising a Monetary Input-Output Table (MIOT), which details monetary inter-industry transactions and final demand, and a supplementary table in physical units that outlines environmental flows linked to economic activities. These extension tables are designed to account for flows essential for economic activities but not directly represented in the MIOT. They capture flows that the "technosphere" cannot supply within a given timeframe. This concept is akin to factors of production, representing inputs necessary for production that are not themselves produced by businesses. Environmentally, this primarily involves boundary flows entering, such as natural resources, or leaving, like pollutant dilution, the economy. Although frameworks like the System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) aim for consistent integration of environmental and economic data, practical data matching is often imperfect. Consequently, IO practitioners must make assumptions regarding the relationship between the physical flows in the EE and the monetary flows in the MIOT. As this paper demonstrates with EE-MIOTs, merging monetary and physical accounts is a complex undertaking, with significant variations in the underlying reasoning.
Environmentally extended monetary input-output tables (EE-MIOTs) form the foundation for global multi-regional monetary input-output (GMRIO) models. These models are crucial for analyzing environmental footprints, particularly those associated with international trade. Research comparing GMRIO models consistently identifies differences in environmental extensions (EEs) as the primary driver of variations in national footprints, such as carbon footprints. These discrepancies in extensions can be attributed to two main factors: the source data utilized and variations in the definition and scope of included flows. Different databases employ distinct source data, for instance, energy or greenhouse gas emission statistics. Furthermore, the scope of flows incorporated into extensions can differ. Studies have demonstrated that carbon footprint results can vary considerably depending on whether extensions are constructed using territorial or residence-based accounting principles. This is largely due to differing allocations of emissions from transport activities. Additionally, the exclusion of bunker fuels, used in international shipping and aviation, due to data handling challenges, can further impact results. Even when bunker fuels are accounted for, their allocation to the MIOT remains complex and requires estimation methods, hindering a complete understanding. Consequently, the varied scope and definitions within extension designs necessitate greater transparency in communicating the included flows and their allocation to the MIOT, as this directly influences the interpretation of findings.
Research explicitly comparing different extension designs is predominantly found within energy input-output analysis (IOA). Two primary energy extension types are discussed: one focusing on energy extraction and the other on energy use. The fundamental distinction between these approaches lies in how energy flows are detailed across the energy conversion chain. Specifically, they differ in attributing energy flows, including transformation and transportation losses, to either industries or final demands within the monetary input-output table (MIOT). Consequently, these two designs offer distinct perspectives on how energy inputs, or direct energy flows, are distributed throughout the socioeconomic system.
While Costanza and Herendeen (1984) were the first to utilize both energy extension designs in a single study, their comparison was not the primary objective. More recently, the energy extraction design has seen frequent application (Chen & Wu, 2017; Wu & Chen, 2017; Zhang et al., 2016), as has the energy use design (Akizu-Gardoki et al., 2018; Lenzen, 1998; Wachsmann et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2014). Driven by a resurgence in consumption-based energy accounting, Owen et al. (2017) conducted the initial comparative analysis of energy footprints using two distinct energy extensions within a GMRIO model. Their findings suggest that both designs are valuable and should be employed for different research inquiries.
This study has two primary objectives. Firstly, we aim to re-evaluate energy footprints using two distinct environmental extensions (EEs) within a single-region input-output (SRIO) framework: one representing the economy's energy supply and the other its energy use. This national-level analysis serves as a complement to the global multi-regional input-output (GMRIO) assessment previously conducted by Owen et al. (2017). Furthermore, we extend their work by thoroughly examining the implications of these different EEs for energy footprints at the product level. A key advantage of SRIO models is their direct derivation from official statistics, ensuring full compatibility with national accounts. While GMRIO databases also utilize national accounts, they often undergo significant modifications during compilation due to balancing algorithms needed to reconcile conflicting data. To isolate the impact of the EE design and reduce uncertainty, we derived both extensions from Austria's official national energy supply-use account. These extensions were then applied to the same SRIO model for Austria across several years (1999, 2007, and 2014). Austria was selected due to the authors' familiarity with its national IO model and detailed energy data. To provide a broader perspective, we also cross-checked our SRIO findings with a GMRIO model and analyzed regional energy footprints using the EXIOBASE dataset.
Our second objective is to clarify the empirical findings by examining them through the lens of the conceptual distinctions between the two extension designs. We employ analogies from hybrid life-cycle assessments (LCAs) to explore these conceptual implications. Drawing on the work of Pauliuk, Majeau-Bettez, and Müller (2015), we utilize a graph-based method to clearly illustrate the various structures of these extensions, specifically detailing the range of physical flows and how they are allocated to industries and final consumers within the monetary input-output table (MIOT).
This study's empirical comparison primarily utilizes a single-region input-output (SRIO) framework, and we will now detail and illustrate the two extension designs within this context. A key distinction between SRIO and global multi-regional input-output (GMRIO) model extension designs is the explicit inclusion and allocation of physical energy import and export flows in SRIO models, which are not directly represented in GMRIO extensions. This difference necessitates a slightly modified and broader terminology for our analysis. Specifically, what has previously been referred to as the "energy-extracted vector" will now be termed the "supply-extension." To align with the SEEA-Energy framework's nomenclature, "extraction" in material and energy flow accounting will be referred to as "natural inputs." This approach allows for a more generalized understanding of how energy flows are accounted for in different model structures.
The primary distinction between the two environmental extensions (EEs) lies in how they represent energy flow stages within the conversion chain and attribute these flows to industries or final demands in the monetary input-output table (MIOT). The System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) structures physical flows through various tables and accounts, with physical Supply-Use Tables (SUTs) being central. These SUTs meticulously balance the total inputs and outputs for industries and markets. Visualizing energy flows within an Energy Supply-Use Table (ESUT) provides a complete picture of the entire energy conversion chain, encompassing all energy flows relevant to both EE designs. Detailed definitions and system boundaries for SEEA-Energy, along with a thorough explanation of the energy flows illustrated in Figure 1, are available in Supporting Information S2. This same supporting document also offers further insights into the graph approach and a more extensive visualization of the two designs.
The supply-type extension accounts for all energy flows entering the Energy Supply-Use Table (ESUT), as depicted on the left side of Figure a. In contrast, the use-type extension captures all energy flows exiting the ESUT, shown on the right side of Figure a. Considering changes in stocks, both the supply-extension (R + W + IM + S_with) and the use-extension (u_in + y_h + S_add + EX + L + O) will sum to identical totals.
The supply-extension captures energy flows at their initial entry into the economy, marking the beginning of the entire energy conversion process. This means that primary energy sources like crude oil, natural gas, coal, and fuelwood are attributed to their respective extraction industries (mining and forestry), while hydropower is assigned to electricity generation. Similarly, energy derived from waste is allocated to waste management industries. Any subsequent energy transformations, processing into secondary products, and delivery to end-users, such as households, are integrated within the monetary input-output table (MIOT) and follow monetary transactions. Conversely, the use-extension focuses on the final stages of the energy conversion chain. Here, both primary and secondary energy products are assigned to the economic entities that consume them, whether they are production industries or households. This includes energy consumed by energy industries themselves, energy used by other industries for production or non-energy purposes, and energy lost during transformation and transportation. Examples include allocating bunker fuels to transport sectors, gasoline for private use to households, and process energy to manufacturing. At this point, energy is either released into the environment as heat or converted into non-energy products. The use-extension implicitly accounts for the physical aspects of all preceding transformation and distribution processes.
In this study's Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model, imports are integrated directly into the Monetary Input-Output Table (MIOT). This treatment considers imported goods as if they were domestically produced, thus incorporating them into the gross production figures of the monetary IO table. As a result, imports of both primary and secondary energy products are assigned to the relevant production industries within the MIOT. For instance, imported coal is attributed to the mining sector, and motor fuels are assigned to the petroleum product manufacturing industry. Conversely, export flows of energy products are directly categorized under the final demand for exports within the SRIO model.
The Austrian Energy Accounts, the basis for our empirical analysis, detail energy supply by origin (domestic and foreign) and energy use across 88 industries and households. This data spans from 1999 to 2016 and covers 39 energy products. The use table specifically outlines industrial energy and non-energy consumption, losses, stock changes, exports, and household consumption. These accounts adhere to the NACE 2008 industry classification, thereby bridging the System of National Accounts and the International Energy Agency's Energy Balances.
To prevent double counting and align the dataset with the SEEA-Energy framework, intermediate energy use by industries was broken down into final energy consumption by non-energy industries, energy industries' own use (O), transformation output (VS), and transformation losses (L). Statistics Austria supplied a raw dataset detailing these energy flows for this purpose. Since official Energy Accounts do not allocate energy supply, losses, or non-energy use to IO industries, adjustments were necessary, as outlined in Section S6 of Supporting Information S2. Figures 2 and 3 visually represent Austria's energy supply and use for 1999, 2007, and 2014. Figure 2(a) illustrates this energy supply and use across these years, while Figure 2(b) maps the 2014 Energy Accounts within the ESUT structure using Sankey flows. The underlying data for these figures is available in Supporting Information S1, with units expressed in peta joules.
The Austrian Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model was built upon monetary supply-use tables (MSUTs) that distinguished 65 industries and 65 products. To align these MSUTs with the industry classifications of the Energy Accounts (EAs), two aggregation steps were necessary. Initially, real estate activities (code 68.A) and imputed rents (68.B) were combined into a single category within the MSUTs. Subsequently, 15 many-to-one aggregations were performed. These adjustments reduced the MSUT industry classification from 65 to 64 industries, thereby matching the more detailed 88-industry EA classification. A concordance table detailing these aggregations is available in Supporting Information S1.
In this study, matrices are denoted by capital letters, vectors by roman minor letters, and scalars by italic minor letters. The construction of the Input-Output (IO) model adheres to the commodity-by-industry approach, as described by Miller and Blair (2009). We utilized an industry-by-commodity total requirement matrix, represented by L. This matrix is derived from basic Monetary Supply-Use Tables (MSUTs) and is calculated as L = D(I - BD)⁻¹, where D signifies the matrix of commodity output proportions and B represents the commodity-by-industry coefficient matrix. A detailed explanation of this process can be found in Section S3 of Supporting Information S2. Each element, lᵢⱼ, within the matrix L indicates the direct and indirect output from industry i necessary to produce one unit of commodity j for final demand. The IO model is further enhanced by incorporating direct energy coefficients, qᵏ = eᵏ(x)⁻¹, which quantify energy flow k per unit of total industry production (x). Consequently, the general formulation for the supply-extended model is Fᵏᵐ = qᵏLŷᵐ.
In the use-extended model, the matrix element $f_{ij}^{km}$ represents the quantity of energy flow $k$ embedded within intermediate input $i$ of final product $j$, as acquired by final demand category $m$. To incorporate direct energy consumption by final demand sectors such as households, exports, and stock changes, the IO model requires the addition of a direct energy flow vector, denoted as $c$. Consequently, the general formulation for the use-extended model is expressed as $F^{km} = \hat{q^k} L \hat{y^m} + c$.
Vector c, representing energy products, aligns with the IO model's commodity classification and contains non-zero entries only for these specific items.
Input-output analysis (IOA) operates under the assumption of homogeneous prices, meaning products are priced identically regardless of whether they are sold to industries, households, or for export. However, this assumption is not always realistic, particularly for energy, which is known to be sold at varying prices to different users, a point recognized in early energy IO studies. Furthermore, IOA presumes product homogeneity. For the current SRIO model, this implies that imported and domestically produced electricity follow identical supply chains, with no inherent structural distinctions between those serving household demand and export markets.
To validate our Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model findings, we utilized the Global Multi-Regional Input-Output (GMRIO) database, EXIOBASE. EXIOBASE's foundation lies in national input-output tables, which are further disaggregated to highlight environmentally significant sectors. SUTs are converted into symmetric input-output tables through an industry-technology assumption. EXIOBASE version 3 offers a detailed breakdown of 200 products and 163 industries, with a primary focus on the EU. It explicitly models the EU-28 and their 16 major trading partners, accounting for approximately 95% of global GDP. The remaining global regions are consolidated into five "Rest of" categories. In total, version 3 encompasses 49 regions and countries. For this study, we employed the 2014 industry-by-industry input-output table. Further details regarding EXIOBASE's energy accounts can be found in supporting information 2 of Stadler et al. (2018).
This section compares energy footprints derived from both supply-extended and use-extended models. Initially, we examine the findings from Austria's Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model, detailing the energy footprints of final products and then comparing those of different final demand categories. Subsequently, we broaden the scope to compare regional energy footprints using a Global Multi-Regional Input-Output (GMRIO) model, incorporating 43 additional countries and five "rest of the world" regions alongside Austria. All analyses presented focus on the year 2014, the most recent period for which comprehensive data were accessible.
Figure 4 presents the energy footprints for 64 aggregated final products in Austria for 2014, encompassing all final demand categories. Both the supply-extended and use-extended models demonstrate a similar distribution pattern of energy to these products. Refined petroleum products (CPA code 19) and electricity (code 35) emerge as the products with the largest energy footprints in both models, albeit with different magnitudes: 307 PJ and 208 PJ for the supply-extension, versus 466 PJ and 373 PJ for the use-extension, respectively. Following these, construction (38) ranks third in the supply-extended model with 93 PJ, while forestry (2) holds this position in the use-extended model with 80 PJ. Generally, the two input-output models allocate energy to comparable final products, though the quantities vary. A notable exception is mining (code 4), where the use-extended model assigns only 5 PJ, placing it 35th in rank, whereas the supply-extended model allocates a significantly higher 85 PJ, ranking it fourth.
When examining 64 product footprints, 44 were larger with a supply-extension, while 17 were larger with a use-extension. Three footprints remained identical, being zero in both models. Of the 44 larger supply-extension footprints, 30 were for services, primarily those listed in the lower section of Figure 4.
Figure 5 visually represents the relative differences in product energy footprints by plotting the supply-extension footprints on the x-axis against the use-extension footprints on the y-axis. A product-to-group assignment list is available in Supporting Information S1. Among the 64 product footprints analyzed, 15 showed deviations within a 15% range, excluding those that were zero. Of these, 10 were from manufacturing, 4 from services, and 1 from the primary agricultural sector. The analysis reveals a tendency for service sector energy footprints to be larger when using the supply-extension, with 32 service footprints being larger compared to 7 that were smaller. The most significant relative deviations were observed in mining and quarrying (code 4), where the supply-extension footprint (87 PJ) was 16.7 times larger than the use-extension footprint (5 PJ). Other notable deviations include waste treatment (factor of 5.7, 9 PJ vs. 2 PJ), warehousing services (factor of 3.2, 18 PJ vs. 5 PJ), and wood products (factor of 3.1). While refined petroleum products (19) and electricity (35) showed large absolute differences, their relative deviations were smaller, with the use-extension exceeding the supply-extension by factors of 1.5 and 1.8, respectively. Figure 5 further suggests that the increased energy allocation to refined petroleum and electricity under the use-extension corresponds to a relatively lower allocation to services, manufacturing, and other product categories.
Examining energy footprints by final demand categories reveals the closest agreement between the supply-extended and use-extended models for household consumption. The supply-extended model attributes 735.4 PJ to household final demand, while the use-extended model assigns 669.1 PJ, a difference of 66.2 PJ. Relative to a hypothetical average of 702.3 PJ, this represents a divergence of approximately 9.4% for household footprints. This is the smallest variation observed when compared to exports, which show a difference of 161.8 PJ (22.7%), and other final demand categories, with a difference of 98 PJ (34%). Discrepancies in the energy footprint of "other final demand" are primarily driven by variations in gross fixed capital formation and government consumption. Gross fixed capital formation exhibits a footprint of 190.1 PJ under the supply design and 110.3 PJ with the use design. Government consumption shows a footprint of 121.3 PJ for the supply-extended model and 74.7 PJ for the use-extended model. The most substantial absolute difference is seen in the energy footprint of exports, where the supply-extended model accounts for 631.1 PJ and the use-extended model for 792.9 PJ. As illustrated in Figure 5, these significant absolute variations in export footprints are largely influenced by fluctuations in the footprints of petroleum products and electricity. Further detailed analysis of final demand energy footprints, broken down by final products, is available in Section S5 of the Supporting Information.
The significant difference in export footprints alters the ranking of final demand categories. Using a supply-extension, households receive the largest energy allocation (735.4 PJ, 43.1%), followed by exports (630 PJ, 37%), and then other final demand (340.5 PJ, 19.9%). In contrast, the use-extension shifts this order, with exports receiving the most energy (792.9 PJ, 46.5%), followed by households (669.1 PJ, 39.2%), and other final demand (244.8 PJ, 14.3%). This demonstrates that the order of final demand footprints is influenced by the chosen extension design. Further analysis in Supporting Information S2 compares these footprints and their variations across 1999, 2007, and 2014, indicating a consistent ranking over time.
To confirm the findings from the Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model, regional energy footprints were calculated using the Global Multi-Regional Input-Output (GMRIO) model, EXIOBASE. This aggregated footprint represents the direct and indirect energy consumption for a country's total final demand, encompassing households, capital formation, government consumption, and other final demand categories, but excluding exports. A key check is that the sum of all supply-extended and use-extended regional footprints should equal the total global primary energy input from nature, which was 573,509 PJ in 2014. For Austria's total final demand, the supply-extended footprint was approximately 1,339 PJ, while the use-extended footprint was 1,722 PJ, indicating a difference of about 25% when considering a hypothetical mean. The analysis, illustrated in Figure 7, further revealed that 24 out of the 49 regions analyzed showed footprints outside the 15% deviation threshold between the two extension designs.
Significant discrepancies in energy footprints between supply-extended and use-extended models were noted for several regions using EXIOBASE. The United States, RoW Asia and Pacific, and China exhibited the largest absolute variations. On a relative basis, Australia's supply-extended footprint was over 125% larger than its use-extended counterpart, while Norway's supply-extended footprint was approximately 95% larger. These results, detailing the extent of these differences, are fully available in Supporting Information S1.
Energy footprints calculated using single-region input-output (SRIO) models demonstrate sensitivity to the chosen extension design, resulting in altered rankings of final demand categories. To investigate whether these differences persist in a global context, the study crosschecked SRIO findings with energy footprints derived from the global multi-regional input-output (GMRIO) dataset, EXIOBASE. The results confirmed that energy footprints remain sensitive to the extension design, irrespective of whether an SRIO or GMRIO framework is employed. This underscores the significant impact of extension design choices on the outcomes of energy footprint analysis.
At the product level, our SRIO analysis revealed that energy footprints can diverge significantly, varying by several orders of magnitude. Out of 64 product footprints, only 15, primarily in manufacturing, showed deviations below 15%. However, critical energy products like electricity, refined petroleum, and mining exhibited much larger percentage differences. Given the growing use of input-output analysis for policy and climate mitigation strategies, these discrepancies are crucial for prioritizing efforts. Such variations are particularly important for corporate responsibility initiatives and footprint accounting for organizations and products, where environmentally extended input-output models are increasingly employed.
Our analysis revealed a tendency for service sector energy footprints to be larger when using a supply-extension, with 32 services showing higher footprints compared to only 7 with lower footprints. This suggests that the choice of extension design structurally biases the calculation of service sector energy footprints in EE-MIOTs. This observation is particularly significant given the ongoing discussion of service sectors as drivers of green growth, the sharing economy, and a circular low-carbon economy. Consequently, any analysis of the environmental impacts associated with services must carefully consider and critically evaluate the specific extension design employed.
Both supply- and use-extended EE-MIOTs share the same system boundary, adhering to national accounting standards. While the MIOT captures all economically valued flows, both energy extensions track the same energy flow. The divergence arises because monetary and energy conversion chains intersect at different points depending on the perspective, necessitating explicit allocation assumptions.
Conceptually, the supply-extended model aligns with traditional Life Cycle Assessment's economic value-based burden allocation. In this approach, energy supplied to energy industries is distributed to other industries based on their monetary expenditures. This distribution is influenced by Input-Output Analysis's assumptions of product and price homogeneity, as detailed in chapter 3.
The use-extended model's structure aligns with tiered hybrid approaches in Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and Input-Output Analysis (IOA). Tiered hybrid models divide a system into distinct modules. Lower-order energy needs, both direct and upstream, are analyzed in a separate process analysis module. Higher-order requirements, such as those from extraction and processing, are then handled by an input-output table. This tiered approach is mirrored in the use-extension design. First-order energy use, like fuel consumption by households, exports, government, and investment, is directly accounted for in the environmental extension using energy statistics, independent of monetary values or price assumptions. The monetary input-output table then estimates and assigns remaining higher-order energy needs. Within the environmental extension, second-order energy use is allocated to the specific energy-consuming industries. Transformation losses from energy production and the energy industries' own consumption, considered third-order energy use, are then distributed through the input-output table along their respective supply chains to final demand. Consequently, the use-extended model employs two supplementary modules: a data-driven, often physical module from energy statistics for direct final demand energy use, and a monetary IO module for all indirect, higher-order energy requirements, thereby extending the physical module's scope.
Based on our conceptual analysis, energy footprints calculated using supply-extended and use-extended approaches should be considered distinct indicators. The observed discrepancies are not simply errors stemming from input-output analysis's homogeneity assumptions. Rather, these indicators are derived from environmentally extended monetary input-output tables with fundamentally different system structures, making each valid within its own framework. The supply-extended footprint prioritizes the monetary aspect, highlighting the economic forces driving energy supply. It views consumer spending as an incentive for energy production, reflecting how energy industries generate revenue. Conversely, the use-extended design emphasizes the physical dimension of production and consumption. It breaks down energy supply by its diverse economic applications and sector-specific production methods, revealing sector-level technology compositions and the direct energy intensity of various activities. Despite using similar terminology, the results from both approaches offer different types of information.
In the Austrian Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model, the extraction of both energy and non-energy mineral products, including metallic and non-metallic minerals, is consolidated into a single "mining and quarrying" sector. This aggregation is a consequence of Input-Output (IO) modeling's assumption of product homogeneity. Consequently, energy flows attributed to this combined sector are consequently channeled through both energy and non-energy supply chains. Austria's mineral extraction primarily consists of substantial quantities of non-metallic minerals, with smaller amounts of metallic minerals and relatively minor volumes of natural gas and oil. Given this composition, it is inferred that the monetary transactions within the "mining and quarrying" sector in the official Monetary Supply-Use Tables (MSUT) and Monetary Input-Output Tables (MIOT) largely reflect the flow of non-energy products. The simultaneous allocation of energy flows to both energy and non-energy supply chains is posited as a likely reason for discrepancies observed between supply-extended and use-extended energy footprints. A more granular disaggregation of the SRIO model, specifically the underlying MSUT, by separating homogeneous product groups, could potentially lead to greater convergence between these two types of energy footprints.
Even with more disaggregated monetary supply-use tables (MSUTs) and input-output tables (MIOTs) that distinguish energy from non-energy mining products, similar to the EXIOBASE global multi-regional input-output (GMRIO) example, considerable deviations persist between supply-extended and use-extended energy footprints. A significant factor contributing to these discrepancies is the assumption of homogeneous energy prices. The supply-extended IO model relies on this assumption for allocating energy flows. In contrast, the use-extended model bypasses energy price assumptions by implicitly incorporating all transformations, losses, and distribution processes that occur before the final use phase. Consequently, a supply-extended hybrid IO model that represents energy product flows in physical units, thus avoiding homogeneous price assumptions, might show greater convergence with the use-extended IO model. Future research could explore whether further disaggregation of MIOTs or the use of hybrid IO tables (HIOTs) with mixed units would lead to even stronger convergence between these two types of energy footprints.
Both the supply-extension and use-extension approaches are valuable, with their suitability depending on the specific research question and the perspective on responsibility within the energy conversion chain. The choice between focusing on the upstream (origin/source) or downstream (actual energy use) aspects of energy is crucial. The considerable variations observed highlight the significance of the extension design when using environmentally extended input-output tables (EE-MIOTs) for monitoring energy efficiency and emission reduction targets, as these targets often fall within the same percentage range as the observed divergences. The selection of an extension also implies an assumption about responsibility in the energy conversion chain and where interventions to curb energy consumption should be directed. If the focus is on extraction and primary energy supply, and extractive sectors are considered more responsible, then the supply-extension is more appropriate. Conversely, if industrial energy use across sectors is seen as the primary driver of consumption and emissions, and final demand for products is viewed as the ultimate cause, then the use-extension, which examines the distribution of energy use, may be more informative. Consequently, different extensions would be employed to model the impact of measures targeting various stages of the energy conversion chain. This discussion about responsibility and the choice of accounting framework is also prevalent in the literature on carbon accounting.
This research explored the conceptual and empirical consequences of using two distinct, common energy extension designs. Our comparative analysis of supply-extended and use-extended SRIO and GMRIO models demonstrated significant variations in energy footprints. These findings echo ongoing discussions in Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), highlighting the conceptual distinctions between the two extension approaches. Crucially, even when both EE-MIOTs use similar terminology, their outputs represent fundamentally different types of information. Consequently, this study emphasizes the need for greater precision in communicating and detailing extension designs. This will enable more insightful interpretations of results, enhance transparency, and improve the reproducibility of modeling outcomes. Ultimately, industrial ecologists are encouraged to critically and openly examine their underlying assumptions regarding responsibility attribution. This process is vital for identifying effective interventions within the socio-economic system and requires a deeper consideration of actors, their interrelationships, and their decision-making capabilities, a core element of sociometabolic research.
