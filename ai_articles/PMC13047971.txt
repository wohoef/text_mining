Self-expandable metal stents (SEMS) are a standard treatment for unresectable malignant biliary obstruction. This study aimed to identify predictors of SEMS occlusion in patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer causing biliary tract obstruction. The study retrospectively analyzed 120 patients who underwent their first placement of partially covered SEMS (PC-SEMS) between January 2003 and January 2016 for distal malignant biliary obstruction due to unresectable pancreatic cancer. The researchers evaluated PC-SEMS occlusion rates and employed Cox regression analysis to identify potential predictors. The analysis revealed a PC-SEMS occlusion rate of 37%, with a median time to occlusion of 359 days. Biliary sludge (61%) and tumor ingrowth (30%) were the primary causes of occlusion. Cox multivariate regression analysis identified inadequate alkaline phosphatase/gamma-glutamyl transferase decline, defined as less than a 50% decrease within two weeks of placement, as the sole independent predictor of stent occlusion (hazard ratio, 2.86; 95% CI, 1.28-6.25; p = 0.01). In conclusion, inadequate decline in alkaline phosphatase/gamma-glutamyl transferase levels is a predictor of occlusion for first-time PC-SEMS placement in patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer and biliary tract obstruction.

In the United States, pancreatic cancer ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer mortality. Survival rates are grim, with only 4% to 5% of patients living five years post-diagnosis, largely because the disease is seldom detected in its resectable stages. For symptomatic malignant biliary obstruction, especially in unresectable cases, endoscopic biliary stent placement serves as a recognized palliative therapy. This intervention aims to restore bile flow, thereby enhancing a patient's prognosis and quality of life. Studies suggest that self-expandable metal stents (SEMS) offer superior patency compared to plastic stents. Consequently, SEMS are often chosen over plastic stents for managing malignant biliary obstructions in patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer.
Metal stents, while effective, are prone to occlusion over time. This blockage can result from various factors, including tumor ingrowth or overgrowth, inflammatory-induced mucosal hyperplasia, biliary sludge, or food impaction. Covered SEMS were designed to mitigate tumor ingrowth, a significant cause of obstruction with uncovered stents. However, the effectiveness of partially covered SEMS (PC-SEMS) remains debated. Furthermore, there is a lack of comprehensive data on the factors influencing PC-SEMS patency in unresectable pancreatic cancer patients. Consequently, this study sought to identify predictors of PC-SEMS occlusion in this patient population and to assess their overall survival following biliary PC-SEMS placement.
This retrospective study identified patients who received their initial partially covered self-expandable metal stent (PC-SEMS) for palliative management of distal malignant biliary obstruction due to unresectable pancreatic cancer. The enrollment period spanned from January 2003 to January 2016 at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. Patients were excluded if they were lost to follow-up within three months of stent placement. Pancreatic cancer diagnoses were confirmed through pathological or characteristic radiological evidence. Comprehensive data, including demographic, clinical, laboratory, and imaging information, along with endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography details, were gathered. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines, receiving approval from the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital.
Patients underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography in a prone position, administered sedation with intravenous midazolam. Using a standard duodenoscope, bile duct stricture and stent lengths were measured via fluoroscopy after contrast injection with a ruled catheter or guidewire. Once the anatomy and stricture were assessed, experienced endoscopists positioned the PC-SEMS. The stent's distal end was then placed within the duodenal lumen, extending about 1 cm beyond the papilla.
To identify potential predictors of metal stent occlusion, we reviewed existing literature and collected data on several factors. These included patient age (above or below 65), gender, previous biliary drainage procedures, and the decline in serum bilirubin levels (categorized as adequate, inadequate, or no icterus). We also considered serum alkaline phosphatase/gamma-glutamyl transferase (ALP/GGT) levels, tumor size, the presence of liver metastasis, ascites, duodenal invasion, bile duct stricture length, whether an endoscopic sphincterotomy was performed prior to stent placement, and whether the patient received anticancer therapy. An adequate decline in serum bilirubin was defined as a decrease of over 50% or normalization within two weeks post-stent placement. Patients undergoing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or chemoradiotherapy were grouped under anticancer therapy.
Serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) decline was also assessed as a predictor of metal stent occlusion. An "adequate" decline was defined as a drop of over 50% or normalization within two weeks post-placement. Additionally, antiplatelet drug use was evaluated as a potential predictive factor.
The study's primary objective was to assess the rate and duration of stent occlusion. Stent occlusion was defined by the time elapsed from initial PC-SEMS placement until the recurrence of obstruction or cholangitis necessitating endoscopic re-intervention to determine the occlusion's cause. For patients who did not experience obstruction during their lifetime, the stent's patency period was considered equivalent to their survival duration. Early PC-SEMS occlusion was specifically defined as obstruction occurring within three months of placement. The study's secondary objective focused on overall survival for patients who received PC-SEMS placement, with overall survival measured from the time of stent placement to the patient's death.
Results are presented as counts, percentages, medians, and interquartile ranges. Patients without stent occlusion were censored at their last follow-up or death.
To analyze stent occlusion and survival times, the Kaplan-Meier technique was employed. Initial assessments for predictors of PC-SEMS occlusion and prognostic factors for overall survival utilized univariate Cox regression analysis, with results presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with a p-value under 0.20 proceeded to multivariate Cox regression analysis for adjusted HRs and 95% CIs. Statistical significance was determined by a p-value less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 15.
The study identified 130 patients who received their initial PC-SEMS for unresectable pancreatic cancer-related biliary obstruction. Ten patients were excluded due to insufficient follow-up, leaving 120 for analysis. Table 1 details patient characteristics, noting that 49% had liver metastasis, 26% had ascites, and 29% had duodenal invasion. A significant majority, 81%, received anticancer therapy.
PC-SEMS occlusion occurred in 37% of patients (44 out of 120), with early occlusion seen in 13% (16 patients). The median time to occlusion was 359 days. Biliary sludge was the most frequent cause (61%), followed by tumor ingrowth (30%). Notably, tumor ingrowth contributed to a greater proportion of non-early occlusions (38%) compared to early occlusions (12%).
Table 3 presents the findings from univariate and multivariate analyses concerning predictors of PC-SEMS occlusion. Initially, univariate analysis indicated that prior biliary drainage, insufficient decline in ALP/GGT levels, and duodenal invasion were significantly linked to an increased risk of PC-SEMS occlusion (p < 0.05 for all). However, subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis identified inadequate ALP/GGT decline as the sole independent predictor of stent occlusion, with a hazard ratio of 2.86 (95% CI, 1.28-6.25; p = 0.01). Further supporting this, log-rank and Kaplan-Meier survival analyses demonstrated a significantly higher rate of stent occlusion in patients with inadequate ALP/GGT decline compared to those with adequate decline (p < 0.001).
In this study, the median survival time for patients was 233 days, with 73% of the 120 participants succumbing during the follow-up period. Table 4 presents the findings from univariate and multivariate analyses concerning prognostic factors for overall survival. Univariate analysis indicated that several factors were significantly associated with a reduced survival period, including male gender, previous biliary drainage, insufficient decline in bilirubin, inadequate decline in ALP/GGT levels, liver metastasis, duodenal invasion, bile duct stricture exceeding 25 mm, absence of prior endoscopic sphincterotomy, lack of anticancer therapy, and early stent occlusion. However, multivariate Cox regression analysis identified inadequate ALP/GGT decline as the sole independent predictor of overall survival, demonstrating a hazard ratio of 8.02 (95% CI, 2.10-30.60; p < 0.01).
This single-center retrospective study aimed to investigate the incidence of PC-SEMS occlusion and identify its predictors in unresectable pancreatic cancer patients experiencing biliary tract obstruction. Our findings indicated a PC-SEMS occlusion rate of 37%, aligning with previously reported obstruction rates of 28%-38%. The median time to PC-SEMS dysfunction was 359 days, also consistent with the 357 to 444-day median times to recurrent biliary obstruction found in prior research. Any minor discrepancies between our results and existing literature could be attributed to variations in patient demographics and treatment approaches, which may have impacted stent patency duration.
Stent occlusion in patients with malignant biliary obstruction is commonly attributed to factors such as tumor ingrowth or overgrowth, sludge or stone formation, and food impaction. In this study, biliary sludge emerged as the predominant cause of PC-SEMS occlusion. This finding aligns with a multicenter, prospective study by Isayama et al., which also identified sludge formation as the primary reason for stent occlusion in both partially covered stainless steel and nitinol SEMS. A potential mechanism for sludge accumulation on PC-SEMS involves bacterial adherence and subsequent biofilm formation on the stent membranes. Consequently, developing strategies to mitigate biofilm formation is crucial for maintaining the long-term patency of PC-SEMS.
Our study observed a higher rate of PC-SEMS occlusion from tumor ingrowth compared to prior research. However, early occlusion within three months due to tumor ingrowth was infrequent in our cohort. While a multicenter trial also noted tumor ingrowth with PC-SEMS, further comparative studies examining stent characteristics are needed.
Prior research on predictors for the occlusion of initially placed partially covered self-expandable metal stents (PC-SEMS) in malignant biliary obstruction is limited. While Yokota et al. found that PC-SEMS offered a longer patency period compared to uncovered and fully covered SEMS, and that PC-SEMS use itself was an independent factor reducing recurrent obstruction risk, our study aimed to identify specific patient-related predictors. Our findings revealed that inadequate alkaline phosphatase/gamma-glutamyl transferase (ALP/GGT) decline was not only an independent predictor of PC-SEMS occlusion but also a significant factor associated with poorer overall survival, as indicated by multivariate Cox regression analysis. This contrasts with some previous studies that focused on bilirubin levels. For instance, Matsuda et al. identified serum total bilirubin as a key factor influencing plastic stent patency, even developing a model to predict early occlusion based on high initial bilirubin. Brountzos et al. found no factors affecting metallic stenting patency but noted that a post-stenting bilirubin level below 4 mg/dL was a crucial predictor of survival. Eum et al. also linked normalized serum bilirubin levels to both stent patency and extended survival. However, our study suggests that an inadequate ALP/GGT decline serves as a more sensitive indicator of reduced bile flow than bilirubin levels. This diminished bile flow likely facilitates the adhesion of proteins and bacteria to the stent's inner surface, thereby increasing the likelihood of clogging. Consequently, the heightened risk of biliary tract infection associated with inadequate ALP/GGT decline may explain its correlation with reduced overall survival.
Further research is needed regarding the link between duodenal invasion and PC-SEMS occlusion. A multicenter retrospective study by Hamada et al. identified duodenal invasion as a risk factor for early SEMS dysfunction in pancreatic cancer patients. In this study, all PC-SEMS were placed across the papilla of Vater. This placement might have increased the likelihood of duodenobiliary reflux of duodenal contents, potentially leading to bacterial biofilm and sludge formation within the bile duct. However, in our investigation, duodenal invasion, diagnosed endoscopically rather than pathologically, did not emerge as a factor associated with PC-SEMS occlusion.
This study's strengths include its focus on a homogeneous group of patients with malignant biliary obstruction from unresectable pancreatic cancer, a condition known for aggressive recurrence and poorer prognosis. Furthermore, the exclusive use of PC-SEMS in this research is advantageous, as stent occlusion causes can vary based on SEMS type and characteristics, such as axial and radial forces.
This study's limitations include its retrospective design, introducing potential biases. The relatively small patient cohort was drawn from a single institution. Furthermore, the absence of detailed anticancer treatment data and evaluation of treatment response could have influenced stent occlusion and survival outcomes. Finally, data on stent migration, a known issue with covered SEMS, was not collected.
Inadequate decline in alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels predicts occlusion of first-time partially covered self-expandable metal stents (PC-SEMS) and indicates a poor prognosis in unresectable pancreatic cancer patients. Therefore, clinicians should routinely monitor ALP and GGT, alongside total bilirubin, both before and after PC-SEMS placement. This monitoring helps identify patients with malignant biliary obstruction who face a higher risk of stent occlusion.
