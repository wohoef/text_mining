This study investigated the role of microRNAs (miRNAs) in sporadic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), focusing on their potential to regulate the BRCA1 tumor suppressor gene. While familial TNBC often involves BRCA1 mutations, sporadic cases frequently show transcriptional inactivation of BRCA1, sometimes due to promoter methylation. More recently, post-transcriptional regulation of BRCA1 by miRNAs has also been identified. To explore this, researchers examined the expression of miRNAs in a panel of ten human breast cell lines: nine derived from sporadic breast cancers and one from benign breast epithelium. These cell lines were characterized for their receptor and cytokeratin status using immunocytochemistry. The study specifically measured the expression of five miRNAs predicted to target BRCA1, employing qRT-PCR. Additionally, comprehensive expression profiles of 84 known breast cancer-related miRNAs were generated across these cell lines using PCR Array and qRT-PCR. To evaluate the functional impact of selected miRNAs on breast cancer development, specific miRNAs and their inhibitors ("antagomirs") were exogenously expressed in MDA-MB-231 (TNBC) and MCF7 (luminal) breast cancer cells. The profiling results identified four candidate miRNAs (miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, and miR-153) as potentially involved in TNBC. Functional assays showed that miR-10b and miR-26a could down-regulate BRCA1 expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells, unlike miR-146a. miR-153, however, only reduced BRCA1 expression in MCF7 cells. Further analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data corroborated that miR-146a exhibited significantly higher expression in TNBC tumors compared to other breast cancer subtypes. These findings collectively suggest that specific miRNAs contribute to TNBC development by modulating BRCA1 expression.

Globally, breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer among women, with approximately 1.38 million new diagnoses annually, accounting for 23% of all cancer cases. Breast cancers are categorized into four primary molecular subtypes based on their hormone and growth factor receptor expression. Luminal A and B subtypes exhibit expression of both estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors. The HER2-enriched subtype is characterized by the amplification and overexpression of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). In contrast, the triple-negative subtype lacks expression of all these receptors. This classification system effectively predicts patient prognosis, with luminal A tumors having a favorable outlook, while triple-negative tumors, comprising 10-15% of all breast cancers, are typically aggressive and associated with a poorer prognosis.
The BRCA1 tumor suppressor gene is frequently inactivated in triple-negative breast cancers. This link is particularly strong in familial cases, where 85% of BRCA1-mutated tumors are triple-negative. In sporadic triple-negative breast cancers, BRCA1 inactivation often occurs at the transcriptional level, potentially due to promoter methylation mediated by ID4. Furthermore, recent research indicates that microRNAs can also regulate BRCA1 at the post-transcriptional level.
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short, non-coding RNA molecules, typically 19–25 nucleotides in length. Their primary function involves post-transcriptional control of gene expression. This regulation occurs when miRNAs bind to the 3′-untranslated regions (3′-UTR) of messenger RNAs (mRNAs), leading to either the degradation of the mRNA or the repression of its translation [14–16]. These regulatory molecules are known to participate in a variety of biological processes [17–19]. In the context of human breast cancer, miRNAs exhibit dual roles, functioning as either tumor suppressors (examples include miR-206, miR-17-5p, miR-125a, miR-125b, miR-200, let-7, miR-34, and miR-31) or oncogenes (such as miR-21, miR-155, miR-10b, miR-373, and miR-520c) [20]. The BRCA1 gene, a known tumor suppressor, often shows reduced expression in ER-negative and high-grade breast cancers [21], with a notable downregulation in triple-negative breast cancers [22]. Given BRCA1's critical role, any disruption in its regulation can impact tumor development [23]. The list of miRNAs that either regulate BRCA1 expression or are targets of BRCA1 is continually expanding. For instance, miR-182 has been shown to downregulate BRCA1, which can impair DNA repair and influence breast cancer treatment outcomes [24]. Similarly, miR-146a and miR-146b-5p have been observed to reduce BRCA1 gene expression in sporadic triple-negative breast cancers [18]. Conversely, some research indicates that miR-146a expression can increase alongside BRCA1 levels, suggesting a potential role for miR-146a in BRCA1-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) expression [25].
To identify microRNAs potentially regulating BRCA1 and influencing breast cancer development, we analyzed miRNA expression profiles across nine human breast cancer cell lines and one benign breast epithelium cell line.
The study utilized ten cell lines, comprising nine derived from human breast cancer and one from benign breast epithelium, all maintained at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 in a humidified incubator. The benign breast epithelium cell line, MCF10a, was sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Its culture medium was DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), enriched with 10 % horse serum, 20 ng/ml EGF, 100 ng/ml cholera toxin, 500 ng/ml hydrocortisone, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 20 ng/ml gentamicin. The characteristics of these cell lines, including their BRCA1 mutational status and expression of various receptors and cytokeratins, are detailed in Table 1.
Several cell lines were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), specifically MCF7, T47D, MDA-MB-231, HCC1937, and MDA-MB-436. These were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium, enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 20 ng/ml gentamicin. Asterand (Royston, Hertfordshire, UK) supplied the SUM149PT and SUM1315MO2 cell lines, which were cultivated in Ham’s F12 medium as per the manufacturer's guidelines. The SUM1315-LXSN and SUM1315-BRCA1 lines originated from the SUM1315MO2 cell line, which carries a 185delAG BRCA1 mutation, and were stably transfected with either empty LXSN or LXSN-BRCA1 plasmids, respectively. The BRCA1 mutation status for the remaining cell lines has been documented previously by Elstrodt et al.
Immunocytochemistry was employed to assess the protein status of ER, PR, HER2, cytokeratin5/6, cytokeratin14, EGFR, and cKIT. Cells were initially fixed with Preservcyt solution, followed by the creation of cytoblocks using a Shandon Cytoblock kit. Automated immunostaining was conducted on 3 μm-thick sections using a BenchmarkXT stainer. An expert pathologist then semi-quantitatively scored the results under a light microscope.
To identify miRNAs targeting BRCA1's 3'-UTR, five predictive algorithms were employed: TargescanHuman, Tarbase, Miranda, MicroCosmTargets, and PicTar.
We selected miRNAs predicted to target BRCA1 by at least three algorithms, utilizing default parameters for all of them.
For RNA extraction, the QIAzol reagent and Qiagen miRNeasy mini kit were employed, following the manufacturer's guidelines. The quantity and integrity of the extracted RNA were then verified using both a spectrophotometer and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 with the Agilent RNA 6000 nano kit. Subsequently, one microgram of this total RNA underwent reverse transcription utilizing a miscript II RT kit from Qiagen, France. For quantitative real-time PCR, 15 nanograms of the resulting cDNA were analyzed in triplicate. This process was carried out using a miscript SYBER Green PCR kit (Qiagen, France) on both an ABI7900HT system and an Applied Biosystems ViiA™ 7 Real-Time PCR system. Specific miRNA primers, detailed in supplementary Table S1, were sourced from miScript Primer Assay (Qiagen, France). Normalization of all samples was achieved against internal controls, specifically U6 (Hs-RNU6-2–1 miscript Primer Assay) and 18S rRNA, with fold changes determined via relative quantification (RQ = 2-∆∆CT).
To profile 84 miRNAs associated with breast cancer initiation or progression, the Human Breast Cancer miScript miRNA PCR Array (MTHS-109ZE-4) from Qiagen, France, was employed following the manufacturer's instructions. This method facilitates real-time PCR analysis of mature miRNAs on an ABI7900HT system. The process involved a miScript SYBR Green PCR kit, which includes a miScript Universal Primer and QuantiTect SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, and reverse transcription of 250 nanograms of total RNA using a miScript II RT kit.
To prepare for analysis, cells were plated at a density of 300,000 per well in 6-well plates. After 24 hours, transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent, introducing either miRNAs (mimics or inhibitors, sourced from Qiagen, France, and detailed in supplementary Table S2) or siRNAs. The siRNA specifically targeting BRCA1 (siBRCA1) was a 5 nmol siGENOME SMART pool from Thermo Scientific, Dharmacon (M-003,461–02–0005). A mock control (Tmock) involved transfecting cells with only the reagent. Cells were then harvested three days post-transfection following a PBS wash.
To assess cell proliferation, MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells were initially plated at 3000 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. Following this, cells underwent transfection with various agents: Tmock (transfection reagent only), siBRCA1, miR-146a, anti-miR-146, miR-153, miR-10b, or miR-26a, utilizing Lipofectamine 2000. After 48 hours, a CCK-8 assay was performed to measure in vitro proliferation, with absorbance readings taken at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.
To analyze miRNA expression in breast cancer, data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database was utilized, encompassing both clinical and miRNA sequencing information from invasive breast cancers. From this dataset, 519 patient samples were chosen based on their ER, PR, and HER2 status. This allowed for a comparative analysis of miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, and miR-153 expression profiles. Among these, 88 cases exhibited a triple-negative phenotype (negative for ER, PR, and HER2), while 431 cases tested positive for at least one of these receptors.
Statistical significance was determined using Student's t-test, with a p-value of 0.05 or less indicating a meaningful difference between group means.
To pinpoint microRNAs (miRNAs) involved in triple-negative breast cancer, we conducted expression profiling using a miScript miRNA PCR Array, which screens 84 miRNAs linked to breast tumorigenesis. Our analysis involved ten distinct cell lines: one benign breast epithelial line (MCF10a), two luminal breast cancer lines (MCF7, T47D), and seven triple-negative breast cancer lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, HCC1937, SUM149PT, SUM1315MO2, SUM1315-BRCA1, and SUM1315-LXSN). Each cell line's characteristics were previously determined by immunocytochemistry, as detailed in Table 1. Our objective was to identify miRNAs specifically associated with triple-negative breast cancers. To achieve this, we compared miRNA expression across these different cell line subgroups. Initially, we focused on six miRNAs (miR-10b, miR-15b, miR-26a, miR-155, miR-206, and miR-485-5p) that the PCR Array indicated were differentially expressed in at least two subgroups (Table 2, Table S3). To confirm these PCR Array findings, we subsequently performed quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) on these six miRNAs across all ten cell lines. This validation step revealed that only miR-10b and miR-26a exhibited detectable and robust expression levels in these cell lines. The remaining miRNAs were either expressed at very low levels or were entirely undetectable. Further examination of the expression patterns of these two well-expressed miRNAs showed distinct trends. miR-10b was found to be highly expressed in three of the triple-negative breast cancer cell lines: MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, and SUM1315MO2 (Fig. 1a). In contrast, miR-26a was expressed in all ten cell lines, but notably, its expression was higher in the triple-negative cell lines compared to the luminal cell lines (MCF7, T47D) (Fig. 1b). These findings suggest that miR-10b and miR-26a warrant further investigation as potential biomarkers in triple-negative breast cancer.
We aimed to identify microRNAs (miRNAs) crucial for triple-negative breast cancer development by targeting the BRCA1 gene's 3'-untranslated region (UTR). Given that BRCA1 expression is often reduced in these cancers, we conducted a computational search using five algorithms: TargescanHuman, Tarbase, Miranda, MicroCosmTargets, and PicTar.
Our analysis focused on a subset of five microRNAs (miRNAs) identified through computational prediction as potential regulators of BRCA1. These miRNAs were chosen from a larger list (Table S4) based on two criteria: prior evidence of their overexpression in breast tumors and their ability to down-regulate BRCA1 (miR-146a and miR-146b-5p), or their frequent prediction by at least three algorithms to interact with the BRCA1 3'-UTR (miR-132, miR-212, miR-153). To confirm their expression, we performed qRT-PCR on these five miRNAs across ten cell lines. We observed elevated miR-146a levels in three triple-negative breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, SUM1315-BRCA1) and increased miR-153 expression in four triple-negative cell lines (MDA-MB-436, SUM1315MO2, SUM149PT, HCC1937). In contrast, miR-146b-5p, miR-132, and miR-212 were detected in all cell lines, but without significant expression differences across triple-negative, luminal, or benign types. Consequently, our subsequent investigations concentrated on miR-146a and miR-153, in addition to miR-10b and miR-26a.
Following expression profiling and computational analyses, four specific miRNAs—miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, and miR-153—were chosen for further investigation. To assess their impact on endogenous BRCA1 expression, we introduced these miRNAs (using mimics) and their inhibitors (antagomirs) into two BRCA1 wild-type cell lines: MDA-MB-231 (a triple-negative line) and MCF7 (a luminal line). The effectiveness of the miRNA mimics and inhibitors was confirmed via qRT-PCR, with sufficient efficiency observed for miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, anti-miR-146a, and miR-153. Additionally, the efficiency of BRCA1 siRNA was validated. This experimental setup allowed us to explore potential differential effects of these miRNAs on BRCA1 expression across distinct breast cancer subtypes.
We investigated the impact of specific miRNAs on BRCA1 expression in MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cell lines. Following transfection with miR-146a, anti-miR-146a, or a mock control, our analysis revealed no apparent regulatory effect of miR-146a on BRCA1 expression. In contrast, miR-153 exhibited a differential effect: it led to a decrease in BRCA1 expression in MCF7 cells, while surprisingly causing an increase in BRCA1 expression within MDA-MB-231 cells. Further experiments involved transfecting both cell lines with miR-10b. This consistently resulted in a reduction of BRCA1 expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells. Similarly, the introduction of miR-26a into these cell lines also led to a diminished BRCA1 expression. These findings collectively indicate that both miR-10b and miR-26a are capable of down-regulating BRCA1 expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells. All BRCA1 expression levels were quantified by qRT-PCR and normalized using 18S.
Our investigation aimed to ascertain if miR-146a influences endogenous EGFR expression in our cell lines. We transfected MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells with miR-146a, anti-miR-146a, Tmock, and siBRCA1, then compared EGFR expression levels (Fig. S4). Contrary to previous findings by Kumaraswamy et al. that BRCA1 induces miR-146a to repress EGFR, our results showed no impact of miR-146a on EGFR expression in these specific cell lines.
To evaluate how specific miRNAs influence cell growth, MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells were treated with miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, anti-miR-146a, siBRCA1, or a mock transfection reagent. Proliferation was then measured using a CCK-8 assay after 48 hours. In MDA-MB-231 cells, miR-26a significantly boosted proliferation, while miR-146a had an inhibitory effect when compared to mock-treated cells. Conversely, in MCF7 cells, miR-153, miR-10b, and miR-26a all led to a notable reduction in cell proliferation relative to the mock control. These findings are visually represented in Fig. 3.
The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database provided clinical and miRNA expression data for an in silico analysis of primary breast cancer tissues. This allowed for a comparison of miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, and miR-153 expression across different tumor types. Specifically, 88 triple-negative breast tumors (lacking ER, PR, and HER2) were compared against 431 tumors positive for at least one of these receptors. Statistical differences in mean expression levels were determined using Student's t-test. The analysis revealed that miR-146a exhibited significantly higher average expression in triple-negative tumors (p = 0.000005). Conversely, miR-153 and miR-10b showed significantly lower average expression in triple-negative tumors compared to luminal tumors (p = 0.00045 and p = 0.00038, respectively). No significant difference in average expression was observed for miR-26a between these tumor groups (p = 0.6234).
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are increasingly recognized for their crucial role in the epigenetic control of gene expression across numerous cellular processes. This study utilized expression profiling, transfection, and in silico analyses to pinpoint potential miRNA biomarkers for triple-negative breast cancers, a subtype accounting for 10-15% of all human breast cancers and drawing significant research attention. The identified candidates include miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, and miR-153.
Our investigation revealed elevated miR-10b levels in triple-negative breast cancer cell lines when contrasted with luminal cell lines. However, analysis of TCGA data presented a different picture, showing significantly reduced average miR-10b expression in triple-negative tumors compared to other breast tumor types. Previous research by O’Day and Lal indicated that miR-10b is overexpressed exclusively in metastatic cancer cells, where it drives tumor cell migration, invasion, and metastasis in living organisms. While miR-10b is frequently observed at high levels in various human cancers, Biagioni et al. reported its downregulation in tumor tissues relative to normal counterparts, attributing this to hypermethylation in the upstream CpG islands of the miR-10b/10b* locus. Our findings demonstrate that miR-10b can suppress BRCA1 expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells, suggesting that elevated miR-10b might contribute to silencing BRCA1 in cells with wild-type BRCA1. Furthermore, miR-10b has been implicated in TGF-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in breast cancer, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target. Its role in promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis through post-transcriptional regulation of HOXD10 has also been established. Collectively, these observations suggest an oncogenic role for miR-10b in breast cancer cells. Paradoxically, Biagioni et al. observed that introducing exogenous miR-10b diminished the proliferative capacity of MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells both in vitro and in vivo. Our own results align with this, showing that miR-10b significantly curbed MCF7 cell proliferation (p = 0.006). Consequently, restoring miR-10b expression could represent a promising avenue for breast cancer therapy.
Our investigation identified miR-26a as a potential biomarker for triple-negative breast cancer. We observed that miR-26a reduced BRCA1 expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells, hinting at an oncogenic role in breast cancer. However, the function of miR-26a appears to be multifaceted, varying with tissue and tumor type. For instance, while we noted that miR-26a boosted proliferation in MDA-MB-231 cells and suppressed it in MCF7 cells, other research indicates it can hinder breast cancer cell proliferation and migration by repressing MCL-1, and enhance sensitivity to paclitaxel. Conversely, miR-26a has also been shown to stimulate ovarian cancer cell proliferation by targeting ERα. Furthermore, our analysis of the TCGA database revealed no significant difference in miR-26a's average expression between triple-negative and other breast tumors.
Our investigation, utilizing both cell line analysis and TCGA data, revealed a significant overexpression of miR-146a in triple-negative breast cancers, a finding consistent with previous research [18, 25]. However, our experiments showed no impact of miR-146a on BRCA1 expression in MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells. This contrasts with Gracia et al.'s report that miR-146a downregulates BRCA1 in triple-negative sporadic breast cancers [18]. This divergence might stem from the different cell lines employed in exogenous expression assays; Gracia et al. used HeLa cells alongside MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-157, and MDA-MB-436 mammary cell lines. Further, our findings align with another study indicating miR-146a's ability to hinder invasion and migration in the highly metastatic MDA-MB-231 cell line [40], as we observed miR-146a inhibiting proliferation in these cells. A recent hypothesis suggested that BRCA1 might suppress EGFR expression by elevating miR-146a levels in breast cancer cells (HCC1937) [25]. Our data supports this, showing increased miR-146a expression in BRCA1 wild-type SUM1315-BRCA1 cells compared to BRCA1-mutated SUM1315-LXSN cells. Additionally, exogenous miR-146a reduced proliferation in EGFR-rich MDA-MB-231 cells but not in EGFR-negative MCF7 cells. Despite this, qRT-PCR analysis of EGFR expression in cells with exogenous miR-146a did not show significant regulation of the EGFR gene by this miRNA. These observations collectively point to an intricate feedback loop between miR-146a and BRCA1, likely influenced by the specific cellular environment.
Our investigation, utilizing bioinformatics tools, revealed a binding site for miR-153 within the 3′-untranslated region (UTR) of the BRCA1 gene. Experimental findings showed that miR-153 led to a decrease in BRCA1 expression in MCF7 cells, yet an increase in MDA-MB-231 cells. This discrepancy aligns with varied observations in existing literature. For instance, previous research by Anaya-Ruiz et al. demonstrated that inhibiting miR-153 significantly suppressed growth, reduced proliferation, and triggered apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells, suggesting an oncogenic role for miR-153 in breast cancer initiation and progression. Similarly, Wu et al. indicated that miR-153 promotes proliferation in prostate cancer cells by silencing PTEN, a mechanism consistent with our observation that miR-153-induced BRCA1 upregulation did not impact MDA-MB-231 cell proliferation. Conversely, Zhao et al. reported that transient introduction of miR-153 into glioblastoma multiforme stem cells (GBM-SCs) inhibited their stem-like characteristics, suppressed growth, and induced apoptosis. These results parallel our findings in MCF7 cells, where despite BRCA1 downregulation by miR-153, cell proliferation was inhibited. The differing responses between MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells might stem from variations in their intrinsic miR-153 levels, with MCF7 cells exhibiting higher levels compared to the lower levels in MDA-MB-231 cells. This is further supported by TCGA data, which showed significantly lower average miR-153 expression in triple-negative breast cancers. Therefore, the role of miR-153 appears multifaceted, and further research into its targets is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of its involvement in cell proliferation and carcinogenesis.
Our findings highlight miR-10b, miR-26a, miR-146a, and miR-153 as promising biomarkers for triple-negative breast cancer. These microRNAs could be crucial for developing new targeted therapies. However, translating these discoveries into clinical practice is complex, necessitating further research to fully elucidate their functions.
