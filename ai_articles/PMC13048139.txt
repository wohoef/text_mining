This study investigated the efficacy and safety of different treatment approaches for acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (DVT). With the recent emergence of novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs) as a primary treatment for DVT, the research aimed to determine the optimal duration, effectiveness, and safety of rivaroxaban, both as a standalone therapy and when combined with catheter-directed intrathrombus thrombolysis (CDT). This is particularly relevant given the widespread use of aggressive thrombus removal strategies for acute iliofemoral DVT. The study followed patients with recent acute iliofemoral DVT for an average of 25.7 months. One group received combined CDT and rivaroxaban, while the control group received rivaroxaban alone. Various diagnostic methods, including duplex ultrasonography, plethysmography, venography, and computed tomographic venography, were used to analyze DVT progression, treatment outcomes, and factors influencing vein patency and the development of postthrombotic syndrome (PTS). Results indicated a significant difference in vein patency between the two groups. At 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, the CDT group showed cumulative patency rates of 43.2%, 64.9%, 75.7%, and 72.2%, respectively. In contrast, the control group's patency rates were considerably lower at 8.5%, 36.2%, 55.3%, and 57.4% for the same time points. The overall comparison of patency rates between the groups was statistically significant (log-rank test p = 0.009). Furthermore, patients in the CDT group experienced less severe PTS, with a median Villalta score of 3 (IQR 2-5) at 24 months, compared to 6 (IQR 4-8) in the control group (p = 0.001). The incidence of PTS was also lower in the CDT group (35.1%) than in the control group (63.8%) (p = 0.017). Bleeding events, however, were numerically higher in the CDT group (27%) compared to the control group (17%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.4). Interestingly, a Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that 6 months of rivaroxaban treatment was associated with significant cumulative patency at 24 months (p = 0.016). In conclusion, the study suggests that both the type and duration of treatment—whether rivaroxaban alone or combined with CDT—can influence vein patency at 24 months. Specifically, a 6-month lysis rate and the presence of an obstructive vein were identified as factors that can impact PTS development. The authors recommend a larger, randomized trial to definitively confirm these findings.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism, collectively known as venous thromboembolism (VTE), are associated with cardiovascular complications and death following events like myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke. DVT in the lower extremities can be categorized as either proximal, affecting the popliteal region or areas above it, or distal, which is confined to calf vein thrombosis. Both types of DVT increase the likelihood of recurrent DVT and postthrombotic syndrome (PTS). Current guidelines from the American College of Chest Physicians suggest treating acute proximal DVT with either traditional anticoagulants (like LMWH followed by VKAs such as warfarin) or novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs, including rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban). While effective for VTE, conventional anticoagulants don't actively dissolve clots, preserve valve function, or prevent PTS; indeed, 49% to 60% of proximal DVT patients treated solely with anticoagulants develop PTS within two years.
Over the last decade, novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs) have largely superseded vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in treating proximal deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Rivaroxaban, a NOAC, effectively treats proximal DVT and prevents recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE) with 6 to 12 months of therapy.
Acute iliofemoral DVT, a type of proximal DVT, involves thrombi in the iliac draining veins or common femoral vein. Patients with this condition face a higher risk of poor clinical outcomes and a worse prognosis when the proximal thrombus is more extensive, especially when compared to femoropopliteal DVT. To address this, both the American Venous Forum and the Society for Vascular Surgery have suggested thrombolytic approaches for the prompt removal of thrombi and to enhance treatment results in these patients. Studies, including the CaVenT trial and a recent Egyptian study, indicate that catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) can facilitate early thrombus dissolution and potentially avert the progression of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), with minimal hemorrhagic complications.
This study investigated the safety and efficacy of rivaroxaban, both alone and combined with CDT, for acute iliofemoral DVT. It also explored risk factors for postthrombotic syndrome, as this treatment combination had not been previously studied.
This study enrolled 94 patients between January 2014 and December 2017, all presenting with acute iliofemoral DVT symptoms for less than 21 days. Typical symptoms included limb swelling, pain, discoloration, and venous claudication. DVT diagnosis and extent confirmation relied on either duplex sonography or computed tomographic venography (CTV). The research received approval from the Taipei Veterans General Hospital's institutional review board (IRB No: 2015-030-015AC), and informed consent waivers were secured. Patients were excluded if they had conditions like active malignancy, severe anemia, significant renal failure (eGFR <30), active internal bleeding, thrombocytopenia, bilateral DVT, or contraindications to thrombolysis, such as recent major surgery or intracranial disease.
Patients in both the CDT and control groups received rivaroxaban (15 mg twice daily for 3 weeks, then 15-20 mg daily) after initial therapy. Control group patients were hospitalized and given LMWH enoxaparin (1 mg/kg subcutaneously twice daily for up to 3 days). The total rivaroxaban treatment duration, ranging from 3 to 12 months, was determined by clinical status and sonography.
Percutaneous transcatheter methods, including CDT for thrombus removal, angioplasty, or stenting for venous obstructions, were used on all patients. The CDT protocol was adapted from the CaVenT study.
Prior to the procedure, antithrombotic medications were stopped to ensure an international normalized ratio below 1.5. To begin CDT, an intravenous bolus of 3000-5000 U of unfractionated heparin was given, aiming for an activated clotting time exceeding 200 seconds. Percutaneous access was then established in either the lesser saphenous or popliteal vein, guided by ultrasound. Following vein puncture, a 5- or 6-Fr introducer sheath was inserted using the Seldinger technique, and venography was conducted to assess thrombus extent.
To prevent bleeding, daily monitoring of serum fibrinogen, hemoglobin, and platelet count guided urokinase dosage adjustments during continuous infusion (1000-1500 U/kg/h for 48-72 hours). Thrombolysis was paused, or fresh frozen plasma administered, if fibrinogen levels fell below 150 mg/d.
To prevent thrombus formation during CDT, unfractionated heparin (UFH) was continuously infused via the access sheath at 5-10 U/kg/h, maintaining an aPTT 1.5-2.0 times the control. On day two or three, patients underwent repeat venography. Angioplasty addressed significant stenosis, with stenting implemented for over 70% residual stenosis after repeated angioplasty.
Following the procedures, systemic unfractionated heparin (UFH) was given via the sheath to keep aPTT between 1.5 and 2.5 times the control level on the day of operation. The next day, patients began rivaroxaban, with aspirin (75 mg/day) added for those with stents. All patients wore elastic bandages or thigh-high graduated compression stockings and were encouraged to ambulate promptly. Safety and efficacy outcomes were the study's endpoints.
To assess the effectiveness of the treatments, several outcomes were measured, including the early and immediate patency of iliofemoral veins, the ability of valves to function correctly, the reopening of blocked veins, changes in the amount of thrombus, and the development of postthrombotic syndrome (PTS). These efficacy outcomes were monitored using clinical duplex ultrasonography (US) at various intervals: at the beginning of the study, after one week, and then at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months. Doppler US was specifically employed to evaluate both venous patency and the function of the venous valves. Valve incompetence was identified when reflux exceeded 0.5 seconds, with the duration of reflux indicating the severity. Vascular specialists and experienced technicians conducted these 15-color Doppler US examinations. To track the progression of thrombosis, a modified thrombus score (TS) was utilized, building upon existing scoring systems by Porter and Moneta, and Haenen et al. This scoring involved classifying vein segments according to standard definitions, encompassing five proximal deep vein segments (external iliac, common femoral, proximal femoral, middle femoral, and popliteal), four distal deep vein segments (two posterior and two anterior tibial veins), and three superficial vein segments (long saphenous above and below the knee, and lesser saphenous). The TS quantified venous obstruction: 0 for a compressible, open, thrombus-free vein; 1 for a partially occluded vein with a Doppler flow signal; and 2 for a noncompressible, completely occluded vein lacking a flow signal. The cumulative TS was derived by adding the scores from all 12 venous segments.
At 24 months, patients underwent CT scans or high-probability lung scans to document pulmonary embolism. Other safety outcomes tracked were major or minor bleeding events and recurrent DVT.
The Villalta PTS scale assessed post-thrombotic syndrome at 24 months. Thrombolysis degree was classified using the Venous Registry Index and venography, both before and within 48 hours of each CDT procedure.
Categorical data were presented as percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test assessed the normality of numerical variable distributions. Intergroup comparisons for numerical data utilized either the independent-samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Chi-square testing was applied to categorical variables and frequencies, and Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for time-to-patency analysis.
Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05. Predisposing factors for PTS were identified using univariate and multivariate logistic regression, with all analyses conducted in SPSS v22.
The study initially enrolled 94 patients, consisting of 51 women and 43 men, with an average age of 69.8 ± 16.9 years (ranging from 20 to 90 years). These participants were followed for an average of 25.7 ± 2.5 months. Ultimately, data from 84 patients were analyzed, as 10 individuals were excluded during the study period. Exclusions included two patients lost to follow-up in the control group and one in the CDT group. Additionally, four control group patients died from malignancy-related organ failure, while three CDT group patients died due to malignancy-related organ failure or cardiogenic shock. Among the initial cohort, 41 received CDT followed by rivaroxaban, and 53 were treated with rivaroxaban alone.
Table 1 details patient demographics and clinical characteristics. The control group had a median age of 75 years (IQR: 66-85), significantly older than the CDT group's 63 years (IQR: 50.5-81) (p = 0.017). Malignancy was also significantly more prevalent in the control group (42.6%, 20 patients) compared to the CDT group (13.5%, 5 patients) (p = 0.008).
After CDT, venography assessed thrombolysis in the CDT group. Complete lysis was noted in seven patients, while partial lysis (grade II) occurred in twelve. These venography results aligned with ultrasound findings, showing no significant discrepancy. Additionally, percutaneous angioplasty was performed on 14 out of 37 CDT patients.
Table 3 details the venous outcomes, revealing a significantly higher thrombus resolution rate in the CDT group. At one month, 75.2% of CDT patients showed resolution, compared to 22.9% in the control group. This trend continued, with 89.4% resolution at six months for CDT versus 71.6% for controls. Early recanalization was also notably more pronounced in the CDT group within the first three months (p < 0.001). The median rivaroxaban treatment duration was similar between groups, at 10 months for CDT and 12 months for controls (p = 0.124).
At 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, the control group showed venous patency rates of 8.5%, 36.2%, 55.3%, and 57.4%, respectively. In contrast, the CDT group achieved significantly higher patency, with rates of 43.2%, 64.9%, 75.7%, and 75.7% at these same time points. A notable difference in venous patency existed between the groups over the initial 12 months.
Figure 1 illustrates the cumulative patency over 24 months for both the CDT and control groups, as determined by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and life table data, showing a statistically significant difference (log-rank test, p = 0.009). When examining the duration of rivaroxaban treatment, a Kaplan–Meier curve analysis (Fig. 2A) revealed that patients receiving rivaroxaban for less than 6 months exhibited a higher 24-month patency rate compared to those treated for longer periods, with this difference being statistically significant (log-rank test, p = 0.016). Conversely, no significant difference was found in cumulative patency when comparing rivaroxaban treatment durations of 5 months or less versus more than 5 months (p = 0.627), as shown in Figure 2B.
At 12 months, no significant difference in valvular incompetence was found between the CDT and control groups (62.2% vs. 66%, p = 0.719). However, a notable disparity in postthrombotic syndrome (PTS) was evident. The CDT group exhibited a lower incidence of PTS (35.1%) compared to the control group (63.8%, p = 0.017). This was further reflected in the median Villalta scores at 12 months, with the CDT group scoring 3 (2-5) and the control group scoring 6 (4-8) (p = 0.001).
At 24 months, CTV and Doppler US showed no significant statistical difference in findings (McNemar test, p = 0.219).
At 24 months, CTV imaging identified obstructed veins in 35 patients. These obstructions manifested as various anatomical abnormalities, including narrowing with irregular walls, partial or total occlusion of iliofemoral and femoropopliteal veins, and May-Thurner syndrome (21 cases). Specific locations affected were the common femoral vein near the inguinal area (22 cases), the inferior vena cava (5 cases), and irregular obstructions in the femoropopliteal (11 cases) and iliofemoral veins (19 cases).
To pinpoint clinical factors influencing PTS development, a univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, examining variables such as age, gender, body mass index, thrombus resolution rate, treatment duration, leg immobilization, DVT division, malignancy, valvular incompetence (VI), and obstructed veins (Table 5). This analysis identified six clinical factors significantly linked to PTS risk. These included the type of treatment (OR = 3.26, 95% CI: 1.3-8.01, p = 0·01), the presence of an obstructed vein on CTV at 24 months (OR = 18.6, 95% CI: 5.9-58.65, p < 0·001), the 1-month lysis rate (OR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03-0.44, p = 0.002), the 3-month lysis rate (OR = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.001-0.09, p < 0·001), the 6-month lysis rate (OR = 0.0001, 95% CI: 0.0001-0.1, p < 0·001), the treatment duration (OR = 1.104, 95% CI: 1.03-1.19, p = 0.008), and recurrent DVT (OR = 4.15, 95% CI: 1.22-14.07, p = 0.022). Subsequently, these significant factors, along with other potential factors with p-values below 0.2, were subjected to multivariate logistic regression. Multiple rounds of multivariate regression were performed to assess the 1-, 3-, and 6-month lysis rates in relation to other potential PTS factors at 24 months. Ultimately, after adjusting for baseline thrombus score, treatment duration, treatment method, VI, and recurrent DVT using the anterior stepwise regression method, two clinical factors emerged as independently associated with an increased likelihood of PTS: an obstructed vein (OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 1.34-21.24, p = 0.021) and the 6-month lysis rate (OR = 0.01, 95% CI: 0-0.27, p = 0.009).
In the CDT group, three patients did not complete thrombolytic treatment due to significant bleeding complications. One female patient experienced spontaneous arterial bleeding near the iliopsoas muscle, necessitating over 10 units of packed red blood cells and embolization. Another patient, male, developed upper gastrointestinal bleeding that required endoscopic intervention. The third patient's thrombolysis was ineffective due to massive hematuria, a complication of a pre-existing nephrostomy for renal stones. While rivaroxaban doses were adjusted for other bleeding events observed in both groups, the overall incidence of bleeding was 27% in the CDT group versus 17% in the control group (p = 0.267), with no significant differences in major or minor bleeding between the groups.
No significant difference in recurrent DVT incidence was observed between the CDT (6/37) and control (11/47) groups (p = 0.632). Similarly, pulmonary embolism rates did not differ significantly. Eight patients experienced recurrent DVT within 3-6 months post-rivaroxaban, while others relapsed within three months of stopping the medication. Additionally, six patients showed thrombus progression during treatment.
The study evaluated the effectiveness and safety of rivaroxaban, both as a standalone treatment and combined with CDT, for acute iliofemoral DVT. This condition typically involves a significant thrombus burden, leading to a higher risk of embolization and adverse clinical outcomes. Research indicates that early, effective thrombolysis offers better venous outcomes and preserves iliofemoral vein function compared to rivaroxaban monotherapy.
Previous research indicates that catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) effectively achieves short- and midterm venous patency, with reported 6-month patency rates ranging from 60% to 85% due to its favorable thrombus lysis. However, there is limited data on venous patency when rivaroxaban is used alone or in combination with CDT for acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Our current study observed that the CDT group, receiving CDT combined with rivaroxaban, demonstrated significantly higher median thrombus resolution rates: 77.8% at 1 month, 83.3% at 3 months, 100% at 6 months, and 100% at 12 months. In contrast, the control group, treated with rivaroxaban alone, showed lower resolution rates: 50% at 3 months, 72.7% at 6 months, and 100% at 12 months. Furthermore, cumulative venous patency in the CDT group was 43.2% at 3 months, 64.9% at 6 months, 75.7% at 12 months, and 75.7% at 24 months. The control group exhibited considerably lower patency rates: 8.5% at 3 months, 36.2% at 6 months, 55.3% at 12 months, and 55.3% at 24 months. These preliminary findings suggest that combining CDT with rivaroxaban leads to earlier and more effective thrombus removal compared to rivaroxaban monotherapy. A survival analysis using Kaplan–Meier curves confirmed a statistically significant difference in cumulative 24-month patency between the two groups (log-rank test, p = 0.009). Therefore, CDT plus rivaroxaban appears to achieve superior acute, short-term, and midterm venous patency through early thrombolysis.
Limited data exist on midterm post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS) development and venous function in acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (DVT) patients receiving either rivaroxaban alone or combined with catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT). While existing literature indicates that CDT and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) effectively reduce thrombi and prevent recurrent DVT and subsequent PTS, and rivaroxaban inhibits thrombus development and recurrent VTE in acute proximal DVT, this study provides new insights. Our findings show that patients in the CDT group experienced a more favorable degree of PTS at 24 months. Multivariate analysis specifically linked obstructed veins (identified by CTV) and the 6-month lysis rate to PTS development. This suggests that early and effective thrombolysis can decrease thrombus burden, thereby reducing inflammation, vein remodeling, and the risk of developing PTS.
Patients treated with CDT and rivaroxaban showed rapid symptom resolution, likely due to effective thrombolysis and early vein recanalization, as evidenced by regular ultrasound. Conversely, rivaroxaban alone achieved symptom resolution mainly through collateral vein bypass and minor, asymmetrical recanalization. This suggests that the combined therapy offers early, direct recanalization and can reduce PTS development by superior thrombus resolution within one to six months.
At the 24-month follow-up, all patients underwent CTV, revealing vein obstructions in 35 individuals. Of these, 28 patients also had documented vein obstructions via Doppler US combined with D-dimer testing. Given the non-significant difference (p = 0.219) between these two diagnostic methods, Doppler US offers a practical and accurate means to monitor thrombus evolution and vein patency during follow-up. However, CTV at 24 months provides a more precise visualization of anatomical irregularities, particularly in the iliac vein or IVC. The CTV scans identified various abnormalities in the 35 patients with obstructed veins, including narrowing (n=14), partial or total occlusion of the iliofemoral veins, and May-Thurner syndrome (n=21). Specific anatomical sites affected included venous stricture in the common femoral vein near the inguinal area (n=22), the IVC (n=5), and irregular obstructions in the femoropopliteal (n=11) and iliofemoral veins (n=19).
Anatomic abnormalities in the affected leg, as revealed by CTV, could impact vein patency and venous complications in acute iliofemoral DVT patients. This suggests tailoring supplementary treatment strategies based on individual CTV findings.
Further randomized, prospective studies are needed to assess supplementary treatment strategies.
The combination of CDT and rivaroxaban was found to be a safe and effective treatment for acute iliofemoral DVT. Bleeding complications are a significant consideration with both thrombolysis and rivaroxaban therapy. In this study, the majority of patients (34 out of 37) in the CDT group completed their treatment. The rate of major bleeding events in this group was consistent with previous research. Of the 10 patients who experienced major bleeding events, three necessitated blood transfusions and consequently discontinued CDT. While other bleeding events occurred in both treatment groups, leading to rivaroxaban dose adjustments, the overall incidence of bleeding was not significantly higher in the CDT group.
Recurrent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) incidence did not significantly differ between the two treatment groups. In the control group, 11 out of 47 patients experienced recurrent DVT or thrombus progression during follow-up. Specifically, five of these progressions occurred during rivaroxaban treatment, while six developed after its cessation. Among these 11 patients, six presented with bleeding events and tested positive for lupus anticoagulant, two had ischemic stroke, and three had a history of malignancy. Within the CDT group, three patients had incomplete CDT due to significant bleeding, leading to thrombus progression within the initial three months. An additional three patients in this group developed recurrent thrombosis during the follow-up period. Of these, two had ischemic stroke and protein C and S deficiencies, and one experienced malignant recurrence. For all patients experiencing recurrent DVT, rivaroxaban therapy was extended beyond three months.
While prior research suggests prolonged rivaroxaban therapy for unprovoked and recurrent DVT, our current study presents a contrasting observation regarding venous patency. The Kaplan-Meier analysis of patency over time indicated that patients receiving rivaroxaban for six months or less achieved better 24-month venous patency compared to those treated for longer durations (log-rank test, p = 0.016). This seemingly counterintuitive result, where "simple" rivaroxaban alone did not yield satisfactory 24-month patency, is explained by the combination of CDT and rivaroxaban. The CDT-plus-rivaroxaban approach, when administered for six months, led to earlier and more effective thrombus removal within the first one to six months, thereby contributing to superior cumulative patency compared to the control group. Consequently, our findings suggest that for acute iliofemoral DVT, an effective and early thrombolytic treatment combined with six months of rivaroxaban is advisable. Conversely, if rivaroxaban is used as a standalone treatment for acute iliofemoral DVT, a duration exceeding six months might be necessary. Future randomized, prospective studies are crucial to eliminate confounding variables and clarify these observations.
Fourteen patients in the CDT group underwent angioplasty and showed no occluded lesions on CTV after 24 months. To improve both short- and long-term venous patency in iliofemoral DVT, angioplasty and stents should be considered alongside thrombolytic and anticoagulant therapies. Further large-scale, randomized studies are needed to compare the outcomes of these adjuvant treatments.
For patients with severe DVT who do not have multiple comorbidities, CDT can effectively achieve rapid thrombus resolution and recanalization, thereby lowering the incidence of PTS. However, this invasive approach carries an elevated risk of bleeding. Conversely, rivaroxaban alone offers the benefit of endogenous thrombolysis, potentially providing a safer treatment option. Therefore, a minimum of six months of oral rivaroxaban is recommended for acute iliofemoral DVT managed with CDT. While rivaroxaban monotherapy might be safer, its advantage could be diminished in patients with comorbidities, who may necessitate longer treatment durations. The study did not support a routine invasive thrombolytic strategy for DVT patients with multiple comorbidities.
This study has several limitations. A key concern is its non-randomized design, despite prospective data collection. Additionally, the small patient numbers in subgroup analyses and potential selection bias due to demographic heterogeneity are noteworthy. Future randomized studies are needed to definitively compare the safety and efficacy of rivaroxaban alone versus rivaroxaban combined with CDT.
Compared to rivaroxaban alone, CDT combined with rivaroxaban offers faster thrombus reduction and earlier vein patency. Key predictors for PTS development were the 6-month thrombolysis rate and the presence of obstructed veins.
The study's findings suggest a potential link between the type and duration of rivaroxaban treatment (alone or with CDT) and 24-month vein patency. Confirmation through a larger randomized trial is needed.
