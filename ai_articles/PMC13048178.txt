Antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATLI), a frequent adverse effect of anti-TB medications, has prompted ongoing discussion regarding the efficacy of regular liver function monitoring in mitigating its impact. This study sought to examine the current practices of liver test monitoring for patients undergoing anti-TB treatment in Taiwan and to determine how scheduled monitoring influences the risk of ATLI. For this retrospective analysis, patients treated at our hospital between 2009 and 2017 were included. A total of 1062 patients participated, with 469 (44.2%) receiving consistent liver function monitoring, categorized as the "good monitoring group." ATLI was identified in 100 (9.4%) patients. The good monitoring group demonstrated a higher rate of early ATLI detection compared to the "poor monitoring group" (14.7% versus 5.2%). Furthermore, ATLI was identified significantly earlier in the well-monitored patients, with a shorter average time to diagnosis (21.4 days versus 61.6 days, p < 0.01). These patients also exhibited a lower peak serum alanine aminotransferase level (276.1 IU/L versus 507.1 IU/L, p = 0.05). The findings indicate that less than half of patients receiving anti-TB drugs had their liver function regularly monitored. The study concludes that scheduled liver function monitoring aids in the earlier detection of ATLI cases, thereby reducing the severity of liver injury. Consequently, the importance of implementing periodic liver function monitoring tests for patients on anti-TB treatment should be reinforced.

Antituberculosis (TB) drug-induced liver injury (ATLI) is a significant adverse effect associated with anti-TB medications. The primary drugs used in TB treatment, such as isoniazid, rifampicin, and pyrazinamide, are known to possess hepatotoxic potential. ATLI can manifest in various ways, from subtle elevations in aminotransferase levels to severe liver damage or even hepatic failure. Ensuring patient safety and facilitating TB control necessitates the prevention of ATLI. Several strategies have been put forth to prevent or mitigate ATLI, including regular liver function monitoring, genetic testing for drug-metabolizing enzymes, modifying anti-TB drug regimens, and administering liver-protective agents. Among these, regular liver function monitoring appears to be the most straightforward method for ATLI prevention. Nevertheless, robust evidence supporting the efficacy of liver function monitoring is limited, with current recommendations largely stemming from expert opinions rather than empirical data.
Guidelines from organizations like the UK's NICE, the ATS, and the WHO generally recommend liver function monitoring only for patients at higher risk of hepatotoxicity. This includes individuals with pre-existing liver issues, abnormal baseline liver tests, a history of alcohol abuse, or prior drug-induced liver injury (DILI). However, the Taiwanese CDC has a different approach, advising liver function tests for all patients starting anti-TB treatment at specific intervals: 2, 4, and 8 weeks. This contrasts with statin therapy, where regular monitoring is deemed unnecessary due to low hepatotoxicity rates, even in high-risk individuals. Consequently, the benefit of routine liver function monitoring for all patients undergoing anti-TB treatment in preventing ATLI remains a question requiring further investigation.
This study investigated liver test monitoring practices for anti-TB patients in Taiwan and evaluated how scheduled monitoring affects the risk of drug-induced liver injury.
Patients treated for TB at our tertiary referral hospital from 2009 to 2017 were included in this study.
Patients diagnosed with active TB infection, confirmed by acid-fast stain, nucleic acid amplification test, or M. TB culture, who received first-line anti-TB therapy and completed their full treatment course at the hospital were included. Excluded were individuals under 18 years old, those with non-TB mycobacterium or multi-drug resistant TB, patients starting second-line anti-TB treatment, and those lost to follow-up.
The study analyzed baseline and follow-up data, including liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP), bilirubin, creatinine, hepatitis B and C status, alcohol habits, HIV status, concurrent medications, cancer status, and physician monitoring adherence.
Patients were categorized into two groups based on liver function monitoring. Those adhering to Taiwanese CDC guidelines, with assessments at baseline and at 2, 4, and 8 weeks of treatment, formed the "good monitoring" group. Patients with incomplete monitoring fell into the "poor monitoring" group.
Antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATLI) was defined by the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network (DILIN) criteria. This included cases where serum ALT or AST levels exceeded five times the upper limit of normal (ULN) in patients with normal baseline liver function, or five times their baseline level if baseline liver function was already abnormal. Additionally, ATLI was diagnosed if serum total bilirubin was above 2.5 mg/dL alongside elevated ALT, AST, or ALP, provided hemolysis was not present. A liver abnormality was characterized by a rise in serum ALT, AST, ALP, or total bilirubin exceeding twice the ULN or baseline.
ATLI severity was graded from 1 (mild) to 5 (fatal) using DILIN criteria. Active cancer was defined as any vital cancer receiving treatment. The ATS identified chronic alcohol use, positive HBsAg or anti-HCV, pre-existing liver disease, pregnancy, postpartum status, and HIV infection as risk factors for ATLI.
The study received Institutional Review Board approval (2017-10-015AC) and adhered to the 1975 Helsinki Declaration.
Patient data, presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise noted, underwent analysis using Student's t-tests or chi-squared tests for group comparisons. Multivariate logistic regression assessed ATLI risk. All analyses were performed with SPSS 21.0, with a p-value less than 0.05 signifying statistical significance.
The study included 1062 patients, with an average age of 67.1 years and 70.4% being male. Among them, 10.0% were HBV carriers, and 4% had hepatitis C. A significant portion, 18.5%, had extrapulmonary tuberculosis, most commonly affecting the pleura, lymph nodes, or spine. Additionally, 9.9% of patients had active cancer, with lung cancer being the most frequent type. Further details regarding the baseline characteristics of these patients can be found in Table 1.
After anti-TB treatment, 15.9% of patients (169) experienced liver abnormalities, with 9.4% (100) diagnosed with ATLI. Three cases of liver dysfunction were excluded from ATLI diagnosis due to alternative causes like autoimmune hepatitis, pancreatic cancer with jaundice, or liver cancer treatment.
Antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATLI) occurred with a mean latency of 33.8 days. Of the 100 ATLI patients, 21% experienced severe to fatal DILI. Within the study cohort, deaths were attributed to liver injury (five), TB (15), cancer (16), and other causes (13). No patients required liver transplantation for ATLI.
In this study, 44.2% of patients (469 individuals) were categorized into the "good monitoring" group, while the remaining 55.8% (593 individuals) fell into the "poor monitoring" group. Analysis revealed that the good monitoring group exhibited notably higher baseline levels of serum ALT (p = 0.04) and AST (p < 0.01). Furthermore, this group had a greater proportion of HBV carriers (12.6% compared to 7.9% in the poor monitoring group, p = 0.01). Over the study period, there was a significant year-by-year increase in the proportion of patients in the good monitoring group (p < 0.01). The incidence of both general liver abnormalities (23.5% vs. 9.9%, p < 0.01) and antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATLI) (14.7% vs. 5.2%, p < 0.01) was significantly higher in the good monitoring group. While monitoring status did not correlate with ATLI-related mortality (p = 0.11), the peak ALT levels observed were lower in the good monitoring group (276.1 ± 346.5 vs. 507.1 ± 604.8, p = 0.05). Crucially, the good monitoring group experienced a significantly shorter latency period for ATLI development (21.4 ± 25.6 days vs. 61.6 ± 36.2 days, p < 0.01).
Following the diagnosis of anti-TB drug-induced liver injury (ATLI), 91% of affected patients had their anti-TB medications stopped. Subsequently, 80% of these patients were given a second attempt with first-line anti-TB drugs once their liver function normalized or nearly normalized, with 57% of these attempts proving successful. In 21% of cases, patients either could not tolerate a rechallenge with first-line drugs or switched to second-line regimens due to ATLI. Of those on second-line therapy, only two individuals were unable to tolerate the treatment. The study found no significant variations in ATLI management strategies between patients who received good monitoring and those who received poor monitoring.
Multivariate logistic regression identified several factors associated with ATLI susceptibility: positive HBsAg, positive anti-HCV, age over 65, disseminated TB, and active cancer. Notably, positive anti-HCV and advanced age significantly elevated the risk of severe or fatal ATLI. While active cancer showed a trend towards increasing severe and fatal ATLI, this association did not reach statistical significance.
Antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATLI) is a frequent adverse effect of anti-TB treatment. This study found that regular liver function monitoring was vital for detecting ATLI in all patients undergoing anti-TB therapy. Additionally, individuals with HBV or HCV, older age, disseminated TB, or active cancer demonstrated an increased risk of developing ATLI.
While the importance of monitoring liver function to prevent and manage ATLI is widely accepted, there's no universal agreement on the best approach. Current guidelines often focus on monitoring patients at higher risk for ATLI. For instance, NICE guidelines suggest monitoring for individuals with existing liver conditions, abnormal baseline liver tests, or a history of alcohol or drug misuse. Similarly, the WHO advises monitoring for those with pre-existing liver disease. The ATS recommends regular liver function checks for individuals with chronic alcohol use, viral hepatitis, pre-existing liver diseases, pregnancy, recent postpartum status, concurrent hepatotoxic drug use, or a history of DILI or HIV infection. In contrast, the Taiwanese CDC's guidelines advocate for liver function monitoring in all patients undergoing anti-TB treatment, irrespective of their baseline liver status or pre-existing liver conditions.
Regarding the frequency of liver function monitoring, neither the NICE nor the WHO guidelines specify a schedule. The ATS, however, advises baseline liver function tests for all patients and then regular monitoring every two to four weeks during the initial two to three months for those at high risk. In contrast, the Taiwanese CDC recommends monitoring at baseline and at two, four, and eight weeks of treatment. These Taiwanese guidelines aim for early ATLI detection and prevention of severe liver damage. Our study supports the effectiveness of the Taiwanese CDC's approach, demonstrating that regular liver function monitoring leads to earlier identification of ATLI cases and reduces the severity of liver injury.
In this study, patients in the "good monitoring" group exhibited a higher prevalence of pre-existing liver function abnormalities and HBV carrier status, both recognized contributors to anti-TB drug-induced liver injury (ATLI). This group also presented with a greater incidence of disseminated tuberculosis. It is plausible that the severity of TB and the presence of these risk factors prompted physicians to conduct more frequent liver function checks. However, instances where patients missed scheduled tests could lead to an inaccurate assessment of physician adherence. The study observed that pulmonologists demonstrated better adherence to Taiwanese CDC guidelines than infectious disease specialists or surgeons. A notable finding was the significantly shorter latency period for ATLI in the well-monitored group compared to the poorly monitored group. The proportion of patients in the good monitoring group also rose annually, attributed to enhanced education and advocacy efforts. Prompt identification of early liver dysfunction typically results in closer patient follow-up, potentially averting ATLI progression. Conversely, asymptomatic ATLI cases might go undetected in the poorly monitored group, leading to an underestimation of its overall incidence. Consequently, regular liver function monitoring appears to enhance the detection rate of ATLI.
Previous research has indicated a link between liver function monitoring and outcomes in patients undergoing anti-TB treatment. A study by Chih et al. analyzed severe anti-TB drug-induced liver injury (ATLI) patients who voluntarily reported their cases, finding that those without monitoring experienced more severe liver damage and higher mortality rates compared to monitored patients. Similarly, Wu et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study suggesting that scheduled monitoring could decrease hospitalization rates, reduce liver injury, and lower mortality. However, the present study did not find a significant association between monitoring status and ATLI-related mortality. Several factors might explain this discrepancy. Firstly, the previous studies had different patient selection criteria: Chih et al. included voluntarily reported severe cases, and Wu et al. focused solely on patients with ATLI, whereas the current study examined consecutive patients. Secondly, Chih et al.'s study involved cases with more severe liver injuries than those in the present research. Finally, the older age of patients in this study and a lower overall mortality rate may have influenced the observed results, making statistical significance harder to achieve.
Previous research has established varying timelines for the onset of antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATLI). One study by Chih et al. reported a mean latency of 8.2 weeks. More recently, a UK study by Abbara et al. found that ATLI typically occurred within 12.5 days, with the vast majority manifesting within 8 weeks. Similarly, the REMoxTB study in the UK identified ATLI in 3.0% of patients at a median of 28 days. A Korean retrospective study indicated that 67.6% of patients developed ATLI within 30 days. The current study's observed mean latency of 33.8 ± 34.6 days aligns with these findings, reinforcing the recommendations from the ATS and Taiwanese CDC for scheduled liver function monitoring during the initial two months of anti-TB treatment.
Previous research aligns with our findings that individuals over 65 and those with positive anti-HCV have a significantly increased risk of severe and fatal antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury. Consequently, regular liver function monitoring is strongly advised for these high-risk patients.
Active cancer patients showed an increased risk of ATLI in this study, though the underlying cause remains unclear. Potential contributing factors to liver injury in these individuals include immune system abnormalities, poor nutrition, and concurrent chemotherapy.
Regular liver function monitoring, aligning with Taiwanese CDC policy, enhances ATLI detection across all patients. While mortality rates were comparable between monitored and unmonitored groups, this likely stems from prompt drug cessation upon ATLI diagnosis and a low number of fatalities.
This study has several limitations. The accuracy of recorded alcohol consumption and comorbidities might have been compromised, potentially affecting the assessment of hepatotoxicity susceptibility. Additionally, the incidence of ATLI in the poorly monitored group may be underestimated, as asymptomatic cases could have been overlooked without regular monitoring. Finally, the retrospective nature of the study, while necessary to evaluate physician adherence to Taiwanese CDC guidelines, means it was not prospective.
This study found that fewer than half of patients on anti-TB drugs underwent regular liver function monitoring. Scheduled monitoring facilitates earlier detection of anti-TB drug-induced liver injury (ATLI), leading to reduced liver damage. Therefore, the practice of periodic liver function tests for these patients should be reinforced and promoted.
