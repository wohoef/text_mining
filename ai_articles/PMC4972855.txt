Tumor progression, characterized by cell migration, invasion, and metastasis, involves the activation of specific proteases, either membrane-bound or secreted into the extracellular environment. Given TNF-α's recognized role in various aspects of tumor progression, this study aimed to investigate its impact on breast cancer cell migration and its connection to the localization of membrane-associated proteases within lipid rafts. To achieve this, researchers employed wound scratch healing and Transwell migration assays to examine TNF-α's effects on both hormone-dependent (MCF7) and hormone-independent (MDA-MB-231) breast cancer cells. The study also analyzed the expression and secretion of three matrix metalloproteases (MMP9, MMP2, and MT1-MMP) and two dipeptidyl peptidases (CD26 and FAP-α) using RT-PCR, Western blotting, and gelatin zymography. Additionally, Western blotting was used to assess the activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. The findings revealed that TNF-α stimulated breast cancer cell migration, which coincided with increased MMP9 secretion, but not MMP2, into the culture media. Furthermore, TNF-α dose-dependently increased the expression of dipeptidyl peptidases CD26 and FAP-α. Importantly, TNF-α also led to a higher concentration of all five proteases within the lipid rafts of the tested breast cancer cells, irrespective of cell type. Mechanistically, TNF-α activated the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, evidenced by elevated ERK1/2 phosphorylation. Inhibition of the MEK/ERK1/2 pathway with U-0126 reduced TNF-α-induced MMP9 secretion and prevented the increased protease concentration in lipid rafts. These results suggest that TNF-α-driven activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway contributes to breast cancer cell migration. This occurs through both the upregulation of MMP9, CD26, and FAP-α, and the enhanced concentration of these proteases, alongside MT1-MMP and MMP2, within lipid rafts. Consequently, TNF-α could represent a promising therapeutic target for breast cancers responsive to its stimulation.

The advancement of cancer relies on several cellular processes, notably the cytokine-driven encouragement of cell movement and the creation and activation of various proteolytic enzyme systems. A critical stage in invasion and metastasis involves the breakdown of the robust structural protein network that serves as a natural impediment. This breakdown is linked to the activation of specific proteases, which are either bound to cell membranes or released into the extracellular environment. Proteases are vital for both breaking down extracellular matrix (ECM) components and for processing various soluble bioactive substances and shedding numerous cell surface proteins. Their production is meticulously controlled by signals from cytokines, growth factors, and mechanical stress. Many cells heighten their proteolytic activity when exposed to inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), which is often elevated in human epithelial cancers, including breast cancer. TNF-α plays multiple roles in cancer biology, including inducing cell death, stimulating cancer cell survival and proliferation, and fostering angiogenesis, tumor cell migration, and invasion.
Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and dipeptidyl peptidases (DPPs) are well-established contributors to the processes of cancer invasion and metastasis [1, 7–9]. MMPs, which are peptidases requiring zinc and calcium, are crucial for the restructuring of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and thus influence the aggressiveness of cancer cells. One specific MMP, membrane-type metalloproteinase 1 (MT1-MMP, also known as MMP14), belongs to the membrane-anchored MMP family [10]. It begins as a 64-kDa pro-enzyme, which is then proteolytically cleaved by furin to become an active 54-kDa protein. Beyond its direct role in breaking down ECM fibrillar components, proteoglycans, cell surface receptors, and cell adhesion molecules, MT1-MMP also initiates the activation of other zymogens, specifically metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2/gelatinase A) and, indirectly, metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9/gelatinase B) [2, 11, 12]. MMP2 and MMP9, both gelatinases within the MMP family, are particularly important in the initial stages of cancer cell invasion and tumor vascularization. They achieve this by cleaving key basement membrane (BM) components like type IV collagen, laminin, and elastin. Like other soluble MMPs, MMP2 and MMP9 are secreted as inactive pro-enzymes. Their activation can occur through an autocatalytic process or by cleavage from other proteases, including serine proteases. Beyond their direct ECM degradation capabilities, MMP2 and MMP9 also influence cell signaling. They can release the active ectodomain of fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR-1) and activate transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1). While secreted into the extracellular space, MMP2 and MMP9 can also localize to the surface of tumor cells. In this location, they can interact with complexes such as MT1-MMP/TIMP-2, integrins like αvβ3 and α3β1, and/or CD44, thereby facilitating local ECM degradation and enhancing the invasive potential of cancer cells [2, 7, 8, 13].
CD26 (DPPIV) and FAP-α (also known as seprase or F19 cell surface antigen) are serine proteases belonging to the dipeptidyl peptidase (DPP) family, characterized by their post-proline dipeptidyl aminopeptidase activity. These enzymes, when active, exist as transmembrane glycoprotein complexes, either homodimeric or heterodimeric, each weighing approximately 200 kDa. While their genes, FAP-α and DPPIV, are found in close proximity on chromosome 2 (at q23 and q24.3, respectively), and their encoded proteins share 52% amino acid sequence identity, they exhibit distinct cellular and substrate specificities. CD26 is a widely distributed peptidase that releases various biologically active peptides, influencing processes like cell growth, migration, invasion, neovascularization, and immune system activation. In contrast, FAP-α, which selectively cleaves larger proteins and possesses collagen type I-specific gelatinase activity, is primarily found in myofibroblast-like cells within tumor stroma, fibrotic and granulation tissues, and certain cancer cells. Both CD26 and FAP-α, along with MT1-MMP and gelatinases, are known to concentrate at sites of extracellular matrix degradation, specifically in invadopodia (F-actin-based membrane protrusions) or in lipid rafts, which are organized membranous micro-domains involved in cell-matrix adhesion.
Lipid rafts, characterized by their denser packing compared to the surrounding non-raft membrane, play a crucial role in cellular function. They act as organizational hubs for numerous signaling proteins, including various receptors (integrin and non-integrin types) and enzymes like kinases, phosphatases, and membrane-associated proteases, all of which are essential for regulating cell movement. The specific location of these proteins, whether within or outside lipid rafts, dictates their functional roles. These specialized membrane microdomains are intrinsically linked to the precise delivery, arrangement, and activation of molecules critical for cancer metastasis at the leading edge of migrating cells. Furthermore, lipid rafts are indispensable for the development and growth of membrane protrusions. Disrupting these rafts has been shown to diminish the migratory capacity of tumor cells.
The precise link between cytokine-driven increases in cancer cell migration and invasion, and the heightened activity of ECM-dedegrading enzymes concentrated in lipid rafts, remains to be fully understood. To investigate this, we treated both estrogen-dependent MCF7 and highly invasive, hormone-independent MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells with TNF-α. Our subsequent analysis focused on changes in cell migration, alongside the levels of two dipeptidyl peptidases (FAP-α and CD26) and three metalloproteases (MT1-MMP, MMP2, and MMP9). Furthermore, we examined how TNF-α influenced the concentrations of these proteases within detergent-resistant membranes (DRMs), specifically exploring the involvement of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway.
Breast cancer cells, specifically MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 lines, were grown in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The culture medium used was DMEM, enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 mg/ml streptomycin. Cells were seeded into either 12-well plates or 10 cm Petri dishes and allowed to proliferate until they achieved 70–90% confluency. Following this, the cells underwent an overnight starvation period in serum-free DMEM. Subsequently, they were exposed to 10 ng/ml TNF-α for 24 hours, with control groups receiving no TNF-α. After treatment, conditioned medium was harvested for substrate zymography, and the cells themselves were lysed for the extraction of both total RNA and protein.
Various reagents and materials were sourced for the study. The MEK/ERK inhibitor U0126 (CAS 109511–58-2) was acquired from Santa Cruz, while TNF-α (cyt-223-b) was supplied by ProSpec-Tany TechnoGene Ltd. For immuno-detection, a cholera toxin subunit B-HRP conjugate (C-34,780) was obtained from Molecular Probes. Blotting membranes (162–0094) were purchased from Bio-Rad, and Millicell Hanging Cell Culture Inserts (8.0 μm, PIEP15R48) came from Merck Millipore. A range of primary antibodies were utilized: rabbit anti-phospho-ERK1/2 (T202/Y204, Cell Signaling), mouse anti-ERK1/2 (L34F12, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-MMP-9 (AB19016, Merck Millipore), rabbit anti-MMP2 (VARP20016_T100, Aviva), rabbit anti-MMP14 (PA5–13,183, Thermo Scientific), rabbit anti-FAP-α (GTX102732, GeneTex), goat anti-DPPIV/CD26 (AF1180, R&D Systems), rabbit-anti-Cortactin (H-191, sc-11,408, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.), goat anti-actin (I-19, sc-1616, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.), and mouse anti-FL-2 (B6, sc-28,320, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.). Secondary antibodies for immuno-detection included HRP-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (sc-2313), donkey anti-goat (sc-2020), and goat anti-mouse (sc-2005), all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., along with Pierce™ECL Western Blotting Substrate (32,106, Thermo Scientific).
RNA was extracted from samples using an RNeasy kit (Qiagen, USA). Subsequently, RT-PCR reactions were carried out following the manufacturer's protocol for a one-step RT-PCR kit (Clontech Laboratories, Inc., A Takara Bio Company). Each 20 μl RT-PCR reaction mix incorporated 1 μl of the extracted total RNA and 0.25 pmol of each specific primer. The reverse transcription phase involved incubation at 50 °C for 1 hour, followed by 5 minutes at 94 °C. For FAP-α transcript amplification, the primer pair 5′-GCT GGA GCT AAG AAT CCC GTT GTT CG-3′ (sense) and 5′-TGC TTG GAG GAT AGC TTC CAA TGC T-3′ (antisense) was used, resulting in a 544 bp product. The PCR cycling conditions for FAP-α were 30 seconds at 94 °C, 45 seconds at 60 °C, and 1 minute at 68 °C, repeated for 30 cycles. To ensure no genomic DNA contamination, RT-free PCR controls were included. CD26 transcripts (473 bp) were amplified using the primers 5′- GGA AGA TGG AAC TGC TTA GTG GCA CG -3′ (sense) and 5′- TCT CAG CCC TTT ATC ATT CAC GCT GC -3′ (antisense). For MMP-14 transcripts (395 bp), the primer pair 5′-TCA TGA TCT TCT TTG CCG AGG GCT-3′ (sense) and 5′-TTT ATC AGG AAC AGA AGG CCG GGA-3′ (antisense) was employed. Following amplification, PCR products were separated and visualized on 1% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide. Images were then digitized, and densitometry analysis was performed using ImageJ software. For semi-quantitative analysis, the expression levels of the target transcripts were normalized against co-amplified actin, a housekeeping control. The actin primers, 5′-TAC AAT GAG CTG CGT GTG GCT CCC CCC G-3′ (sense) and 5′-AAT GGT GAT GAC CTG GCC GTC AGG C-3′ (antisense), produced a 479 bp product under the same amplification conditions. The pixel density of each PCR product was calculated relative to the actin transcript pixel density, which was assigned a value of 1.
To isolate membrane micro-domains, 15 x 10^6 cells underwent lysis for 30 minutes on ice. This lysis was performed using a TNE buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.4) supplemented with 1% Triton X-100 and a protease inhibitor cocktail. Following lysis, the samples were subjected to ultracentrifugation through a discontinuous sucrose gradient, specifically a 5%/30%/40% gradient. The preparation for ultracentrifugation involved combining the cell lysates in a 1:1 ratio with 85% sucrose in TNE buffer containing 1% Triton X-100. This 1 ml mixture was then carefully placed at the bottom of a 4.5 ml centrifuge tube. A layer of 2.7 ml of 30% sucrose was added above this mixture, followed by an overlay of 0.8 ml of 5% sucrose in TNE buffer. The centrifugation process was carried out in a Beckman Ti60 rotor at 35,000 RPM for 16 hours at 4 °C. After centrifugation, eight fractions, each 550 μl in volume, were collected sequentially from the top to the bottom of the tube. These fractions were then analyzed to identify detergent resistant membranes (DRM) and detergent soluble membranes (DSM). GM1 ganglioside and total protein content were assessed via dot blotting, utilizing a peroxidase-linked cholera toxin B subunit (CTX-HRP) and Ponceau S, respectively. Flotillin-2 content was determined by immunoblotting. DRM fractions, characterized by their low density, are known to exhibit higher concentrations of GM1 and flotillin-2, while high-density DSM fractions contain significantly lower levels of these markers.
Following fractionation, proteins were prepared for SDS-PAGE. Each protein fraction was first precipitated using 10% trichloroacetic acid, then rinsed twice with cold acetone. The resulting protein pellets were resuspended in 50 μl of 4x Laemmli sample buffer. Prior to electrophoresis, samples were heated for 10 minutes at 95 °C. Electrophoresis was conducted by loading 10 μl of each sample onto 10% SDS polyacrylamide gels. After separation, proteins were transferred overnight at 95 mA to nitrocellulose membranes. These membranes were then blocked for one hour at room temperature with 5% non-fat milk in PBS (pH 7.4), followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software for semi-quantitative digital image analysis, with results presented as ratios of integrated density values comparing treated to untreated control cells.
To assess gelatinolytic activity, conditioned media and sucrose gradient fractions were prepared by combining them with a sample buffer containing 62.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 0.05% bromophenol blue, at a 1:0.5 volume ratio. Thirty microliters of these non-reduced samples were then loaded onto 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels, which had been co-polymerized with 2 mg/ml gelatin. Following semi-native electrophoresis, the enzymes were reactivated by two washes with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) containing 2.5% Triton X-100 for 30 minutes at room temperature to remove SDS. Subsequently, the gels were incubated at 37 °C for 20 hours in a solution of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) supplemented with 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2, 1 μM ZnCl2, and 0.05% Brij-35. Finally, the gels were stained with 0.12% Coomassie blue and destained using a solution of 5% acetic acid and 10% ethanol in water.
To assess cell migration, a Transwell assay was performed using MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells. Initially, 2.5 x 10^4 cells, suspended in 1 ml of serum-free medium, were placed in the upper chamber of an 8 μm pore size insert within a 12-well plate. The lower chamber contained DMEM enriched with 1% FBS, acting as a chemoattractant. Cells were given time to adhere before being exposed to 10 ng/ml TNF-α and subsequently incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Following incubation, the inserts were carefully removed. Non-migratory cells on the upper membrane surface were cleared using a cotton swab. Cells that successfully migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were then fixed with 5% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 minutes at room temperature. After washing with PBS, these cells were treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 minutes. Staining was performed using a DAPI solution (Sigma-Aldrich Mounting Medium with DAPI). A Zeiss LSM 510meta microscope, set at 20× magnification (Plan Apochromat 20×/0,8), was used for examination. Images were captured from five random fields, and migrating cells were quantified using ImageJ software. The mean number of migrating cells was determined for each experimental condition, allowing for the calculation of differences in migration rates.
To assess wound closure rates, MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells were initially grown in Petri dishes until they reached 90% confluence in complete growth medium. Following an overnight starvation period, four distinct wounds were created in the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 μl pipet tip. After a gentle rinse, the cells were exposed to 10 ng/ml TNF-α. Imaging was performed immediately before TNF-α treatment and again after 24 hours. An inverted Zeiss microscope, fitted with a digital camera and a 10×/1.25 numerical aperture objective, captured these phase-contrast images. Image-Pro Plus software (version 5.1.2.59) was used for image acquisition, and ImageJ software (version 1.43u) was employed to quantify the wound areas.
To investigate how TNF-α influences the production and release of gelatinases, MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells were exposed to varying concentrations of TNF-α for 24 hours in serum-free conditions. This treatment led to a notable, dose-dependent rise in MMP9 levels, both within the cells (lysates) and in the surrounding culture medium. The most pronounced cellular reaction to TNF-α was observed when concentrations ranged from 10 to 100 ng/ml (Fig. 1a). Conversely, when examining MMP2, TNF-α treatment did not result in any significant alterations in its protein levels. This was consistently observed in both cell lysates, as determined by Western blotting, and in the conditioned media, where gelatin zymography was used for analysis (Fig. 1a-b). These findings indicate a selective effect of TNF-α, specifically increasing MMP9 synthesis and activity in breast cancer cells, while MMP2 levels remained largely unaffected. The data presented in Fig. 1, including bar graphs showing relative fold changes, represent the average results from at least three independent experiments, with statistical significance denoted by asterisks.
Our investigation into the impact of TNF-α on breast cancer cell movement revealed a clear increase in migration for both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cell lines. This observation was consistently demonstrated through two distinct experimental approaches: a wound healing assay and a Transwell migration assay. In the wound healing experiments, a noticeable difference in the rate of wound closure was observed. Cells exposed to TNF-α exhibited significantly faster closure of the created wound compared to their untreated counterparts. Specifically, after 24 hours, a 10 ng/ml concentration of TNF-α led to an approximately 6% improvement in wound closure for MDA-MB-231 cells and about a 9% improvement for MCF-7 cells, when compared to control cells that received no TNF-α. Visual representations of this effect, including representative phase-contrast images and bar graphs illustrating relative changes in wound closure, are provided in Fig. 2a. Further corroboration of this enhanced migratory behavior came from the Transwell assay. This method involved counting the number of cells that successfully traversed 8 µm diameter pores over a 24-hour period. The results from this independent assay reinforced the finding that TNF-α treatment promotes increased cell migration. Data from the Transwell assay, including representative images of migrating cells and bar graphs detailing the number of transmigrating cells, are presented in Fig. 2b. All reported data represent the mean ± SEM from three independent experiments, with statistically significant differences indicated (P < 0.05, P < 0.005, P < 0.0001) when compared to untreated control cells.
Our initial investigation focused on comparing the mRNA expression of CD26, FAP-α, and MT1-MMP in breast cancer cells. We used RT-PCR to analyze cells treated with 10 ng/ml TNF-α for 24 hours against untreated control cells. While all three genes showed an increase in mRNA transcript levels following TNF-α stimulation (as depicted in Fig. 3a), the effect on FAP-α and MT1-MMP expression was only minor. This pattern was consistent across both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cell lines. These mRNA findings were subsequently corroborated by examining the corresponding protein levels through Western blotting. Densitometric analysis of protein bands, normalized to actin, revealed a dose-dependent upregulation of all three proteins (Fig. 3b). However, significant alterations were predominantly observed for CD26. Although FAP-α protein levels also showed statistically significant increases, these were only apparent at higher TNF-α concentrations (ranging from 20 to 100 ng/ml). Figure 3 illustrates the impact of TNF-α on the expression of CD26, FAP-α, and MT1-MMP at both mRNA and protein levels. Specifically, Figure 3a details the RT-PCR analysis of transcript levels in MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells after 24 hours of stimulation with 10 ng/ml TNF-α or no treatment. Gene expression fold changes for CD26, FAP-α, and MT1-MMP in both cell lines are presented in bar graphs, normalized to actin. Figure 3b displays the Western blot analysis of protein levels from cell lysates, treated with varying TNF-α concentrations for 24 hours, using specific antibodies against CD26, FAP-α, and MT1-MMP. Bar graphs indicate fold changes in protein levels relative to TNF-α concentration, normalized to actin. All data represent the mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments, with *P < 0.05 indicating significance compared to untreated controls.
To investigate the impact of TNF-α on the localization of membrane-associated proteases within lipid rafts, a discontinuous sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation method was employed. This technique allowed for the separation of detergent resistant membranes (DRM), which are rich in lipid raft components, from detergent soluble membranes (DSM). MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells were either treated with 10 ng/ml TNF-α for 24 hours or left untreated. Following this, eight fractions were collected sequentially from the top of the gradient. To confirm the presence of lipid raft material, each fraction was analyzed for specific markers. Flotillin-2 content was assessed via Western blotting, while GM1 content was determined by dot blotting using a peroxidase-linked cholera toxin B subunit. Ponceau-S staining was also used to visualize total proteins across the fractions. Fraction 2 consistently exhibited the highest concentrations of both flotillin-2 and GM1, leading to its identification as the DRM fraction. Conversely, fraction 7, showing significantly lower levels of these markers, was designated as the DSM fraction. Immunoblotting was then utilized to visualize membrane-associated proteases in both the DRM and DSM fractions, as well as in whole cell lysates from TNF-α treated and untreated control cells. A notable finding was the increased concentration of FAP-α, CD26, and MT1-MMP in the DRM fractions of TNF-α stimulated MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells. Specifically, CD26 showed fold changes of 8.01 and 1.26, FAP-α 1.68 and 2.03, and MT1-MMP 3.84 and 25.47, respectively. Further analysis, using gelatin zymography and Western blotting, revealed a slight increase in both MMP9 and MMP2 concentrations within the DRM fraction following TNF-α induction, with fold changes of 1.23 and 1.37, respectively. Additionally, changes in the concentration of invadopodia-associated proteins, actin and cortactin, were observed in the DRM fractions of TNF-α treated cells through immunoblotting. Cortactin levels increased by a fold change of 2.02, and actin by 24.87, in the DRM fractions. Importantly, total protein levels of these invadopodia-associated proteins remained unchanged under TNF-α stimulation, indicating a redistribution rather than an overall increase in expression.
To investigate if membrane-associated proteases accumulate in lipid rafts following TNF-α stimulation, we analyzed the concentrations of FAP-α, CD26, and MT1-MMP within DRM fractions. These fractions were obtained from MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells, either untreated or exposed to 10 ng/ml TNF-α for 24 hours, using Western blotting. Figure 4b illustrates the distribution of these three transmembrane proteases across total cell lysates, low-density (DRM), and high-density (DSM) fractions from both TNF-α-treated and control cells. Our findings revealed elevated levels of all these proteins in the DRM fractions of TNF-α-treated cells compared to untreated controls in both cell lines. Additionally, increased levels of MMP9 and MMP2 were detected in the DRM fractions of TNF-α-treated cells through gelatin zymography and Western blotting. Notably, Figure 4c demonstrated that only the active forms of gelatinases were present in the DRM fractions.
To verify if TNF-α's effect on protease localization within lipid rafts is linked to cell migration, we examined actin and cortactin levels in sucrose gradient fractions from both TNF-α-treated and control cells. These proteins are crucial for invadopodia formation, which are membrane protrusions typical of aggressive cancer cells. Our analysis revealed that TNF-α treatment led to elevated concentrations of both actin and cortactin in the DRM fractions of MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells.
Our investigation into the impact of TNF-α on MDA-MB-231 cells revealed a significant elevation in ERK1/2 phosphorylation following TNF-α exposure, as determined by Western blot analysis (Fig. 5a). To further explore the role of the MAPK/ERK pathway, we utilized U-0126, a known selective inhibitor of MEK, which is crucial for activating ERK1/2 kinases. Pre-treating MDA-MB-231 cells with 10 μM U-0126 effectively prevented the TNF-α-induced secretion of MMP9 into the conditioned media (Fig. 5b). Subsequent Western blot analyses of MMP9 within the DRM fractions of cells that received both U-0126 pre-treatment and TNF-α stimulation demonstrated that inhibiting the MAPK/ERK pathway counteracted the TNF-α-driven increase in protease concentrations within these fractions (Fig. 5c). This inhibitory effect was also observed for MT1-MMP, despite the fact that TNF-α treatment did not cause a notable change in the overall levels of this protease. These findings collectively suggest a critical involvement of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway in mediating the TNF-α-induced accumulation of proteases in lipid rafts.
Tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), a crucial cytokine in inflammatory processes, has a complex role in cancer. While initially recognized for its ability to cause cell death in certain cancers, more recent research indicates its capacity to promote tumor growth. This includes stimulating processes like cellular transformation, multiplication, blood vessel formation, and the spread of cancer cells. Elevated TNF-α levels are commonly observed in many aggressive malignancies, such as breast cancer, and are often linked to poor patient outcomes. Although mainly generated by macrophages, TNF-α can also be produced by various stromal cells and even cancer cells themselves, allowing it to function both locally and on the producing cells. Despite its established role in boosting tumor cell movement and invasion, the precise mechanisms driving these effects remain unclear.
Our investigation utilized various in vitro techniques to evaluate how TNF-α influences the movement of breast cancer cells. We observed a significant increase in the migration of both hormone-dependent and aggressive triple-negative breast cancer cells when exposed to TNF-α. This was demonstrated through enhanced wound closure in a scratch assay and greater cell transit in a Transwell assay. These findings align with previous research indicating TNF-α's role in promoting migration in other cancer types, such as melanoma, bladder, cervical, and leukemia cells. A key observation in our study was that this TNF-α-stimulated cell migration correlated with elevated secretion of MMP9 into the culture medium, whereas MMP2 levels remained unchanged. While MMP2 expression is generally stable across cell types, MMP9 production and release are known to be highly responsive to biological cues. TNF-α is recognized as a potent physiological stimulant for MMP9 expression, an enzyme crucial for sustained cellular migration, including in cancer. Other studies have linked MMP9 to breast cancer cell migration and invasion, mediated by interactions with integrins and CD44 receptors. Furthermore, experiments with MMP9 knockout mice have highlighted its indispensable role in the migration of smooth muscle and immune cells, showing that interfering with MMP9-cell surface interactions can severely reduce cell motility and tumor invasiveness.
TNF-α treatment led to a significant increase in CD26, a dipeptidyl peptidase. Concurrently, all proteases examined showed a notable accumulation within lipid rafts following TNF-α exposure. This occurred irrespective of whether TNF-α caused substantial or minor alterations in their mRNA or protein levels, as seen with MT1-MMP.
Lipid rafts are increasingly recognized for their crucial involvement in cancer cell adhesion and migratory processes [18, 34]. These specialized membrane regions, rich in cholesterol and sphingolipids, resist non-ionic detergents and are directly linked to cellular adhesion structures like focal contacts and invadopodia. This connection highlights their importance in cell-extracellular matrix interactions and the breakdown of matrix components. In various malignant cancers, including breast cancer, these highly organized membrane domains are known to activate cell survival signaling pathways. Furthermore, they contribute to resistance against targeted cancer therapies, with their disruption shown to reduce this resistance in cancer cells [18]. Lipid rafts are notably concentrated at the leading edges of invadopodia in different breast cancer cell types. Intervening with cholesterol levels, for instance, using methyl-β cyclodextrin (MβCD) or statins, has been shown to hinder the disassembly of focal adhesions and impair both the formation and function of invadopodia [18, 21, 34, 35]. Invadopodia themselves are F-actin and cortactin-rich membrane protrusions that develop on the ventral surface of aggressive cancer cells, such as MDA-MB-231. They exhibit localized proteolytic activity against extracellular matrix components, a potency attributed to the concentration of proteolytic enzymes at sites of active actin polymerization [34–36]. Several vital invadopodial components, including proteases like MT1-MMP and CD26, have been identified within lipid rafts [18, 21, 37–39].
Our investigation revealed that external TNF-α stimulation led to an increased concentration of MT1-MMP, CD26, and FAP-α within the lipid rafts of various breast cancer cell lines. Furthermore, the lipid rafts of these TNF-α-treated cells showed an enrichment of the gelatinases MMP9 and MMP2. While MMP2 and MMP9 are typically secreted, previous research indicates their presence in smaller quantities on the surface of diverse cell types, including breast cancer cells. Interestingly, in the detergent-resistant membrane (DRM) fractions, we exclusively detected the active forms of MMP9 and MMP2, even though both pro-enzymes and active forms were present in the detergent-soluble membrane (DSM) fractions. This observation implies that lipid rafts might play a role in activating these membrane-associated gelatinases. Given the established link between cancer cell migration and invasiveness and the formation of actin- and cortactin-based protrusions, we proceeded to examine how TNF-α stimulation influenced the accumulation of these proteins in the lipid rafts of breast cancer cells. This was done to explore the potential involvement of invadopodia in TNF-α-driven cell migration, especially since TNF-α has been previously shown to induce invadopodia formation in MDA-MB-231 cells. Our findings indicated changes in the levels of both actin and cortactin within the lipid rafts of TNF-α-stimulated cells. Specifically for cortactin, only a truncated version, identified as cortactin splice variant 1 (SV1), was observed in the DRM fraction.
Various growth factors and cytokines, such as TNF-α, are known to control MMP9 expression through the ERK pathway. Prior research indicated that G-CSF could elevate MT1-MMP gene expression and protein production in hematopoietic cells, also facilitating MT1-MMP's integration into lipid rafts via a PI3K-dependent mechanism. Our current findings demonstrate that the increased presence of proteases in lipid rafts, stimulated by TNF-α, relies on the activation of the ERK signaling pathway. We further observed that using U-0126, a specific inhibitor of MEK/ERK, effectively prevented the TNF-α-driven accumulation of MMP9 and MT1-MMP within lipid rafts. Given that TNF-α had only a minor impact on MT1-MMP's mRNA and protein levels, its role in boosting protease incorporation into lipid rafts appears distinct from its function as an inducer of protease gene expression.
Our findings indicate that TNF-α promotes breast cancer cell migration. This effect is linked to an elevated presence of proteases within lipid rafts on the cell membrane. Specifically, MT1-MMP, CD26, and FAP-α are directly associated, while MMP9 and MMP2 are indirectly associated, with these lipid rafts. This process is mediated by the activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. Although the accumulation of protease activity in lipid rafts is recognized as vital for cellular migration and invasion, the precise molecular mechanisms governing this phenomenon remain largely unknown. Further research is needed to fully understand this intricate process and identify potential therapeutic targets.
