This retrospective study aimed to assess a single surgeon's 9-year experience with minimally invasive total knee arthroplasty (MIS-TKA), specifically examining the learning curve and trends in clinical outcomes. The research analyzed data from 4107 MIS-TKAs performed on 3403 patients between March 2004 and February 2013, utilizing a modified mini-midvastus approach. A standardized postsurgical care protocol was applied to all participants. Outcomes were tracked across consecutive 3-month intervals to identify changes in surgical proficiency, radiographic alignment, and patient functional scores. Key findings revealed a significant increase in MIS-TKA procedures performed over the study period. As the surgeon's experience grew, tourniquet time decreased substantially, from an average of 70 minutes to approximately 35 minutes. Intraoperative complications, totaling 65 (1.68%), showed a significant downward trend. Radiographic malalignment also decreased, with a steady, desired alignment of 6° achieved by the 15th interval. Patient functional assessments, including the Knee Society Score (KSS) and general function scores, demonstrated notable improvements, rising from 87.4 to 91.5 and 92.6 to 96.8, respectively. The study concluded that while initial proficiency in MIS-TKA can be attained within a year, continuous experience leads to enhanced technical skill, improved postoperative alignment, and better patient function.

Minimally invasive total knee arthroplasty (MIS-TKA) is increasingly favored for treating advanced osteoarthritis, offering benefits such as reduced pain and faster recovery compared to conventional TKA. Conventional TKA, with its larger incision, can disrupt the quadriceps mechanism, lead to greater soft tissue trauma, and result in prolonged pain, hospital stays, and delayed rehabilitation. MIS-TKA aims to mitigate these issues through minimized surgical trauma, leading to quicker functional recovery. However, MIS-TKA is a technically complex procedure where success hinges on accurate component alignment, ligament balance, prosthetic choice, and surgeon expertise. Concerns have been raised regarding MIS-TKA, including potentially longer operative times, increased risks of tourniquet-related complications like ischemia and infection, and a higher likelihood of component malalignment due to limited visualization.
Conflicting results exist in clinical studies comparing MIS-TKA and conventional TKA, with recent RCTs offering no definitive conclusions. While surgical experience is acknowledged as crucial for MIS-TKA success, no prior studies have directly compared early versus late learning curve phases for MIS-TKA clinical outcomes.
This study aimed to investigate whether surgeons could achieve proficiency in MIS-TKA during the latter phase of their learning curve. Specifically, it questioned if postoperative alignment variations would improve and if patients' functional outcomes would be enhanced in this later stage.
This retrospective study analyzed 4107 minimally invasive total knee arthroplasties (MIS-TKA) performed by a single surgeon between March 2004 and February 2013. MIS-TKA was defined by a wound incision measuring less than 12 cm in knee extension and a modified mini-midvastus approach. Excluded from the analysis were revision TKAs (n=105), conventional TKAs (n=43), and cases involving multipurpose surgery. Conventional TKAs excluded patients with severe rheumatoid arthritis (n=16), severe trauma (n=6), hemophilia (n=10), HIV (n=8), or other immune diseases (n=3). Multipurpose surgeries included TKA combined with tumor resection or implant removal in the same incision. The final cohort comprised 4107 MIS-TKAs. Patient demographics revealed a majority of female patients (77.3%, n=2632), with a mean age of 71 years, consistent across genders. Mean body mass index (BMI) was 27.2 Kg/m² for males and 29.7 Kg/m² for females, with a significant proportion of both genders classified as obese (52.2% of males, 65.4% of females) based on Taiwanese Department of Health criteria. Osteoarthritis was the primary indication for surgery (94%, n=3858), followed by rheumatoid arthritis (2.2%, n=91) and other conditions (3.8%, n=158). Preoperatively, the average range of motion (ROM) was 131.1° ± 11.4°. Bilateral TKAs were performed on 704 patients (34.3%, 1408 knees), while 2699 patients (65.9%) underwent unilateral TKA, resulting in an equal distribution of right and left knees analyzed. Data collection received ongoing Institutional Review Board approval from Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taiwan.
Surgical proficiency in minimally invasive total knee arthroplasty (MIS-TKA) was assessed using three key indicators: tourniquet time, patellar tracking, and intraoperative complications. To account for variations in wound closure times among less experienced surgeons, tourniquet time was chosen as the primary metric for evaluating changes in technical skill, rather than overall surgical duration. The tourniquet was applied at the start of the skin incision, inflated to a pressure of systolic blood pressure plus 110 mmHg (with a minimum of 260 mmHg), and released once the prosthetic components were securely cemented in place. Following tourniquet deflation, the knee remained in full extension until the bone cement began to harden, after which junior surgeons completed the wound closure. To mitigate the risk of anterior knee pain and subsequent revision surgery, all patellae were resurfaced. Patellar tracking was evaluated using a modified "towel clip" technique, categorized as "excellent," "good," or "concerned." A comprehensive record was kept of all intraoperative complications, including soft tissue injuries, iatrogenic fractures, implant malposition (such as prosthesis overhang), and issues arising from bone cutting, such as insufficient cuts leading to postoperative flexion contracture, excessive cuts requiring thicker polyethylene inserts for stability, or over-cutting of the patella (leaving less than 11 mm thickness).
Radiographic assessment involved bipedal standing anterior-posterior views of the knee in full extension. A senior radiologist at a different hospital independently measured angular deviations using SmartIris software. The tibia-femoral angle, representing the coronal plane alignment between the femoral and tibial anatomical axes, was analyzed. A neutral alignment was defined as 6° of valgus, with deviations exceeding 3° classified as varus (<3.0°) or valgus (>9.0°), aligning with recommendations for normal tibiofemoral joint alignment (5°-7° valgus).
Knee function was assessed using KSS and functional scores six to eight months post-surgery. Data for 110 patients were unavailable during this period.
The surgical approach involved a modified incision, angled approximately 20° horizontally compared to the standard midvastus method. For the distal femur, an intramedullary guide was employed, setting the alignment at a 6° valgus angle. Optimal femoral external rotation was achieved by using Whiteside's line and the posterior condyle axis (PCA) as reference points. The proximal tibia was cut perpendicular to its mechanical axis with an extramedullary alignment guide. Two types of TKA prostheses were utilized: the Scorpio NRG (Stryker Orthopaedics, USA) for the first 18 months and the NexGen High Flex (Zimmer Inc., USA) for the subsequent period. Following component implantation and cementing, joint stability and implant positioning were reassessed. The thickness of bone fragments removed during surgery was also documented.
All patients received a standardized postsurgical care regimen. Rehabilitation began immediately with continuous passive motion (CPM) and full weight-bearing. Antibiotics were administered intravenously for one day, and thromboprophylaxis was given to high-risk individuals. Drains were removed within one to two days. These rehabilitation protocols remained consistent throughout the study period.
Descriptive statistics for the data were presented as mean ± SD. Outcomes and variables were analyzed across consecutive 3-month intervals, determined by the surgery date. Linear regression, using the equation y = mx + b, was employed to assess changes over time, with 'm' indicating the rate of increase or decrease. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 16.0), with a p-value of ≤0.05 deemed statistically significant.
Throughout the study period, there was a significant increase in the number of minimally invasive total knee arthroplasties (MIS-TKAs) performed, with a growth rate of 1.3% every three months. The average tourniquet time, initially 70 minutes in the first year, progressively decreased to approximately 35 minutes by the 30th interval as the surgeon's experience grew. Concurrently, intraoperative assessments indicated a significant improvement in patellar balancing, with an increasing percentage of knees achieving excellent outcomes.
A total of 65 intraoperative complications, representing 1.58% of the cases, were documented. Soft tissue injuries included two superficial medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries and one total popliteal tendon tear. Bone-related complications involved inappropriate bone cuts in the posterior femur (10 cases), distal femur (10 cases), lateral tibia (9 cases), and patella (9 cases). Iatrogenic fractures occurred in the femoral lateral condyle (10 cases), medial condyle (6 cases), femur shaft (2 cases), and tibia plateau (2 cases). Additionally, there were three instances of femoral prosthesis malposition and one of tibial prosthesis malposition. Notably, the frequency of these complications showed a decreasing trend as operative experience and time progressed.
The average tibia-femoral angle in the coronal plane was 6.5° ± 0.1°, with 89.0% of knees falling between 5.0° and 8.0°. Initially, tibia-femoral angles showed considerable variation, which persisted until the 15th three-month interval. While the average alignment angles remained consistent across all measurements at three-month intervals, the proportion of malalignment decreased, demonstrating a trend towards a narrower range over time. Steady, desired alignment of 6° was achieved by the 15th three-month interval. However, a minor increase in alignment deviations was observed thereafter, attributed to a change in prosthetic components from Stryker to Zimmer.
Measurements taken at 3-month intervals showed statistically significant improvements in both KSS knee and functional scores. Initially, the mean KSS knee score was 87.4, rising to 91.5 by follow-up. Similarly, the KSS function score increased from 92.6 to 96.8, demonstrating a notable enhancement compared to the early learning curve stages.
This study has several limitations. Primarily, it reflects the learning curve of a single surgeon, which is inherently variable and influenced by individual training and innate abilities. Analyzing a learning curve across multiple surgeons, each with unique skills, preferences, personalities, and surgical volumes, would present significant challenges in drawing definitive conclusions. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of this study, despite being conducted at a single institution with a large patient cohort, means that certain variables were not controlled. The absence of complete or available long-term follow-up data also prevents comprehensive analysis of many clinical outcomes and the survivorship of knee components. It is also important to acknowledge that the learning curve presented may not solely represent the individual surgeon but also the performance of the entire surgical team. Enhancements in anesthesia, surgical support staff, critical care, nursing, and other aspects of patient management are intrinsically linked to surgical performance. Consequently, the findings may not be directly generalizable to different healthcare settings. Nevertheless, this series of 4107 MIS-TKA cases, by examining changes in surgical skills, radiographic outcomes, and functional results over a nine-year period, offers valuable insights for both advocating and questioning the use of MIS-TKA.
Demand for MIS-TKA is increasing globally, including in Taiwan, where cases in 2013 were 2.5 times higher than in 2004. This trend is attributed to societal shifts, increased information access, evolving patient demographics and expectations, longer lifespans, and health insurance policies.
Learning curves are inherent to new surgical techniques, with prior MIS-TKA studies suggesting optimal experience is gained within 10 to 100 operations, often with stringent patient selection. This study, however, utilized MIS-TKA for 98.9% of primary TKA patients. Steady postoperative alignment was achieved by the surgeon after approximately 280 cases, corresponding to the fourth 3-month interval, indicating the completion of their learning curve.
Advancing MIS-TKA proficiency involves increasing surgical volume and adapting surgical approaches, instruments, and team support. A modified mini-midvastus incision, while beneficial for restoring extension, can be limited by the quadriceps muscle's bulk, obstructing the surgical view. To overcome this, thorough quadriceps undermining, the use of a specialized angled Hohmann retractor for the patella, and MIS-specific cutting jigs are crucial for improving visualization. Maintaining controlled knee flexion and extension throughout the procedure is also essential for creating adequate intra-articular working space.
A competent surgeon fosters a learning environment, educating ancillary staff to enhance team synergy, which significantly impacts surgical outcomes and MIS-TKA success. For instance, experienced support staff proactively monitoring PMMA polymerization timing helps reduce tourniquet time.
Over nine years, the surgeon's proficiency in MIS-TKA steadily grew. This resulted in a significant reduction in tourniquet time, halved from 70 to 35 minutes, alongside improved patella tracking and fewer intraoperative complications.
Certain intraoperative complications are predictable with MIS-TKA. Inappropriate posterior femur condyle bone cutting can lead to femur component malrotation, resulting in patellar tracking issues and poor posterior condylar offset. To improve visualization of the posterior femur condyle, posterior cuts may require measurement in full flexion. Overcutting the lateral tibia bone can cause valgus malalignment, which preoperative templating might help prevent. Unequal femur condylar bone cutting, stemming from notch chamfer malposition, can consequently cause iatrogenic femur condylar fractures. However, employing effective surgical approaches, appropriate retractors, and experienced surgical skills can collectively mitigate these complications.
Surgical proficiency significantly influences outcomes, as evidenced by improvements in postoperative radiographic alignment and patient functional scores. The study's findings demonstrate that MIS-TKA outcomes, in terms of both efficiency and quality, were markedly better in the later stages of the surgeon's learning curve compared to the initial phase. Consequently, the influence of the learning curve should be a key consideration in future MIS-TKA research.
This 9-year study of a single surgeon's MIS-TKA experience confirms that while complications are possible, accumulating experience leads to significant improvements in surgical skills, postoperative alignment, and patient function. The findings underscore the importance of considering the surgeon's learning curve when implementing or evaluating MIS-TKA.
