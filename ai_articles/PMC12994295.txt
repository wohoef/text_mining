This study investigated the role of CD81, a tetraspanin membrane protein, in osteosarcoma, a type of bone cancer. While CD81 has been linked to cancer cell proliferation and metastasis in other contexts, its specific involvement in osteosarcoma was largely unexplored. To address this, researchers first examined CD81 expression across various cell lines, including the human osteoblast hFOB1.19 and osteosarcoma lines Saos2, MG63, and 143B. Subsequently, they employed siRNA to inhibit CD81 expression in osteosarcoma cells and then evaluated key cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, and invasion. The study also delved into the molecular mechanisms by analyzing the phosphorylation of Akt, Erk, JNK, and p38 via Western blotting, and by measuring the expression levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP). For in vivo assessment, a CRISPR/Cas9 system was utilized to create a stable CD81 knockout in 143B cells, which were then transplanted into mice to observe effects on tumor formation and lung metastasis compared to control groups. The findings revealed that CD81 was present in both osteoblast and all osteosarcoma cell lines, with the 143B osteosarcoma line showing notably high expression. Inhibition of CD81 led to a reduction in osteosarcoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, this inhibition suppressed the phosphorylation of Akt and Erk, and decreased the expression of MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP, with MMP-9 exhibiting the most significant reduction. In the animal model, mice receiving CD81-knockout 143B cells demonstrated significantly less tumor growth and lung metastasis. In conclusion, these results suggest that CD81 inhibition can suppress intracellular signaling pathways, thereby reducing tumorigenesis and lung metastasis in osteosarcoma cells.

Osteosarcoma, the most common primary malignant bone tumor, predominantly affects children and young adults [1]. Historically, in the 1970s, amputation was the sole treatment, resulting in a grim 5-year survival rate below 20%. While chemotherapy has dramatically improved patient outcomes, increasing the 5-year survival to 65-70% and often allowing limb preservation, a significant challenge remains [2]. A substantial portion of patients, 30-40%, still face tumor recurrence or metastasis, particularly to the lungs [3]. Lung metastasis carries a dire prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of only 20% [4]. Given the limitations of current chemotherapy and the lack of new effective treatments for over four decades, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.
Humans possess 33 identified tetraspanin types, such as CD9, CD63, CD81, CD82, and CD151. These membrane proteins, characterized by four transmembrane domains, act as molecular organizers or facilitators by forming complexes with other membrane proteins. They can also create tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs) on the cell surface. Through their role in anchoring integrins and other proteins at TEMs, tetraspanins regulate various cellular functions, including motility, fusion, and proliferation, by influencing intracellular signaling. This regulatory capacity suggests a potential impact on the invasion, proliferation, and metastasis of malignant bone tumors.
CD81, a 26 kDa tetraspanin protein, is found in various cell types. While not crucial for T and B lymphocyte development, studies in CD81-knockout mice indicate its potential importance for B cell proliferation and immune function [7, 8]. Beyond immune roles, CD81 is implicated in regulating mitosis [9] and increasing the motility and metastatic capacity of hepatocarcinoma and malignant melanoma cells [10, 11]. Its recognition as an exosome biomarker [12] suggests that new anticancer therapies targeting CD81 could be beneficial. Although CD81 expression has been observed in human osteoblast (hFOB1.19) and osteosarcoma (MG63) cells [13], its specific contribution to osteosarcoma progression remains unexplored. This paper aims to elucidate CD81's role and underlying molecular mechanisms in osteosarcoma.
Human osteosarcoma cell lines Saos2 (CRL-7939), MG63 (CRL-1472), and 143B (CRL-8303), along with the human fetal osteoblast line hFOB1.19 (CRL-11372), were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Rockville, MD, USA). These cells were grown in 10 cm dishes at 37 °C with 5% CO2, using Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin. Osteosarcoma cells underwent sub-culturing every 2–3 days, while hFOB1.19 cells were sub-cultured every 4–5 days. Cells were passaged with 0.05% trypsin once they reached 70–80% confluence.
For cell transfection, the growth medium was first exchanged for antibiotic-free DMEM containing 10% FBS. Subsequently, 1.0 nM of each siRNA was prepared by incubating it with RNAiMAX and Opti-MEM for 15 minutes. Cells were then transfected with these siRNA preparations following the manufacturer's guidelines. Three distinct purified siRNA duplexes (siCD81-A, siCD81-B, siCD81-C) specifically targeting the human CD81 gene were acquired from Qiagen, alongside a negative control siRNA (siNeg).
Gene expression analysis involved several steps. Initially, total RNA was isolated from the cells using ISOGEN II (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). This RNA was then converted into cDNA through reverse transcription, a process facilitated by a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Subsequently, real-time RT-PCR was performed to quantify specific gene expression. This was achieved using a Real-time PCR Master Mix (Toyobo) and a 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The probes and primers for this assay, designed by Life Technologies' Assays-by-Design Service, targeted CD81, MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP genes. The PCR cycling parameters included 40 cycles, each comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 15 seconds, followed by annealing and extension at 60 °C for 1 minute. Gene expression changes were determined relatively using the comparative threshold cycle (CT) method, with 18S rRNA serving as the internal control for normalization. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the final results represent the average of four independent experiments.
To analyze CD81 expression via ELISA, cells were initially cultured in 12-well plates until 90% confluence, with a seeding density of 1.0 × 10^6 cells/well in growth medium. Following this, the cells were detached and resuspended to a concentration of 1.0 × 10^6 cells/ml. Cell pellets were then isolated by centrifugation at 5000×g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. To disrupt cell membranes, these pellets underwent two freeze-thaw cycles. Finally, CD81 expression was quantified using a Cusabio Biotech Co., Ltd. ELISA kit, with optical density measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.
Protein extraction from cells began with two washes in PBS, followed by lysis in RIPA buffer. For analysis, 35 μg protein aliquots underwent SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequent transfer to Hybond-P polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. These membranes were then probed with specific primary human monoclonal antibodies against total and phosphorylated forms of ERK, p38, JNK, and Akt (all at 1:1000 dilution). To ensure consistent loading, an anti-β-actin antibody (1:1000) served as a control. After washing with TBS-Tween 20, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000) were applied for one hour. Finally, chemiluminescence emission was detected using a Chemi-Lumi One Marker.
For flow cytometry analysis, osteosarcoma cells were initially cultured in 12-well plates at 1.0 × 10^5 cells/well under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). After 24 hours, cells were collected, washed twice with PBS, and then suspended at a concentration of 1 × 10^6 cells/40 μl. These suspensions were incubated with 25 μl of CD81-PE in the dark at 4°C for 45 minutes. Following two more washes, 500 μl of PBS was added to create single-cell suspensions for subsequent analysis using a FACS Vantage flow cytometer.
To assess cell proliferation, 143B and MG63 cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 10^3 cells per well, each containing 200 μl of medium. The cells were then separated into two experimental groups: a control siRNA group and a siCD81 group. siRNA transfections were conducted the subsequent day, following the previously outlined protocol. Cell proliferation was monitored over a 72-hour period, with measurements taken every 24 hours. At each measurement point, 10 μl of 0.45 mg/ml MTT reagent was introduced to each well. Following a 4-hour incubation at 37 °C, 100 μl of SDS was added to dissolve the resulting formazan crystals. Absorbance was then quantified at 550 nm using a spectrophotometer. All experimental conditions were performed in triplicate.
To evaluate cell motility, scratch wound healing assays were performed. 143B and MG63 cells were initially seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 10^5 cells/well and allowed to reach about 90% confluence. These cells were then separated into two experimental groups: a control siRNA group and a siCD81 group. A "scratch" was created in each well using a CELL Scratcher. Following this, the wounded cell monolayers were incubated in serum-free migration assay buffer, and siRNA transfection occurred the next day. Microscopic images were taken at 0 and 48 hours from the identical location in each well. ImageJ software was employed to quantify the wound healing area. Each sample was tested in triplicate.
To assess cell invasion, a Cell Cytoselect 24-well kit from Cell Biolabs (USA) was utilized following the manufacturer's guidelines. 143B or MG63 cells, at a density of 2.5 × 10^6, were introduced into the upper chambers of the wells. These cells were treated with either 1.0 nM siCD81-A or a negative control siRNA (siNeg) and suspended in serum-free medium. The lower chambers contained DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After a 48-hour incubation period at 37 °C, non-invading cells on the upper membrane surface were carefully removed using cotton swabs. The cells that successfully invaded and adhered to the bottom surface of the membranes were then fixed with methanol for 10 minutes, followed by a 10-minute staining with 0.5% crystal violet at room temperature. Cell counts were performed by examining three random microscopic fields. Following a wash step, the inserts were moved to new, empty wells, and 200 µl of extraction solution was added. After a 10-minute incubation, the optical density (OD) was measured at 550 nm.
To inactivate the CD81 gene in 143B cells, a CRISPR/Cas9 system was employed. This involved identifying a 140 bp sequence within the N-terminal CD81 region (NM_004356) for targeted double-strand breaks. A 20-base target sequence (TGAGGTGGCCGCCGGCATCT) was selected to achieve CD81 knockout. Guide RNAs (gRNAs), formed by annealing two complementary oligonucleotides, were then ligated into a pLentiCRISPR v2 vector containing Cas9, obtained from GeCKO Laboratory. These vectors were packaged in HEK293FT cells (from Gojo Kami) before being introduced into 143B cells. Successful CD81 silencing in the resulting Clone KO1 and KO2 cells was verified through genomic sequencing of the edited locus, real-time RT-PCR, and flow cytometry.
To assess tumor formation and metastasis in vivo, male BALB/c nude mice, aged four weeks, were obtained from Shimizu Laboratory Supplies. All animal procedures adhered to institutional guidelines and received approval from the Animal Care Committee of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (code no. M29–137). For tumor formation studies, 5.0 × 10^6 143B or 143B-KOCD81 (KO1 or KO2) cells were subcutaneously injected into the backs of the mice. Tumor sizes were monitored every five days, and volumes were determined using the formula V = L × W^2 / 2, where L and W denote the longest and shortest diameters. Twenty days post-inoculation, mice were euthanized, and tumor weights were recorded. In metastasis assays, 8.0 × 10^5 143B or 143B-KOCD81 (KO1) cells were injected into the lateral tail veins. After four weeks, the lungs were harvested. To differentiate tumor colonies from lung tissue, metastatic lungs were submerged in Bouin solution, and metastases were visually counted. Lungs were then fixed, embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) for imaging at 40× and 100× magnification using Nikon software.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22. All experiments were run either two or three times, and results are presented as the mean plus or minus the standard deviation. To identify significant differences between groups, a parametric one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by Tukey-Kramer tests for specific comparisons. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Our investigation into CD81 expression involved analyzing both mRNA and protein levels across various cell lines. We examined a human osteoblast cell line, hFOB1.19, alongside three human osteosarcoma cell lines: Saos2, MG63, and 143B. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of CD81 mRNA revealed no significant differences in expression between the hFOB1.19 osteoblasts and any of the osteosarcoma cell lines (Fig. 1a). However, when assessing CD81 protein expression via ELISA, we observed its presence in all tested cell lines (Fig. 1b). Notably, the 143B osteosarcoma cell line exhibited a significantly higher level of CD81 protein compared to hFOB1.19 cells and the other osteosarcoma cell lines. Furthermore, a correlation was observed where increased CD81 expression corresponded with a higher propagation potential among the three osteosarcoma cell types. This suggests a potential link between CD81 levels and the aggressiveness of osteosarcoma.
To assess knockdown efficacy, we utilized MG63 cells, known for CD81 expression, and 143B cells, which displayed the highest CD81 levels among the osteosarcoma lines examined. Both cell types were transfected with three distinct siCD81 molecules and a negative control. After 48 hours, CD81 mRNA levels were quantified. All three siCD81 variants (siCD81-A, siCD81-B, and siCD81-C) effectively reduced CD81 mRNA expression across all tested cell types. SiCD81-A demonstrated the most potent inhibition (Fig. 1c), leading to its selection for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, ELISA confirmed that siCD81-A significantly suppressed CD81 protein expression in both MG63 and 143B cells (Fig. 1d).
To investigate the impact of CD81 on the malignant characteristics of osteosarcoma cells, we conducted experiments on MG63 and 143B cell lines. These cells were treated with either siCD81, designed to inhibit CD81 expression, or a control siRNA. Subsequently, we assessed their proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities. Our findings revealed a significant reduction in cell proliferation after CD81 inhibition. While no immediate differences were observed within the first 48 hours for both MG63 and 143B cells, a marked suppression in proliferation was evident by 72 hours, as measured by an MTT assay (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the migratory capacity of these osteosarcoma cells was also significantly impaired. Using a scratch wound healing assay, we observed a substantial decrease in migration in both siCD81-treated MG63 and 143B cells after 48 hours (Fig. 2b). Similarly, the invasive potential of the cells was significantly diminished following CD81 knockdown. An invasion assay demonstrated a notable reduction in invasion capabilities in siCD81-transfected MG63 and 143B cells at the 48-hour mark (Fig. 2c). These results collectively indicate that downregulating CD81 expression effectively suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of osteosarcoma cells. The data from these experiments, including representative images and quantitative measurements, are presented in Fig. 2, illustrating the inhibitory effects of CD81 knockdown on these key tumorigenic activities.
Following CD81 downregulation in MG63 and 143B osteosarcoma cells, Western blotting was employed to analyze the phosphorylation status of key signaling proteins. This included Akt, known for its role in osteosarcoma cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration, as well as Erk, JNK, and p38, all components of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. The results indicated a clear suppression of both Akt and Erk phosphorylation in both cell lines. Conversely, JNK phosphorylation showed a slight increase. Interestingly, CD81 inhibition had no discernible impact on p38 phosphorylation in either MG63 or 143B cells. These findings, depicted in Figure 3, highlight the differential effects of CD81 knockdown on these intracellular signaling pathways.
Our study explored the connection between CD81 expression and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP), known contributors to osteosarcoma invasion. We observed that reducing CD81 levels in MG63 and 143B cells led to a significant decrease in the expression of MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP. Notably, MMP-9 expression showed the most pronounced suppression in both cell lines. This indicates a strong link between CD81 inhibition and the reduced expression of these invasion-related MMPs.
Our research successfully developed CD81 knockout osteosarcoma 143B cells through the application of the CRISPR/Cas9 system. This involved permanently eliminating CD81 protein expression. Following the design of a specific CD81 target sequence and subsequent transfection, we identified two distinct CD81 knockout 143B cell lines, designated KO1 and KO2. Genomic sequencing confirmed that these lines exhibited either base deletions or insertions across both alleles, specifically labeled as KO1-A, KO1-B, KO2-A, and KO2-B (Fig. 5a). Further analysis using quantitative RT-PCR demonstrated a significant reduction in CD81 mRNA expression in both KO1 and KO2 cells when compared to the wild-type 143B cells (Fig. 5b). Consistent with these mRNA findings, flow cytometry also revealed a clear and substantial decrease in CD81 protein expression within the KO1 and KO2 cell lines (Fig. 5c).
To investigate the in vivo impact of CD81 expression on osteosarcoma growth and lung metastasis, we utilized 143B cells with CD81 knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9. For tumor propagation, an in vivo model was established by subcutaneously transplanting osteosarcoma cells into mice. The CD81 knockout 143B cell groups (KO1 and KO2) demonstrated significantly reduced tumor propagation compared to the 143B wild-type (control) group (Fig. 6a). While no discernible difference was observed on day 5 post-transplantation, significant differences in tumor growth emerged from day 10, becoming statistically significant by day 15 (Fig. 6b). By day 20, both the volume and weight of the resected tumors were markedly lower in both the KO1 and KO2 groups (Fig. 6b and c). These results strongly suggest a role for CD81 in osteosarcoma propagation. To assess CD81's involvement in lung metastasis, a separate metastasis model was created by injecting osteosarcoma cells into the caudal vein of mice. After 28 days, the mice were euthanized, and their lungs were examined. The KO1 group exhibited a clearly lower number of lung metastases compared to the control group (Fig. 6d). Visual quantification of metastatic nodules by two experienced researchers (NM and TS) confirmed a significantly reduced count in the KO1 group (Fig. 6e). Further pathological examination using HE staining revealed a greater abundance of micro-metastases in the control group. Notably, in the control group, intravascular osteosarcoma cells were observed invading the lung parenchyma through the vascular walls. Conversely, in the KO1 group, cells remained confined within the vessels, with virtually no invasion of the lung parenchyma through vascular walls (Fig. 6f). Collectively, these in vivo findings indicate that CD81 plays a crucial role in the propagation, metastasis, and invasion of osteosarcoma cells.
CD81, a protein found in various cancer cells, is known to form complexes with other tetraspanins and integrins, influencing cancer cell propagation, invasion, and metastasis. Our research revealed that osteosarcoma cells also express CD81 mRNA and protein. Notably, higher levels of CD81 protein in these cells corresponded with increased proliferation and invasion rates, suggesting a potential link between CD81 expression and osteosarcoma malignancy.
CD81's role in cell proliferation, particularly through the Erk/MAPK pathway, is well-established, with previous research linking it to tumor growth in squamous cell carcinoma, as well as lung and breast cancers. While its involvement in the proliferation of various carcinomas is recognized, its specific impact on osteosarcoma cell proliferation remained unexplored until this study. Our findings demonstrate a significant reduction in osteosarcoma cell propagation upon CD81 inhibition. This effect appears to be mediated by the suppression of Erk phosphorylation, mirroring observations in hepatocellular carcinoma. This suggests CD81's regulatory influence on osteosarcoma cell proliferation via Erk phosphorylation. Additionally, we noted a modest increase in JNK phosphorylation when CD81 was inhibited. Given that JNK activation can induce apoptosis and curb tumor cell proliferation, this further implies CD81's involvement in osteosarcoma proliferation.
CD81 is implicated in cancer metastasis, as evidenced by its role in enhancing cell motility and invasion. For instance, in melanoma, CD81 elevates Akt-dependent MT1-MMP levels, contributing to increased invasiveness [11]. Similarly, it promotes integrin-mediated cell migration in ES and dendritic cells [22, 23]. Furthermore, a complex formed by CD81 and CD9 has been shown to drive integrin- and PKCα-mediated migration in breast cancer cells [24]. Our in vitro findings in osteosarcoma cells align with these observations, demonstrating that reducing CD81 expression (knockdown) significantly curtails their migration and invasion capabilities. This suggests that similar molecular mechanisms observed in other cancer types are likely at play in osteosarcoma. A key aspect of cancer cell migration and invasion involves various molecules, including integrins and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) such as MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP. These MMPs are crucial for cancer metastasis because they degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM), which normally acts as a physical barrier to spreading cancer cells. Humans possess 23 types of MMPs [29]. Among these, MMP-2 and MMP-9 are particularly important for breaking down collagen IV, a major component of the ECM [30]. Additionally, MT1-MMP, found on the surface of cancer cells, activates MMP-2 [31]. In osteosarcoma, elevated expression of MMP-2 is associated with a poorer prognosis [32, 33]. High levels of MMP-9 in osteosarcoma are also linked to increased proliferation, metastasis, and migration [34, 35]. Notably, 143B osteosarcoma cells exhibit higher expression of MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP compared to other osteosarcoma cell lines [36, 37]. Our research revealed that inhibiting CD81 expression in osteosarcoma cells led to a significant downregulation of MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP expression. This suppression was accompanied by a reduction in the activation of Akt and Erk pathways, both known to be involved in cell migration and invasion. These results indicate that CD81 influences the expression of MMP-2, MMP-9, and MT1-MMP by modulating Akt and Erk phosphorylation, thereby regulating osteosarcoma cell invasion. Moreover, our in vivo studies showed that CD81 is involved in lung metastasis of osteosarcoma cells. Given the correlation between MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression levels and life expectancy in osteosarcoma patients [32–35], we propose that inhibiting CD81 expression could potentially lead to a reduction in MMP-2 and MMP-9 levels, ultimately improving clinical outcomes for osteosarcoma patients.
The CRISPR/Cas9 system, first demonstrated for in vitro sequence-specific DNA cleavage by Jinek et al. in 2012, was later shown by Cong et al. to be applicable to human and mouse cells. This technology enables functional gene knockout by cleaving target DNA, leading to frame shift mutations. Its utility extends to studying various cancers, including carcinomas, sarcomas, and human osteosarcomas. In our study, we utilized the CRISPR/Cas9 system to manipulate the CD81 gene sequence in human osteosarcoma 143B cells, effectively knocking out CD81 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. This intervention significantly suppressed the tumor-forming capability of these cells in vivo, suggesting a consistent mechanism of action with in vitro observations. Furthermore, we observed a marked reduction in lung metastasis in these CD81 knockout cells in vivo. Pathological analysis revealed distinct differences between the control and CD81 knockout groups. Control osteosarcoma cells readily invaded through vascular walls to form metastatic lesions within the lung parenchyma. Conversely, in the CD81 knockout group, osteosarcoma cells largely remained confined within vessels, with minimal invasion of lung parenchyma and significantly fewer metastatic lesions. We attribute this reduced invasion and metastasis to the suppressed MMP-mediated invasive potential resulting from CD81 inhibition. While our investigation did not delve into the intricate details of CD81 inhibition's impact on downstream Erk, JNK, and Akt signaling pathways, our findings clearly indicate CD81's involvement in activating these pathways and modulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). It's important to note that osteoblasts also express CD81, raising the possibility that CD81 suppression could negatively affect bone metabolism. Therefore, future research should involve clinical specimens and explore the interactive relationships between CD81 and integrins in osteosarcoma.
Developing small molecular drugs or monoclonal antibodies to inhibit CD81 expression could improve osteosarcoma patient prognosis. This inhibition may suppress tumor growth and metastasis, according to our findings.
