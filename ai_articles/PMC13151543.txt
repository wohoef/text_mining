The EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA) evaluated a health claim submitted by DSM Nutritional Products, concerning docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and enhanced memory function. This evaluation, requested through the UK Competent Authority under Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 (Article 13(5)), aimed to scientifically substantiate the proposed claim. The Panel confirmed that DHA is adequately characterized and that improved memory function represents a beneficial physiological effect. To assess the claim, the Panel reviewed various studies. Out of 11 human intervention studies deemed suitable for drawing conclusions, only two indicated a positive impact of DHA supplementation on memory. Conversely, one study reported inconsistent findings, another showed a detrimental effect, and a significant majority of seven studies found no effect of DHA on memory outcomes. The Panel noted that these conflicting results could not be attributed to variations in study design, DHA source or dosage, participant baseline characteristics, or study duration. Furthermore, the Panel considered meta-analyses of intervention studies provided by the applicant, which similarly failed to demonstrate evidence for a DHA effect on memory function. Three observational studies, from which conclusions could be drawn, also did not establish an association between dietary DHA intake and memory function. Based on this comprehensive review of the evidence, the Panel concluded that a direct cause-and-effect relationship between DHA consumption and improved memory function has not been scientifically established.

The EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA) assessed a health claim application from DSM Nutritional Products. The application, submitted through the UK's Competent Authority, sought authorization under Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, Article 13(5). The NDA's task was to provide an opinion on the scientific evidence supporting a link between docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and improved memory function.
The application sought a health claim for DHA's memory benefits, based on new scientific evidence.
DHA, a well-characterized n-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid, is the food constituent for this health claim. The Panel deems DHA sufficiently characterized.
The applicant proposed a health claim that docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) improves memory function in healthy adults. Memory, defined as the cognitive ability to retain and recall learned information, involves various distinct processes like episodic, working, short-term, and semantic memory. The Panel agreed that improved memory function represents a beneficial physiological effect.
The applicant submitted 15 human intervention studies, 13 observational studies, and three meta-analyses to support their claim.
Of the fifteen human intervention studies initially considered, four were excluded due to methodological flaws, leaving eleven for analysis regarding the health claim. These eleven studies investigated DHA from three sources: two utilized microalgae-derived DHA, eight used fish oil-derived DHA, and one employed phosphatidylserine (PS)-DHA. Among the microalgae-based studies, one demonstrated a positive impact on episodic memory, but not working memory, in older adults with age-related cognitive decline who consumed 900 mg of DHA for 24 weeks. Conversely, the other microalgae study reported a negative effect on episodic memory and no effect on semantic memory in young adults after 50 days of 400 mg DHA intake. The eight fish oil studies, which involved daily DHA doses from 252 mg to 1.55 g over 12 weeks to 24 months, yielded varied results. Only one of these studies indicated an improvement in memory among adults with mild cognitive impairment. Another fish oil study produced inconsistent findings, while the remaining six showed no effect of DHA supplementation on memory. Finally, the single study using PS-DHA also found no impact on memory function.
The applicant's meta-analyses, synthesizing data from 15 intervention studies, failed to demonstrate a DHA effect on memory. These analyses, which considered all DHA doses, sources, and subject demographics, showed no impact on episodic, semantic, or working memory. Therefore, the Panel finds no evidence supporting a DHA-memory link from these meta-analyses.
Of the 13 observational studies submitted by the applicant, ten were deemed unsuitable for evaluating the claim. Nine of these studies focused on blood DHA levels rather than dietary intake, while one presented only a composite cognitive score, lacking specific memory test results. The three remaining observational studies, which were considered relevant, ultimately failed to demonstrate a link between dietary DHA consumption and improved memory function. Therefore, the evidence from these studies does not support the proposed health claim regarding DHA's effect on memory.
The Panel evaluated the evidence from 11 human intervention studies concerning docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and memory function. Only two of these studies indicated a beneficial effect from DHA supplementation. Conversely, one study reported a negative impact, another yielded inconsistent findings, and the remaining seven studies demonstrated no effect on memory outcomes. This body of research led the Panel to conclude that most human intervention studies failed to show a positive effect of DHA on memory. Furthermore, the Panel determined that the conflicting results observed across these studies could not be attributed to variations in study design, DHA source, dosage, participant baseline characteristics, or study duration. The Panel also considered meta-analyses of intervention studies submitted by the applicant, which similarly did not provide evidence for a DHA effect on memory function. Additionally, three observational studies, from which conclusions could be drawn, did not establish an association between dietary DHA and memory function. Based on this comprehensive assessment, the Panel concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship between DHA consumption and improved memory function has not been scientifically substantiated.
No cause-and-effect link was found between DHA intake and improved memory. Most human studies showed no effect, and conflicting results lacked clear explanations.
Health claims on food products are regulated by EC No 1924/2006, which sets forth harmonized rules for their authorization within the Community. Generally, such claims are prohibited unless they adhere to the Regulation's specific and general requirements, receive authorization, and are listed among approved claims under Articles 13 and 14. Article 13(5) specifically addresses the inclusion of claims based on new scientific evidence or those seeking proprietary data protection. These claims, distinct from those concerning disease risk reduction or children's development and health, can be added to the Community's list of permitted claims referenced in Article 13(3). This framework ensures that all health claims are rigorously substantiated before public dissemination, maintaining consumer trust and product integrity.
Applicants submit health claim applications to a Member State's national authority, as per Article 18. This authority then forwards the application and any supporting data to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) for review.
EFSA was tasked with evaluating scientific data supporting a health claim regarding docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and improved memory function. This evaluation, conducted under Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 Article 16(3), would inform EFSA's opinion on the claim's scientific substantiation.
This opinion does not authorize DHA marketing or affirm its safety. It also does not classify DHA as a foodstuff. Such assessments are outside the scope of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
The applicant's proposed claim wording, scope, and usage conditions are provisional. These aspects may be altered following the authorization process outlined in Article 18(4) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
The Panel previously evaluated DHA, finding favorable outcomes for normal brain function (2010) and development (2014).

The applicant conducted a comprehensive literature review to identify studies relevant to the health claim. This involved searching Ovid/Medline and Embase databases up to January 2013, utilizing terms related to DHA, EPA, their dietary sources, and memory. To ensure thoroughness, reference lists of identified studies, relevant review articles, and meta-analyses were also manually examined. The search was subsequently updated in September 2014 to incorporate any newly published trials. Inclusion criteria for studies were defined as controlled clinical trials (both randomized and non-randomized) or observational studies that investigated the impact of DHA intake, either alone or in conjunction with EPA. These studies needed to involve DHA from conventional or fortified sources (foods or supplements) and assess memory outcomes in healthy adults living in the community, including those with mild memory complaints. Conversely, studies were excluded if they failed to report DHA and/or EPA intake, or if participants had existing diagnoses of Alzheimer's disease, dementia, vascular dementia, stroke, head injury, substance abuse, metabolic disturbance, depression, or other behavioral or neurological disorders. Furthermore, studies were excluded if the mean baseline Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores of participants were below 24, indicating advanced cognitive decline, or if over 10% of subjects were using psychotropic, antidepressant, stimulant, or AD/dementia-treating medications.
The applicant submitted 15 human intervention studies, 13 observational studies, and three meta-analyses to support their claim.
A single-arm trial (Richter et al., 2010) investigated phosphatidylserine (PS) with DHA and EPA for episodic and working memory. However, lacking a control group, this study provided insufficient evidence for the claim's scientific substantiation.
A double-blind, randomized controlled trial by Johnson et al. (2008) investigated the impact of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and/or lutein on memory functions in 57 women aged 60-80 over four months. The study assessed episodic, working, short-term, and semantic memory. While within-group changes in memory scores were analyzed using paired t-tests, the researchers did not report between-group comparisons. The applicant was asked to provide a statistical analysis suitable for placebo-controlled designs, specifically one that would evaluate differences in changes across study arms over time for the measured memory outcomes. However, this crucial analysis was not supplied. Consequently, the Panel determined that this study's findings could not be used to scientifically support the health claim.
Kotani et al. (2006) investigated the impact of DHA and arachidonic acid (ARA) on various memory functions, including episodic, semantic, and short-term memory. Their study involved patients diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), organic brain lesions, and Alzheimer's disease (AD). Specifically, 21 MCI patients (12 male, 9 female, mean age 61.8 years) were randomly assigned to one of two groups for 90 days. One group received a daily supplement containing 240 mg DHA and 240 mg ARA, while the other received a 240 mg placebo. Additionally, 10 patients with organic brain lesions and 8 AD patients were given the DHA+ARA supplement, though they were not part of the randomized, placebo-controlled arm. The statistical analysis focused solely on within-group changes from baseline to the study's conclusion; no between-group comparisons were reported. The applicant was asked to provide a statistical analysis suitable for placebo-controlled designs, but this was not supplied. Consequently, the Panel determined that this study's findings could not scientifically support the health claim.
Of the 12 human intervention studies, two used microalgae-derived DHA, nine used fish oil-derived DHA, and one used PS-DHA.
A double-blind, parallel, multi-center randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Yurko-Mauro et al. (2010) investigated the impact of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) on episodic and working memory in healthy older adults experiencing age-related cognitive decline (ARCD). ARCD, as defined by the Diagnostics and Statistical Manual (DSM-IV), signifies an objectively identified decline in cognitive function linked to aging, where individuals may struggle with recalling names or appointments or solving complex problems. Participants were identified as having ARCD if their baseline immediate or delayed recall score on the Logical Memory subtest of the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS Version III, 1997) was at least one standard deviation below the mean for adults aged 25–35 years. The study recruited 485 participants, all aged 55 or older, with ARCD (282 females, 203 males) across 19 sites. Exclusion criteria included a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score below 26 or a mean daily DHA consumption exceeding 200 mg in the two months prior to randomization. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: a DHA group, receiving 900 mg of DHA daily (derived from single-cell oil from *Schizochytrium* sp.), or a placebo group, receiving a daily dose of a 50% corn oil/50% soy oil mixture. The intervention lasted 24 weeks. All supplement capsules were designed to be indistinguishable in color, size, and appearance. The average age of participants in both groups was 70 years. Episodic memory was evaluated using the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) Paired Associate Learning Test (PAL), the CANTAB Pattern Recognition Memory (PRM) test, and the CANTAB Verbal Recognition Memory (VRM) test (Robbins et al., 1994). Working memory was assessed with the CANTAB Spatial Working Memory (SWM) test. These assessments were conducted at baseline, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks. A power calculation indicated that 325 participants were needed to detect an effect size of 0.19 for PAL 6 pattern error scores, with an alpha level of 0.05 and 80% power, accounting for a 10% dropout rate. Of the initial 485 participants, 437 completed the study. Analysis of CANTAB scores at 24 weeks, using ANCOVA with factors for treatment (DHA or placebo), site, age group (55–69 years or ≥ 70 years), and education, and including baseline scores as a covariate, revealed significant findings. The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, which carried forward the last observation for missing data, showed that the DHA group made significantly fewer PAL 6 pattern errors at week 24 compared to the placebo group (p = 0.032). Furthermore, the DHA group demonstrated a significantly higher number of correct responses in the VRM test for both immediate recall (p = 0.018) and delayed recall (p = 0.012). However, no significant differences between the treatment groups were observed for PRM performance or SWM performance. The Panel concluded that this study provided evidence for a positive effect of 24 weeks of 900 mg daily DHA consumption on episodic memory in older adults with ARCD, but not on working memory.
Benton et al. (2013) investigated the impact of DHA on memory in 305 young adult females (average age 21.8 years). This double-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) spanned 50 days, during which participants daily consumed either 400 mg of DHA, sourced from *Crypthecodinium cohnii*, or a corn oil placebo. The study was executed in two distinct phases, with the second phase commencing three months after the first. Episodic memory was evaluated using a word list recall task. This involved presenting participants with three distinct lists, each containing 30 nouns, at a rate of one word per second. Following presentation, participants had two minutes to write down as many words as they could remember. Both immediate and delayed (25-minute) recall were measured at baseline, day 25, and day 50. Semantic memory was assessed through a capitals recall test, where participants attempted to name the capital cities of 30 countries. If a capital could not be recalled, participants rated their "feeling of knowing" on a 6-point scale. This semantic memory test was administered at the 50-day assessment, but only to half of the study's participants (those in the second phase). The study did not report a power calculation. A total of 285 participants completed the trial, and their data were included in the analysis. The Panel noted that the analysis focused solely on these completers. Episodic memory results were analyzed using a three-way repeated-measures ANOVA, considering treatment (DHA vs. placebo), time (baseline, day 25, day 50), and recall time (immediate vs. delayed) as factors. The results, presented as means ± SEM, indicated a significant three-way interaction for word list recall (F(2,566) = 4.41, p < 0.01). Specifically, the DHA group forgot more words (3.2 ± 0.19) than the placebo group (2.6 ± 0.19, p < 0.01) by the study's conclusion. Conversely, no significant differences between groups were observed for the semantic memory capitals recall test. In summary, this study revealed a negative effect of 400 mg/day DHA consumption over 50 days on episodic memory in young adults, while demonstrating no effect on semantic memory.
A double-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Karr et al. (2012) investigated the impact of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) on episodic memory in 43 college students, 29 of whom were female. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups for a four-week period: an intervention group receiving 2,000 mg of fish oil daily (providing 480 mg DHA and 720 mg EPA), or a control group receiving 2,000 mg of coconut oil as a placebo. Episodic memory was evaluated using the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT), administered at both baseline and after four weeks of supplementation. The RAVLT involves seven stages designed to assess verbal learning and memory. In the initial five stages, participants were presented with a 15-word list (List A) five times, with immediate recall requested after each presentation. This was followed by an interference trial where a second word list (List B) was presented and recalled. Subsequent stages assessed recall of the original List A: Stage 6 involved recall after the interference from List B, and Stage 7 involved recall after a 20-minute delay. Memory performance was quantified by the number of words correctly recalled in each of the seven stages, as well as by two summary measures derived from subtracting the number of words recalled in Stage 5 from those recalled in Stages 6 and 7. The study did not report any power calculations. For the analysis of episodic memory, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was employed. The factors considered were treatment (fish oil vs. placebo), time (baseline vs. week 4), and various RAVLT stages (stages 1–5, stage 6, stage 7, and the summary measures 7-5 and 6-5). Two participants, one from each group, were excluded from the analysis due to non-compliance with the supplementation protocol. Consequently, the data analysis was conducted solely on the remaining 41 participants who completed the study. No significant differences were observed between the groups for Stages 1–5 of the RAVLT. However, statistically significant treatment × time interactions were identified for Stage 6 (F(1,39) = 4.45, p = 0.04), Stage 7 (F(1,39) = 5.65, p = 0.02), and the summary measure 7-5 (F(1,39) = 4.30, p = 0.045). These interactions suggest that the effect of treatment on memory performance changed over time. Despite these significant interactions, the study did not include post hoc comparisons to determine the specific time points at which the intervention and control groups differed in their RAVLT scores. The applicant was requested to provide these post hoc analyses to ascertain whether the fish oil group demonstrated significantly superior memory performance compared to the placebo group by the end of the supplementation period. The applicant, however, did not furnish additional analyses, instead referring to the interactions as reported in the original publication. Upon review, it was noted that for each significant time × treatment interaction, the fish oil group's performance appeared to improve throughout the study, while the control group's performance declined. Specifically, baseline RAVLT scores for Stage 6 were 11.57 ± 2.36 for the placebo group and 9.85 ± 3.28 for the fish oil group; at week 4, these scores were 11.33 ± 2.06 and 10.80 ± 3.14, respectively. For Stage 7, baseline scores were 11.48 ± 2.96 for placebo and 10.00 ± 3.43 for fish oil; at week 4, they were 10.29 ± 3.26 and 10.50 ± 3.61, respectively. However, a critical observation was made: the baseline RAVLT scores exhibited apparent differences between the fish oil and placebo groups. This disparity at baseline, rather than a genuine treatment effect, could have potentially driven the observed time × treatment interactions. Given these methodological concerns and the lack of comprehensive post hoc analyses, it was concluded that this study does not provide sufficient evidence to substantiate the claim regarding DHA's effect on memory function. Therefore, no definitive conclusions could be drawn from this research for the scientific substantiation of the health claim.
Sinn et al. (2012) investigated the impact of DHA-rich and EPA-rich fish oils on various memory functions in 50 volunteers over 65 years old (16 female) experiencing mild cognitive impairment (MCI), defined as age-unrelated cognitive decline without dementia. This double-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) assigned participants to one of three daily treatments for six months: EPA-rich fish oil (0.16 g DHA, 1.67 g EPA; n = 17), DHA-rich fish oil (1.55 g DHA, 0.40 g EPA; n = 18), or a placebo (safflower oil with 2.2 g linolenic acid; n = 15). The average age across these groups ranged from 73 to 74.9 years. Memory assessments were conducted at baseline and study conclusion, encompassing episodic, short-term, working, and semantic memory. Episodic memory was evaluated using the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT). Short-term memory was measured by the Digits Forward subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS), while working memory was assessed with the Digits Backward subtest of the WAIS. Semantic memory was tested through the Boston Naming Task (Lezak, 1995) and a verbal fluency test (Bryan et al., 1997) where participants generated words based on specific letter criteria. The study's power calculation, as reported in the publication, indicated a requirement of 66 participants (22 per group) to detect a medium effect size (d = 0.50) with 80% power in a repeated-measures within-between interaction design for three groups. However, the specific variable used for this calculation and the methodology were not clearly detailed. Forty participants completed the study (control group n = 11; DHA-group n = 16; EPA-group n = 13). Linear mixed models were employed for the intention-to-treat (ITT) population (n = 50) to analyze the effects of the DHA-rich or EPA-rich fish oil compared to the placebo. This multilevel model was deemed suitable for handling missing data, allowing for the use of all available information and prediction of values for all participants at each time point. Results showed a statistically significant improvement only in Initial Letter Fluency scores (semantic memory) within the DHA-rich fish oil group when compared to the placebo group (p = 0.04). No significant differences were observed between the EPA-rich fish oil group and the placebo group, nor were there significant effects on any other memory measures. The Panel noted the absence of correction for multiple comparisons in the statistical analysis. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this study, involving a daily intake of 1.55 g DHA from fish oil for six months, did not demonstrate an effect on episodic, short-term, working, or semantic memory in subjects with MCI, with the exception of a single semantic memory measure.
A double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Jackson et al. (2012) investigated the impact of DHA-rich and EPA-rich fish oils on memory functions in 159 young adult volunteers, aged 21.9 to 22.7 years on average. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three daily intake groups for 12 weeks: EPA-rich fish oil (200 mg DHA, 300 mg EPA), DHA-rich fish oil (450 mg DHA, 90 mg EPA), or a placebo (olive oil). Memory assessments were conducted at both the beginning and end of the study. Episodic memory was evaluated using tests such as Immediate Word Recall, Delayed Word Recall, Delayed Word Recognition, Delayed Picture Recognition, and Names-to-Faces Recall. Working memory was assessed through Numeric Working Memory, Alphabetic Working Memory, Spatial Working Memory (Corsi Blocks), a Three-back Task, and Telephone Number Working Memory. Semantic memory was measured using a verbal fluency test. All these cognitive assessments were administered via the Computerised Mental Performance Assessment System. Data analysis involved one-way between-subject ANCOVA, with baseline measurements serving as covariates. Pre-planned comparisons were made using t-tests to compare the placebo group with both the DHA-rich and EPA-rich fish oil groups. Of the initial participants, 140 adhered to the study protocol and were included in the final data analysis (48 in the control group, 46 in the DHA group, and 46 in the EPA group). A statistically significant treatment effect was observed for Names-to-Faces Recall (F(2,136) = 3.73, p = 0.026). However, contrary to a beneficial effect, participants in both the DHA-rich fish oil group (p = 0.047) and the EPA-rich fish oil group (p = 0.013) performed worse, showing fewer correct matches compared to the placebo group. No other memory tests demonstrated significant effects. It is important to note that no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons in the statistical analysis. Considering these findings, the Panel concluded that this 12-week study, involving a daily consumption of 450 mg DHA from fish oil, did not demonstrate a positive effect on episodic, working, or semantic memory in young adults.
A double-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Dangour et al. (2010) investigated the impact of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) on memory functions in 867 cognitively healthy adults, aged 70 to 79 years, of whom 390 were female. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: a treatment group receiving a daily dose of fish oil containing 500 mg DHA and 200 mg eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), or a control group receiving a placebo of olive oil. The intervention lasted for 24 months. The study evaluated several aspects of memory. Episodic memory was assessed using the California Verbal Learning Test (CVLT), which included a 16-item word list recall. Immediate and delayed recall of a short story from the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS) and a spatial memory test were also employed. Additionally, three tests of prospective memory were administered. Working memory was measured using the Digits Backward subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS), while semantic memory was evaluated via a verbal fluency test. Short-term memory was assessed with the Digits Forward subtest of the WAIS. All memory tests were conducted at baseline and again at the conclusion of the study. A composite episodic memory score was derived by summing the z-scores from the CVLT, location memory, and story recall measures. Other memory measures were incorporated into a broader global cognitive function composite score. However, this global score also included non-memory measures such as processing speed, reaction time, and executive function, rendering it unsuitable for drawing conclusions specifically about memory function in the context of this claim. The study’s power calculation indicated that 332 participants per group were needed to detect a 0.3 standard deviation difference in delayed free recall of a 16-item word list between the treatment groups. A total of 748 participants completed the study, with cognitive function data available for 744 individuals (375 in the fish oil group and 369 in the control group). Data analyses were performed exclusively on the completers. Results indicated no significant differences between the treatment groups in the total number of words correctly recalled in the first three CVLT trials or in the CVLT delayed recall. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the composite memory scores after 24 months. In summary, the study found that a daily intake of 500 mg DHA from fish oil over a 24-month period did not produce a significant effect on episodic, short-term, working, or semantic memory in cognitively healthy older adults.
A 12-month double-blind randomized controlled trial by Lee et al. (2013) investigated the impact of fish oil supplementation on memory function in 36 elderly participants (27 female) with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a daily dose of DHA-rich fish oil (1.29 g DHA, 450 mg EPA) or a corn oil placebo (1.8 g linolenic acid). Memory assessments were conducted at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. The study evaluated episodic, working, and short-term memory using various standardized tests. Episodic memory was measured with the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) and two subtests from the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised (WMS-R). Working memory was assessed using the Digit Span Backward subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS), while short-term memory was evaluated with the Digit Span Forward subtest of the WAIS. No power calculation was reported for the study design. Memory score changes were analyzed using repeated-measures ANCOVA, accounting for treatment, time, baseline age, blood pressure, and years of schooling. Bonferroni corrections were applied for multiple comparisons in post hoc analyses. Significant treatment by time interactions were observed for digit span (F = 9.89; p < 0.0001) and RAVLT delayed recall (F = 3.99; p < 0.05). For digit span, the fish oil group showed an increase from 8.0 at baseline to 9.6 at 12 months, while the placebo group decreased from 8.5 to 8.0. In RAVLT delayed recall, the fish oil group increased from 6.7 to 8.1, whereas the placebo group decreased from 6.1 to 5.0 over the 12-month period. No other memory tests showed significant effects. The researchers also computed a composite memory score by averaging z-scores from the RAVLT, WMS-R, and Digit Span Backward tests. The change in this composite score from baseline to 12 months was significantly greater in the fish oil group compared to the placebo group (difference: 0.799, 95% CI: 0.339–1.258, p < 0.01). Based on these findings, the Panel concluded that this study demonstrated an improvement in memory function in adults with MCI after 12 months of daily consumption of 1.29 g DHA from fish oil.
A double-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Stonehouse et al. (2013) investigated the impact of DHA supplementation on episodic and working memory in 228 healthy adults. Participants, aged 18 to 45 years, were selected based on a low dietary intake of DHA (under approximately 200 mg EPA+DHA per week). They were randomly assigned to one of two groups for a six-month period: one group received fish oil containing 1.16 g DHA and 0.17 g EPA daily, while the other received a placebo of high oleic acid sunflower oil. Memory function was assessed at both baseline and the study's conclusion using a Computerised Mental Performance Assessment System. Episodic memory was evaluated through tests such as Immediate Word Recall, Delayed Word Recall, Delayed Word Recognition, and Delayed Picture Recognition. Working memory was measured using Letter-Number Sequencing, Spatial Working Memory (Corsi Blocks), and n-back Tasks. Composite memory scores were derived by averaging the z-scores from individual memory tests, encompassing memory, working memory, and their respective reaction times. The study’s power calculations indicated a requirement of 32 participants per sex and treatment group to detect a 0.5 difference in z-score for memory domains, with 80% power and a 0.05 significance level. Data analysis involved ANCOVA models to adjust for baseline cognitive function scores and other covariates, including education, primary language (English vs. other), age, and baseline blood DHA concentrations. Additionally, sex and the presence of the apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4) allelic variant were incorporated as independent variables to explore potential interactions with treatment. Of the initial cohort, 176 participants completed the trial and were included in the final analyses (85 in the DHA group, 91 in the placebo group). It was noted that data analyses were exclusively based on these completers. The findings revealed a significant interaction between sex and treatment for episodic memory (p = 0.01). Specifically, women in the DHA group demonstrated greater improvement compared to the placebo group (p = 0.01), whereas no significant differences were observed among men (p = 0.20). A significant treatment effect was also found for the reaction time of episodic memory, with the DHA group exhibiting faster responses than the placebo group; however, the sex × treatment interaction for this outcome was not significant. Similarly, the reaction time of working memory showed a significant treatment effect, with the DHA group responding faster than the placebo group (p = 0.002). The sex × treatment interaction was also significant (p = 0.03) for working memory reaction time, indicating that men in the DHA group completed the task faster than men in the placebo group (p = 0.001), though no significant difference was reported for women (p = 0.39). No significant effects were observed for working memory itself, and APOE4 status did not influence treatment responses for any memory outcome. Despite some observed improvements in episodic and working memory in healthy adults, particularly in specific subgroups and for reaction times, the Panel concluded that the effects of DHA consumption on memory were inconsistent across different tests and sexes within this six-month study. Therefore, the study did not provide consistent evidence for a beneficial effect of DHA supplementation on memory.
In a 2012 triple-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial, Stough and colleagues investigated the impact of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) from tuna oil on episodic and working memory. The study involved 112 healthy adults, aged 45 to 80 years, who were randomly assigned to one of two groups for 90 days. One group received DHA-rich fish oil, providing 252 mg of DHA and 60 mg of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) daily, while the control group received a placebo of soybean oil. Memory function was assessed using a comprehensive battery of tests from the cognitive drug research (CDR) computerised assessment system. Episodic memory was evaluated through tasks such as Immediate Word Recall, Delayed Word Recall, Delayed Word Recognition, and Delayed Picture Recognition. Working memory was measured using the Spatial Working Memory (SWM) and Numeric Working Memory tests. These individual test results were then used to compute CDR factor scores for Speed of Memory, Secondary Memory, and Working Memory. Assessments were conducted at the beginning and end of the 90-day intervention period. The researchers performed post hoc power calculations, which indicated that with the final sample size of 74 participants and an alpha level of 0.05, there was a 99% probability of detecting a "medium" effect size (f = 0.25). However, the power to detect a "small" effect size (f = 0.1) on cognition was considerably lower, at only 40%. It is important to note that the specific cognitive outcome variable used for these power calculations was not explicitly detailed. During the trial, 19 participants withdrew, and an additional 19 were excluded from the analysis due to inadequate compliance (defined as less than 90% adherence to the supplementation regimen). Consequently, the final analysis included 74 participants: 38 in the fish oil group and 36 in the placebo group. The data analysis was exclusively performed on this per-protocol (PP) population, with no demographic information provided for the initial intention-to-treat (ITT) population of 112 participants. Statistical analysis involved mixed-design repeated-measures ANCOVA, with time (baseline versus post-treatment) as a within-subjects factor, treatment group (DHA versus placebo) as a between-subjects factor, and age as a covariate. This analysis was applied to all cognitive outcomes. The findings revealed no statistically significant effects for any of the memory measures assessed. Therefore, the study concluded that daily consumption of 252 mg of DHA from fish oil for 90 days did not demonstrate an effect on either episodic or working memory in healthy adults.
Van de Rest et al. (2008) conducted a 26-week, double-blind, three-arm, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) to investigate the impact of fish oil supplementation on various memory functions. The study enrolled 302 cognitively healthy participants, including 135 females, with an average age of 70 years and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores exceeding 21. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three daily treatment groups: a "high dose" fish oil group receiving 847 ± 23 mg of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and 1,093 ± 17 mg of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA); a "low dose" fish oil group receiving 176 ± 4 mg DHA and 226 ± 3 mg EPA; or a placebo group primarily consuming oleic acid. Memory assessments were performed at baseline, 13 weeks, and 26 weeks. Episodic memory was evaluated using the Word Learning Test (Van der Elst et al., 2005). Working memory was measured with the Digits Backward subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) (Wechsler, 1981), while short-term memory was assessed using the Digits Forward subtest of the WAIS. Semantic memory was evaluated through the Verbal Fluency Test (Van der Elst et al., 2005). Additionally, a composite memory score was derived by averaging the z-scores from Immediate and Delayed Word Learning, Word Learning Recognition, and Backward Digit Span. The study's power calculation, based on the Word Learning Test, indicated a requirement of at least 63 participants per group to detect a 4-point difference with 80% power and an alpha level of 0.05. Repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) was employed to analyze differential changes among the three intervention groups over time, with the treatment group as a factor and memory composite and short-term memory test scores as dependent variables. A Dunnett's post hoc test was then used to compare mean changes in the two treatment groups against the control group. The findings revealed no significant treatment effects at any time point. Therefore, the Panel concluded that the daily consumption of 850 mg DHA from fish oil for 26 weeks did not demonstrate an effect on episodic, working, short-term, or semantic memory in cognitively healthy older adults.
Witte et al. (2013) conducted a 26-week, double-blind, parallel randomized controlled trial investigating the impact of daily DHA and EPA supplementation on various memory functions in healthy adults. Sixty-five participants, aged 50–75 years and with Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores above 26, were randomly assigned to either a fish oil group (receiving 880 mg DHA and 1,320 mg EPA) or a placebo group (receiving sunflower oil). The average ages of the two groups (65 years for DHA+EPA and 62.9 years for placebo) were comparable. The study evaluated episodic memory using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), which included measures of immediate and delayed recall, as well as recognition. Working memory was assessed via the Backward Digit Span Test, and short-term memory through the Forward Digit Span Test. Semantic memory was measured with the Verbal Fluency Test. A composite memory score was derived by averaging z-scores from AVLT learning, delayed recall, recognition, and the backward digit span. Statistical analysis involved a repeated-measures ANOVA, considering treatment (fish oil vs. placebo) and time (baseline vs. week 26) as factors, with Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons. No power calculations were reported. The findings indicated no significant effects on the composite memory score, nor on short-term memory. Therefore, the Panel concluded that 880 mg/day of DHA from fish oil over 26 weeks did not improve episodic, working, semantic, or short-term memory in healthy adults.
A 15-week randomized controlled trial by Vakhapova et al. (2010) investigated the impact of phosphatidylserine-docosahexaenoic acid (PS-DHA) on various memory functions in 157 non-demented elderly individuals experiencing memory complaints. Participants, with a mean age of 73 years, were required to have a Memory Complaint Questionnaire score of at least 25, indicating self-reported memory loss, and to be free of dementia, confirmed by a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of at least 27 for college-educated individuals or 26 for others, and a Clinical Dementia Rating Scale score of 0.5 or less. Additionally, their scores on the NexAde computerized cognitive assessment tool had to be at or below the mean norm. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: a PS-DHA group, receiving a daily dose of 300 mg PS and 79 mg DHA+EPA (with a 3:1 DHA/EPA ratio), or a placebo group, receiving cellulose. Memory assessments were conducted at baseline and at the study's conclusion. Episodic memory was evaluated using the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT), the Rey Complex Figure Test (RCFT), and immediate and delayed pattern recall tasks from the NexAde battery. Working memory and short-term memory were assessed using digit span backward and forward tasks, respectively, also from the NexAde battery. NexAde also generated composite memory scores based on individual tests of memory recognition, recall (episodic memory), and spatial short-term memory. The study did not report a power calculation. Data analysis, primarily using ANCOVA with baseline performance and MMSE scores as covariates, focused on the per-protocol (PP) population of 122 participants (66 in the PS-DHA group; 65 in the placebo group). This PP population excluded nine individuals (six from placebo, three from PS-DHA) due to insufficient compliance (less than 65%). While immediate recall scores from the RAVLT were significantly higher in the PS-DHA group compared to placebo within the PP population (p = 0.041) at 15 weeks, this difference was not observed in the completers' population (n=131). No other RAVLT outcomes (total learning, delayed recall, recognition) or RCFT results showed significant effects. Furthermore, no statistically significant differences between groups were found for any NexAde tasks, including those for working memory and short-term memory. The Panel observed that only one of four RAVLT outcomes demonstrated a significant improvement in the PS-DHA group, and this was only within the PP population. Crucially, the study did not apply a correction for multiple comparisons, and no significant differences were found for working or short-term memory. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this study, investigating PS-DHA consumption over 15 weeks, did not provide evidence of an overall improvement in memory function.
Of the eleven human intervention studies considered for substantiating the claim, the Panel observed varied outcomes regarding docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) supplementation and memory function. Two studies utilized DHA derived from microalgae. One of these, involving healthy older adults with age-related cognitive decline, demonstrated an improvement in episodic memory but not working memory after 24 weeks of 900 mg DHA consumption (Yurko-Mauro et al., 2010). Conversely, the other microalgae-derived DHA study, conducted on young adults, reported a negative impact on episodic memory and no effect on semantic memory following 50 days of 400 mg DHA intake (Benton et al., 2013). The remaining nine studies investigated DHA from other sources. Eight intervention studies employed DHA sourced from fish oil, with daily dosages ranging from 252 mg to 1.55 g over periods spanning 12 weeks to 24 months. Among these, only one study indicated an improvement in memory among adults with mild cognitive impairment (Lee et al., 2013). Another study yielded inconsistent results regarding DHA's effect on memory (Stonehouse et al., 2013). The remaining six fish oil studies found no discernible effect of DHA supplementation on memory outcomes (Van de Rest et al., 2008; Dangour et al., 2010; Jackson et al., 2012; Sinn et al., 2012; Stough et al., 2012; Witte et al., 2013). The single study that used phosphatidylserine-DHA (PS-DHA) similarly showed no effect on memory function (Vakhapova et al., 2010). In summary, the Panel concluded that the majority of the human intervention studies did not support a beneficial effect of DHA supplementation on memory. Furthermore, the Panel noted that the observed inconsistencies in results across these studies could not be attributed to differences in study design, the origin or dosage of DHA, the baseline characteristics of the participants, the sample size, or the study duration.
The applicant performed meta-analyses on data from 15 intervention studies to assess DHA's impact on memory. These analyses, partially published by Yurko-Mauro et al. (2015), included comparisons both between groups (against placebo) and within groups (from baseline) for episodic, semantic, and working memory. Specifically, 62 data points addressed episodic memory, 15 for semantic memory, and 21 for working memory. Random effects meta-analyses were employed to calculate weighted group mean differences and Hedge's g scores, standardizing these differences by the studies' pooled standard deviation. The studies encompassed healthy individuals, with nine studies involving those without mild memory complaints and six with such complaints. Their durations varied from 28 to 730 days, averaging 4–6 months. The majority (eleven studies) focused on older adults aged 45–80, with DHA dosages up to 1.55 g/day and sample sizes ranging from 8 to 867 participants.
DHA supplementation had no effect on episodic, semantic, or working memory. This was consistent across all DHA doses, sources, and subjects, regardless of their age or baseline cognitive status.
The Panel found that sensitivity analyses, which examined age, baseline cognitive status, and DHA dose, yielded inconsistent results. Therefore, these analyses offered no clear evidence supporting DHA's effect on memory function.
Meta-analyses failed to show DHA's effect on memory.
Thirteen observational studies were submitted by the applicant. Nine of these studies, focusing on blood DHA levels rather than dietary intake, and one study reporting only a composite cognitive score, offered no conclusive evidence for the claim. The three remaining studies, which examined dietary DHA or fish oil supplements, also failed to demonstrate an association with memory function.
The Panel determined that ten observational studies provided insufficient evidence to substantiate the claim. Nine of these studies examined the link between blood DHA levels and memory, but lacked data on dietary DHA intake. Another study presented only a composite cognitive score, without specific memory test results. Consequently, these studies could not support a connection between DHA consumption and improved memory function.
Three observational studies investigated the link between DHA or fish oil consumption and memory function in adults. Kalmijn et al. (2004) conducted a cross-sectional study involving 1,613 participants aged 45–70 years, finding no significant relationship between DHA+EPA intake and episodic memory impairment risk. Similarly, Whalley et al. (2004) performed another cross-sectional study with 301 64-year-old participants, observing no notable difference in episodic memory scores between those who used fish oil and those who did not. Furthermore, a prospective cohort study by Kesse-Guyot et al. (2011) included 3,294 adults aged 35–60 years, and this research also revealed no significant association between DHA intake and episodic memory scores. Across all these studies, the Panel concluded that DHA consumption demonstrated no association with episodic memory in adult populations.
The Panel concluded that three observational studies, despite providing relevant data, did not establish a link between dietary DHA intake and improved memory function.
The Panel evaluated evidence regarding DHA and memory function, considering 11 human intervention studies. Of these, only two indicated a beneficial effect of DHA supplementation on memory. Conversely, one study reported a negative impact, another yielded inconsistent findings, and the remaining seven showed no effect on memory outcomes. The Panel concluded that most intervention studies did not support a positive effect of DHA on memory. Furthermore, the observed inconsistencies among studies could not be attributed to variations in study design, DHA source or dosage, participant characteristics, or study duration. Additionally, the Panel reviewed meta-analyses of intervention studies submitted by the applicant, which also failed to demonstrate an effect of DHA on memory function. Similarly, three relevant observational studies provided no evidence for a link between dietary DHA intake and memory function. Consequently, the Panel determined that a cause-and-effect relationship between DHA consumption and improved memory function has not been established.
No cause-and-effect link was found between DHA intake and improved memory. Most human studies showed no effect, and conflicting results lacked explanation. Meta-analyses and observational studies also provided no supporting evidence.
The Panel determined that docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), the subject of the health claim, is adequately characterized. While improved memory function is a beneficial physiological effect, the Panel concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship between DHA consumption and enhanced memory has not been established. This decision considered the applicant's proposed claim that DHA "contributes to improved memory function" in the general healthy adult population.
DSM Nutritional Products submitted a health claim application (serial No: 0438_UK) to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on July 24, 2015. This application, concerning docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and its potential to improve memory function, was made under Article 13(5) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, which governs health claims based on new scientific evidence. The scientific evaluation process began on September 15, 2015. During this period, the EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA) twice requested further information from DSM Nutritional Products. The first request was issued on October 30, 2015, leading to a suspension of the evaluation on November 11, 2015, as per Article 18(3) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. EFSA received the applicant's response on November 20, 2015, allowing the evaluation to resume. A second set of questions was agreed upon by the NDA Panel's Working Group on Claims on January 20, 2016. Consequently, the scientific evaluation was again suspended on February 5, 2016, in accordance with Article 18(3). The applicant provided their reply on February 18, 2016, and the evaluation recommenced. Following a thorough assessment of all submitted data, the NDA Panel formally adopted its opinion on the scientific substantiation of the health claim regarding DHA and improved memory function on March 30, 2016, through a written procedure.
