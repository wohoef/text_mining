This research investigated how intense, acute wrestling and subsequent sauna exposure influenced cortisol and insulin levels in elite male wrestlers. Fourteen healthy, voluntary participants were included in the study following approval from the health ethics committee. Blood samples were collected at three distinct times: initially at rest, then immediately after a wrestling exercise session, and finally after a two-day rest period followed by sauna exposure. Hormone concentrations were analyzed using an IMMULITE 2000 auto-analyzer. Due to the non-normal distribution of the collected data, nonparametric statistical methods were employed via SPSS. The findings indicated no significant alterations in either cortisol or insulin levels immediately after the acute wrestling exercise. However, following sauna exposure, a notable decrease in cortisol levels (P < .05) was observed, while insulin levels remained unchanged. A comparison between the post-wrestling and post-sauna measurements revealed significantly higher cortisol values after wrestling (P < .05), though no significant difference was found for insulin. In summary, the study concluded that wrestling training did not significantly impact cortisol or insulin, whereas sauna exposure led to a significant reduction in cortisol but had no short-term effect on insulin.

Globally, wrestling is a widely popular Olympic sport. Its physical requirements are substantial, demanding peak levels of maximal and explosive strength, endurance, and efficient movement of heavy loads. Wrestlers also need excellent reaction times to respond to opponents' actions, alongside highly developed anaerobic and aerobic energy systems.
Wrestlers often employ various strategies for rapid weight reduction to meet their competition weight categories. This is especially crucial for the official weigh-in, typically held the day before a competition. To achieve the necessary weight, athletes resort to methods like intense exercise, exposure to heat (which can include passive dehydration), and restricting both food and fluid intake (Lambert & Jones, 2010).
Sauna use is a common practice for athletes, particularly in weight-category sports like wrestling, where it's employed for rapid weight reduction. Beyond its role in athletic training, sauna is also valued for promoting physical and mental recovery (Tsonis, 2017; Pilch, 2008; Kukkonen-Harjula & Kauppinen, 2006). The physiological impact of sauna extends to muscle circulation, the respiratory system, and notably, the endocrine system. Exposure to high heat, similar to exercise, induces physiological stress within the body (Huether & McCance, 2009). Cortisol, a hormone released in response to stress (Hackney, 2006), plays a crucial role in carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism (Junqueira et al., 1993). Elevated stress, such as during exercise, typically leads to increased cortisol secretion (Hackney). Conversely, when blood glucose levels rise, the hormone insulin is released (Günay, Kara, & Cicioğlu, 2006). Insulin is vital for promoting an anabolic state, facilitating the storage of carbohydrates and lipids, supporting protein synthesis, and enabling glucose uptake by cells, thereby lowering blood glucose (Ersöz, 2002).
This study aimed to differentiate the impact of high-intensity acute wrestling and sauna exposure on key hormonal biomarkers. Specifically, it sought to compare the physiological stress induced by exercise with that caused by thermal stress from sauna. The research was carried out prior to the competition season.
Fourteen elite male wrestlers from Turkey, all experienced in international tournaments including European Championships, volunteered for the study. Their average age was 23.43 ± 4.1 years, with a height of 172.8 ± 4.8 cm, body mass of 74.9 ± 14.5 kg, and BMI of 24.9 ± 3.4 kg/m2.
Participants were required to be non-smokers, have over five years of wrestling experience, and no history of cardiovascular or endocrine disease. They also couldn't be taking medications or endocrine supplements, and all gave informed consent.
Participants underwent all testing sessions between 4:00 PM and 5:00 PM. A competition-style wrestling exercise session was conducted. Following a two-day rest period, subjects were exposed to a sauna. Body weight and height measurements were taken using precision scales before and after the wrestling exercise, and after the sauna exposure.
Participants engaged in a 30-minute match-style warm-up. This was followed by high-intensity wrestling training, structured as three 2-minute sessions, requiring maximal effort from the wrestlers.
Following a 48-hour rest period, participants underwent sauna sessions. These involved three 20-minute exposures to temperatures between 80-100°C and 10-15% humidity, interspersed with two-minute breaks at ambient room temperature.
Blood samples, approximately 10 ml each, were collected from the antecubital vein at three points: pre-exercise, post-wrestling, and post-sauna. These samples were immediately placed in EDTA-treated tubes on ice, and the separated plasma was subsequently frozen at -80°C for hormone analysis.
Expert biochemists at Atatürk University Medical Faculty Biochemistry Laboratories conducted all hormone analyses. These measurements, utilizing manufacturer's reagents, were performed on an Immulite 2000 analyzer.
Given the non-normal distribution of the data, as determined by preliminary analysis, non-parametric statistical methods were employed. SPSS 19 was utilized for these analyses, specifically the Friedman and Wilcoxon tests, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.
Both wrestling and sauna treatments led to a significant reduction in participants' body mass and body mass index. Post-sauna cortisol levels were significantly lower than both resting levels and those observed after acute wrestling exercise. Conversely, acute wrestling exercise did not significantly alter cortisol levels compared to rest. Importantly, neither wrestling nor sauna exposure produced a significant change in insulin levels.
This study aimed to isolate the impacts of intense wrestling and sauna exposure on key hormonal markers.
The study's experimental conditions, encompassing both wrestling exercise and sauna exposure, effectively impacted participants' body weight and, consequently, their Body Mass Index (BMI). This observed decrease in body mass and BMI was consistent with existing research. The weight reduction in the wrestlers resulted from the high intensity of the exercise and the elevated body heat induced by the sauna. Nevertheless, the extent of these reductions was relatively minor.
Our findings showed no change in either cortisol or insulin levels when comparing pre- and post-exercise measurements. While this outcome was unexpected given numerous studies reporting significant post-exercise cortisol increases (e.g., Kraemer et al., 2001), it aligns with other research that also found no differences in cortisol after wrestling (e.g., Ratamess et al., 2013). The absence of the anticipated cortisol elevation after exercise remains unexplained, though Daly et al. (2004) suggest that the timing of cortisol sampling after exercise is a crucial factor influencing the detection of significant changes.
Following wrestling exercise, our study observed a non-significant trend towards lower insulin levels. This finding aligns with research by Roemmich & Sinning (1997), who also found no significant changes in insulin levels in young wrestlers post-exercise. Similarly, Kraemer et al. (2001) noted a substantial increase in blood glucose after wrestling competition, yet insulin levels remained unchanged. Conversely, other studies have reported different outcomes; Cicioğlu and Onay (2002) documented a significant reduction in wrestlers' insulin after bicycle ergometer exercise. Furthermore, a decrease in insulin levels has been linked to prolonged exercise durations in various studies (Koistinen et al., 1998; Kowalska et al., 1999; Torjman et al., 1999; Essig et al., 2000). It is possible that despite the high intensity of our wrestling exercise, its duration was insufficient to elicit a significant reduction in insulin.
Our findings revealed a notable reduction in wrestlers' cortisol levels following sauna exposure (Table 1). This outcome, however, diverges from existing literature. For instance, Iwase et al. (2013) observed no change in cortisol after sauna exposure at 70°C. Conversely, Pilch et al. (2008) reported a significant increase in cortisol among 10 female participants after sauna, and Pilch et al. (2013) also found elevated cortisol levels in 9 trained middle-distance runners after a 15-minute sauna session. The limited research on sauna and cortisol levels presents inconsistent results. We hypothesize that the unexpected decrease in cortisol in our study might be attributed to the elite wrestlers' chronic sauna exposure, leading to physiological adaptation to high temperatures. Additionally, the longer duration of our sauna sessions compared to those in other studies could be a contributing factor.
Our findings show no significant change in insulin levels following sauna exposure. This aligns with other limited research on insulin and sauna, where studies by Lammintausta et al. (1976) and Jenssen et al. (1988) similarly reported no alteration in insulin values after sauna compared to rest.
