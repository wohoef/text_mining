This prospective study aimed to validate GRASP MRI as a noninvasive method for assessing tumor angiogenesis in lung cancer patients, comparing its perfusion parameters with the histological count of microvascular density (MVD). MVD, determined by CD34 staining of CT-guided biopsy specimens, is the current gold standard for evaluating tumor angiogenesis. However, the potential of perfusion MRI as a noninvasive alternative has not been extensively studied, especially in lung cancer. The study involved 25 lung cancer patients (16 male, 9 female, average age 57.3 ± 11.7 years). A 3T MRI scanner was used, employing a prototype golden-angle stack-of-stars sequence. Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MR data were collected during free breathing, and reconstructed using the GRASP technique, achieving a temporal resolution of approximately 3 seconds per phase. Perfusion analysis was then conducted using a standard Tofts model to derive two key parameters: the volume transfer coefficient (Ktrans) and the interstitial volume (Ve). Statistical analysis included Pearson correlation for repeatability and relationships between parameters, and one-way ANOVA with LSD-t for comparisons across groups. The repeatability of Ktrans and Ve measurements was high, with correlation coefficients of 0.983 and 0.972, respectively. Across all patients, the mean Ktrans was 0.33 ± 0.22 min−1, Ve was 0.25 ± 0.12, and MVD was 49.68 ± 27.08 vessels/0.723 mm2. Subgroup analysis showed varying mean values for adenocarcinoma (Ktrans: 0.36 ± 0.26 min−1, Ve: 0.27 ± 0.13, MVD: 49.09 ± 29.84 vessels/0.723 mm2), squamous cell carcinoma (Ktrans: 0.34 ± 0.17 min−1, Ve: 0.26 ± 0.12, MVD: 53.85 ± 23.53 vessels/0.723 mm2), and small-cell carcinoma (Ktrans: 0.13 ± 0.15 min−1, Ve: 0.14 ± 0.06, MVD: 37.20 ± 28.28 vessels/0.723 mm2). A significant positive correlation was observed between Ktrans and MVD across all patients (r = 0.738, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that high spatiotemporal resolution DCE-MRI using GRASP is a promising noninvasive alternative to histological MVD counts for assessing tumor angiogenesis in lung cancer.

Globally, lung cancer is a primary and growing cause of cancer-related mortality for both genders. Tumor angiogenesis, a process driven by a tumor's reliance on its vascular supply, is crucial for evaluating tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and for guiding treatment and prognosis. The established method for assessing tumor angiogenesis is the histological count of microvascular density (MVD). This count acts as a biomarker, indicating tumor angiogenesis and correlating with lymphatic and distant metastasis, as well as histopathological classification. Consequently, MVD is a valuable surrogate for predicting tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential. However, histological MVD counting is an invasive, complicated, and laborious procedure. A significant drawback is that tumor angiogenesis is often higher in the periphery, and tumors are inherently heterogeneous. This means that specimens from a single localized area may not accurately reflect the overall angiogenesis of the entire tumor, potentially leading to inaccurate estimations. Furthermore, MVD alone cannot assess vascular hyperpermeability, a key tumor characteristic, as it lacks information on vascular permeability. Therefore, a noninvasive method capable of evaluating whole-tumor angiogenesis in living subjects is urgently needed.
Dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) offers a noninvasive method for assessing tumor angiogenesis, potentially replacing the histological microvascular density (MVD) count. This technique avoids radiation exposure and excels at providing detailed soft-tissue contrast. Its utility in evaluating tumor vascular function and treatment response has been acknowledged and recommended by committees like the Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Technologies and Imaging Committees of the Experimental Cancer Medicine Centre, following reviews of early clinical trials. Despite its promise, DCE-MRI currently lacks standardized protocols for data acquisition, reconstruction, and image analysis. Furthermore, achieving an optimal balance between spatiotemporal resolution, volumetric coverage, and managing physiological motion, especially in organs like the lung, presents significant challenges.
GRASP (Golden-angle RAdial Sparse Parallel) MRI is an innovative imaging framework that integrates multicoil compressed sensing with golden-angle radial sampling, offering rapid and continuous MRI capabilities. This technique has demonstrated significant promise for dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI). Its radial sampling design facilitates uninterrupted data acquisition, even during free breathing. The synergy of compressed sensing and parallel imaging allows for superior temporal and spatial resolution, making it suitable for various dynamic imaging scenarios. A key advantage of the golden-angle radial sampling is its support for a dual-mode reconstruction approach, enabling both qualitative morphological evaluation and quantitative perfusion analysis concurrently. GRASP's effectiveness has been proven across numerous clinical applications, including its recent adaptation for free-breathing DCE-MRI in the assessment of pulmonary lesions. These initial investigations highlight GRASP's considerable potential to enhance the utility and efficiency of DCE-MRI for routine clinical practice. However, it is still unclear if GRASP can replace the histological counting of microvascular density (MVD) for angiogenesis studies. Consequently, this study aimed to explore the relationship between perfusion parameters obtained from high-resolution GRASP MRI and MVD measurements in tumor specimens from lung cancer patients. The central hypothesis was that free-breathing, high-resolution DCE-MRI using GRASP could serve as an effective, noninvasive method for evaluating tumor angiogenesis and tracking treatment responses in this patient population.
The Institutional Medical Research Ethics Committee granted approval for this prospective investigation. Between January and December 2017, forty consecutive patients (26 men, 14 women; average age 57.1 ± 13.9 years) were enrolled. These individuals had previously confirmed lung lesions. Before their MRI scans, all participants gave informed consent, which covered the potential use of their CT-biopsy and MVD data if accessible. Inclusion criteria for recruitment stipulated that patients must have a documented history of at least one primary pulmonary lesion, identified in prior examinations, with a size exceeding 15 mm. However, certain conditions led to the exclusion of MR data from the final analysis. Specifically, patients were excluded if they lacked pathological biopsy results, did not have corresponding MVD results, or presented with benign lesions. After applying these exclusion criteria, the study ultimately included data from 25 patients (16 men, 9 women; average age 57.3 ± 11.7 years). This final cohort comprised 15 patients with adenocarcinomas, 8 with squamous cell carcinomas, and 2 with small-cell lung cancer. The average lesion size among these 25 patients was 4.9 ± 0.9 cm.
For each patient, a 281-second radial scan was conducted on a 3T Magnetom TimTrio MR scanner from Siemens Healthineers (Erlangen, Germany). Patients were instructed to breathe normally throughout the continuous data acquisition, which utilized a prototype golden-angle stack-of-stars sequence. Approximately 20 seconds after data acquisition began, a full dose of Magnevist (0.2 mmol/kg, Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany) was injected at 3 mL/sec. Imaging parameters included a repetition time of 3.40 msec and an echo time of 1.64 msec. The field of view was 320 × 320 × 120 mm3, with an in-plane matrix of 256 × 256 and a flip angle of 12°. This resulted in an in-plane spatial resolution of 1.25 × 1.25 mm2. Twenty-four partitions were acquired, each with a 5 mm slice thickness, and 3000 spokes were collected per partition.
GRASP reconstruction employed a multicoil compressed sensing framework, solving a cost function to balance data consistency and sparsity.
The GRASP reconstruction process utilized a multicoil compressed sensing framework to solve a specific cost function. This function involved several key components: 'F' represented the nonuniform fast Fourier transform operator, while 'C' denoted coil sensitivities, which were derived from an artifact-free image created by averaging all collected spokes. The dynamic multicoil radial k-space, organized into distinct contrast phases, was represented by 'y'. The dynamic image series undergoing reconstruction was 'x', and 'T' served as a sparsifying transform. To achieve sparsity, the L1 norm (sum of absolute values) was employed. Data consistency, ensuring reconstructed images aligned with acquired k-space measurements, was maintained using the L2 norm (square root of the sum of squares). The balance between this data consistency and sparsity was managed by 'λ', a weighting parameter. For this study, the sparsifying transform used was first-order temporal finite differences, aimed at minimizing temporal total variation during optimization. Each contrast phase in the GRASP reconstruction comprised 34 consecutive spokes, yielding a temporal resolution of approximately 3 seconds per phase. The entire image reconstruction, implemented in C++ software, ran on a 64-core Linux server with 256 GB memory. Following reconstruction, images were interpolated to 40 partitions, resulting in a 3 mm slice thickness. The reconstruction of each 4D GRASP dataset typically took between 35 and 40 minutes.
An experienced radiologist (L.C.), blinded to pathological results, conducted the perfusion analysis using the FireVoxel online image processing software. This analysis followed a previously established protocol, detailed in Fig. 1. For each patient's data, the radiologist manually identified two regions of interest (ROIs) on two adjacent slices. These ROIs were carefully positioned to correspond with the biopsy site identified during the CT-guided procedure. A visual example in Fig. 2 illustrates how an ROI (marked by a red ellipse) was placed on an MR image to align with the biopsy needle's position on a CT image. To enhance the signal-to-noise ratio and improve fitting accuracy, the signal intensity of individual pixels within each ROI was averaged for every slice. This averaged signal enhancement curve was then processed using a standard Tofts model to derive two key perfusion parameters: the volume transfer coefficient (Ktrans) and the interstitial volume (Ve). The Ktrans and Ve values obtained from these two consecutive slices were subsequently averaged, labeled as Ktrans_1 and Ve_1 in Fig. 1. The arterial input function (AIF), crucial for perfusion analysis, was determined by placing an ROI on the descending aorta, operating under the assumption that contrast concentration and signal intensity shared a linear relationship.
To evaluate repeatability, the radiologist re-analyzed all datasets three days later, recalculating Ktrans and Ve (labeled as Ktrans_2 and Ve_2). The final Ktrans and Ve values (Ktrans_Final and Ve_Final) were then determined by averaging the results from both analyses. These averaged values were subsequently used for comparison with the MVD data.
For demonstration, Ktrans and Ve maps were created at a pixel-by-pixel level for a single slice in four distinct pathological cases. Unlike the main analysis, signal curves were not averaged, allowing for the generation of these detailed maps. To further illustrate the consistency of perfusion measurements, Ktrans and Ve values were also acquired from the healthy erector spinae muscle. Detailed results for these analyses are available in the Supplementary Materials.
Before counting, tumor specimens from CT-guided biopsies were coated with marking ink (Hematoxylin-Eosin Staining Kit) and fixed for 24 hours in 10% buffered formaldehyde.
To identify microvessels, a pathologist (Y.W.), experienced in clinical pathological diagnosis for five years, analyzed immunohistochemical slices. These slices were prepared by highlighting tumor microvessels through CD34 staining of endothelial cells, utilizing a standard advin-biotin immunoperoxidase complex technique. This method, adapted from Weidner et al., involved examining each specimen slice under low-power magnification (×40) to locate regions with the densest concentration of capillaries and small vessels. Subsequently, the most vascularized area was chosen, and a ×200 field was captured and saved as a JPEG image. Individual microvessels were identified as brown-stained endothelial cells distinctly separated from surrounding microvessels, tumor cells, and mesenchymal cells. However, large vessels, defined as having lumens exceeding eight red blood cells in diameter or possessing thick muscular walls, were not included in the microvessel count.
To assess the repeatability of perfusion measurements, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed. For comparing quantitative perfusion and MVD results across different histological types, tumor sizes, and nodal metastasis statuses, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by the least significant difference (LSD)-t method for multiple comparisons. Patients were categorized into two groups based on tumor size (greater than 3 cm vs. 3 cm or less) and nodal metastasis status (positive vs. negative) for these comparisons. The 7th edition of the TNM staging system for non-small-cell lung cancer guided the determination of metastasis status. Additionally, Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine the relationships between quantitative perfusion parameters and MVD. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v. 18.0; Chicago, IL), with a P-value below 0.05 indicating statistical significance.
The study found strong repeatability in perfusion measurements. The Pearson correlation coefficient for Ktrans (Ktrans_1 vs. Ktrans_2) was 0.983 (P < 0.001), and for Ve (Ve_1 vs. Ve_2) it was 0.972 (P < 0.001). Across all 25 lung cancer patients, the final Ktrans value averaged 0.33 ± 0.22 min−1, while the final Ve was 0.25 ± 0.12. The corresponding MVD for this patient group was 49.68 ± 27.08 vessels/0.723 mm2. When examining specific histological types, patients with adenocarcinoma (n=15) exhibited Ktrans, Ve, and MVD values of 0.36 ± 0.26 min−1, 0.27 ± 0.13, and 49.09 ± 29.84 vessels/0.723 mm2, respectively. For squamous cell carcinoma patients (n=8), these values were 0.34 ± 0.17 min−1, 0.26 ± 0.12, and 53.85 ± 23.53 vessels/0.723 mm2. In the two small-cell carcinoma patients, Ktrans, Ve, and MVD were 0.13 ± 0.15 min−1, 0.14 ± 0.06, and 37.20 ± 28.28 vessels/0.723 mm2, respectively.
The study analyzed perfusion parameters and MVD across various patient characteristics, as detailed in Figure 3. While Ktrans, Ve, and MVD values appeared elevated in adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma compared to small-cell carcinoma, these differences lacked statistical significance (P > 0.05). Similarly, tumors larger than 3 cm (19 patients) exhibited lower Ktrans and MVD than those 3 cm or smaller (6 patients), but this observation was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Furthermore, metastasis-positive tumors showed higher Ktrans and MVD than metastasis-negative tumors, though this difference also did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). The assessment of tumor angiogenesis is crucial in cancer research and treatment. This is because tumor angiogenesis, a key characteristic of malignant tumors, initiates various neovascularization patterns when activated. This process ultimately results in increased microvascular growth and blood supply within the tumor. Such an enhanced blood supply then fuels the tumor's growth, invasion, and metastasis by providing essential nutrients and oxygen. Furthermore, abnormalities in tumor vascular structure lead to heightened vascular permeability, elevating the risk of tumor invasion and metastasis. The uneven distribution of tumor vessels also contributes to hypoxia, making tumor cells more aggressive. Consequently, analyzing tumor vasculature through perfusion measurements can help predict the effectiveness of antiangiogenic or vascular disruptive therapies.
Previous research has established a link between DCE-MRI perfusion parameters, like Ktrans and maximal enhancement rate, and tumor angiogenesis in animal models of lung cancer. Despite its common use in clinical settings, conventional DCE-MRI often produces images with limited temporal resolution, suitable only for qualitative evaluation. This limitation stems from the inherent speed constraints of MRI and its susceptibility to physiological motion. GRASP MRI offers a solution to these challenges. This technique facilitates continuous, accelerated data acquisition, allowing for simultaneous qualitative and quantitative perfusion analysis through the reconstruction of images with adaptable temporal resolutions. GRASP has already been successfully integrated into clinical practice and validated across various applications. Building on this, the current study aimed to further explore the correlation between GRASP MRI-derived perfusion parameters and MVD, assessing GRASP's potential as an alternative method for evaluating tumor angiogenesis.
This study's initial findings, derived from a patient cohort with various lung cancer subtypes, revealed a notable positive correlation between microvascular density (MVD) and the volume transfer coefficient (Ktrans) measured using GRASP imaging. This outcome supports our initial hypothesis that increased perfusion corresponds to higher MVD in lung cancer. The clinical importance of this preliminary result lies in its validation of perfusion measurements against established clinical methods for assessing tumor angiogenesis. This suggests that GRASP could serve as a valuable, noninvasive, free-breathing imaging technique for monitoring tumor growth and treatment response in cancer therapy. Furthermore, GRASP offers an advantage over the limitations of histological MVD counts by providing spatially resolved perfusion data, enabling a pixel-by-pixel representation of the entire tumor. Interestingly, our analysis indicated a weak correlation between the interstitial volume (Ve) and MVD. This might be attributed to the typical assumption in pharmacokinetic models that contrast agent distribution is confined to the vascular plasma and extravascular extracellular spaces. Given that vascular volume usually constitutes a small percentage (less than 5%) of the total tissue volume, the variability in Ve may be less pronounced than that of MVD.
This study aimed to compare perfusion measurements and microvascular density (MVD) across various lung cancer histological types, building on previous histopathological research suggesting differing angiogenesis levels among these types. While our findings indicated that Ktrans, Ve, and MVD were elevated in adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma compared to small-cell carcinoma, these differences did not reach statistical significance. This aligns with prior research utilizing DCE-CT perfusion. Furthermore, we observed that Ktrans and MVD were higher in lung tumors measuring 3 cm or less, as opposed to those exceeding 3 cm, though this difference was also not statistically significant. This contrasts with a previous study by Li et al., which reported significantly higher perfusion parameters (including perfusion index, peak intensification intensity, and blood flow) in smaller lung tumors using DCE-CT. This discrepancy could be attributed to our study's smaller sample size, suggesting a need for further investigation with a larger patient cohort. Concerning nodal metastasis, our results mirrored those of Li et al., showing that Ktrans and MVD were higher in metastatic lung tumors than in non-metastatic ones, although this trend also lacked statistical significance.
Previous research on tumor microvascular density (MVD) and quantitative perfusion parameters has yielded conflicting results. While some studies, aligning with our findings, demonstrated a positive correlation between MVD and perfusion metrics like peak intensification intensity and blood flow, others reported a negative relationship. This divergence likely stems from the inherent heterogeneity of tumor angiogenesis. Standard MVD measurement typically focuses on the most vascularized "hot-spot" regions. If the area chosen for MVD counting doesn't precisely correspond to the region analyzed for perfusion, it can introduce bias or even lead to contradictory outcomes. To mitigate this, our study ensured consistency by selecting regions of interest (ROIs) for perfusion quantification based on CT-guided biopsy images, thereby matching the MVD counting area.
Several limitations of this pilot study warrant consideration. One notable constraint was the relatively small sample size, particularly the inclusion of only two patients with small-cell lung cancer. Consequently, future research should involve a larger patient cohort to thoroughly validate the findings presented here. Another point to address is the methodology for perfusion analysis. This study operated under the assumption of a linear relationship between the contrast agent concentration and signal intensity. While incorporating a separate T1 map could enhance the precision of perfusion measurements, this would introduce additional complex steps. However, previous studies have indicated that the current assumption is acceptable and sufficient for comparing quantitative perfusion parameters across various applications. Furthermore, the scope of this study was limited to assessing the correlation between MVD and quantitative perfusion parameters exclusively within the biopsy region. This approach was chosen to validate the findings against existing clinical standards. Once this initial validation is complete, future work can extend perfusion quantification to encompass the entire lesion. Finally, the manual selection of regions of interest (ROIs) for perfusion analysis, guided by corresponding CT-biopsy images, presented a challenge. This method necessitates significant expertise from the users to precisely identify the target areas. A more accurate approach could involve co-registration of CT and MR images for improved ROI selection. Nevertheless, achieving accurate registration of CT and MR images in a moving organ, especially when imaging parameters differ, is a complex task demanding advanced and dependable algorithms, which will be a focus of future investigations.
This study's initial findings indicate that GRASP DCE-MRI, with its high temporal resolution, offers a promising noninvasive method for quantifying tumor angiogenesis in lung cancer patients. The observed correlation between Ktrans from GRASP MRI and MVD could potentially apply to other cancer types as well. Further details are available in the article's online supplementary materials.
