The EFSA Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms (GMO) previously evaluated the three individual genetic events that constitute the three-event stack oilseed rape (OSR) MS8 × RF3 × GT73. This Scientific Opinion focuses on the safety assessment of this three-event stack OSR, as well as previously unapproved subcombinations (MS8 × GT73 and RF3 × GT73). These subcombinations were assessed independently of their origin for food and feed uses, import, and processing, with the exclusion of isolated seed protein for food. The combination of the MS8, RF3, and GT73 OSR events within the three-event stack did not present any molecular, agronomic/phenotypic, or compositional characteristics that warranted further investigation. Consistent with prior assessments and the application's scope, the GMO Panel found no safety concerns for food and feed containing trace levels of the glyphosate oxidoreductase (GOX)v247 protein derived from the three-event stack OSR. However, the Panel could not assess the safety of protein-rich products from the three-event stack OSR, such as rapeseed protein isolates intended for animal feed. Due to the incomplete risk assessment for these protein-rich products, the GMO Panel is unable to finalize the food and feed safety assessment for the subcombinations MS8 × GT73 and RF3 × GT73 within the application's scope. The two-event stack OSR MS8 × RF3 falls outside the scope of this application. Considering the application's scope, the mode of action of the introduced traits, and available data on the three-event and two-event stack OSR MS8 × RF3, the GMO Panel concluded that various combinations of the MS8, RF3, and GT73 events would not pose environmental concerns.

The EFSA Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms (GMO Panel) was tasked with providing a Scientific Opinion on the safety of genetically modified oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73, a three-event stack tolerant to glufosinate-ammonium and glyphosate. This assessment, submitted under application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75 by Bayer CropScience AG and Monsanto, also covered specific subcombinations, referred to as 'subcombinations independently of their origin' per Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 503/2013. The application's scope allows for the market placement of the three-event stack oilseed rape and previously unapproved subcombinations (MS8 × GT73 and RF3 × GT73), irrespective of their origin. These are intended for food and feed uses, import, and processing, with the exclusion of isolated seed protein for food.
Subcombinations, defined as any pairing of two events within the three-event stack oilseed rape, arise from segregating progeny in harvested seeds. The safety of these subcombinations, found in the embryo and albumen, is assessed as part of the overall evaluation of the three-event stack oilseed rape in Section 4 of this Scientific Opinion.
Combinations of two events (MS8, RF3, or GT73) can also be considered "subcombinations." These can be developed through conventional breeding and marketed separately from the three-event stack. Oilseed rape stacks formed this way, excluding MS8 x RF3 (which is outside the application's scope), are evaluated for risk in Section 5 of this scientific opinion.
Following EFSA GMO Panel guidance for stacked events, the risk assessment for application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75 focused on event stability, expression, and potential interactions. This evaluation of the three-event stack oilseed rape and its subcombinations was based on prior assessments of the individual MS8, RF3, and GT73 events, as well as the two-event stack MS8 × RF3.
The GMO Panel based its Scientific Opinion on available data for the three-event stack oilseed rape and subcombinations, Member State comments, and scientific literature.
The three-event stack oilseed rape (OSR) was created through conventional crossing of three individual OSR events. The parent lines, MS8 and RF3, were developed to produce hybrid seeds (MS8 × RF3) for increased yield via hybrid vigour. MS8 expresses Barnase and phosphinothricin acetyl-transferase (PAT) proteins, while RF3 expresses Barstar and PAT proteins. Both MS8, RF3, and their two-event stack MS8 × RF3 have undergone previous safety assessments by the EFSA GMO Panel without any identified concerns. The GT73 OSR line, which expresses glyphosate oxidoreductase (GOX)v247 and CP4 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS) proteins, was also previously assessed. No safety concerns were identified for GT73, with the exception of protein-rich materials like isolated seed protein, for which a complete assessment could not be performed. Updated bioinformatic analyses and applicant reports have not revealed any new safety issues regarding the three single OSR events since their prior evaluations. Consequently, the GMO Panel maintains that its earlier conclusions on the safety of these single events remain valid, specifically for GT73 in products containing trace levels of GOXv247 protein.
The risk assessment for the three-event stack oilseed rape involved molecular characterization of inserted DNA and protein expression analysis. Agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional characteristics were comparatively evaluated. The safety of newly expressed proteins and the overall food/feed were assessed for toxicity, allergenicity, and nutritional aspects. Additionally, environmental impacts and the post-market environmental monitoring plan were examined.
Molecular analysis confirmed the integrity of the stacked events in oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73. Protein expression levels in the three-event stack showed expected variations compared to parental lines. Consequently, no interactions were identified that would compromise the integrity of the events or alter the expression levels of the newly introduced proteins.
No further food and feed safety assessment is needed for the identified differences in seed composition, agronomic, or phenotypic characteristics between the three-event stack oilseed rape and its conventional counterpart.
The GMO Panel concluded that food and feed derived from the three-event stack oilseed rape, containing trace amounts of the GOXv247 protein, do not present safety concerns, aligning with prior assessments. This includes products like oil, pollen, and toasted meal. However, the Panel cannot evaluate the safety of rapeseed protein isolates or similar products from this oilseed rape. This limitation stems from a lack of essential data for the GOXv247 protein's safety assessment. While food-grade rapeseed protein isolates are outside the application's scope, protein-rich products for animal feed are included, and their use is growing.
Should viable seeds of the three-event stack oilseed rape be accidentally released into the environment, the GMO Panel found no evidence suggesting an increased likelihood of feral plants or their wild relatives establishing and spreading. This holds true unless the plants are exposed to glufosinate-ammonium or glyphosate herbicides. Even with such herbicide exposure, the Panel believes the environmental impacts would not differ from those of conventional oilseed rape. Furthermore, within the scope of application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75, interactions with the biotic and abiotic environment were deemed not relevant. The Panel also identified no risks associated with the unlikely, though theoretically possible, horizontal gene transfer of recombinant DNA from the three-event stack oilseed rape to bacteria.
The GMO Panel concluded that the three-event stack oilseed rape poses no safety concerns for the environment if viable seeds are accidentally released. This conclusion considers the introduced traits, comparative analysis results, exposure routes, and exposure levels. Potential interactions between the individual events within the stack were also deemed not to raise safety concerns.
This application covers specific subcombinations of the oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73, namely MS8 × GT73 and RF3 × GT73, which have not been previously authorized. The two-event stack MS8 × RF3 is excluded from this scope. The safety assessment of these subcombinations relies on the evaluations of the individual events, data from the three-event stack, and any other relevant subcombination data. However, due to the inability to fully assess the safety of the three-event stack for protein-rich products like rapeseed protein isolates used in animal feed, the GMO Panel cannot complete the safety assessment for the subcombinations within the application's scope.
No post-market monitoring is deemed necessary for food and feed derived from the three-event stack oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73, as no safety concerns were identified regarding trace levels of the GOXv247 protein. However, the GMO Panel cannot currently recommend post-market monitoring for rapeseed protein isolates or similar products originating from this oilseed rape.
The GMO Panel found the applicant's Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plans to be consistent with the scope of the three-event stack oilseed rape and the previously assessed two-event stack MS8 x RF3. The Panel also agreed with the proposed reporting intervals within the PMEM plan.

Field trials in Canada over four growing seasons provided data on the agronomic, phenotypic, and seed composition of the three-event stack oilseed rape, as presented in application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75.
For the comparative assessment, the GMO Panel prioritized the 2011 field trials, as they met the criteria outlined in the GMO Panel's guidance for assessing food and feed from GM plants. Previous trials in 2008 and 2009 were deemed unsuitable by the Panel. The 2008 trials used a GM parental line as a comparator, while the 2009 trials used two negative segregants. The Panel concluded that relying solely on parental lines or negative segregants could overlook unintended differences arising from genetic modification. Although the 2010 trials utilized a conventional counterpart, natural variation was assessed using three commercial GM oilseed rape lines.
In Canada's typical summer oilseed rape growing regions, field trials were conducted across eight locations in 2011. Each site featured a randomized complete block design with four replicates, growing the MS8 × RF3 × GT73 oilseed rape, its conventional counterpart, and three distinct non-GM reference varieties. All were treated with standard pesticides. The MS8 × RF3 × GT73 oilseed rape also received its intended herbicides. The chosen comparator was a non-GM oilseed rape line with a genetic background closely matching that of MS8 × RF3 × GT73, as confirmed by its pedigree, making it a suitable conventional counterpart for the trials.
The 2011 field trial data on agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional characteristics were statistically analyzed according to the GMO Panel's recommendations. This analysis involved two key tests: a difference test to ascertain if the GM plant varied from its conventional counterpart, and an equivalence test to determine if the GM plant's traits fell within the natural variation observed in non-GM commercial reference varieties. As per EFSA guidelines, the equivalence test results were classified into four categories. These categories ranged from Category I, signifying full equivalence, to Category II, suggesting equivalence is more probable than non-equivalence. Category III indicated that non-equivalence was more likely than equivalence, while Category IV denoted non-equivalence. These classifications aided in drawing conclusions about the presence or absence of equivalence.
In the 2011 field trials, nine agronomic and phenotypic endpoints were assessed. These included early stand count, seedling vigour, plant counts before and after herbicide application, flowering duration (start and end), plant height, maturity date, and seed yield.
The three-event stack oilseed rape, when not treated with intended herbicides, showed no statistically significant differences compared to its conventional counterpart.
When treated with herbicides, the three-event stack oilseed rape showed statistically significant differences in flowering end, seed yield, and plant height compared to its conventional counterpart. For flowering end and seed yield, equivalence tests confirmed that the stack's results fell within the range of non-GM reference varieties (equivalence category I). However, for plant height, the equivalence test indicated that the mean value for the stack was outside the established limits, with its confidence interval overlapping one limit. This suggests non-equivalence for plant height is more likely than equivalence (equivalence category III). The potential environmental impact of this observed difference in plant height was subsequently assessed in Section 4.4.

This is because no single piece of information or method can reliably predict allergenicity. When multiple proteins with potential adjuvant activity are present, any interactions that could enhance this activity and affect the GM crop's allergenicity are also assessed.

*B. napus* (oilseed rape) is an allotetraploid species with a genome constitution of AACC. It likely evolved from the hybridization and polyploidization of two diploid species: *B. rapa* (AA) and *B. oleracea* (CC). This annual plant is cultivated for agricultural purposes.
Oilseed rape (B. napus) can survive and establish populations outside of cultivated areas. Research indicates that this species is capable of forming self-perpetuating populations in seminatural and ruderal environments across various countries. Oilseed rape is characterized as an opportunistic species, readily germinating and exploiting disturbed sites, such as mowed areas, for rapid resource acquisition. However, in undisturbed natural habitats, oilseed rape struggles to establish stable, long-term populations. This is likely due to a lack of germination sites free from competition and increased exposure to environmental stressors that limit its fitness. While feral populations may initially establish in competition-free areas, they tend to decline over several years. Persistence is more likely in habitats that experience regular disturbance. The continued presence or reappearance of oilseed rape populations in a given location can be attributed to several factors. These include new seed spills, germination from existing soil seedbanks, seed dispersal by established feral plants, or the movement of feral seed to new areas.
The three-event stack oilseed rape (MS8 × RF3 × GT73) confers tolerance to glufosinate-ammonium and glyphosate herbicides. This is achieved through the CP4 epsps, gox, and pat genes, which provide a potential agronomic and selective advantage when the plants are exposed to these herbicides.
Agronomic and phenotypic data for the three-event stack oilseed rape were collected from field trials in Canada over multiple growing seasons. Only the 2011 data set enabled a suitable comparison between the GM oilseed rape and its conventional counterpart. This dataset revealed a reduced plant height in the three-event stack oilseed rape when treated with the intended herbicides. This difference indicated a higher likelihood of non-equivalence compared to non-GM reference varieties. Other measured plant characteristics showed no significant differences. Given that plant fitness depends on performance throughout its lifecycle, this observed difference in height is unlikely to enhance the survival, reproductive capacity, competitiveness, or invasiveness of the MS8 × RF3 × GT73 oilseed rape.
Data comparing seed dormancy between the three-event stack oilseed rape and its conventional counterpart were not provided. However, there is no evidence suggesting that tolerance to glufosinate-ammonium or glyphosate alters seed dormancy in GM herbicide-tolerant oilseed rape compared to non-GM comparators. Seed dormancy is primarily influenced by parental genetic background rather than the acquisition of herbicide tolerance traits.
The three-event stack oilseed rape's general characteristics are unchanged from its conventional counterpart, meaning its capacity to form feral populations in ruderal habitats persists. Consequently, seed spills during import could lead to feral MS8 × RF3 × GT73 oilseed rape plants. However, these are unlikely to form stable populations long-term. If exposed to glufosinate-ammonium or glyphosate herbicides, these feral plants may gain a selective advantage, potentially increasing their local occurrence. Such events are expected to be confined to herbicide-treated areas with low biodiversity, thus minimizing environmental impacts.
Feral oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73 plants from seed import spills are expected to be rare and primarily found in ruderal habitats. Consequently, these plants are unlikely to cause additional agronomic or environmental impacts compared to conventional oilseed rape.
Gene transfer requires pathways for DNA movement, either through horizontal transfer or vertical gene flow from feral plants resulting from spilled seeds.
Previous assessments by the GMO Panel have evaluated the potential for horizontal gene transfer of recombinant DNA from the individual oilseed events MS8, RF3, and GT73, as well as the two-event stack MS8 × RF3. These evaluations did not identify any concerns regarding the unlikely, yet theoretically possible, transfer of recombinant genes to bacteria, whether in the gut or other environments. The three-event stack oilseed rape expresses genes of bacterial origin, including the CP4 epsps gene from *Agrobacterium sp.*, the *gox* gene from *O. anthropi*, the *bar* gene from *S. hygroscopicus*, and the *barstar* and *barnase* genes from *B. amyloliquefaciens*. No sequences facilitating double homologous recombination were found. Since natural variants of these genes are already present in environmental bacteria, homologous recombination and gene replacement are not expected to confer novel properties or selective advantages to microbial communities. Furthermore, no synergistic effects among the recombinant genes that would increase the likelihood of horizontal gene transfer, such as combinations of recombinogenic sequences, were identified. Given that the three-event stack oilseed rape is produced through conventional crossing, the close linkage of different events is highly improbable due to their genomic separation. Consequently, the GMO Panel concludes that, within the scope of application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75, the horizontal transfer of recombinant genes from this oilseed rape to bacteria, while theoretically possible, does not pose any environmental safety concerns.
Accidental spillage of imported oilseed rape seeds can lead to feral plants in disturbed habitats. These feral plants pose a potential risk of transferring recombinant DNA to sexually cross-compatible plants. This pathway to harm is considered within the scope of application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75, given the biology of oilseed rape.
As an open-pollinating crop, oilseed rape can cross-pollinate with other Brassica species. Feral oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73 plants, originating from spilled seeds, could potentially pollinate adjacent oilseed rape crops. While seeds shed from these cross-pollinated crops might develop into GM volunteers in subsequent plantings, the probability of this occurring under import conditions is very low.
Oilseed rape can hybridize with wild relatives. While many such hybrids are documented, transgene introgression in field conditions has only been confirmed for oilseed rape x B. rapa progeny.
Transgene introgression into wild relatives, specifically *B. rapa* hybrids, requires feral GM oilseed rape to overlap in flowering time and space with compatible species. Following this, transgenes must be passed through successive backcross generations or selfing to stabilize within the recipient's genome. These biological barriers limit the frequency of hybrids and backcrosses with *B. rapa*. Consequently, reported instances of such introgression in fields, ports, roadsides, and riverbanks have been found to be low.
The GMO Panel views the presence of feral oilseed rape MS8 × RF3 × GT73, along with pollen dispersal and subsequent cross-pollination, as not inherently harmful to the environment. This is because there's no indication that the herbicide tolerance traits would increase the potential for gene flow, fitness, persistence, or invasiveness of either the GM oilseed rape or compatible wild relatives. However, if these cross-compatible plants are exposed to herbicides like glufosinate-ammonium or glyphosate, they may gain a selective advantage, potentially leading to a higher prevalence. Such occurrences would likely be confined to areas treated with herbicides, thus minimizing environmental impacts. Consequently, the GMO Panel concludes that the adoption of herbicide tolerance traits by cross-compatible plants would not result in additional agronomic or environmental consequences.
The GMO Panel concludes that gene spread from the three-event stack oilseed rape in Europe will not differ from conventional varieties, even with herbicide exposure. Therefore, no increased environmental effects are anticipated.
The GMO Panel considers interactions between feral oilseed rape plants, resulting from seed spills, and target organisms irrelevant, as no target organisms exist.
Given the application's scope and minimal environmental exposure, the GMO Panel deems interactions between feral oilseed rape plants from seed spills and non-target organisms irrelevant.
The GMO Panel considers that potential interactions with the abiotic environment and biogeochemical cycles are not relevant issues, given the application's scope and the low level of environmental exposure.
Should viable seeds of the three-event stack oilseed rape be accidentally released into the environment, the GMO Panel found no evidence suggesting an increased likelihood of feral plants or their wild relatives establishing and spreading. This holds true unless the plants are exposed to glufosinate-ammonium or glyphosate herbicides. Even with such herbicide exposure, the Panel believes the environmental impacts would not differ from those of conventional oilseed rape. Interactions with the biotic and abiotic environment were deemed not relevant within the scope of application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75. Furthermore, no risks were identified concerning the unlikely, yet theoretically possible, horizontal gene transfer of recombinant DNA from the three-event stack oilseed rape to bacteria.
The GMO Panel concludes that accidental release of viable MS8 × RF3 × GT73 oilseed rape seeds into the environment would not pose safety concerns. This conclusion is based on the novel event combination, introduced traits, comparative analysis results, exposure routes, and limited exposure levels.
The three-event stack oilseed rape, combining MS8, RF3, and GT73, shows no molecular, agronomic, phenotypic, or compositional issues requiring further investigation.
Previous assessments indicated no safety concerns for food and feed derived from the three-event stack oilseed rape containing trace levels of GOXv247 protein, such as in oil, pollen, or toasted meal. A post-market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plan, as outlined in Directive 2001/18/EC, serves two primary objectives. Firstly, it aims to validate assumptions made during the environmental risk assessment (ERA) regarding the occurrence and impact of potential adverse effects associated with the GMO or its use. Secondly, it seeks to detect any unforeseen adverse effects on human health or the environment that were not identified in the initial ERA.
While EFSA's mandate excludes the final adoption of post-market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plans, as these are risk management tools, the GMO Panel provides its scientific opinion on the applicant's submitted PMEM plan.
No case-specific monitoring is required as the environmental risk assessment found no potential adverse environmental effects from the three-event stack oilseed rape or its subcombinations.
The applicant's proposed Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plans for the three-event stack oilseed rape involve a multi-faceted approach. Operators, including import and processing federations, will report any observed adverse effects of GMOs on human health and the environment to the applicant through a centralized system. A new coordinating system from EuropaBio will collect this information. Additionally, existing surveillance networks will be utilized. The applicant intends to submit an annual PMEM report.
The GMO Panel found the applicant's proposed Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plans to be consistent with the scope of the three-event stack oilseed rape and the previously assessed two-event stack MS8 x RF3. They agreed with the applicant's suggested reporting intervals. However, the PMEM plan for the three-event stack oilseed rape lacked provisions for the two-event stacks that had not yet been assessed by the GMO Panel.
The GMO Panel acknowledges the applicant's proposed management systems to limit environmental exposure from accidental releases of viable MS8 × RF3 × GT73 oilseed rape seeds.

The process for evaluating the genetically modified oilseed rape MS8 x RF3 x GT73 began with a request submitted to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) by the Competent Authority of the Netherlands on October 20, 2009. This request, referencing application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75, was for the placing on the market of this genetically modified oilseed rape, a joint submission from Bayer CropScience and Monsanto Europe S.A./N.V., in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. EFSA formally acknowledged receipt of this request from the Competent Authority of the Netherlands on November 4, 2009. Subsequently, EFSA initiated the completeness check process by sending a letter to the applicant on November 27, 2009, requesting additional information. The applicant responded on January 15, 2010, providing a timeline for their submissions. Further timeline extensions were communicated by the applicant on February 1, March 2, and March 18, 2010. On March 29, 2010, the applicant submitted a consolidated version of the dossier, which also included an extension to the scope of the application. EFSA continued its completeness check, issuing another request for additional information to the applicant on April 16, 2010. The applicant provided a timeline for responses on May 31, 2010, followed by further timeline extensions on June 18 and August 2, 2010. A letter from Monsanto on September 16, 2010, addressed the authorization to refer to specific data. On September 21, 2010, the applicant submitted additional information as part of the completeness check and responded to the request for data access. The completeness check continued with EFSA requesting more information on December 6, 2010. The applicant provided a timeline for responses on January 24, 2011, and an extension on April 28, 2011. Additional information was submitted by the applicant on July 18, 2011, prompting another request for information from EFSA on August 17, 2011. The applicant provided a timeline for responses on September 19, 2011, and an extension on December 15, 2011. In early 2012, Monsanto submitted a letter on February 7, 2012, regarding authorization to refer to application data. This was followed by the applicant providing additional information on February 16, 2012, as part of the completeness check. EFSA requested further information on March 6, 2012, and the applicant submitted additional details on April 20, 2012. On May 11, 2012, EFSA issued a "Statement of Validity" for the application EFSA-GMO-NL-2009-75, confirming the application's compliance with regulatory requirements for the placing on the market of genetically modified oilseed rape MS8 x RF3 x GT73. Concurrently, on the same date, EFSA informed the applicant that the clock was stopped due to missing information related to single events in applications EFSA-GMO-BE-2010-81 and EFSA-GMO-NL-2010-87. The clock was restarted on January 25, 2013, following the finalization of the assessment for these related applications. The assessment process resumed with EFSA requesting additional information on April 2, 2013, and stopping the clock again. The applicant responded on May 16, 2013, providing the requested information and spontaneous supplementary details. EFSA issued further requests for information on June 3 and June 25, 2013, maintaining the stopped clock. The applicant provided timelines for responses on July 17 and August 1, 2013, followed by additional information on September 2, 2013. Notably, on September 10, 2013, the applicant requested a change to the scope of the application, which was submitted to the European Commission (EC). EFSA continued to request additional information on December 6, 2013, keeping the clock stopped. The applicant provided a timeline for responses on January 20, 2014, and submitted further information on February 4, 2014. EFSA issued another request for information on June 11, 2014, and the applicant provided a timeline for responses on July 25, 2014, followed by additional information on September 12, 2014. In early 2015, the applicant sought clarifications on the application's progress via an email on January 9, 2015. EFSA provided clarifications on February 10, 2015, and then issued a request for additional information on February 13, 2015, maintaining the stopped clock. Further requests for information were made on April 17, 2015, with the applicant responding on May 4, 2015. EFSA reiterated a request for additional information on April 17, 2015, and the applicant provided more details on May 28, 2015. On June 11, 2015, the applicant requested a modification to the scope of the application, which was submitted to the EC. EFSA issued another request for information on June 24, 2015, and the applicant provided further clarifications on the scope on August 13, 2015, along with additional information. EFSA requested more information on October 19, 2015, and the applicant responded on November 10, 2015. The clock was restarted on January 19, 2016, after EFSA received the necessary information. The applicant provided spontaneous additional information on January 21 and January 25, 2016. On January 22, 2016, the EC communicated with EFSA regarding the scope of the application, and EFSA responded to the EC on January 28, 2016. EFSA requested updated additional information from the applicant via email on February 18, 2016. The applicant provided clarifications on the scope on February 29, 2016, and submitted updated additional information on March 11, 2016, following the modification of the application's scope.
