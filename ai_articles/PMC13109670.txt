Hexapods can access confined spaces more effectively by using advanced motions that involve wall footholds. Despite their potential, the practical implementation of these motions on hexapods has not been previously explored. While a standard hexapod design with three degrees of freedom per leg is kinematically capable of these movements, the specific joint requirements remained unknown. This paper analyzes wall walking and chimney walking, two types of advanced motion, to determine these joint requirements. Experimental validation confirmed that a standard hexapod can indeed perform these advanced maneuvers.

Legged robot locomotion offers benefits over other propulsion methods, a topic extensively covered in research.
Legged robot locomotion's advantages have led to competitions like DARPA Robotics Challenge (disasters), ARGOS (refineries), and Eurathlon (urban/indoor settings). These events serve as testing grounds for advanced legged robots, simulating real-world inspection and intervention tasks.
Current robotics challenges primarily focus on 2D movement, where robots navigate obstacles roughly their own size. These environments, like doorways or stairwells, are typically wide enough for the robot's footprint. However, the complexities of non-planar motion within confined spaces have received little attention. For instance, a hexapod can traverse a gap narrower than its own footprint by utilizing vertical surfaces for support, as illustrated in Figure 1(a). Figure 1(b) further demonstrates the potential for achieving 3D motion in specific scenarios.
This article details non-planar hexapod maneuvers, called advanced motions, by examining the scenarios presented in Figure 1.
The literature has not previously addressed the maneuvers depicted in Figure 1, specifically wall walking (Figure 1a) and chimney walking (Figure 1b).
This article focuses on understanding the joint demands for advanced hexapod motions, which are expected to be greater than those for 2D movements. A primary goal is to determine how crucial design elements influence these joint requirements, specifically examining the interplay between a robot's link lengths, its motion plan, and the resulting joint needs. A significant aspect of this research involves assessing the capability of a conventionally designed, "off-the-shelf" hexapod to perform these advanced maneuvers.
The article proceeds by first outlining existing hexapod motion and joint characterization methods. Subsequently, it delves into a detailed analysis of joint requirements for advanced motions, followed by a description of the Corin hexapod's mechanical design. The paper then presents and discusses the validation experiments and their outcomes. Finally, the work concludes with a summary and a look ahead to future research.
This section outlines common hexapod movements and then details methods for selecting hexapod links and joints. Figure 2 defines the terminology used throughout this article.
Hexapod movement involves two distinct stages. During the transfer phase, a leg repositions itself between footholds. Conversely, the support phase sees the robot's body propelled in the intended direction by the legs bearing its weight.
Typically, hexapods place their feet on the ground. Even on uneven terrain like grass or rocks, these contact points are generally considered to be on the ground surface.
Kinematically, inclined surfaces don't impact hexapod movement, allowing their generalization alongside ground surfaces for foothold analysis.
Previous research has explored limited wall-surface movements, such as a climbing strategy for overcoming large obstacles.
Previous research on hexapods using wall surfaces, such as navigating narrow paths with a semi-elliptical shell, has been limited compared to the advanced motions explored in this study.
Hexapod design involves numerous interconnected parameters, including base width, length, and leg link dimensions. These parameters influence joint requirements like range of motion, velocity, and torque, which then feedback and impact the design choices themselves. To simplify this complex selection process, designers often begin by either defining the design parameters and then determining joint requirements, or by establishing joint requirements first and subsequently choosing the design parameters.
Design parameters are chosen using bio-inspired, pragmatic, or optimization methods, sometimes combined. Bio-inspired methods derive design decisions from biological organisms.
Hexapod joint characterization often employs static analysis or motion simulation. Design choices can be pragmatic, considering factors like the deployment environment or actuator torque limits. Alternatively, optimization-based strategies might prioritize metrics such as energy consumption, mobility, or dexterity.
Current hexapod literature consistently focuses on two-dimensional movements when addressing both link parameter selection and joint characterization, often utilizing predefined link parameters. However, understanding how non-planar hexapod motion impacts these aspects is crucial. To address this, the subsequent section will delve into a static analysis of advanced motions. This analysis aims to illuminate the influence of design parameters on joint characteristics, ultimately facilitating the selection of suitable design parameters and actuators for hexapods performing advanced maneuvers.
To understand the joint requirements for a standard hexapod performing advanced motions, this section analyzes the impact of key design parameters. Specifically, it examines how body width and leg link length influence the maximum torque and range of motion needed for such movements. This analysis is crucial for selecting appropriate actuators based on design parameters, or conversely, for determining design parameters given actuator capabilities. The study focuses on characterizing the specific advanced motions illustrated in Figure 1 to detail these relationships.
For motion analysis, several assumptions were made: movement is semi-static and discretized, occurring solely within the y-z plane. The robot's entire mass is centered geometrically, and tangential forces are confined to the friction cone's edge or interior. Additionally, foot contact forces from multiple support legs on one side are evenly distributed.
The robot model is simplified to two legs, each with two degrees of freedom, based on the second assumption. For force distribution analysis, gaits employing two support legs per side are treated as a single leg. The coxa link, l1, is incorporated into the lumped body width, lw.
This study examines two distinct characteristic movements: wall transition (Figure 4(b)) and chimney walking (Figure 4(a)). Wall transition, where the robot moves sideways to approach a wall, is investigated first. This motion serves as a preliminary step to understanding wall walking (Figure 1(b)), as it involves a greater joint torque requirement. This increased demand stems from the extended horizontal moment arm between the robot's center of mass and its feet. The total energy cost (E) is calculated by integrating power over the motion sequences. In the discretized scenario, this involves the joint torque (τns), joint velocity (q̇ns), and joint position (qns) for each leg.

Figure 8 illustrates the Corin hexapod's mechanical design. This agile and versatile robot is specifically engineered for multi-legged research into advanced motions.
A rectangular body was chosen for its minimal footprint during wall and chimney walking, unlike hybrid or hexagonal designs that result in wider profiles.
A rectangular body shape was chosen for the hexapod, as it minimizes the footprint width necessary for wall and chimney walking. This contrasts with hybrid or hexagonal designs, where the middle legs protrude further, as shown in Figure 9. To facilitate upside-down operation, two extra actuators were integrated into the body, ensuring sensors like cameras or LiDAR maintain an upright orientation. The leg mounting configuration mirrored that of the DLR Crawler.
The hexapod's standard insect-like leg, featuring three degrees of freedom, offers a larger workspace than parallel manipulators. Its hip incorporates two joints: the coxa (q1) and the femur (q2).
For a symmetrical workspace around the sagittal plane, the q2 joint is positioned along the x-axis of q1, with their rotation axes perpendicular.
Robotics Dynamixel MX64s actuators, chosen for their high torque, power each joint.
Each foot is equipped with a three-axis capacitive force sensor for 3D contact force measurement, facilitating force/position control. Additionally, the rubber feet offer high friction and damping, minimizing slippage and impact forces.
Link parameters were chosen to maximize robot mobility and ensure joint torque demands for characteristic motions remained within the pre-selected actuator constraints. This approach prioritized actuator capabilities in the design process.
Link length selection did not prioritize energy consumption (E) due to the lack of a universal minimum for both wall transition and chimney walking. Instead, the joint torque constraint restricted parameter selection, setting a maximum overall width (low) of 0.90 m. To enhance mobility, the robot's footprint (lw and lbl) was minimized, while leg link length was maximized. This meant maximizing low and minimizing lw.
With a maximum overall width of 0.9m and a minimum lumped body width of 0.3m, each leg achieves a total length of 0.3m (l2+l3). Within these parameters, various link ratios are permissible.
Given the torque constraint, various link ratios were viable. However, a 1:1 ratio for l2:l3 was chosen due to its superior manipulability, optimizing the robot's range of motion.
After choosing the link ratio, the initial wall contact foot position for wall transition required joint angles of q2=65° and q3=−155°.
The joint angles required for the first wall contact foot position during wall transition are q2=126° and q3=−156°, as depicted in Figure 10. The length of l1 was chosen to accommodate q2's necessary range of motion. Subsequently, lbw was determined using equation (1), and lbl was set to 0.23m.

Corin was developed through both simulations and physical construction to investigate how a standard hexapod can perform non-planar movements. This initial study concentrated on the robot's capacity to achieve static and semi-static poses, which are crucial for transitioning between floor, wall, and chimney walking. While acknowledging the approach's limitations, this quasi-static assessment offers fundamental insights into the demands placed on a hexapod designed for non-planar maneuvers.
Corin, a physical hexapod, underwent testing in basic non-planar environments. An external 12V umbilical supplied power, reducing the robot's total weight to 4.6 kg. Experiments utilized plain wood or rubber mats as environmental surfaces. The friction coefficients for Corin's rubber feet on these surfaces were estimated at 0.83 and 0.87, respectively, a range consistent with the prior design analysis.
To assess its static stability, the robot underwent testing for both wall and chimney walking. For wall transitions, the number of supporting feet was progressively decreased from six to three while the robot held specific transitional poses. Similarly, for chimney walking, support was reduced from six to four legs. These experiments confirmed the robot's ability to maintain its static posture, aligning with the prior design analysis.
The robot's joints were managed by their integrated position controllers. During evaluations, the robot executed 0.05 m translations along all three axes from each pose, maintaining six-leg support. This open-loop process, lacking active body orientation regulation, confirmed the feasibility of non-planar movements with discontinuous phase gaits.
The robot's static pose was assessed for chimney walking by placing it centrally within various chimney widths, each incrementally increasing by 0.05 m. For wall transition experiments, the robot's pose was determined relative to the floor-wall junction. All evaluations, which included recording leg positions and robot pose parameters (detailed in Table 3), were conducted in triplicate.

All joint actuators operated significantly below their stall current limits. While the use of low PID gains, due to the absence of higher-level controllers, led to a minor 4° maximum error in position tracking, this deviation was deemed acceptable. Therefore, the robot proved capable of executing the wall transition maneuver.
During chimney walking experiments, the Corin hexapod remained stable even when legs 1 and 6 were lifted from the walls. Lifting leg 1 caused a slight forward tilt, as the contact forces from the five remaining support legs created a moment around the robot's center of mass. However, this initial tilt was corrected when leg 6 was subsequently lifted, demonstrating the symmetrical nature of the robot's balance. Overall, the contact forces did not produce significant moments, as the robot's orientation remained largely unchanged.

Existing literature's methods for determining torque limits in 2D hexapod movement on flat ground consistently indicate that τ2 is less than τ3. This is anticipated in 2D motion because the q2 joint is typically farther from the foot's contact point than q3, leading to a longer moment arm and thus a greater τ2 demand. However, this study's analysis reveals the opposite for wall and chimney walking, where τ2 is smaller than τ3.
Foot contact forces in wall and chimney walking do not directly engage q3, leading to different torque demands compared to 2D movements.
Standard torque characterization methods, typically used for 2D hexapod movement, prove inadequate for determining torque demands during non-planar motions. This underscores the necessity for the more sophisticated analysis detailed in this research.
This study is limited to static and semi-static maneuvers, which are foundational for full maneuvers. Achieving full maneuvers necessitates advanced high-level controllers incorporating force feedback and torque control.
The current robot, relying solely on position controllers, is restricted to static and semi-static movements. Future research will focus on developing the advanced controllers required for executing full, dynamic motions.
This paper introduces a static, two-dimensional analysis of advanced hexapod movements, focusing on wall and chimney walking. This analysis revealed how link parameters influence the required joint torque and also determined the necessary joint range of motion. The findings from this analysis guided the selection of link parameters for the Corin hexapod, which largely adheres to conventional hexapod designs. Experimental validation using a physical Corin hexapod confirmed the feasibility of these two advanced motion types. The research demonstrates that even hexapods with standard designs can perform complex maneuvers, provided their link lengths and joint actuators are appropriately chosen. This capability allows hexapods to access environments previously considered unreachable.
During experiments, the robot occasionally lost balance due to position control. Implementing a high-level controller with torque and foot force feedback to evenly distribute foot forces could resolve this. Additionally, employing low-level impedance controllers on each leg would enhance robustness on uneven terrain, thereby reducing the computational load on the high-level controller.
