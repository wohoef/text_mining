Hexapods could significantly expand their access to confined spaces by employing advanced motions that use wall footholds. Despite this potential, practical applications of such movements on hexapods remain unexplored. While standard hexapod designs, featuring three degrees of freedom per leg, are kinematically capable of these motions, the specific joint requirements for their execution are unknown. This paper addresses this gap by analyzing wall walking and chimney walking, two advanced motion types, to determine these joint requirements. Experimental validation confirms that a standard hexapod design can indeed perform these advanced maneuvers.

Literature describes three methods for characterizing joint torques.
Joint torque characterization methods from the literature, typically applied to 2D hexapod motion, were compared to the analysis presented here. These methods consistently show that the torque required for joint 2 (τ2) is less than for joint 3 (τ3) in 2D movements. This is because joint 2 is usually further from the foot contact point, leading to a longer moment arm and thus a greater torque requirement. However, our analysis of wall and chimney walking reveals the opposite: τ2 is smaller than τ3. This difference arises because, in these advanced motions, foot contact forces do not act directly through joint 3. This comparison demonstrates that standard torque characterization methods are inadequate for non-planar motions, underscoring the necessity of the more advanced analysis detailed in this article.

Hexapod motion involves two phases: transferring a leg to a new foothold and moving the body with supporting legs. Typically, footholds are on the ground, even on irregular or inclined surfaces. However, some movements utilize non-ground surfaces, like climbing large obstacles or navigating narrow passages with a specialized shell. These existing uses of wall surfaces are limited compared to the advanced motions proposed here.

This work's limitation to static/semi-static maneuvers precedes full maneuvers. Full maneuvers necessitate high-level controllers with force feedback and torque control, which are not implemented here.
The HyQ platform demonstrates that chimney walking demands substantial torque. Therefore, we assume a minimum of four support legs to reduce joint torque. With two support legs on each side, the tangential force is evenly distributed, and the minimum normal force prevents slippage.
The robot's current position controllers restrict it to static and semi-static movements. Future work will focus on developing the advanced controllers needed for full motion, enabling dynamic maneuvers beyond these limitations.
This paper analyzes advanced hexapod maneuvers, specifically wall and chimney walking, through a static 2D framework. It establishes the relationship between leg link parameters and the required joint torque, also identifying the necessary joint range of motion. This analysis guided the selection of link parameters for the Corin hexapod, which employs a conventional design. Experimental validation using a physical Corin hexapod confirmed the feasibility of these two advanced motion types. The findings demonstrate that hexapods with standard designs can perform complex maneuvers, provided that appropriate leg link lengths and joint actuators are chosen. This capability allows hexapods to access environments previously considered inaccessible.
Experiments revealed that position control can cause temporary balance loss in the robot. Implementing a high-level controller, utilizing torque and foot force feedback, could address this by evenly distributing foot force. Additionally, low-level impedance controllers on each leg would enhance robust operation on uneven terrain, thereby reducing the computational load on the high-level controller.
