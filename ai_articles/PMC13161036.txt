This observational study assessed the survival rate of 43 lithium disilicate overlays (IPS e.max Press) placed on 8 patients over a mean follow-up of 32 months (range 19-45 months). The primary objective was to evaluate the survival of these overlays when used to increase occlusal vertical dimension (OVD) through minimally invasive techniques, while also examining restoration thicknesses at various tooth locations. The OVD increase was meticulously planned using diagnostic wax-ups and occlusal treatment plans. Before adhesive cementation, restoration thicknesses were measured. Survival rates were determined using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Results indicated a high survival rate of 97.7% at 32 months, with no fractures observed. One instance of infiltration required endodontic treatment. Analysis revealed that monolithic restorations were thickest on the cusp sides and thinnest in the central fossa. The average dental tissue removed during preparation was 0.98 mm for non-functional cusps, 0.88 mm for functional cusps, and 0.57 mm for the central fossa. In conclusion, lithium disilicate posterior overlays demonstrated an excellent, complication-free survival rate. The material facilitated conservative restorations with minimal thickness. Clinically, these overlays offer a promising 32-month survival rate, enabling conservative tooth preparation and minimizing fracture risk, while also providing excellent esthetics.

Advances in restorative materials and adhesive techniques have enabled the development of minimally invasive preparation methods. These techniques preserve substantial tooth structure, thereby maximizing the reinforcement of dental elements. Furthermore, these new materials address inherent issues with all-ceramic systems, such as fracture susceptibility and antagonist wear. Consequently, indirect adhesive restorations are now frequently used on posterior teeth, yielding excellent outcomes in marginal integrity, aesthetics, and the strengthening of remaining tooth structure, particularly when cusps are covered.
Lithium disilicate stands out as a highly promising restorative material due to its superior mechanical strength, adaptability, and aesthetic qualities. While glass ceramics are typically favored for anterior esthetic restorations, lithium disilicate's robust biomechanical properties also render it suitable for monolithic posterior inlays. This material enables more conservative restorations for posterior teeth, requiring a thickness of only 1.0 mm, a significant reduction from the 1.5 to 2.0 mm typically recommended for porcelain restorations. Furthermore, lithium disilicate overlays offer the advantage of modifying the tooth's occlusal surface, facilitating extensive rehabilitations within complex treatment plans. This capability is particularly beneficial for correcting occlusal relationships or increasing vertical dimension.
This study evaluated the survival rate of lithium disilicate overlays used to increase occlusal vertical dimension. We also assessed restoration thickness at various tooth sites, guided by current literature and our clinical experience with minimally invasive techniques.
This observational descriptive study assessed 43 monolithic lithium disilicate overlay restorations in 8 patients, specifically for increasing occlusal vertical dimension (OVD). These restorations were part of broader oral rehabilitation plans aimed at achieving comfortable occlusion and addressing temporomandibular joint and muscle issues stemming from dental wear or altered occlusal conditions. While various restorative techniques like crowns, bridges, and veneers were employed, this study focused exclusively on overlays. Patients included in the study had posterior overlays, restored altered occlusal conditions, worn teeth, received endodontic treatment, or had prior composite restorations. Conversely, individuals with poor oral hygiene, active periodontitis, probing depths exceeding 4 mm, or implant-supported restorations were excluded. The occlusal plane modifications were meticulously planned during the initial treatment-planning phase.
This study focused on lithium disilicate overlays, examining restoration thickness, the average amount of tooth structure removed during preparation, and the extent of occlusal increase. Data gathered were compared to existing literature. The observation period spanned from December 2014 to January 2018, with restorations followed for 19 to 45 months, averaging 32 months. Survival was defined as a restoration remaining in place, regardless of complications, throughout the study. A single trained examiner assessed the survival of each restoration.
Forty-three IPS e.max Press overlays were placed on 15 maxillary and 28 mandibular teeth, adhering to a specific cementation protocol.
Initially, alginate impressions were taken, leading to cast models and diagnostic wax-ups. These were used to plan the necessary occlusal rise for each tooth. Worn surfaces were waxed on mounted, articulated casts to pre-determine the occlusal increase.
Preparations for cavities adhered to dental anatomy, with margins kept supragingival to align with minimally invasive principles. This approach aimed to conserve tooth structure, minimize trauma, and enhance the long-term prognosis for the prepared teeth, as illustrated in Figure 1 and detailed in Table 1. The cited literature, including studies by Ma Li et al., Petra C. Guess, Fradeani et al., and Morakot Piemjai et al., supports the efficacy of minimal preparations. These studies collectively indicate that lithium disilicate restorations, even at thicknesses as low as 0.5 mm to 1 mm, demonstrate significant load-bearing capacity and fracture resistance, particularly when bonded to enamel. For instance, lithium disilicate bonded to enamel can achieve substantial load-bearing capacity, approaching 75% of that of zirconia with thicknesses between 0.7 and 1.4 mm. Furthermore, the reduction in restoration thickness did not compromise the fracture resistance of onlay restorations, and the preservation of dental tissue, coupled with bonding to enamel, ensures adequate restoration resistance even with minimal thicknesses. The findings highlight that enamel-supported restorations exhibit fracture loads comparable to standard dentin-supported restorations, and thinner preparations for porcelain laminates bonded to enamel can yield better fracture resistance than deeper dentin preparations.
Adhesive cementation of the overlays was performed with Variolink Esthetic. The internal surfaces of the restorations underwent etching with 9.6% hydrofluoric acid for 30 seconds, followed by rinsing and drying. A silane coupling agent was then applied to these surfaces for one minute and air-dried. Finally, a layer of Adhese Universal adhesive was applied without polymerization to prevent any increase in thickness at the tooth-restoration interface.
To ensure a clean operative field, a rubber dam was employed for isolation. The dental cavity was then prepared using 37% orthophosphoric acid for conditioning, with a 30-second application on enamel and 15 seconds on dentin, taking care to protect adjacent teeth. Following thorough rinsing and air-drying of the dental surfaces, an etch-and-rinse bonding agent was applied. This bonding agent was intentionally not polymerized to prevent the buildup of excessive thickness between the tooth and restoration. Adhesive cementation was then carried out using Variolink Esthetic, applied to the internal surface of the overlay. Excess cement was removed with a brush prior to light polymerization, which lasted one minute per surface. Margins were subsequently refined with a rubber pad, and occlusion was checked before final polishing. For statistical analysis, any complication encountered was treated as a distinct event, and cumulative survival was tracked using Kaplan-Meier analysis.
Restoration thickness was determined by combining the occlusal increase achieved and the dental tissue removed during preparation. Measurements were taken using a thickness gauge at the functional cusp, non-functional cusp, and central fossa. The mean and standard deviation were calculated for each of these specific points.
Molar overlay thicknesses at the mesial and distal portions of functional and non-functional cusps were measured and averaged. These thickness findings were then compared to existing literature. Additionally, overlays were categorized into subgroups: very thin, thin, medium, and thick, for further analysis.
To determine restoration thickness, overlay measurements were taken with a millimetric gauge, while dental tissue removal was quantified using a cutter of known size. The occlusal rise was calculated as the difference between these two measurements. Ratios of occlusal increase to tissue removed were then analyzed within the overlay thickness. A t-test was employed to compare the significance of these ratios.
Mean values were reported with standard deviation. P values of 0.05 or less indicated statistical significance.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 22.0.
This study involved 43 lithium disilicate overlays placed on 8 patients, aged 47-67 years, with an average follow-up of 32 months. The restorations were distributed across 18 molars (42%) and 25 premolars (58%), with 15 in the maxillary arch (35%) and 28 in the mandibular arch (65%).
One infiltration was observed, with no fractures of teeth or overlays. The success rate was 97.7% at the average follow-up period.
The lone infiltration required root canal therapy, margin reshaping, and a composite restoration.

Statistical analysis confirmed these values significantly exceeded the 0.7 mm reference threshold for all measured areas. This indicates that the restoration thicknesses at these landmarks were adequate, meeting established clinical requirements.

Restoration thicknesses varied across landmarks: 26% of fossa restorations were 0.5–0.9 mm, and 60% were 1–1.5 mm. Cusps showed higher thicknesses, with only 13% and 20% of non-functional and functional cusps, respectively, being less than 1.5 mm.
Restorations on functional and non-functional cusps were predominantly over 1.5 mm thick (87% and 80%). In contrast, the central fossa areas were thinner, with 86% measuring less than 1.5 mm.

Lithium disilicate was selected due to its hardness and resistance exceeding that of enamel, making it ideal for increasing vertical dimension. This material choice helps match wear rates to natural enamel, thereby mitigating gnathologic issues often associated with extensive occlusal restorations.

The technique facilitated minimally invasive preparations, maintaining appropriate thicknesses and preserving dental tissue, consistent with current recommendations.
Previous research has established that traditional monolithic ceramic systems typically require preparation depths of around 1.5 to 2 mm to withstand tensile stresses on internal cementation surfaces under occlusal forces. While manufacturers often suggest a minimum thickness of 1.5 mm for IPS e.max Press lithium disilicate ceramic, these recommendations are largely derived from laboratory tests and may not fully translate to clinical outcomes. More recent studies, however, have investigated the performance of thinner monolithic lithium disilicate restorations. These investigations have indicated that reducing the thickness of IPS e.max Press does not compromise its bending strength, supporting its use in preparations involving minimal dental tissue removal. Our findings align with this, as the minimum thicknesses we observed were below the 1.5 mm standard recommendation for ceramic restorations. Specifically, we noted minimal tissue removal of 1.1 mm on cusps and 0.7 mm in fossae, without any observed restoration fractures. Regarding overlay thicknesses, our analysis revealed that 26% of restorations at the fossa were between 0.5 and 0.9 mm, and 60% measured between 1 and 1.5 mm. In contrast, cusp areas generally exhibited greater thickness, with only 13% of non-functional cusps and 20% of functional cusps being less than 1.5 mm thick. The preservation of remaining tooth structure is paramount for long-term restoration survival, particularly in cases of weakened dentition due to decay or erosion. This approach leads to less trauma and a better prognosis. A significant factor influencing failure rates under load is the composition of the supporting tooth structure. Enamel, with its higher elastic modulus compared to dentin, plays a critical role. The adhesive cementation of disilicate restorations is crucial here, as lithium disilicate possesses an elastic modulus very similar to enamel. This similarity helps to distribute chewing loads more evenly, reducing stress on the ceramic and thus lowering the risk of fracture. Consequently, thin restorations bonded to enamel may exhibit comparable fracture resistance to thicker restorations bonded to dentin, and enamel-bonded restorations tend to experience fewer mechanical complications over time.
When preparing a tooth for restoration, the primary consideration is the amount of tooth structure that must be removed for the chosen material. Clinicians must also evaluate the remaining tooth condition, esthetics, function, tooth position, and the overall plan for occlusal rehabilitation.
Preparations in this study largely removed 1 mm or less of dental tissue, adhering to minimally invasive principles.
The thickness of the overlays in our study was influenced not only by the amount of dental tissue removed but also by the necessary occlusal increase. This increase was determined during treatment planning to correct occlusal relationships or restore a reduced occlusal vertical dimension (OVD) caused by dental wear. Further analysis revealed specific thickness ratios: on non-functional cusps, 48% of the thickness comprised tooth removal and 52% was for occlusal increase. For functional cusps and the central fossa, these percentages were 47% for tissue removal and 53% for occlusal increase, respectively. Consequently, the requirement for an occlusal increase facilitated more conservative preparations, preserving tooth structure and enabling enamel bonding.
Lithium disilicate IPS e.max Press proved reliable for monolithic restorations, showing a satisfying 32-month survival rate. Its biomechanical properties enabled work with minimal thicknesses of 0.7 mm without compromising strength.
Longer follow-up studies are needed to assess the long-term mechanical behavior and aesthetic changes of these conservative restorations.
