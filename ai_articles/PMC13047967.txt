This study investigated the potential link between heart failure (HF) and central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR), two conditions with intricate and possibly shared underlying mechanisms. The research utilized data from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), spanning 14 years, to determine if HF patients face an elevated risk of developing CSCR. Methodologically, the study employed Pearson chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests for variable analysis, while adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) were used to assess disease development risk factors. Kaplan–Meier analysis provided a comparison of the cumulative incidence of CSCR. The findings revealed a significantly higher incidence of CSCR in the HF patient group (study cohort) compared to the control group (without HF). Specifically, the study cohort, comprising 24,426 HF patients, showed an incidence rate of CSCR that was 4.572 times greater than the control cohort (also 24,426 patients without HF), with a p-value less than 0.001. The overall incidence of CSCR was 30.07 per 100,000 person-years in the HF group, versus 23.06 per 100,000 person-years in the control group. CSCR was also observed to be more prevalent in males. Subgroup analysis further demonstrated that HF patients, irrespective of gender or age, had an increased risk of CSCR diagnosis. For males, the aHR was 3.268, and for females, it was 7.701. In the 20-59 years age group, the aHR was 3.405, and for those aged 60 years or older, it was 5.501, all with p-values less than 0.001. In conclusion, the study established HF as a significant predictor for CSCR. It recommends that HF patients be vigilant for visual impairment, and suggests further prospective studies to delve deeper into the relationship between these two conditions.

Central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR) is a condition with a multifaceted origin. It involves fluid seeping from the choroid, past a compromised retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier, and into the subretinal space, leading to serous retinal detachment or RPE detachment. While its pathogenesis is complex, corticosteroid exposure (both internal and external) is a known risk factor. Annual incidence rates vary, with 0.0058% reported in Caucasians and 0.021% in the Taiwanese population.
Heart failure (HF) is a severe condition where the heart fails to adequately pump blood to the body. This insufficient blood flow, stemming from impaired ventricular function in HF patients, triggers the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. Aldosterone, a key mineralocorticoid, promotes fluid accumulation and tissue remodeling by activating mineralocorticoid receptors (MR), thereby worsening HF and causing organ congestion. Notably, MRs are also present in the neuroretina and choroid, where fluid accumulation linked to their activation plays a vital role in the development of Central Serous Chorioretinopathy (CSCR).
This study utilized Taiwan's National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) to explore the link between heart failure (HF) and central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR).
Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) program, established in 1995, provides coverage for nearly its entire population, encompassing around 23 million individuals. The National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) compiles all claims from this program, offering a wealth of information. This includes fundamental patient details like gender, age, diagnoses, insurance premiums, and geographical data such as season, location, urbanization level, and care provider. Clinicians can access this electronically stored data for statistical research, which is why this study utilized the NHIRD to investigate the link between heart failure and central serous chorioretinopathy.
This retrospective cohort study utilized the NHIRD to identify patients, employing International Classification of Disease, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes for diagnoses of heart failure (HF: 428) and central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR: 362.41). Comorbidities in CSCR patients were also identified and analyzed using these codes. For this study, the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) was modified to CCI_R, excluding diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal disease, pneumonia, liver disease, and coronary artery disease, as these conditions were independently assessed variables. From 2000 to 2013, 28,441 individuals initially met the inclusion criteria. After applying exclusion criteria, 4,015 patients were removed, resulting in a study cohort of 24,426 individuals. A control cohort of equal size (24,426 individuals) was created using one-fold propensity score matching based on gender, age, and index year. Over a 14-year follow-up period, 162 patients were diagnosed with CSCR, comprising 90 from the study cohort and 72 from the control cohort. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.
This study, approved by the Tri-Service General Hospital's Institutional Review Board (TSGHIRB No. 2-105-05-082), did not require patient consent. The NHIRD anonymizes personal data to ensure privacy.
To assess differences in categorical variables like gender, age group, and insurance premium, the Pearson chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were utilized, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The cumulative incidence of CSCR in both cohorts was estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis. Subsequently, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for various factors, to determine the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of HF's impact on CSCR development. All statistical computations were carried out using SPSS software (Version 22.0).
Demographic data for both the study and control cohorts are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the groups concerning mean age (59.66 ± 14.60 years for the study cohort vs. 59.47 ± 12.58 years for the control cohort, p = 0.123), gender, or age group distribution. However, disparities emerged in other characteristics. The study cohort exhibited a lower insurance premium (NT$) compared to the control cohort (p < 0.001). Across both cohorts, roughly half of the participants had insurance premiums under NT$18,000 (55.57%), followed by the NT$18,000-34,999 group (37.30%), and the over NT$35,000 group (7.14%). Furthermore, patients with heart failure (HF) in the study cohort displayed a higher prevalence of various comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus (DM), hyperlipidemia, hypertension (HTN), chronic kidney disease (CKD), peptic ulcers, psychiatric disease, allergic respiratory diseases, and coronary artery disease (CAD). The CCI_R value was also significantly higher in the study cohort (0.31 ± 0.89) compared to the control cohort (0.27 ± 0.95) (p < 0.001). Geographically, more individuals in the study cohort resided in northern and eastern Taiwan, in more urbanized areas, and received care at hospital centers and regional hospitals (p < 0.001).
Using the Kaplan-Meier method, the cumulative risk of CSCR development was assessed. The study cohort exhibited a significantly elevated rate of CSCR compared to the control cohort (log-rank test p < 0.001). This cumulative risk steadily rose each year, reaching 0.37% (90/24,426 individuals) in the study cohort and 0.29% (72/24,426 individuals) in the control cohort by the study's end. The disparity between the groups remained significant throughout the entire follow-up period (p < 0.001 annually).
Cox regression analysis, presented in Table 2, identified several factors influencing CSCR risk. After accounting for various variables, including heart failure (HF), gender, age groups (20-59 and ≥60 years), insurance premium, and pre-existing conditions like DM, hyperlipidemia, HTN, CKD, peptic ulcer, psychiatric diseases, allergic respiratory diseases, and CAD, only HF and male gender showed a significant association with increased CSCR diagnosis. Specifically, HF patients had a considerably higher risk (aHR = 4.572, 95% CI = 3.236-6.461, p < 0.001), as did males (aHR = 1.459, p = 0.02). Conversely, individuals with hypertension (HTN) exhibited a reduced risk of developing CSCR (aHR = 0.339, p = 0.04). Other comorbidities, age groups, and insurance premium did not demonstrate a significant association with CSCR diagnosis (all p > 0.05).
Subgroup analysis revealed a higher overall incidence of CSCR in heart failure (HF) patients, at 30.07 per 100,000 person-years, compared to 23.06 per 100,000 person-years in the control group. This increased risk for CSCR diagnosis in HF patients was consistent across both genders and age groups. Specifically, male HF patients had an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of 3.268, while female HF patients showed an aHR of 7.701 (p < 0.001). Similarly, HF patients aged 20-59 years had an aHR of 3.405, and those aged ≥60 years had an aHR of 5.051 (p < 0.001), indicating an independent association with CSCR development.
This population-based investigation, encompassing 24,426 patients with heart failure and an equal number in a control group, revealed a significantly elevated rate of CSCR development in the heart failure cohort. Our findings, supported by Kaplan–Meier analysis, demonstrated a consistently higher cumulative risk of CSCR in heart failure patients annually. This study is the first to explore the connection between these two conditions.
Our demographic analysis indicated a higher prevalence of CSCR in males, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.459 (p = 0.02), aligning with findings from prior research. For instance, Tittl et al. reported a male-to-female ratio of 2.7:1 in their CSCR cohort. Similarly, Tsai et al. noted a greater annual incidence in males and a peak incidence in middle-aged individuals. Kitzmann et al. also observed that men had over five times the incidence rate of women, with middle-aged patients experiencing higher rates of CSCR. However, our study's age group analysis showed a non-significant trend towards a lower risk of CSCR development in older patients (≥60 years) compared to those aged 20-59 years (aHR = 0.812, p = 0.402). This discrepancy from other studies may stem from our specific focus on CSCR risk in heart failure patients, potentially introducing selection bias.
Our current study observed a reduced risk of CSCR development among patients with hypertension (aHR = 0.339, p = 0.04). While some prior research has indicated hypertension as a potential risk factor for CSCR, these studies did not establish a causal link. Conversely, other investigations have presented conflicting findings. For instance, a 4-year retrospective population-based study by Kitzmann et al. concluded that hypertension was not a significant risk factor for CSCR. Similarly, Chen et al.'s population-based studies found no notable difference in the likelihood of developing hypertension between CSCR patients and control groups. Consistent with these latter findings, our study also did not identify hypertension as a risk factor for CSCR. The precise relationship between hypertension and CSCR remains unclear, necessitating further research into the underlying pathogenic mechanisms.
A subgroup analysis (Table 3) was performed, viewing HF as a potential indicator. This analysis revealed that HF patients consistently faced a heightened risk of developing CSCR compared to the control group, with statistical significance across all gender and age subgroups (p < 0.001). This elevated risk in the HF cohort is potentially attributable to maladaptive endocrine consequences. The unstable systemic conditions in these patients may promote aldosterone–MR activation, leading to multi-organ dysfunction, including cardiac vasculopathy and CSCR.
The aldosterone-mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) system plays a crucial role in various tissues, including the heart, blood vessels, and brain, and its dysregulation can lead to pathological conditions. Within the cardiovascular system, activation of the aldosterone-MR pathway promotes sodium retention, inflammation, fibrosis, remodeling, and endothelial dysfunction, all contributing to cardiovascular impairment. In heart failure (HF) patients, compromised ventricular function leads to persistent activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, resulting in excessive intravascular and extravascular fluid, blood stasis, organ congestion, and thrombus formation. Notably, MRs are also present in ocular structures like the neuroretina and choroid. In vivo studies, such as one by Zhao et al., demonstrated that intravitreal aldosterone injection in an animal model caused choroidal vessel dilation and leakage, leading to choroidal thickening and vasculopathy. This evidence suggests that aldosterone-MR activation-induced vasculopathy offers a plausible explanation for the observed link between HF and central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR) in this study.
Several studies have shown that mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) can effectively treat central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR). For instance, Zhao et al. observed that aldosterone increased the activity of the endothelial vasodilatory potassium channel (KCa2.3) in the choroid. This activation led to vasodilation, leakage of choroidal vessels, and thickening of the choroid. Importantly, this choroidal thickening could be suppressed by co-injecting MRA. In a clinical application, chronic CSCR patients (those with the condition for over four months) who received oral MRA (eplerenone) experienced reduced subretinal fluid, decreased choroidal thickness, and improved visual acuity. These findings, along with other reports indicating MRA benefits for various forms of CSCR (acute, chronic, recalcitrant, or steroid-induced), suggest that MR activation plays a role in CSCR pathogenesis and that MRAs offer a viable treatment option.
Our investigation explored the impact of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) treatment on CSCR risk within the heart failure (HF) cohort, given its established use in CSCR therapy. While HF patients receiving MRA treatment (specifically spironolactone and eplerenone) exhibited a numerically lower risk of CSCR compared to those not on MRA (aHR = 0.697, 95% CI = 0.134-2.805), this finding was not statistically significant (p = 0.482). Similarly, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed no significant difference in the cumulative risk of CSCR when stratified by MRA use (p = 0.694). This lack of statistical significance likely stems from the limited sample size and event numbers in our study. The late emergence of MRA discussions for CSCR treatment (2012–2013), towards the end of our study period (2000–2013), may also contribute to this outcome. Future research incorporating more recent data could yield more conclusive results.
Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. A primary concern is its retrospective design. Furthermore, the absence of imaging data, such as optical coherence tomography and fluorescein angiography, within the database meant that maculopathy diagnoses could not be definitively confirmed. The study's findings may also have limited generalizability, as the population primarily consisted of Han Chinese individuals in Taiwan. Additionally, the restricted enrollment of cohorts to only heart failure patients and a control group could introduce selection bias. Conversely, the study benefits from several notable advantages. The establishment of Taiwan's NHI system in 1995 enabled a longitudinal analysis over an extended period, allowing for a robust comparison of CSCR cumulative incidence between the study and control cohorts—a design more effective than cross-sectional approaches. Moreover, the NHI's near-universal coverage (approximately 99%) across Taiwan ensures that the data utilized originated from a comprehensive, nationwide, population-based database encompassing the medical information of nearly all insured citizens.
The NHIRD data statistically confirm heart failure (HF) as an independent predictor for central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR). Clinicians should consider CSCR in HF patients experiencing visual impairment, facilitating early ophthalmological referral. Future prospective studies incorporating clinical, serological, and angiographic data are needed to clarify the underlying link between HF and CSCR.
