The tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) is a recognized independent prognostic factor in various solid malignancies, including colorectal cancer. The advent of digital pathology presents an opportunity for automating TSR analysis. This study explored the feasibility of computer-aided quantification of intratumoral stroma within whole-slide images of rectal cancer. Our methodology involved two expert pathologists analyzing histological slides from 129 rectal adenocarcinoma patients. They identified specific "stroma hot-spots" and performed visual TSR assessments. Concurrently, a semi-automatic deep learning method was developed and trained to segment all relevant tissue types in rectal cancer histology. This automated system was then applied to the expert-identified hot-spots. Both the visual and automated TSR methods were used to categorize patients into 'stroma-high' or 'stroma-low' groups. This classification enabled a comparative analysis of the prognostic value of each method concerning disease-specific and disease-free survival. The results indicated that automated TSR, with stroma-low as the reference, proved to be a prognostic factor independent of age, gender, pT-stage, lymph node status, tumor grade, and adjuvant therapy. This held true for both disease-specific survival (hazard ratio = 2.48; 95% CI 1.29–4.78) and disease-free survival (hazard ratio = 2.05; 95% CI 1.11–3.78). In contrast, visually assessed TSR did not emerge as an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis. In conclusion, this research demonstrates that TSR is an independent prognostic indicator in rectal cancer when quantified automatically within user-selected stroma hot-spots. The deep learning technology presented here has the potential to be a valuable tool for pathologists in routine diagnostic practice.

Pathological staging of tumors is the primary driver of treatment decisions in most solid malignancies. Globally, the TNM staging system is the standard for assessing prognosis and guiding therapy. However, for some tumor types, TNM staging is insufficient for accurately predicting metastatic potential. For example, TNM stage II colorectal cancer (CRC) exhibits significant variability in patient outcomes [2]. This lack of precision in TNM staging can result in suboptimal therapy planning, leading to both undertreatment and overtreatment. Consequently, there is a critical need for novel biomarkers to facilitate personalized adjuvant treatment strategies for these patient groups.
The tumor-stroma ratio (TSR), which quantifies the relative proportions of tumor and intratumoral stroma, is a well-researched prognostic indicator. This measure is easily obtained by examining hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained tissue sections under a microscope. TSR has demonstrated prognostic value across various solid tumors, such as breast and lung cancers, typically indicating that a higher stroma content correlates with a poorer prognosis. While previous research on colorectal cancer (CRC) has suggested TSR's prognostic significance, it has not yet been integrated into routine pathology reports. This lack of adoption is likely due to inconsistent methodologies and the absence of standardized assessment procedures. Current approaches include visual estimation, systematic point counting, and analysis of digitized whole-slide images (WSI). Although some studies have reported good inter-observer agreement for visual TSR assessment, concerns about the reproducibility of such quantitative pathological feature evaluations persist.
To achieve objective and standardized TSR assessment, previous research utilized image analysis and machine learning algorithms on H&E-stained colorectal cancer (CRC) sections. These earlier methods, however, were limited to analyzing image regions extracted from whole-slide images (WSIs). Computer-aided quantification of tumor and stroma has been proposed, employing automated tissue segmentation on H&E-stained sections through a combination of handcrafted features and machine learning. Additionally, TSR has been calculated using automated point counting on H&E-stained images. Classical machine learning techniques have also been applied to tissue microarrays for tasks like epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) detection via immunohistochemistry. More recently, deep learning algorithms have emerged in computational pathology, demonstrating potential for automating histopathological tasks. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a type of deep learning, have been successfully used for detecting sentinel lymph node metastases and cancer in prostate biopsies. Our prior work demonstrated that a deep learning-based algorithm could differentiate between nine tissue types in CRC WSIs with 93.8% accuracy.
This research utilizes a previously developed convolutional neural network (CNN) to automate tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) assessment specifically within rectal adenocarcinomas, a subset of colorectal cancer (CRC). Prior studies on TSR in rectal cancer are scarce, with one sub-analysis by West et al. [12] failing to confirm its prognostic significance. While Scheer et al. [8] recently indicated TSR's potential for survival prediction in surgically treated rectal cancer patients, its independent prognostic value was limited to cases without lymph node metastasis. This study will evaluate the automated TSR system's performance against human expert assessment and analyze its prognostic impact on both disease-specific and disease-free survival.
A cohort of 154 patients diagnosed with rectal adenocarcinoma stages I-III was utilized for this study. These patients underwent curative surgery between 1996 and 2006 at the Medisch Spectrum Twente hospital in the Netherlands. Crucially, none of these individuals received neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy, nor did they die within 30 days of their surgery. Furthermore, at the time of their operation, patients had no known distant metastases, inflammatory bowel disease, hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), or other prior cancers. Histopathological data were sourced from the Laboratory for Pathology Eastern Netherlands (LabPON), while clinical data came from the Medisch Spectrum Twente hospital and the Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Organization (IKNL). The collected clinicopathological information encompassed tumor grade, invasion depth (pT), and lymph node involvement (pN), all categorized according to the UICC/AJCC TNM staging system. Information on adjuvant therapy and recurrence was also recorded.
For routine diagnostics, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were processed according to standard LabPON procedures, cut to 2 μm, and stained with H&E. For this study, a single slide per patient was chosen, specifically one containing the most invasive tumor portion used for pT-status assessment. These slides were then scanned at ×200 magnification using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer 2.0-HT scanner, resulting in a tissue-level pixel size of approximately 0.455 μm/pixel.
Two experienced observers, each with over a decade of TSR scoring experience, independently evaluated the slides. They employed a conventional light microscope and followed a previously established protocol for TSR assessment. The process began with a broad identification of the tissue area exhibiting the most intratumoral stroma, achieved at low magnification. Subsequently, a single field of view was chosen at 100x magnification for a visual estimation of the tumor-stroma ratio, termed TSR-visual, within a designated circular region. The ideal region for this assessment was characterized by a substantial amount of intratumoral stroma, typically found at the tumor's invasive margin. It also needed to show tumor cells along all its borders. Crucially, the selected area should be free from significant amounts of muscle, mucus, necrosis, or large blood vessels, and should not display tears or tissue retraction artifacts. The observers aimed to select the region with the highest stroma content, or "stroma hot-spot," that satisfied all these criteria. TSR-visual was estimated independently by each observer in 10% increments. Due to the specific microscope and lenses used, the circular region measured 1.8 mm in diameter at 100x magnification. It's important to note the variability in published TSR assessment procedures, including differences in the location and size of evaluated tissue areas and what was measured. For this study, the tumor-stroma ratio was defined as TSR = 100% × [intratumoral stroma area] / [tumor area + intratumoral stroma area]. During the visual estimation, lumen, tears, and other tissue types within the selected circular region were excluded. Finally, the observers convened to identify the most suitable tissue region for TSR assessment. During this consensus meeting, they established a binary TSR consensus score, categorizing cases as either 'stroma-low' or 'stroma-high,' and marked the center of the stroma hot-spot on the glass slide.
To evaluate the utility of a deep learning algorithm for automated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) assessment (TSR-auto), a convolutional neural network (CNN) was created, building upon prior work [20]. This CNN is designed to segment H&E-stained whole-slide images (WSI) of rectal cancer into nine distinct tissue categories: tumor, intratumoral stroma, necrosis, muscle, healthy epithelium, fatty tissue, lymphocytes, mucus, and erythrocytes. The training of the CNN involved manually annotated regions from 74 WSI drawn from the study's patient cohort. These annotations were strategically chosen to encompass a diverse range of tissue types across the WSI, rather than exhaustively annotating a limited number of slides. A pathology researcher and a medical student generated the annotations, with an experienced pathologist reviewing and correcting them as needed. Before processing, a digital staining normalization technique [21] was applied to all WSI to standardize tissue staining intensities, mitigating variations arising from slide preparation. Notably, this study utilized 256 × 256 pixel patches for classification, a method that experimentally enhanced performance and yielded smoother segmentation maps compared to previous approaches [20]. The system's effectiveness was gauged through a five-fold cross-validation process at the WSI level, assessing accuracy across all annotated regions.
For a direct comparison, the CNN-based automated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR-auto) was calculated within the identical circular regions previously identified by the observers for their visual assessment (TSR-visual). These regions, each 1.8 mm in diameter, were extracted from the whole-slide images (WSIs) as approximately 4000-pixel diameter circles. The CNN then processed this extracted image data. Similar to the manual assessment, the CNN's segmentation of the WSI into nine distinct tissue classes allowed for the inclusion and exclusion of specific tissue types. The calculation of TSR-auto followed the same definition as TSR-visual, representing the stroma area as a proportion of the combined tumor and stroma area. Figure 1 illustrates these stroma hot-spot circles, both as selected by observers for TSR-visual and as segmented by the CNN for TSR-auto.
The prognostic capabilities of visually assessed tumor-stroma ratio (TSR-visual) and automatically quantified TSR (TSR-auto) in rectal cancer were compared. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software v24.0. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was employed to assess the agreement between TSR measurements from two observers and the automated method. Relationships between clinicopathological variables and the numerical TSR values were examined using Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests for two- and multi-class variables, respectively. For subsequent analysis, both TSR-visual and TSR-auto were categorized into 'stroma-low' and 'stroma-high' groups. TSR-visual dichotomization followed a pre-established cut-off of >50% for stroma-high, derived from colon cancer studies. Since the optimal cut-off for TSR-auto was undetermined, two methods were used: a 50% cut-off, mirroring TSR-visual (TSR-auto(50%)), and a cut-off based on the median TSR-auto value (TSR-auto(median)), ensuring an even distribution between stroma-low and stroma-high groups.
Cohen's Kappa (κ) was employed to assess inter-observer agreement on dichotomized tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) values. Subsequently, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted, utilizing log-rank statistics to compare disease-specific survival (DSS) and disease-free survival (DFS) distributions. DSS was defined as the interval from surgery to death from rectal adenocarcinoma. For DFS, the primary endpoint was the first occurrence of loco-regional or distant cancer recurrence. If no recurrence was observed, the follow-up period until the last recorded date was used. Both univariate and multivariate analyses were performed for TSR-visual and TSR-auto, employing the Cox proportional hazards model. A two-sided probability value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
Twelve mucinous carcinoma cases were excluded due to differing TSR values. Additionally, twelve cases lacked available slides or data, and one case did not contain invasive carcinoma. This resulted in a final cohort of 129 patients for the study.
The study included 129 rectal cancer patients, with a median follow-up period of 5.6 years (ranging from 2.3 to 8.3 years). The median age of patients at the time of surgery was 67 years, with an interquartile range of 59 to 74 years. Detailed clinicopathological data for these patients are presented in Table 1. Importantly, no statistically significant correlation was found between the clinicopathological variables and the assessed values of either visually estimated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR-visual) or automatically quantified tumor-stroma ratio (TSR-auto). This finding was confirmed by Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests (p > 0.05). Table 1 provides a breakdown of patient demographics and clinical characteristics, categorized by gender, tumor stage (pT and pN status), overall stage (I-III), tumor grade, surgical procedure, and adjuvant treatment status, in relation to different TSR assessment groups.
The automated method's performance was evaluated by comparing its pixel-level predictions against manually annotated ground truth. This analysis yielded an overall accuracy of 94.6%, surpassing previous findings by Ciompi et al. [20]. Detailed per-class sensitivity and specificity values for each tissue type are presented in Table 2, demonstrating the CNN's robust performance across various classifications.
Figure 1 illustrates the CNN's tissue segmentation within four observer-selected circular regions. The segmentation of tumor, stroma, and other tissue types was generally accurate, consistent with the high classification performance. However, minor segmentation errors were noted upon closer examination. Specifically, a thin layer of stroma pixels at the stroma-tumor interface was frequently misclassified as tumor. Conversely, small clusters of tumor cells, such as tumor buds or delicate structures, were occasionally misidentified as stroma.
Inter-observer agreement for visual tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) assessment was moderate, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.736. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of TSR scores between the two observers. When comparing the automated TSR method (TSR-auto) to visual assessment (TSR-visual), the ICCs were 0.475 for observer 1 and 0.411 for observer 2. This indicates a weaker agreement between the automated system and individual visual assessments compared to the agreement between two human observers.
When the visual tumor-stroma ratio (TSR-visual) was dichotomized using a 50% cutoff, the agreement between the two observers was moderate (κ = 0.578). However, using the same 50% cutoff for the automated TSR (TSR-auto) resulted in only fair agreement with TSR-visual (κ = 0.239). This agreement significantly improved to κ = 0.521 when a median cutoff was applied to TSR-auto, defining stroma-low as TSR-auto ≤ 65.47% and stroma-high as TSR-auto > 65.47%. Detailed breakdowns of patient group assignments for both methods are presented in Tables 3, 4, and 5.
Regardless of whether TSR was assessed visually or automatically, survival analysis indicated a poorer prognosis for patients with high stroma content compared to those with low stroma content. For visually assessed TSR, 5-year survival rates were 71.0% for disease-specific survival (DSS) and 65.6% for disease-free survival (DFS) in stroma-low cases, versus 58.8% (DSS) and 49.1% (DFS) in stroma-high cases. When using automated TSR with a 50% cutoff, these rates were 86.6% (DSS) and 76.8% (DFS) for stroma-low patients, and 60.7% (DSS) and 54.9% (DFS) for stroma-high patients. With the median as the cutoff for automated TSR, the 5-year survival rates were 76.1% (DSS) and 70.0% (DFS) for stroma-low patients, and 58.4% (DSS) and 50.7% (DFS) for stroma-high patients. These findings are illustrated in Figure 3, which presents Kaplan-Meier curves for both DSS and DFS.
In terms of disease-specific survival (DSS), the stroma-high group showed significantly worse outcomes than the stroma-low group when assessed by TSR-visual (p = 0.042) and TSR-auto(50%) (p = 0.018). However, this difference was not significant for disease-free survival (DFS) with TSR-visual (p = 0.182) or TSR-auto(50%) (p = 0.066). The TSR-auto(median) method, however, demonstrated significant differences for both DSS (p = 0.007) and DFS (p = 0.021), indicating poorer survival in the stroma-high group. Furthermore, when analyzing stage II rectal cancer patients specifically (n = 45), the stroma-high classification was associated with worse survival, a finding that was statistically significant only for TSR-auto(median) for both DSS (p = 0.003) and DFS (p = 0.015).
Hazard ratios (HR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for both disease-specific survival (DSS) and disease-free survival (DFS), as detailed in Tables 6 and 7. In univariate analyses, all assessed Tumor-Stroma Ratio (TSR) methods demonstrated prognostic value for DSS. Specifically, visually assessed TSR (TSR-visual) yielded an HR of 1.83 (95% CI 1.01–3.30). The automated methods showed higher HRs: TSR-auto(50%) had an HR of 2.71 (95% CI 1.14–6.40), and TSR-auto(median) had an HR of 2.31 (95% CI 1.24–4.30). For DFS, only the automated TSR-auto(median) proved to be prognostic, with an HR of 1.96 (95% CI 1.10–3.51). Further analysis involved stratifying patients by TNM stage. For stage II rectal cancer patients, TSR-auto(median) was the only method that remained prognostic for both DSS (univariate HR = 4.13, 95% CI 1.53–11.16) and DFS (univariate HR = 3.05, 95% CI 1.19–7.81). This highlights the specific utility of the automated median-based TSR in this patient subgroup.
Automated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) proved to be a prognostic indicator, independent of patient age, gender, tumor stage, lymph node status, grade, and adjuvant therapy. This held true for both disease-specific survival (DSS) and disease-free survival (DFS), regardless of whether the 50% cutoff or the median cutoff was used for automated assessment. In contrast, visually assessed TSR did not emerge as an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis.
The prognostic value of tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) has been established across various cancer types. However, visual assessment of TSR necessitates specialized training and can be challenging, particularly for cases near the 50% threshold. This study highlights that for rectal adenocarcinoma, inter-observer agreement in visual TSR assessment is only moderate. Leveraging advancements in slide scanning and machine learning, this research demonstrates, for the first time, the reliable assessment of TSR using an automated deep learning algorithm. The automated system, employing a median cut-off, achieved an agreement with observer consensus (kappa = 0.521) comparable to inter-observer agreement (kappa = 0.578). This automated TSR proved to be a robust independent prognostic factor for both disease-specific survival (DSS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in rectal adenocarcinoma. While the prognostic value of automated TSR was similar to consensus visual assessment for DSS in univariate analysis, this did not hold true in multivariate analysis. Notably, for DFS, only the automatically assessed TSR demonstrated a significant association with outcomes in both univariate and multivariate analyses.
Automated TSR, particularly when using the median as a cut-off, demonstrated prognostic significance in TNM stage II patients. This finding is clinically relevant as treatment decisions for this subgroup remain debated, necessitating further research. TSR could offer valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies in this patient category.
A recent study by Scheer et al. [8] examined TSR using the same patient cohort as the current research. However, their approach differed significantly from the hot-spot method used here. Scheer et al. calculated an average TSR across the entire tumor area on a slide, defining it as carcinoma percentage (CP) and categorizing it into low, intermediate, and high groups. While CP showed prognostic value for both disease-specific survival (DSS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in univariate analysis, its independent prognostic significance was limited. Specifically, CP-intermediate was associated with worse DSS and DFS only in lymph node metastasis-negative cases (n=94) after adjusting for age, grading, pT-stage, and adjuvant treatment. In contrast, the present study found that TSR retained its prognostic value for the entire patient cohort, even after accounting for clinicopathological variables including lymph node status. This divergence in findings is likely attributable to the distinct TSR scoring methodologies. The current study's adoption of a more widely accepted scoring system, which focuses on a specific stroma hot-spot, appears to yield superior results compared to methods that average TSR across the entire tumor area.
Our observer study revealed that visually estimated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) showed prognostic value for disease-specific survival (DSS), though this did not remain statistically significant after accounting for other clinicopathological factors. However, it did not prove to be a prognostic factor for disease-free survival (DFS). Additionally, the agreement between observers was only moderate. This finding contrasts with prior research on colon cancer TSR assessment. A potential explanation for this difference in rectal cancer is the thicker rectal bowel wall, which can make it challenging to differentiate stromal tissue from smooth muscle cells, particularly on darker H&E-stained slides. Muscle tissue, which should be excluded, might be misclassified as stroma by different observers. Moreover, as illustrated in Figure 2, most disagreements occurred near the 50% threshold, highlighting the potential utility of computer-aided TSR assessment for these borderline cases.
This study explored two cut-off values for the automated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR-auto): the 50% threshold used for visual estimation and the median of all measured TSR-auto values. Both cut-offs yielded comparable results, though the 50% threshold showed a slightly higher hazard ratio with a broader confidence interval. Automated TSR assessments generally result in higher stroma percentages than visual estimations. Consequently, the 50% cut-off for TSR-auto showed less correspondence with visually assessed TSR compared to the median cut-off, as indicated by the kappa values. Further investigation and validation of the optimal TSR-auto cut-off in an independent cohort are necessary.
The study cohort excluded patients who received neoadjuvant treatment, a design choice stemming from research by Scheer et al. [8]. This exclusion was due to chemotherapy and radiotherapy altering tissue architecture, which could compromise TSR assessment and its prognostic significance. Consequently, the developed method is particularly useful for guiding treatment decisions in rectal cancer patients who have not undergone preoperative (chemo)radiotherapy. Moreover, the shared histological characteristics between the colon and rectum suggest that this deep learning algorithm could be effectively applied to colon cancer analysis as well.
The current deep learning method for TSR assessment requires manual identification of a stroma hot-spot. Once this input is provided, the system automatically analyzes the selected area within the whole-slide image, classifying it as semi-automatic. However, prior research by Ciompi et al. [20] demonstrated that a similar computer model effectively segmented various tissue types across entire rectal cancer whole-slide images, extending beyond limited regions. This suggests the potential for assessing TSR at both the whole-tumor and whole-slide image levels. Such an advancement would eliminate the need for user-defined hot-spots, enabling large-scale TSR investigation through fully automatic computation. Future research will focus on enhancing the automation of TSR assessment and validating these findings in a substantial independent cohort.
While neither visual nor automated tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) assessment is currently standard in routine pathology, its potential integration with the TNM staging system has been acknowledged by the TNM Evaluation Committee (UICC) and the College of American Pathologists (CAP). To explore this for colon cancers, we are investigating the reproducibility of visual TSR assessment across multiple European centers. Our findings suggest that automated TSR assessment could greatly assist pathologists in daily practice. However, further validation of this deep learning technology on larger, independent datasets is a crucial future research objective. The inherent objectivity and reproducibility of deep learning-based methods for TSR quantification hold promise for its eventual adoption in clinical practice.
