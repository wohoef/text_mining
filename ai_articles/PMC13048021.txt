This study investigated how varying propofol infusion rates impact cardiac output (CO) and the onset of rocuronium's effect. The background for this research stems from the understanding that propofol, particularly when given quickly, can reduce a patient's CO to different extents. This reduction in CO is hypothesized to influence how quickly an effective concentration of a drug builds up at the neuromuscular junction, thereby affecting the onset time of neuromuscular blockers. To explore this, 90 patients were randomly divided into three groups. Each group received propofol at a different infusion rate: Group A at 480 mg/min, Group B at 240 mg/min, and Group C at 120 mg/min, with a consistent dose of 2.5 mg/kg. Following propofol administration, rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) was given to facilitate tracheal intubation. Cardiovascular profiles during anesthesia induction were continuously monitored using a Finometer. Neuromuscular relaxation was assessed via acceleromyography, measuring depression of single twitches of the adductor pollicis muscle. The onset time was defined as the duration from rocuronium injection until 95% twitch depression. Statistical analysis, specifically ANOVA with a post-hoc Tukey test, was employed to compare rocuronium onset times across the groups, with p < 0.05 indicating significance. The results showed a notable decrease in CO in Group A (21.6% ± 4.6%) after induction, which was significantly greater than the decreases observed in Group B (11.6% ± 4.5%) and Group C (9.8% ± 4.6%). Correspondingly, Group A exhibited a significantly longer rocuronium onset time (177.7 ± 17.6 seconds) compared to Group B (121.3 ± 18.3 seconds) and Group C (118.3 ± 12.3 seconds). The study concluded that rapid propofol administration significantly prolongs rocuronium's onset by reducing CO, as detected by the Finometer.

Propofol, a common induction agent, can lead to a notable drop in blood pressure, primarily due to its potent vasodilator and negative inotropic properties. Research using echocardiography on low-risk patients suggests that faster propofol infusion rates intensify this mean blood pressure reduction. This occurs through decreased vascular tone (affecting both preload and afterload), suppressed myocardial contractility, and the prevention of compensatory tachycardia. Despite these findings, propofol's impact on cardiac output (CO) is still debated, with studies reporting outcomes from no effect to a substantial decrease. These inconsistencies likely stem from the varying infusion rates employed, as rapid induction or bolus doses result in significantly higher peak plasma propofol levels compared to slower injections.
To ensure timely airway management and muscle relaxation during surgery, neuromuscular blockade (NMB) is a crucial adjunct to general anesthesia. The speed at which NMB takes effect, known as its onset time, is critical for prompt tracheal intubation. Various elements can impact how quickly an effective drug concentration accumulates at the neuromuscular junction, thereby influencing NMB onset. These factors encompass the drug's potency, the dosage given, cardiac output (CO), and localized muscle blood flow.
Rocuronium bromide is a nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker used in both adult and pediatric patients for rapid sequence and routine tracheal intubation during general anesthesia. Its onset time is significantly influenced by cardiac output (CO). Studies indicate that agents reducing CO can prolong rocuronium's action, whereas those increasing CO may hasten it.
This study sought to identify the optimal propofol infusion rate during general anesthesia induction by examining how varying rates impact rocuronium's onset time. This builds upon prior research that explored the influence of cardiac output on rocuronium's onset, specifically in conjunction with ephedrine administration.
This prospective, randomized study enrolled 90 patients, aged 20-55 years, with ASA physical status I-II, undergoing elective general anesthesia. Institutional review board approval and informed consent were secured. Excluded were individuals with a BMI exceeding 30 kg/m2 or pre-existing neuromuscular, cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal conditions.
Before the procedure, no premedication was given to the patients. Upon arrival in the operating room, a 20-gauge intravenous cannula was placed above the wrist for the continuous infusion of lactated Ringer’s solution at 100 mL/h. Standard monitoring, including blood pressure, electrocardiogram, and pulse oximetry, was established. To assess cardiac output (CO) throughout the general anesthesia induction, the Finometer system (FMS, Finapres Measurement Systems, Arnhem, The Netherlands) was employed, providing continuous, noninvasive, beat-to-beat hemodynamic measurements via volume-clamp techniques. Neuromuscular function was tracked using an acceleromyography device (TOF-Watch-SX Acceleromyograph, Organon, Boxtel, The Netherlands) connected to the ulnar nerve, and the palm skin temperature of the monitored hand was also recorded. Patients were then randomly divided into three cohorts, receiving propofol at infusion rates of 480 mg/min (group A), 240 mg/min (group B), or 120 mg/min (group C).
Following a 3-minute period of preoxygenation with 100% oxygen, initial baseline measurements were taken. Anesthesia was then induced using propofol at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg. Upon the patient losing consciousness after propofol administration, rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) was given, and the evoked response of the adductor pollicis muscle to ulnar nerve stimulation was initiated. Neuromuscular relaxation was assessed by applying repeated single twitches (50 mA, 0.2 ms, 0.1 Hz). The onset time was determined as the duration from the start of rocuronium injection until a 95% reduction in twitch response. Endotracheal intubation proceeded once 95% twitch depression was achieved. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05. Data collection occurred at baseline, post-induction, and then every minute for five minutes after intubation. The rocuronium onset times across the three groups were analyzed using analysis of variance with a post-hoc Tukey test.
Ninety patients participated in the study. All three groups exhibited comparable demographic profiles, including body weight, age, sex, and ASA status, as detailed in Table 1. Palm skin temperature also showed no significant variation between groups, averaging 34.2°C ± 0.87°C. Following propofol administration and rocuronium onset, Group A experienced a notable reduction in cardiac output (CO) compared to baseline (p < 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Conversely, Groups B and C maintained stable CO levels throughout general anesthesia induction. The CO decrease in Group A (21.6% ± 4.6%) was significantly more pronounced than in Group B (11.6% ± 4.5%) and Group C (9.8% ± 4.6%). Furthermore, the onset time for rocuronium was considerably extended in Group A (177.7 ± 17.6 seconds) when compared to Group B (121.3 ± 18.3 seconds) and Group C (118.3 ± 12.3 seconds), as presented in Table 2.
Across all study groups, a notable reduction in mean arterial pressure (MAP) was observed following propofol administration and the subsequent onset of rocuronium, relative to baseline measurements (Fig. 2). Initially, propofol led to an average MAP drop of 20% from baseline. This decrease became more pronounced, reaching 26% of the baseline value, by the time rocuronium's onset was achieved. Interestingly, MAP returned to baseline levels after intubation. Regarding heart rate (HR), no significant changes were detected in any group after propofol administration or rocuronium onset compared to baseline. However, a significant elevation in HR was noted in groups A and B after tracheal intubation when contrasted with their baseline HR (Fig. 3).
A significant delay in rocuronium's onset time was observed with rapid propofol administration (480 mg/min), reaching 177.7 ± 17.6 seconds. This was linked to a substantial 21.6% ± 4.6% reduction in cardiac output (CO), as measured by the Finometer monitor. Conversely, slower infusion rates of 240 mg/min or 120 mg/min led to a more modest 10% to 12% decrease in CO, without extending the rocuronium onset time. These findings suggest a direct relationship between CO and the speed at which rocuronium takes effect.
Propofol, a frequently employed hypnotic for general anesthesia induction, typically involves a dosage of 2 to 2.5 mg/kg for adults under 55 years old and classified as ASA-PS I or II, according to FDA guidelines. Its administration should be titrated at around 40 mg every 10 seconds (240 mg/min) until anesthesia is clinically evident. Induction with propofol impacts hemodynamics by lowering arterial blood pressure, systemic vascular resistance, and cardiac output. This hypotensive effect intensifies with faster, more frequent, or larger bolus administrations. Notably, Group A in this study received propofol at 480 mg/min, which is twice the recommended infusion rate.
The impact of propofol on cardiac output (CO) is not definitively established, despite numerous investigations into its cardiovascular effects. Prior research has yielded inconsistent findings, from no change to a substantial reduction in CO. These discrepancies arise from diverse CO measurement techniques, propofol delivery methods, and experimental settings.
The Finometer monitor was employed in this study to evaluate cardiac output (CO). This device offers continuous, noninvasive, beat-to-beat hemodynamic measurements via volume-clamp techniques, which is preferable to invasive methods for monitoring CO changes during general anesthesia induction. Its reliability has been established in prior research.
The impact of various agents on rocuronium's onset time has been explored in prior research. For instance, ephedrine has been shown to reduce the onset time of rocuronium and enhance intubation conditions by elevating cardiac output (CO). Specifically, Han et al. found that a 70 µg/kg dose of ephedrine, given four minutes before rocuronium, produced the greatest increase in CO, thereby effectively accelerating rocuronium's onset. Conversely, esmolol and a small dose of phenylephrine, when administered after propofol, prolonged rocuronium's onset. This delay is attributed to esmolol's CO-reducing effect and phenylephrine's vasoconstrictive properties during propofol-induced anesthesia. Additionally, combining low-dose ketamine with propofol-rocuronium induction also led to improved intubation conditions and a quicker onset.
Our study observed a notable reduction in mean arterial pressure (MAP) following propofol administration. This aligns with research by Pensado et al., who linked propofol's hypotensive action, particularly from bolus injections, to a direct decrease in systemic vascular resistance. Conversely, propofol's impact on heart rate (HR) in our investigation was negligible, consistent with its lack of clinically significant influence on SA node activity or the intra-atrial conduction system.
Clinicians frequently administer propofol rapidly, despite recommendations for a slower rate of 40 mg every 10 seconds (240 mg/min). This practice is common even though propofol is the standard induction agent and rocuronium is the typical neuromuscular blocker for intubation in stable patients. A smooth induction aims for quick onset of action and optimal intubation conditions while minimizing abrupt hemodynamic shifts. Therefore, healthcare providers must be aware of the potential adverse effects associated with rapid propofol delivery.
Our findings demonstrate that rapid propofol administration during general anesthesia induction can significantly delay muscle relaxant onset by reducing cardiac output (CO). This delayed muscle relaxation, particularly of airway muscles crucial for airway management, poses a potential danger to patients. Conversely, maintaining an appropriate propofol infusion rate (below 480 mg/min) minimizes CO impact, thereby preventing delays in muscle relaxant action.
