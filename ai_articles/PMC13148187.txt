Peripheral nerves in gnathostome vertebrates, including fish, birds, and mammals, connect the nervous system, body, and environment. They achieve this by integrating efferent pathways for movement and organ function with afferent pathways for somatosensation. While evidence suggests instructive interactions between efferent and afferent axon types are crucial for peripheral nerve assembly, this idea faces conflicting findings. Our genetic modeling in zebrafish, chick, and mouse reveals a conserved, hierarchical process of axon type-dependent extension and selective fasciculation that governs peripheral nerve assembly, mirroring the evolutionary emergence of these axon types and circuits in vertebrates.

Vertebrate nervous system evolution often reuses existing axon pathways instead of creating new nerve tracts, integrating novel features into established circuits. The body's segmental organization limits peripheral axon growth. This forces newer primary somatosensory axons (SAs) to utilize the same peripheral nerve tracts as older motor efferent axons (MEs). This shared, confined space likely promotes interactions between axons from both newer and older neuron types, facilitating their integration into common functional assemblies. This mechanism allows for the efficient incorporation of new neural elements within the constraints of an evolving system.
The formation of peripheral nerves has historically been understood as a process where somatosensory axons (SAs) must associate with motor efferent axons (MEs) that extend earlier to reach their peripheral destinations. Evidence supporting this view includes observations from amphibian and avian embryos, where surgical or laser removal of ventral neural tube segments, which contain motor neurons, often led to limb muscles developing without nerve innervation. More recent research in mice has elucidated the mechanisms behind these interactions. It was found that SAs are guided to their targets in the dorsal trunk by molecular cues present on the MEs that extend before them. When the extension of MEs was completely prevented through genetic manipulation, SAs exhibited random growth patterns, extending along either dorsal or ventral pathways without clear direction. Furthermore, specific genetic removal of EphA3 and EphA4 receptor tyrosine kinases, which are restricted to MEs, caused SAs to be selectively misrouted from dorsal to ventral trajectories. These findings underscore the critical role of MEs in guiding SA development and establishing the correct wiring of peripheral nerves.
Recent research on mouse limb innervation suggests a limited cooperative role for motor efferent axons (MEs) and primary somatosensory axons (SAs) in shaping peripheral nerve pathways (Huettl et al., 2011). Specifically, partial genetic removal of MEs in mouse embryos resulted in only minor SA extension abnormalities. Conversely, eliminating dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons or their neuropilin receptor expression caused significant defasciculation of MEs, but not misdirection. However, studies involving surgical interventions in frog and chick embryos presented contrasting findings, indicating that SA and ME trajectories in limbs can develop independently (Wang and Scott, 1999; Wenner and Frank, 1995). Reconciling these divergent results is challenging due to variations in animal models, experimental methods, and the specific peripheral nerve segments examined, hindering direct comparison.
To understand the interactions between primary somatosensory afferent axons (SAs) and motor efferent axons (MEs), we conducted a systematic investigation across three vertebrate species: zebrafish, chick, and mouse. Our initial focus was on the molecularly distinct peripheral axon types in these organisms. In anamniotes, such as zebrafish, the earliest sensory-motor circuits are formed by Rohon Beard cells (RBs), a central nervous system (CNS) neuron population. These RBs facilitate simple larval escape reflexes by transmitting sensory information from the dermis to motor neurons, which then control trunk muscles. Over time, neural crest-derived dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons replace RBs, integrating into circuits that support a broader range of motor behaviors. In zebrafish, this circuit reorganization is evident in the timing of axon extension. Primary ME extension, visualized using a motor neuron-specific regulatory module of the NBT gene driving red fluorescent protein (dsRed), precedes the emergence of SAs from DRGs by approximately 20 hours. SAs were visualized using the neurogelin 1 promoter to drive green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression, which is restricted to DRG neurons. This observation consistently showed SAs extending along pre-established ME trajectories.
Researchers visualized somatosensory afferent axons (SAs) and motor efferent axons (MEs) in chick and mouse models to study peripheral nerve development. In chick, green and red fluorescent proteins were expressed in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and motor neurons, respectively, using enhancer modules from the *Isl1* and *Hb9* genes. Similarly, in mouse, DRG and motor neuron axons were marked with established transgenes, *Brn3atau:lacZ* and *Hb9::eGFP*. Unlike zebrafish, where Rohon Beard circuits form during larval stages, amniotes like chick and mouse directly assemble primary sensory-motor circuits from motor neurons and DRG neurons. Despite these developmental differences, the sequential pattern of peripheral axon extension observed in zebrafish was consistently found in both chick and mouse. Specifically, SAs consistently followed pathways already established by MEs, a finding consistent with prior research. This suggests a conserved chronological hierarchy in peripheral axon development across these vertebrate species.
To investigate if the consistent order of axon extension signifies a primary role for early-extending Motor Efferent axons (MEs) in peripheral nerve formation, we examined Somatosensory Afferent (SA) axon extension when ME extension was inhibited. In zebrafish, injecting morpholino oligonucleotides targeting the Isl1 gene, known to disrupt motor neurogenesis, prevented ME extension. This absence of MEs significantly slowed SA extension and often caused highly irregular SA growth patterns. Similarly, in chick embryos, preventing ME extension by introducing Hb9-driven diphtheria toxin into the neural tube before motor neurogenesis led to a random loss of dorsal or ventral trunk SA pathways within typical peripheral nerve routes. These findings align with prior research in mice, where diphtheria toxin-mediated ablation of motor neuron progenitors also resulted in similar SA pathway disruptions. These experiments collectively suggest that MEs are crucial for guiding the initial and patterned extension of SAs.
The role of motor efferent axons (MEs) in establishing somatosensory axon (SA) trajectories in the trunk is well-documented. We investigated if this conserved function extends to peripheral nerve pathways associated with tetrapod limb development. Our findings indicate that genetic removal of motor neurons or their progenitors before ME extension in chick and mouse embryos significantly reduced SA extension rates, mirroring observations in zebrafish. However, a notable difference emerged between trunk and limb innervation. While SA projections in the trunk, following ME removal, were randomized but largely confined to normal peripheral pathways, lumbar SAs in the limb exhibited highly aberrant projections. These limb SAs frequently deviated from typical peripheral axon trajectories, often failing or experiencing severe delays in extending beyond the limb plexus. This suggests a more critical dependence of limb SA guidance on MEs compared to trunk SAs. Interestingly, when ME extension was only partially or delayed, the impact on peripheral SA trajectory establishment was considerably less severe in both chick and mouse. This outcome aligns with previous research utilizing late-stage surgical interventions or incomplete genetic ablation of motor neurons. These results highlight the nuanced and context-dependent nature of ME-SA interactions in peripheral nerve development, with a particularly pronounced role for MEs in guiding SAs within the complex environment of the developing limb.
To investigate whether motor efferent axons (MEs) establish a template for peripheral nerve assembly by guiding later-extending axon types, we genetically manipulated ME trajectories and subsequently observed somatosensory afferent axons (SAs). In zebrafish, we introduced morpholino oligonucleotides targeting a splice variant of the muscle-specific kinase receptor (MUSK) gene. This method, previously shown to specifically alter ME extension patterns (Zhang et al., 2004), resulted in aberrant ME trajectories. Crucially, SAs in these larvae consistently mirrored the altered paths taken by MEs (Fig. 3A-I), and invariably avoided trajectories that MEs did not occupy (Fig. 3J,K). This suggests a strong dependence of SAs on ME-defined pathways. Parallel experiments in mice further supported this hypothesis. We utilized two established genetic disruptions that induce varying degrees of ME misrouting at a critical binary axon choice point at the base of the limb (supplementary material Fig. S4A-H) (Helmbacher et al., 2000; Kramer et al., 2006; Luria et al., 2008). In the first model, the expression of the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) EphA4, which is restricted to motor neurons, was selectively eliminated in Epha4fx/fx;Olig2Cre embryos via Cre/loxP recombination. The second model involved Ret−/− embryos, where the expression of the RTK Ret, largely motor neuron-restricted during relevant embryonic stages (E9.5-E12), was abolished. Both genetic modifications specifically impacted the dorsal trajectory choice of MEs originating from the lateral division of the lateral motor column by altering their responsiveness to mesenchymal guidance cues (Fig. 3M,O,Q,S,U; supplementary material Fig. S4I-Q) (Helmbacher et al., 2000; Kramer et al., 2006; Luria et al., 2008). Consistent with the zebrafish findings, aberrant ME projections in the mouse hindlimb were closely replicated by SA projections (Fig. 3L-V; supplementary material Fig. S4I-Q). A linear relationship was observed between the extent of ME projections and that of the subsequently extending SAs (Fig. 3W). In both mouse models, SAs consistently adhered tightly to MEs. This observation indicates that the function of EphA4 and Ret is restricted to ME-mesenchyme signaling, rather than directly influencing SA-ME interactions during limb innervation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that SAs consistently favor and closely follow trajectories established by earlier-extending MEs.
Our investigation explored the hierarchical relationship between axon types, specifically examining if primary somatosensory afferent axons (SAs) influence motor efferent axon (ME) extension. In mouse embryos, genetic ablation of dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, which give rise to SAs, resulted in ME defasciculation. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting SAs normally exert repulsive forces on MEs, preventing their aberrant intermingling. However, the absence of DRG sensory neurons also hinders proper DRG assembly, which provides a crucial environment for Schwann cell precursor proliferation. Therefore, the observed ME defasciculation might also, or alternatively, be attributed to a reduced number of these Schwann cell precursors. Crucially, despite the defasciculation, the absence of SAs did not impair the accuracy of ME trajectory or target selection. This observation supports the understanding that MEs typically extend prior to SAs. Consequently, SAs appear to rely on pre-existing ME pathways for establishing normal peripheral trajectories, rather than the reverse. While SAs play a role in maintaining ME fasciculation through repulsive interactions, they do not dictate ME pathfinding.
Sympathetic efferent axons (SEs) from sympathetic chain ganglia (SCGs) are the final axons to extend into the periphery in zebrafish, chick, and mouse, a pattern consistent with prior research (An et al., 2002; Coughlin et al., 1977; Yip, 1990). This late emergence reflects the phylogenetic development of neural crest-derived autonomic circuits (Butler and Hodos, 2005; Holland, 2009). SEs in the trunk follow three main pathways to reach effector organs (Elfvin, 1983). One is a minor medial-visceral route. Another is a longitudinal path along the sympathetic chain or arteries. The third, a lateral route, follows the initial trajectories of motor efferent axons (MEs) and primary somatosensory axons (SAs) (Fig. 5A,L,M,Q). This lateral route runs alongside, but does not overlap with, intersegmental blood vessels (Fig. 6A-C; supplementary material Fig. S6E-J) (Nakao and Ishizawa, 1994). In amniotes, these lateral SEs ultimately extend along existing cutaneous SAs, innervating dermal glands and smooth muscle to contribute to skin thermoregulation circuits (Figs 6D-J and 7C,D) (Elfvin, 1983; Nakao and Ishizawa, 1994).
To investigate the hierarchical dependence of sympathetic efferent axons (SEs) on pre-existing somatosensory afferent axons (SAs) and motor efferent axons (MEs) in peripheral nerve assembly, mouse models were employed. These models allowed for the independent assessment of each axon type's contribution. Specifically, Cre recombination-controlled diphtheria toxin A (DTA) expression lines (R26fxDTA or Isl2lxstopDTA) were crossbred with established mouse lines expressing Cre in motor neuron progenitors, dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, or sympathetic chain ganglia (SCG) neurons. Our findings revealed that aberrant cutaneous SA projection patterns, which occur in the absence of MEs, were precisely replicated by SEs. This included a frequent failure of both SEs and SAs to innervate dermal targets. This observation suggests that MEs are not directly required for the initial peripheral extension of SEs. Instead, MEs indirectly influence SE trajectories by shaping the SA projection patterns. Further experiments involved selectively removing SAs to observe the impact on SE projections. In the absence of SAs, SEs extended peripherally along motor projections. However, they consistently failed to enter cutaneous pathways, resulting in a complete lack of innervation in the trunk dermis. Notably, the development of intersegmental blood vessels remained normal even without MEs and SAs, indicating that the observed failures in cutaneous SE projections were not due to disrupted vascular patterning. Conversely, the selective genetic removal of SEs did not cause any discernible changes in the peripheral projections of MEs or SAs. This aligns with the understanding that SE extension commences after the majority of ME and SA trajectories are already established. Therefore, while some SEs utilize the developing vasculature to reach peripheral organs, trunk cutaneous SE projections are entirely dependent on their association with pre-formed SA trajectories, which are themselves initially guided by MEs.
Our findings reveal a conserved hierarchical process in vertebrate peripheral nerve development, mirroring the evolutionary timeline of axon types and circuits. Motor efferent axons (MEs) initiate this process by actively navigating to skeletal muscles, guided by mesenchymal cues. This establishes an initial network of peripheral pathways, serving as a foundational template for subsequent nerve assembly. Following this, primary somatosensory axons (SAs) utilize these pre-existing ME trajectories to reach their target organs. Finally, specific subsets of sympathetic efferent axons (SEs) then follow these established pathways, likely responding to cues present on the SAs. This ordered sequence of extension and fasciculation events dictates how peripheral nerves form.
The earliest common feature of peripheral nerves in living chordates is the motor efferent axons (MEs) that originate from cholinergic motor neurons and innervate locomotor muscles. These MEs predate both vertebrates and neural crest-derived circuits. Somatosensory afferent axons (SAs) and sympathetic efferent axons (SEs) evolved later, appearing in agnathostomes and gnathostomes, respectively. This sequential emergence allowed SAs and SEs to utilize pre-existing ME pathways to reach peripheral targets. The close proximity of ventral and dorsal peripheral nerve roots, a result of segmental remodeling in gnathostomes, likely reinforced this pattern, facilitating the incorporation of newer axon types into established neural circuits.
The temporal sequence of axon extension significantly impacts interactions between different axon types, potentially explaining the conserved hierarchy of peripheral axon types across species, from fish to mouse. For instance, in mice, the early extension of motor efferent axons (MEs) appears to facilitate the selective fasciculation of somatosensory axons (SAs) with these pioneering MEs. This occurs through the activation of ephrin A proteins on SA growth cones by EphA receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) present on MEs. This arrangement also prevents inappropriate repulsive signaling that would otherwise occur if EphAs on ME growth cones were activated by ephrin A partners on SAs, which could undermine the ME's role in establishing peripheral nerve pathways. Furthermore, the early development of ME and Rohon Beard (RB)-based escape reflex circuits, preceding more complex SA and sympathetic efferent (SE)-based somatosensory and autonomic circuits in anamniotes during larval stages, may have reinforced this hierarchical organization of axon type-dependent interactions.
Understanding how axon-axon interactions contribute to nervous system development requires improved methods for identifying and manipulating distinct axon types. This is crucial because determining axon type-dependent relationships in embryonic development is difficult. The signaling mechanisms governing these relationships may function separately from those influencing how axons respond to non-axonal guidance cues. Consequently, modifying axon-axon signaling typically alters axonal trajectories within pre-established pathways, which are defined by tissue tracts that either permit or restrict axon growth. Therefore, progress in this field hinges on expanding the tools available to unambiguously differentiate or manipulate the various axon types involved.
Our findings offer a framework for investigating the cellular and molecular mechanisms of axon-axon interactions in peripheral nerve assembly. This research could provide a general model for how both older and newer neuron types integrate into shared neural circuits during nervous system development, despite some speculative ontogenetic considerations.
In zebrafish, motor efferent axons (MEs) were visualized using the Tg(NBT:dsRed) transgenic line, which expresses red fluorescent protein (dsRed) under the control of motor neuron-specific regulatory elements from the *Xenopus laevis* neuronal beta-tubulin (NBT) gene (Blader et al., 2003). To label somatosensory axons (SAs), the Tg(-8.4neurog1:GFP) line was employed, utilizing a genomic fragment of the neurogenin 1 gene to drive green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression specifically in DRG neurons (Peri and Nusslein-Volhard, 2008). To investigate the role of MEs, their extension was either prevented or altered. Prevention of ME extension involved co-injecting two morpholino oligonucleotides (MOs), islet1E2-MO and islet1E3-MO, targeting the *Islet1* mRNA. These MOs are known to selectively disrupt motor neurogenesis (Hutchinson and Eisen, 2006). For altering ME extension patterns without complete prevention, an MO against splice variant 1 (SV1) of muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), unp-SV1-MO, was used, as it has been shown to modify ME navigation (Zhang et al., 2004). MO injections were performed at the one- to two-cell stage, with specific dosages: 3 ng each for islet1E2-MO and islet1E3-MO, and 8 ng for unp-SV1-MO. All morpholinos were sourced from Gene Tools.
Chick embryos were prepared by staging fertilized eggs according to Hamburger and Hamilton (1992). Neural tube plasmid injection and in ovo electroporation were performed at HH 12/13, following established protocols (Marquardt et al., 2005). To visualize motor efferent axons (MEs), the Hb9MN promoter, a 4.5 kb fragment of the Hb9 gene containing motor neuron-specific regulatory elements (Lee et al., 2004), was fused to mCherry, a red fluorescent protein with a membrane-tethering myristoylation signal. For co-labeling somatosensory afferent axons (SAs), the Isl1DRG module, a DRG-specific regulatory element from the Isl1 gene, was fused to a minimal TATA box and mGFP (m-green fluorescent protein) (Uemura et al., 2005). It is noted that Isl1DRG also labels some Schwann cell precursors (SCPs), likely from their DRG proliferation niche (e.g., Fig. 1N, asterisks). Therefore, transverse sections with fewer SCPs were chosen for imaging to ensure clear SA visualization. To prevent ME extension, motor neurons were ablated. This was achieved by co-injecting PGKneolox2DTA (Addgene: Plasmid 13449) with Hb9MN::Cre, which facilitated Cre-mediated activation of DTA expression specifically in motor neurons. Following electroporation, the eggs were incubated at 38°C until they reached the appropriate developmental stages.
Mouse studies adhered to the animal welfare regulations of the University Medical Center Göttingen and German law. To visualize motor efferent axons (MEs) and primary somatosensory axons (SAs) simultaneously, researchers crossed the established motor neuron-specific marker line Hb9MN::GFP with the DRG-neuron marker line Brn3atau:lacZ. To investigate the role of MEs, their extension was prevented by breeding the Cre-controlled diphtheria toxin (DTA) expression line Rosa26lxstopDTA with the motor neuron progenitor-specific Cre-driver line Olig2Cre, which selectively ablated motor neuron progenitors. Similarly, to prevent SA extension, the neural crest-restricted Cre-deleter line Wnt1Cre was crossed with the DTA expression line Isl2lxstopDTA. This strategy specifically ablated Isl2+ dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons originating from Wnt1+ neural crest cells, while preserving Isl2+ motor neurons. For experiments involving sympathetic efferent axons (SEs), and to avoid unintended ablation of Isl2+ sympathetic chain ganglion (SCG) neurons, the DRG neuron-restricted Cre line AdvillinCre was interbred with Isl2lxstopDTA. SE extension itself was prevented by crossing DBHCre with Rosa26lxstopDTA. To explore the effects of misrouted MEs in the hindlimb, two approaches were used. Homozygous Epha4flox mice were bred with Olig2Cre to specifically inactivate Epha4 in motor neuron progenitors. Alternatively, embryos homozygous for a Ret-null allele (Retko) were studied. All mouse lines were genotyped using previously described methods.

Motor trajectories were identified in Hb9::eGFP transgenic mouse embryos. DiI-labeled embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 37°C for diffusion, then vibratome-sectioned at 120 μm for analysis.
Embryos were dechorionated, anesthetized with 0.004% tricaine, and mounted in 0.8% low melting agarose on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes. Live imaging was then conducted at 28°C using an LSM5 Live confocal microscope.
To quantify axon interactions in zebrafish larvae, two segments were randomly chosen per larva. Within each segment, motor axon and somatosensory afferent (SA) axon branches were categorized: "yes" if they projected closely with their counterparts, and "no" if they extended independently. The proportion of "yes" and "no" instances was then calculated for both motor and SA axons. For mouse embryos, the diameters of all motor and SA axons within the peroneal nerves were individually measured at the midsection of each embryo.
