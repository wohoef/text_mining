A systematic review was conducted to synthesize findings on Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) construct invariance, noninvariance, and their sources. In November 2017, a comprehensive search across multiple databases (Pubmed, PSYCINFO, PILOTS Web of Science, CINAHL, Medline, Psychological and Behavioral Science Collection) identified 1,169 abstracts. Inclusion criteria for articles were: peer-reviewed, focused on DSM-IV or DSM-5 PTSD invariance as a primary aim, utilized multigroup confirmatory factor analyses, and employed an independent PTSD instrument or module. This process yielded 45 eligible articles. Following PRISMA guidelines, research assistants performed a secondary search and independently extracted data. The review revealed that the DSM-IV dysphoric arousal and DSM-5 hybrid model factors exhibited the highest stability. Conversely, instability primarily stemmed from specific intrusion items (distress to trauma cues), dysphoria/numbing items (traumatic amnesia, foreshortened future, emotional numbness, detachment), and arousal items (hypervigilance). While the PTSD Checklist and PTSD Reaction Index were frequently used in invariance studies, they only showed partial conceptual equivalence of PTSD across subgroups. In contrast, clinician-administered measures demonstrated greater conceptual equivalence. Factors such as age, gender, cultural/linguistic background, and sample diversity had minimal moderating effects on PTSD's symptom structure. This review underscores the necessity of consistently evaluating PTSD construct invariance using established guidelines in all empirical and clinical trials to facilitate valid multigroup comparisons.

When comparing Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) scores across different groups, researchers and clinicians typically employ various measurement tools. These comparisons rely on statistical tests that presuppose the conceptual equivalence of the PTSD construct. This means that individuals with identical underlying PTSD levels are expected to interpret and respond to PTSD-related questions similarly, and the questions themselves should consistently reflect the latent PTSD symptomatology across groups. However, this fundamental assumption of conceptual equivalence may not always hold true. Factors such as group-specific characteristics, developmental stages, or even the impact of interventions can lead to varied interpretations of PTSD items, even among individuals with the same latent PTSD score. In such cases, the observed PTSD scores might not accurately represent the true underlying symptomatology. Therefore, instead of assuming conceptual equivalence, it is crucial to empirically examine it. This systematic review aimed to investigate studies that have explored the conceptual equivalence of PTSD, identifying key patterns and suggesting future research directions.
Psychological research, including studies on Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), increasingly employs invariance testing. This method evaluates a construct's conceptual equivalence and stability, assesses an instrument's structural validity, and determines the clinical utility of research findings. Specifically, measurement invariance examines the relationships between observed variables, such as responses to PTSD items, and the underlying latent PTSD construct. This allows researchers to ascertain if the PTSD construct is comparable across different subgroups, if the measurement of PTSD is consistent, and if PTSD items are interpreted identically by various groups. Establishing measurement invariance is crucial for making valid comparisons of PTSD severity scores and for understanding how PTSD relates to other constructs across diverse populations. Beyond individual item responses, structural invariance investigates associations between latent variables, encompassing the invariance of factor variances, covariances, and means. The process of invariance testing involves a stepwise approach, progressively imposing more restrictive parameters to thoroughly assess the construct's stability across subgroups.
Configural invariance, also known as form invariance, establishes that the latent construct of PTSD is consistently defined across different subgroups. This means that the same number and type of PTSD factors are represented by identical sets of PTSD items, creating a similar factor structure. For example, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) posits that PTSD is conceptualized by four consistent symptom clusters across all subgroups, with the intrusion cluster, for instance, always comprising the same five intrusion items. If configural invariance is not met (i.e., configural noninvariance), the factor structure differs between subgroups. An example of this would be if a PTSD sleep item loads onto the intrusion factor in only one specific subgroup, but not others.
Metric invariance, also known as weak factorial invariance, mandates consistent factor loadings for PTSD items across different subgroups. This means that each PTSD item should be explained by the latent construct to a similar extent. Specifically, a one-unit change in the PTSD factor score must correspond to an equal unit change in the item score across all subgroups. When metric noninvariance occurs, it suggests that subgroups may interpret PTSD items differently, or that certain items are more relevant to one subgroup than another. For example, if the PTSD sleep symptom exhibits metric noninvariance, it could imply a stronger link to arousal in only one specific subgroup. This highlights potential discrepancies in how the construct is measured across populations.
Scalar invariance, also known as strong invariance, ensures that the response scale is used consistently across subgroups. This means that item intercepts, or the starting values of the PTSD scale, must be equivalent. When scalar invariance holds, individuals with the same PTSD factor score will endorse similar probabilities for PTSD-related items, free from differential influence of bias or error like social desirability. For example, if the PTSD hypervigilance item exhibits scalar noninvariance, the mean hypervigilance score in a subgroup does not accurately reflect the underlying PTSD severity.
Strict invariance, also known as error invariance, ensures that the measurement of PTSD items is equally precise and reliable across different subgroups. This means that the unique variance of each PTSD item is consistently explained by common factors across all subgroups. When strict invariance holds, researchers can confidently compare PTSD mean, variance, and covariance estimates between groups using structural invariance tests. Beyond strict invariance, further levels of invariance testing involve examining the equivalence of factor variances and covariances. Factor variances represent the range of scores on the latent PTSD factor, while covariances reflect the intercorrelations between these factor scores (also known as phi invariance). For meaningful comparisons, both the information captured by each factor and the associations between factors must be equal across subgroups. The final step in this hierarchical process is to assess the equality of factor means across subgroups. Achieving this level of invariance implies that the population means of the latent PTSD variable(s) are equivalent across the groups being compared. It is important to note that even if measurement invariance is established, subgroups may still exhibit different latent means and covariances. Measurement invariance primarily validates that the assessment instrument functions identically across subgroups, thereby ensuring the validity of structural invariance tests that compare factor-level statistics.
Understanding invariance testing and its results is crucial. The meaning and implications of subgroup differences vary depending on whether PTSD invariance is being examined or has already been established. Not all subgroups will exhibit invariance in the latent PTSD construct. For instance, different trauma types and patterns might relate to complex PTSD in a conceptually distinct manner from PTSD, or they might lead to different PTSD symptom profiles. When assessing invariance, if subgroup differences affect how PTSD items are endorsed, the PTSD instrument loses its consistency in measurement. This inconsistency introduces bias, making it unsuitable for comparing PTSD severity across subgroups. However, once (non)invariance is established, the reasons for subgroup differences in PTSD scores become clearer, along with their subsequent implications. If PTSD invariance holds, these differences can be attributed to genuine variations in the latent PTSD construct. Conversely, if noninvariance is present, subgroup differences in PTSD scores likely stem from factors other than actual differences in the latent PTSD construct. Consider an example where the endorsement frequency of detachment and sleep items indicates PTSD severity in women, but only the detachment item's frequency indicates severity in men. In this scenario, the sleep item has minimal association with the latent PTSD construct in men. This disparity leads to biased summed scores, rendering meaningful comparisons between men and women impossible. In essence, while subgroups may respond differently to PTSD items (i.e., have varying probabilities of affirmative answers), an invariant PTSD instrument dictates that subgroups with comparable PTSD levels cannot exhibit different probabilities of affirmative answers if their latent PTSD scores are similar. This is a fundamental psychometric principle for an invariant instrument.
Invariance testing, unlike traditional diagnostic methods, accounts for the heterogeneity of PTSD symptoms. While diagnosticians typically sum PTSD item scores, treating each item with equal importance, invariance testing employs a factor-analytic framework. Within this framework, PTSD items are assigned different weights through factor pattern coefficients, reflecting the influence of the latent PTSD construct on individual item responses. This distinction highlights a fundamental difference between how PTSD diagnoses are established and how psychometric properties are evaluated through invariance testing. Consider a scenario where an individual consistently meets the diagnostic criteria for PTSD over time by exceeding an observed cutoff score on a PTSD instrument. Despite this consistent diagnosis, their specific symptom endorsements might vary, presenting different symptom profiles. Such variations can arise from factors like differing developmental maturity, individual interpretations of items, or the complex nature of the latent PTSD construct itself. In these instances, partial invariance analysis can pinpoint specific PTSD items whose responses are influenced by elements beyond a person's core PTSD experience. Therefore, while invariance testing acknowledges and accommodates differences in observed PTSD item scores across various subgroups, its core objective for meaningful comparisons is to ensure that the expected probability of endorsing a PTSD item remains consistent across subgroups for a given level of the latent PTSD factor. This approach ensures that comparisons of PTSD severity or prevalence across groups are based on a conceptually equivalent understanding of the disorder.
The necessity and extent of invariance testing are dictated by research objectives, as not all levels of invariance are universally relevant or expected. For example, comparing group means or latent mean differences does not necessitate strict factorial invariance; metric invariance alone suffices for comparing regression slopes. Given PTSD's multifaceted nature, investigating factorial covariance might be more pertinent than factorial variance or factor mean invariance when the goal is to assess the stability of its dimensional structure. It is important to note that invariance testing is rooted in a latent variable model, where an unobservable PTSD variable is posited to cause observed symptom associations. Consequently, this testing methodology may not be universally applicable across all conceptual and statistical frameworks. For instance, network analysis, which views PTSD as a system of interconnected and causally related symptoms, represents a different conceptualization where invariance testing might apply differently or not at all.
Invariance testing provides valuable insights into the conceptual equivalence and measurement properties of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) across different groups. Understanding whether a PTSD construct is invariant or noninvariant is crucial for both research and clinical applications. When invariance is established, it confirms that the underlying structure of PTSD is consistent across subgroups. This allows researchers to confidently compare PTSD severity and its relationships with other variables, as any observed differences can be attributed to genuine variations in the latent PTSD construct. For instance, if a PTSD instrument is invariant across age groups, then differences in average PTSD scores between younger and older individuals can be interpreted as true age-related differences in PTSD experience, rather than artifacts of how the instrument functions for each group. Furthermore, invariance results contribute to the psychometric evaluation of PTSD instruments. By identifying which items function similarly across subgroups, researchers can validate the instrument's construct validity and ensure it accurately reflects the intended psychological construct. This process can also highlight areas where an instrument might need refinement to reduce bias and enhance accuracy. Conversely, noninvariance signals that an instrument measures PTSD differently across subgroups. This has significant implications, as using a noninvariant instrument without addressing the sources of noninvariance can introduce substantial bias. Such bias can distort diagnostic screening, lead to inaccurate prevalence rates, and compromise the validity of PTSD diagnoses. For example, if a particular PTSD symptom item is interpreted differently by men and women, comparing their total PTSD scores would be misleading. The observed differences might stem from how the item is understood or endorsed, rather than actual differences in PTSD severity. Therefore, identifying noninvariant items is essential for modifying instruments to improve their cross-group applicability and ensuring that comparisons of PTSD scores and their associations with other variables are meaningful. Beyond research, invariance findings have practical implications for policy and resource allocation. Decisions regarding the implementation and funding of PTSD interventions often rely on comparative data, such as the effectiveness of a treatment in different populations. If the measures used to assess PTSD severity in these comparisons are noninvariant, the conclusions drawn about intervention efficacy could be flawed, potentially leading to misdirected resources. Therefore, systematic reviews that investigate the conceptual equivalence of PTSD, like the one conducted, are vital for summarizing findings on invariance and noninvariance, and for pinpointing the specific sources of any identified noninvariance. This knowledge is fundamental for advancing both the scientific understanding and clinical management of PTSD.
Our systematic review, guided by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, aimed to identify studies evaluating the invariance of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) constructs. On November 11, 2017, we conducted comprehensive searches across multiple databases, including Pubmed, PSYCINFO, Published International Literature on Post Traumatic Stress, Web of Science, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature, Medline, and Psychological and Behavioral Science Collection. Our search strategy employed a broad range of keywords to capture relevant literature. These terms encompassed "PTSD," "posttraumatic stress disorder," and various aspects of invariance testing, such as "measurement invariance," "factorial invariance," "structural invariance," "factorial stability," "factor structure," "latent structure," "symptom structure," "crossvalidation," "structure model," "comparison," "confirmatory factor analysis," "multiple-group," "multi-sample," "invariance," and "stability." For inclusion, articles had to meet specific criteria: they needed to be peer-reviewed, published in English, and have the examination of PTSD invariance (based on DSM-IV or DSM-5) as a primary study objective. Furthermore, the studies had to measure PTSD using either a standalone instrument or a diagnostic interview module and evaluate invariance through a multigroup confirmatory factor analytical (MGCFA) approach. We specifically chose MGCFA over a multiple-indicators multiple-causes (MIMIC) model because MGCFA concurrently assesses the equivalence of all facets of the PTSD symptom structure, including factor variances and covariances. In contrast, the MIMIC model presupposes configural invariance and assumes, rather than empirically tests, equal factor loadings and consistent residual variances across subgroups. The initial abstract screening was performed by two independent teams, each comprising two trained research assistants. Subsequently, two coauthors reviewed the remaining articles to confirm their adherence to the inclusion criteria. A secondary reference search was also conducted on the final selection of articles. Finally, two research assistants independently extracted data using standardized coding forms.
Our initial search yielded 1,168 unique articles, supplemented by one from secondary screening, totaling 1,169. Research assistants then screened abstracts, excluding 1,081. Subsequently, 88 full-text articles underwent review by two coauthors, with 45 meeting the inclusion criteria. This systematic review ultimately incorporated 96 multigroup (MG) analyses, each representing a comparative invariance analysis of the PTSD construct across different subgroups (e.g., male versus female participants).
The characteristics of the samples analyzed in the included studies are detailed in Supplemental Table S1. The majority of the 96 multigroup (MG) analyses, specifically 72 (75.0%), focused on adult populations. A substantial portion, 61 (63.5%), used samples from the United States, and 35 (36.5%) primarily involved White participants (over 60%). Gender-specific samples were less common, with 6 (6.7%) MG analyses examining all-male groups and 5 (5.6%) examining all-female groups. Regarding the methodology, most MG analyses, 83 (86.5%), applied DSM-IV criteria, and 86 (89.6%) relied on self-report measures. The PTSD Checklist (PCL) was the most frequently used instrument, appearing in 51 (53.1%) of the analyses. The studies predominantly (81 MG analyses, 84.4%) compared two subgroups. The most common bases for subgroup differentiation were time points following traumatic events (17 MG analyses, 17.7%), gender (16 MG analyses, 16.7%), and the type or level of traumatic event exposure (14 MG analyses, 14.6%). Examples of trauma-related subgroupings included different trauma types (Hetzel-Riggin, 2009), Criterion A versus non-Criterion A traumatic events (Zelazny & Simms, 2015), direct versus indirect trauma exposure (Frankfurt et al., 2016), violent versus nonviolent stressors (Saul et al., 2008), and references to a worst traumatic event versus overall trauma history (Elhai et al., 2009). Less frequently examined subgroup distinctions included cultural/linguistic status (13 MG analyses, 13.5%), deployment-related time points (8 MG analyses, 8.3%), sample diversity (8 MG analyses, 8.3%), deployment status (6 MG analyses, 6.3%), psychopathology and diagnosis-related variables (5 MG analyses, 5.2%), administration mode and measure types (4 MG analyses, 4.2%), age (3 MG analyses, 3.1%), educational attainment (1 MG analysis, 1.0%), and intervention time points (1 MG analysis, 1.0%).
This study examined the invariance of various Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) factor-analytical models, as detailed in Supplemental Tables S2 and S3, with statistical aspects outlined in Supplemental Table S4. The analysis primarily focused on models derived from both DSM-IV and DSM-5 criteria. For DSM-IV, several models were investigated. The three-factor model, however, received limited empirical support (Elhai & Palmieri, 2011). More commonly studied were the four-factor emotional numbing (EN) model, which separated avoidance and numbing into distinct factors (King et al., 1998; Sack et al., 1997), and the four-factor dysphoria model, which introduced a nonspecific dysphoria factor (Simms et al., 2002). The five-factor dysphoric arousal (DA) model further distinguished dysphoric and anxious arousal symptoms within the arousal cluster (Elhai, Biehn, Armour et al., 2011). Regarding DSM-5 criteria, the standard four-factor DSM-5 model, encompassing intrusions, avoidance, negative alterations in cognitions and mood (NACM), and alterations in arousal and reactivity (AAR), showed a close resemblance to the DSM-IV EN model (Friedman et al., 2011). Similar to their DSM-IV counterparts, DSM-5 dysphoria (Miller et al., 2013) and DA (Elhai, Biehn, Armour et al., 2011) models were also explored. More complex DSM-5 models included a six-factor anhedonia (AN) model, which differentiated negative from positive affect within NACM symptoms (Liu et al., 2014), and a six-factor externalizing behavior (EB) model, proposing an EB factor for emotional dysregulation (Tsai et al., 2015). A seven-factor hybrid model integrated various DSM-5 components (Armour et al., 2015). While the invariance literature includes numerous modifications to these PTSD models, this review focused on the most extensively researched factor categorizations due to feasibility. The majority of multigroup (MG) analyses (93.8%) investigated the invariance of PTSD clusters, with only a small proportion (6.3%) examining the one-factor PTSD model. For DSM-IV models, the EN model was most frequently analyzed (34.4% of MG analyses), followed by the dysphoria model (26.0%), the DA model (11.5%), and the three-factor model (6.3%). In contrast, DSM-5 models were less frequently studied, with the DSM-5 model examined in 5.2% of MG analyses, the EB and AN models in 2.1% each, and the hybrid model in 5.2%. This disparity (86.5% for DSM-IV vs. 13.5% for DSM-5) is attributable to the more recent introduction of DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. A significant portion of MG analyses (78.1%) initially compared the fit of different PTSD models before proceeding with invariance testing on the optimal model.
Invariance testing proceeds through a series of increasingly restrictive models. Model A, configural invariance, allows all parameter estimates to vary between subgroups. Subsequent models impose additional constraints: Model B (metric invariance) constrains factor loadings, Model C (scalar invariance) constrains item intercepts, and Model D (strict invariance) constrains residual error variances. Model D's constraints are not carried forward. Model E, which constrains factor variances and covariances, is compared to Model C. Finally, Model F, which constrains factor means, is compared to Model E. If noninvariance is detected, partial invariance is established by releasing problematic constraints before further testing.
Among the studies reviewed, 24% of multigroup (MG) analyses investigated all levels of invariance, while 34.4% examined three levels. Only 17.7% of MG analyses explored partial invariance when noninvariance was detected. For the one-factor PTSD model, scalar noninvariance was linked to specific items: one intrusion item (re-experiencing traumatic events) and two arousal items (sleep and concentration difficulties) in two MG analyses. Additionally, two other MG analyses identified avoidance and numbing parcel factor loadings and intercept values as sources of scalar noninvariance. Within DSM-IV models, intrusion items were identified as sources of metric noninvariance. The covariance between intrusion and avoidance clusters also contributed to factor covariance noninvariance in one MG analysis. In DSM-IV dysphoria models, metric noninvariance stemmed from intrusion items (intrusive memories, distress to trauma cues), dysphoria/numbing items (detachment, diminished interest, traumatic amnesia, restricted affect, foreshortened future, anger), and the arousal item (hypervigilance). Detachment, diminished interest, and traumatic amnesia items also showed scalar noninvariance. For DSM-IV emotional numbing (EN) models, metric noninvariance was associated with intrusion items (physiological distress to trauma cues), numbing items (traumatic amnesia, foreshortened future, emotional numbness, reduced positive affect), avoidance items, and arousal items (startle, hypervigilance). Scalar noninvariance was observed for items related to foreshortened future, startle, and psychological distress to trauma cues. Furthermore, arousal-avoidance factor covariance was a source of factor covariance noninvariance.
Model fit in invariance testing is typically assessed using individual fit indices. While a significant chi-square value indicates good model fit, other metrics like the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) are often employed to mitigate the chi-square's sensitivity to large sample sizes. For comparing different invariance models, researchers utilize changes in these values, specifically chi-square change (Δχ2), ΔCFI, ΔRMSEA, and/or ΔSRMR. In our review, a substantial majority of multigroup (MG) analyses (86.5%, n=83) reported the Δχ2 criterion, with a smaller proportion (31.3%, n=30) also including the ΔCFI criterion. Notably, 12.5% (n=12) of MG analyses did not report any change values. Only a small fraction (5.2%, n=5) treated PTSD symptoms as continuous indicators, and all of these employed recommended maximum likelihood (ML)-based estimators. Overall, ML-based estimators were favored by most MG analyses (74.0%, n=71) over mean- and variance–adjusted weighted least squares (WLSMV) estimators (16.6%, n=15). WLSMV-based MG analyses demonstrated a higher degree of statistical rigor across several aspects compared to ML-based analyses. They more frequently compared baseline models to identify optimal subgroup models (93.3% vs. 73.2%), conducted missing data analyses (73.3% vs. 43.7%), and investigated partial invariance (26.7% vs. 15.5%). Furthermore, WLSMV analyses more often identified scalar (85.7% vs. 67.6%), strict (75% vs. 39.4%), and factor variance/covariance invariance (66.7% vs. 47.4%). Among the five MG analyses that utilized binary variables, three (5.2%) conducted separate metric invariance tests.

Among DSM-IV models, the dysphoric arousal (DA) model showed the highest degree of invariance across most levels (Models A–F), with 11 (100%), 10 (90.9%), 7 (70%), 8 (80%), 8 (100%), and 4 (57.1%) MG analyses demonstrating invariance, respectively. The three-factor, emotional numbing (EN), and dysphoria models were also extensively tested for invariance. For DSM-5 models, the hybrid model exhibited the most invariance, though not for Model F. The anhedonia (AN) model factors showed invariance for Models A–C, while the externalizing behavior (EB) model factors failed to achieve even minimal invariance (Model A) in two (100%) MG analyses.
The PCL and PTSD-RI, both self-report instruments, were the most frequently evaluated for invariance levels. The PCL showed invariance in 47 (92.2%) of analyses for Model A, 36 (76.6%) for Model B, 12 (34.3%) for Model C, 10 (41.7%) for Model D, 12 (42.9%) for Model E, and 2 (14.3%) for Model F. Partial invariance was also observed for Models B, C, and E with the PCL. For the PTSD-RI, invariance was found in 8 (100.0%) analyses for Model A, 4 (50.0%) for Model B, 3 (50.0%) for Model C, 3 (50.0%) for Model D, 4 (57.1%) for Model E, and 4 (66.7%) for Model F. In contrast, the PTSD-Q and PRIM, though less frequently studied (one analysis each), exhibited invariance across all tested levels. Specifically, the PTSD-Q demonstrated invariance for Models A, B, D, and E, while the PRIM showed invariance for Models A, B, and C. This highlights a varied performance in invariance across different self-report PTSD measures.
Clinician-administered measures of PTSD showed varying degrees of invariance across different models. The Clinician Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS) exhibited invariance for Models A, B, and partial C, with two out of three analyses supporting Model A and all three supporting Model B. Two analyses also showed partial C invariance. In contrast, the Diagnostic Interview Schedule–PTSD module only demonstrated Model A invariance in all three analyses where Models A and B were assessed. The National Survey of Adolescents–Replication PTSD module displayed broader invariance, with two analyses supporting Models A through E, though Model F showed noninvariance. Similarly, the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview 6 (M.I.N.I.–6) PTSD module achieved invariance for Models A through C in both analyses conducted.
Our analysis of invariance levels across various subgroups revealed consistent patterns, with most subgroups demonstrating invariance across nearly all assessed levels. Notably, age, gender, cultural/linguistic status, and sample diversity generally exhibited strong invariance. For instance, all three age-related multigroup (MG) analyses showed invariance for Models A, E, and F, with two of three (66.7%) for Model B, and one of two (50%) for Models C and D. Similarly, gender comparisons indicated high rates of invariance across Models A-E (ranging from 78.6% to 88.9% of MG analyses), though only one of nine (11.1%) demonstrated invariance for Model F. Cultural/linguistic comparisons also largely supported invariance, with all MG analyses showing invariance for Partial C, Partial E, and F, and high rates for Models A and B (92.3% and 77.8% respectively). Diverse samples consistently showed invariance for Models A, Partial B, C, and Partial E. In contrast, variables such as deployment time point, education, and intervention time point were less frequently examined for all invariance levels.
Our review reveals a disparity between the scientific and clinical utility of invariance research. While many empirical and clinical studies compare total PTSD scores, few investigations have specifically examined the invariance of the overall, single-factor PTSD construct. This limited research suggests a need for caution when comparing the general PTSD construct across different subgroups. Although all analyses consistently demonstrated configural invariance, meaning the overarching PTSD factor maintained a consistent pattern and strength of factor loadings across subgroups, some analyses indicated metric noninvariance. This implies that even with identical underlying PTSD severity, observed scores could differ between subgroups. Therefore, to establish definitive conclusions regarding its clinical utility, more research is essential to thoroughly investigate the invariance of the widely adopted one-factor PTSD model. This will help bridge the gap between theoretical understanding and practical application in PTSD assessment.
Our analysis revealed that the DSM-IV Dysphoric Arousal (DA) and DSM-5 hybrid models exhibited greater invariance across most levels when compared to their established counterparts, such as the DSM-IV three-factor and DSM-5 four-factor models. This stability suggests a consistent and comparable representation of the PTSD symptom structure, aligning with existing empirical evidence. The findings also underscore the potential clinical utility of the recently introduced and debated hybrid model, warranting further investigation. However, the scope of our conclusions is constrained by the limited number of multigroup (MG) analyses available for certain PTSD models, especially those based on DSM-5 criteria, and the varying levels of invariance tested. For example, while the Anhedonia (AN) model factors showed promising conceptual equivalence by demonstrating invariance for Models A–C, more comprehensive testing across additional invariance levels was not conducted. A critical consideration for invariance testing is the number of indicators defining a factor, as this could influence the results. Both the DSM-IV DA model (specifically avoidance and anxious arousal factors) and the DSM-5 hybrid model (including avoidance, externalizing behaviors, anxious arousal, and dysphoric arousal factors) contain factors defined by only two items. While this might enhance domain homogeneity, it can paradoxically reduce factor stability. The ease of identifying statistically significant correlations between two variables, even if they lack substantive connection, means that two items might appear correlated without genuinely reflecting a shared underlying construct. This scenario could negatively impact the assessment of invariance. Furthermore, researchers conducting invariance testing must ensure that the chosen referent indicator for a latent factor does not introduce differential item functioning. This concern is particularly pronounced when a latent construct is defined by only two items. Therefore, the relationship between the number of indicators used to define a factor and the resulting invariance findings requires further empirical scrutiny.
Studies investigating the invariance of DSM-IV models generally exhibited greater statistical rigor than those focusing on DSM-5 models. A larger proportion of DSM-IV multigroup (MG) analyses (59.0% vs. 30.8% for DSM-5) compared baseline models to identify the most suitable and parsimonious model within each subgroup. Furthermore, a higher percentage of DSM-IV MG analyses (31.3% vs. 23.1% for DSM-5) explicitly detailed methods for handling missing data, often employing maximum likelihood (ML)-based estimators. Partial invariance was also more frequently examined in DSM-IV studies (22.9% vs. 0% for DSM-5). Conversely, DSM-5 MG analyses more consistently utilized the recommended ΔCFI and Δχ2 values for determining invariance (76.9% vs. 51.8% for DSM-IV). Interestingly, 6.0% of DSM-IV MG analyses, but none of the DSM-5 analyses, treated PTSD variables as continuous, despite recommendations for ML-based estimators in such cases. The appropriateness of treating five-option items as categorical or continuous remains debated, depending on sample and model characteristics. Additionally, MG analyses employing weighted least squares with mean and variance adjustment (WLSMV) demonstrated superior statistical rigor and higher levels of invariance compared to those using ML-based estimators. A single DSM-IV study, however, inappropriately examined metric invariance with binary variables. These findings underscore the need for future DSM-5 invariance research to enhance statistical rigor, especially given the increasing importance of invariance testing in future DSM revisions.
Invariance testing follows a hierarchical structure, where each level builds upon the preceding one. A significant statistical concern arises when studies proceed to examine higher levels of invariance despite detecting noninvariance at an earlier stage, such as metric invariance. When noninvariance is identified, the recommended approach is to investigate partial invariance. This step helps guide subsequent actions based on the research goals, clinical justification for invariance testing, practical considerations, and the intended application of the measurement tool. Removing noninvariant items from a PTSD assessment instrument is an option, but it carries potential drawbacks. This could diminish the breadth of PTSD domains covered, reduce symptom heterogeneity, and compromise the clinical utility of allowing diverse PTSD item endorsements for diagnosis, making it the least practical solution. Instead, researchers can address noninvariance by incorporating it into statistical calculations to enhance the accuracy and interpretability of findings, modifying the assessment instrument, or discontinuing its use for the specific subgroups under consideration. At a minimum, achieving configural invariance and appropriately managing any subsequent noninvariance in PTSD research is crucial.
Our review identified specific PTSD items contributing to construct instability, influenced by the subgroups, PTSD model, and invariance levels studied. Intrusion symptoms like intrusive memories and distress from trauma cues, alongside dysphoria/numbing symptoms such as traumatic amnesia, anhedonia, foreshortened future, emotional numbness, restricted affect, and detachment, consistently showed instability. Hypervigilance, an arousal symptom, also proved unstable. Notably, most of these unstable items (anhedonia, distress to trauma cues, intrusive memories, traumatic amnesia, foreshortened future, emotional numbness, detachment, and hypervigilance) exhibited varying factor loadings across subgroups defined by deployment status (e.g., pre- vs. post-deployment). This suggests that deployment experiences and the passage of time may alter individuals' interpretations of these PTSD items, potentially due to the psychological effects of deployment and subsequent shifts in worldview. Therefore, studies comparing deployment-differentiated subgroups must consider these non-invariant PTSD items.
Our review revealed that responses to items assessing "foreshortened future" and "traumatic amnesia" were influenced by factors beyond core PTSD severity. These factors included co-occurring pain, the method of PTSD assessment, or the time elapsed since deployment. This aligns with existing research that questions the significance of these items within PTSD symptomatology, potentially leading to the modification of the "foreshortened future" item in the DSM-5. Furthermore, our findings indicated that most numbing/dysphoria items, such as anhedonia, contributed to the non-equivalence of the PTSD construct across different groups. This finding contributes to the ongoing discussion regarding the specificity of these symptoms to PTSD. Consequently, removing or modifying these specific items could enhance the validity of PTSD assessment instruments for particular subgroups, leading to more accurate measurements.
While the PCL and PTSD-RI were the most frequently studied instruments for invariance, they primarily exhibited configural and metric invariance. This suggests that even commonly used PTSD measures require initial invariance testing before comparing PTSD estimates across different subgroups. In contrast, measures like the PRIM and PTSD-Q, despite limited data, showed promise in consistently measuring the PTSD construct, demonstrating invariance at all assessed levels. Further research on the invariance of these measures is warranted. Both clinician-administered and non-interview self-report PTSD measures generally showed equivalent configural and metric invariance. However, clinician-administered measures achieved higher levels of invariance, including scalar, strict factorial, and factor variance/covariance invariance (excluding factor means), compared to self-report measures. Despite their robust psychometric properties and capacity to reduce misinterpretations, it is noteworthy that clinician-administered PTSD measures, such as the CAPS and diagnostic interview modules like the M.I.N.I., have undergone less invariance testing. This disparity highlights a potential gap in the evaluation of these crucial assessment tools.
The PTSD factor structure generally demonstrated stability and comparability across subgroups, as evidenced by widespread support for configural invariance despite diverse PTSD models. Our findings suggest that age, gender, cultural/linguistic factors, and sample diversity have minimal moderating effects on PTSD symptom structure. However, responses to PTSD items may not reflect true differences in the latent PTSD construct for subgroups defined by administration mode, measure type, psychopathology, diagnostic variables, deployment status, or educational attainment. Notably, only one study investigated PTSD invariance in an intervention-receiving sample, a crucial area for validating clinical trial data. This highlights a significant gap in research, given the importance of such studies for accurate interpretation of treatment outcomes.
Our findings are subject to several limitations. Methodological inconsistencies in invariance testing across studies hindered our ability to draw definitive conclusions. Additionally, our review excluded studies employing alternative invariance testing methods, such as item-response theory or MIMIC models, to maintain a focused scope. However, we noted some congruity between our reviewed MGCFA studies and unreviewed MIMIC model studies. For example, Marshall (2004) used MGCFA to compare the PCL across English and Spanish, finding invariance for Models A–C and E, a result echoed by Miles et al. (2008) who used a MIMIC model and found minimal differential item functioning. Conversely, Hall et al. (2012) utilized a MIMIC model to assess PCL invariance across genders, identifying noninvariance for Models C–E, which partially diverged from our reviewed studies that indicated Model C noninvariance but Model D–E invariance. Future research should address these discrepancies and consider investigating unpublished papers to mitigate publication bias. Furthermore, there is a clear need for invariance examinations across a broader range of subgroups, particularly those defined by varying trauma types, given their significant influence on post-traumatic outcomes.
The generalizability of invariance testing is limited by its reliance on a latent variable model, as well as the specific PTSD instruments, subgroups, and models investigated in the reviewed studies. Therefore, numerous replication studies are essential to achieve broadly applicable conclusions. A meta-analysis would further aid in quantifying the significance between models and their implications. Our review's scope was also constrained by the limited number of studies focusing on DSM-5 symptoms, yielding only preliminary insights into DSM-5 PTSD invariance. Additionally, we were unable to address ongoing debates within the invariance literature, such as establishing statistical thresholds for identifying meaningful construct differences and invariance violations. Nevertheless, assessing and addressing noninvariance is crucial. This process advances scientific understanding of the measurement and structural validity of PTSD assessment tools, alongside clarifying how PTSD symptoms manifest differently across subgroups. Ensuring that instrument components and item-to-factor relationships are consistent across subgroups is a prerequisite for valid latent-level comparisons. Continued investigation into PTSD invariance remains a vital endeavor for both trauma research and clinical practice.
