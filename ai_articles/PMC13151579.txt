The EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) evaluated the safety of extending the use of beetroot red (E 162) in dietary foods for special medical purposes (FSMP) for children aged 1 to 3 years, following a European Commission request. Currently, food colours are not permitted in any food for infants and young children, including FSMPs. The applicant argued that colours are necessary to make these specialized foods appealing and palatable, which is crucial for patient compliance with prescribed dietary regimes. The Panel assessed potential risks for children with cow's milk allergy or other protein allergies, concluding there was no increased risk of sensitivity or intolerance to beetroot red extract in this specific group. Exposure assessments were conducted, considering scenarios where FSMPs provided the total daily protein requirement (up to 3 g protein/kg body weight). At the proposed use level of 20 mg/L of beetroot red (expressed as betanin) in the final diluted product, the intake of betanin was found to be below the amount typically ingested by the same age group from the use of beetroot red as a general food additive. Other exposure scenarios, based on different assumptions, also remained below the high-level intake range of betanin for this age group. Therefore, the ANS Panel concluded that the proposed extension of beetroot red (E 162) use in FSMPs for young children aged 1 to 3 years at 20 mg/L would not pose a safety concern.

The EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) has issued a scientific opinion, at the European Commission's request, on the safety of extending the use of beetroot red (E 162). This opinion specifically addresses its addition to dietary food for special medical purposes (FSMP) for young children aged 1–3 years.
Food colours are currently banned in all foods for infants and young children, including FSMPs.
The applicant argues that food colors are necessary to make dietary foods for special medical purposes (FSMPs) appealing and palatable. This is crucial for patient adherence to prescribed dietary regimens, which is vital for their health. These FSMPs are designed to meet daily nutrient needs or supplement insufficient dietary intake. Specifically, the applicant stated that beetroot red (E 162) added to FSMPs aims to support protein requirements, thereby promoting the physiological growth and development of the target young children.
In 2015, EFSA re-evaluated the safety of beetroot red (E 162). While EFSA determined that the existing data were insufficient to establish an acceptable daily intake (ADI) for beetroot red according to its specifications, the Panel noted that the coloring agents in E 162 are naturally present in the diet and have a long history of consumption. Furthermore, exposure to betanin, the primary coloring principle, from the use of beetroot red as a food additive is comparable to dietary intake from regular food. Consequently, the Panel concluded that, at the reported usage levels, beetroot red (E 162) is not a safety concern for its current application as a food additive. The Panel's 2015 assessment also estimated high-level exposure to beetroot red (E 162) in toddlers aged 1-3 years, from its use as a food additive, to be between 1.4 and 2.8 mg betanin/kg body weight per day.
The Panel deemed existing toxicological assessments of beetroot red (E 162) valid. Therefore, no further reconsideration was deemed necessary to address the mandate's terms of reference.
To address concerns about potential increased allergy and intolerance risks in children with cow's milk or other protein allergies, the applicant was asked for more information. EFSA's focused literature search revealed no significant reports of beetroot allergy or intolerance in the general population. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this specific group of children would not face a higher risk of sensitivity or intolerance to beetroot red extract. It was also noted that while both intolerance and allergy are adverse food reactions, they stem from different mechanisms. Allergy involves an immune response, whereas intolerance does not. Therefore, there is no current basis to suggest that individuals with allergies are more susceptible to intolerance.
The Panel recognized that the dietary habits of individuals consuming FSMPs differ significantly from those in the general population due to their medical conditions. Consequently, the Panel incorporated these unique dietary patterns into its exposure estimations for beetroot red (E 162), employing various scenarios. To facilitate this, the applicant put forth an exposure scenario involving two representative FSMP products, each containing beetroot red at the suggested concentration of 20 mg/L in the final diluted form. This scenario was predicated on the assumption that patients would fulfill their entire daily protein requirement, which the applicant set at 3 g/kg body weight per day, exclusively through FSMPs colored with beetroot red at the proposed level. The application further detailed that these FSMP products, whether liquid or powdered, contain between 8–10 g of protein equivalent per 100 g or 100 mL. At the proposed use level of 20 mg/L of beetroot red (E 162) in the final FSMP product, the Panel calculated this to equate to 0.20–0.25 mg of betanin (the coloring principle) per gram of protein in the diluted product. Building upon this, the Panel then computed two distinct exposure scenarios. The initial scenario was based on the standard protein recommendations of 14.5 g per day for children aged 1–3 years. The second scenario, however, posited a situation where patients would meet their entire daily protein requirement, as defined by the applicant at 3 g/kg body weight per day, solely from FSMPs containing beetroot red.
In the most extreme exposure scenario, a Food for Special Medical Purposes (FSMP) product containing beetroot red (E 162) could fully meet a child's protein needs (3 g protein/kg body weight). This would lead to a maximum intake of beetroot red (E 162) of 0.6–0.8 mg/kg body weight daily. This intake is lower than the betanin amount typically ingested by toddlers aged 12–35 months from beetroot red's use as a food additive, which ranges from 1.4–2.8 mg betanin/kg body weight per day. Additionally, another assessed exposure scenario, based on different assumptions, also resulted in an intake below that from beetroot red's established food additive use.
The ANS Panel concluded that the proposed extension of use of beetroot red (E 162) at 20 mg/L in the final diluted FSMP for young children aged 1-3 years is not of safety concern.
Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 governs food additive use in the EU. Only additives on the Union list, specifically Annex II, can be marketed and used in foods, provided they meet specified conditions.
Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, Article 16, prohibits food additive use in foods for infants and young children, including specialized medical foods, as defined by Directive 2009/39/EC. Exceptions exist only if explicitly stated in Annex II of the Regulation.
Regulation (EU) No 609/2013 will replace Directive 2009/39/EC from July 20, 2016. This new regulation from the European Parliament and Council establishes a framework for food intended for infants and young children, food for special medical purposes (FSMP), and total diet replacements for weight control, encompassing FSMP within its scope.
Regulation (EU) No 609/2013 provides definitions relevant to this assessment. Specifically, Article 2(2)(b) defines a "young child" as an individual between one and three years of age. Furthermore, Article 2(2)(g) defines "food for special medical purposes" (FSMP). This category encompasses food that has undergone special processing or formulation and is intended for the dietary management of patients, including infants. FSMPs are to be used under medical supervision and are designed for the complete or partial feeding of individuals who have difficulties with taking, digesting, absorbing, metabolizing, or excreting ordinary food or its nutrients. This also includes patients with medically determined nutrient requirements that cannot be met by dietary modifications alone.
In 1997, the Scientific Committee for Food (SCF) assessed the safety of specific colorants in foods for special medical purposes (FSMP) intended for young children. The SCF deemed mixed carotenes, beetroot red, chlorophylls, and anthocyanins safe for use in FSMP for children aged 12 months and older, up to proposed maximum levels in the final consumed product: 30 mg/L for mixed carotenes, and 20 mg/L for beetroot red, chlorophylls, and anthocyanins. However, the SCF advised against using colors in products for children with cow's milk or other protein allergies, hypothesizing a potential increased risk of intolerance. Regarding carotenes, naturally derived mixed carotenes were considered acceptable for FSMP. The SCF could not provide an opinion on synthetic beta-carotene pending its separate review.
In 2012, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) reassessed the safety of mixed carotenes and β-carotene. EFSA determined that the use of synthetic β-carotene and mixed β-carotenes derived from sources like palm fruit oil, carrots, and algae as a food colorant posed no safety concerns. This conclusion was contingent on the intake from their use as a food additive and supplement not exceeding the levels naturally consumed through regular food consumption, estimated at 5–10 mg per day.
The acceptability of using sweeteners in special medical foods for young children has not yet been assessed.

Given the applicant's proposed use, EFSA is now asked to determine if the SCF's conclusions still hold for carotenes (E 160a) and beetroot red (E 162) in these specific applications.

For this opinion, the definition of Food for Special Medical Purposes (FSMP) from Commission Directive 1999/21/EC applies. FSMPs are a category of foods for particular nutritional uses. They are specially processed or formulated for the dietary management of patients and must be used under medical supervision. These foods are intended for the complete or partial feeding of individuals who have difficulties taking, digesting, absorbing, metabolizing, or excreting ordinary foods or their nutrients. This also includes individuals with other medically determined nutrient requirements. Their dietary management cannot be achieved solely by altering their normal diet, using other foods for particular nutritional uses, or a combination of both.
A new regulation, Regulation (EU) No 609/2013, concerning food for special medical purposes (FSMP) and food for infants and young children, including those under 12 months, is set to take effect on July 20, 2016. This regulation provides a definition for FSMP: "food specially processed or formulated and intended for the dietary management of patients, including infants, to be used under medical supervision." Such food is designated for the complete or partial feeding of individuals who have difficulties with the intake, digestion, absorption, metabolism, or excretion of ordinary foods or their nutrients. It also applies to those with other medically determined nutritional needs that cannot be met by simply altering their normal diet.
Information on beetroot red (E 162)'s existing uses, authorisations, and evaluations from the EU and international bodies has been supplied by the applicant.
The Panel recently re-evaluated beetroot red (E 162) as a food additive, considering prior evaluations and authorisations.
Beetroot red (E 162) is a Group II food colour authorized for use in all foodstuffs at quantum satis (QS) levels.
Dietary foods for special medical purposes (category 13.2) can use Group II food colours. However, this authorization excludes products intended for infants and young children for special medical purposes (category 13.1.5), including special infant formulas.
In 1997, the Scientific Committee for Food (SCF) assessed the use of carotenes (E 160a), beetroot red (E 162), chlorophylls (E 140), and anthocyanins in foods for special medical purposes (FSMPs) intended for children aged 1 to 3. The SCF specifically examined whether children with particular medical conditions might exhibit different reactions to these colorants compared to healthy individuals. Despite limited biological data at the time, the SCF did not foresee any predisposition to issues with these four colorants arising from conditions like inborn errors of metabolism, intestinal, liver, or kidney diseases, or general feeding difficulties. However, the SCF did express concern regarding allergies, suggesting a potential predisposition to intolerance to colorants. Consequently, they deemed the use of colors inadvisable in products for children with cow's milk or other protein allergies.
In Australia and New Zealand, Beet red was permitted as a colouring for processed foods under Schedule 1 of the Food Standards Code at the time the initial dossier was prepared. Schedule 1's listing for "Foods for Special Dietary Uses" (category 13.3) did not impose restrictions on colour use in FSMPs for children over one year old.

The assessment adhered to EFSA's transparency guidance for risk assessment (2009) and followed existing EFSA Scientific Committee guidelines.
The ANS Panel evaluated the proposed extension of beetroot red (E 162) use following the 'Guidance for submission for food additive evaluations' (EFSA ANS Panel, 2012b).
The Panel was unable to utilize the standard FAIM (Food Additives Intake Model) tool for exposure assessment in this evaluation. This limitation arose because children aged 1–3 years receiving FSMPs have distinct dietary consumption patterns compared to the general population due to their medical conditions. Their diets rely on FSMPs, which either meet their complete nutritional needs or supplement insufficient intake. The applicant proposed adding beetroot red (E 162) to enhance the appeal and palatability of these FSMPs. To estimate exposure in this specific population, the Panel employed one of the applicant's proposed daily protein intake scenarios, which involved two different FSMP products. Furthermore, the Panel calculated exposure to beetroot red (E 162) in FSMPs by considering the typical protein content (8–10 g per 100 mL or mg) detailed in the dossier, alongside the proposed use level.
The Panel calculated an additional exposure scenario using protein dietary reference values established in 2012 by the EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA).
Beetroot red (E 162), proposed for extended use, complies with current specifications in Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012, according to the applicant.
The applicant proposes using beetroot red (E 162) in special medical foods for children aged 1-3 years. The aim is to enhance product appeal and palatability, thereby improving adherence to prescribed dietary plans. The proposed usage level is 20 mg/L in the final diluted product.
In 1997, the SCF specifically excluded children with cow's milk or other protein allergies from the authorized use of natural colors in foods for special medical purposes. The SCF hypothesized that this vulnerable group might face a heightened risk of intolerance to various dietary components, including beetroot red and beta carotene extracts. This reasoning was reportedly derived from an earlier report.
Beetroot red (E 162), per EU specifications, contains significant amounts of carbohydrates (42–56%) and proteins (5–8%).
To investigate potential increased risks of allergy and intolerance in children with cow's milk or other protein allergies, the applicant provided EFSA with a literature review. This review examined available evidence concerning such risks. The applicant's conclusion was that the literature search revealed no reports of beetroot intolerance or allergy in children with confirmed allergies to cow's milk or other whole proteins, including wheat, egg, nuts, and peanuts.
The Panel found no significant reports of beetroot allergy or intolerance in the general population. Considering previous findings on allergy and intolerance from 1995, the Panel determined that this specific population group faces no increased risk of sensitivity or intolerance to beetroot red extract. Consequently, the risk assessment can rely on the recent re-evaluation of beetroot red as a food colour conducted in 2015.
The Panel observed that while both food intolerance and allergy are adverse reactions, they stem from different mechanisms. Allergy involves the immune system, whereas intolerance does not. Consequently, there's no current reason to believe individuals with allergies are more susceptible to intolerance.
For patients with food allergies or inborn errors of metabolism, dietary management primarily involves modifying the diet to reduce or eliminate specific macronutrients, such as protein.
The applicant proposed two scenarios for incorporating beetroot red (E 162) into FSMPs for children. Both scenarios assumed that children would fulfill their entire daily protein requirement, set by the applicant at 3 g/kg body weight, exclusively from FSMPs containing beetroot red. Within one of these scenarios, specific product examples were detailed, reinforcing the assumption of sole protein reliance on these colored FSMPs. Based on these scenarios, the concentration of beetroot red (E 162) in the final diluted product was estimated to fall between 0.01 and 0.03 mg per gram of protein.
The application stated that FSMP products, whether liquid or powdered, contain 8–10 grams of protein equivalent per 100 grams or 100 milliliters. Consequently, at the proposed use level of 20 mg/L of beetroot red (E 162) in the final diluted FSMP, the concentration of beetroot red, measured as the coloring principle betanin, would equate to 0.20–0.25 mg per gram of protein.
