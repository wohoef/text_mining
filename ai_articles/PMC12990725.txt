Gemcitabine (GEM) is the standard first-line chemotherapy for locally advanced pancreatic cancer, but resistance to this drug presents a major clinical hurdle. This study aimed to determine if exosomes originating from GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer stem cells (CSCs) facilitate communication between sensitive and resistant cells, thereby influencing drug resistance. To investigate this, GEM-sensitive BxPC-3 (BxS) and PANC-1 cells were exposed to exosomes derived from GEM-resistant BxPC-3 CSCs (BxR-CSCs). The researchers then assessed the impact of these exosomes on drug resistance, cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and miRNA expression in the BxS and PANC-1 cells. Key miRNAs implicated in GEM resistance were identified, and the specific role of miR-210 in mediating drug resistance was examined both in laboratory settings and in living organisms. The results demonstrated that exosomes from BxR-CSCs conferred GEM resistance to BxS and PANC-1 cells. These exosomes also suppressed GEM-induced cell cycle arrest, counteracted GEM-induced apoptosis, and enhanced tube formation and cell migration. Higher levels of miR-210 were found in BxR-CSCs and their derived exosomes compared to their GEM-sensitive counterparts. Furthermore, exposure to BxR-CSC-derived exosomes led to increased miR-210 expression in BxS and PANC-1 cells in a GEM dose-dependent manner. In BxS cells, transfection with miR-210 mimics induced biological alterations, including activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, mirroring the effects of BxR-CSC-derived exosomes. In conclusion, these findings suggest that exosomes from GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer stem cells transfer drug resistance characteristics to GEM-sensitive pancreatic cancer cells through the delivery of miR-210.

Pancreatic cancer, a prevalent and aggressive malignancy, has seen limited success in patient management thus far. In 2018, the American Cancer Society reported around 460,000 new cases globally, leading to an estimated 430,000 deaths. Gemcitabine (GEM), a nucleoside analog, is a first-line FDA-approved drug that functions by integrating into genomic DNA to impede DNA synthesis. For the past decade, GEM, either alone or in combination therapies, has been a cornerstone in treating locally advanced pancreatic cancer. Nevertheless, its efficacy is compromised by the emergence of resistance following extended treatment. While interventions like resveratrol-induced downregulation of nutrient-deprivation autophagy factor-1 and metformin have shown promise in enhancing GEM sensitivity in GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer, the underlying molecular mechanisms driving this resistance progression remain an area requiring further investigation.
The tumor microenvironment is a complex ecosystem populated by various cell types, including adult stem cells, stromal cells, and cancer stem cells (CSCs). These cells engage in intercommunication, influencing tumor progression. CSCs, a unique subset of tumor cells, possess self-renewal capabilities and multi-lineage differentiation potential. They are implicated in cancer initiation, recurrence, and metastasis, often exhibiting heightened resistance to radiation and chemotherapy. Due to their role in accelerating tumor growth, CSCs present a significant therapeutic hurdle. While CSC interactions with their microenvironment are recognized as crucial for developing drug resistance, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms are still under investigation. Emerging research suggests that exosomes might facilitate cell-cell communication within the tumor microenvironment, potentially shedding light on how CSCs can transfer drug resistance between sensitive and resistant cancer cells.
Secreted by diverse cell types, exosomes are small, 30–120 nm lipid bilayer vesicles. They are internalized by adjacent or remote cells, fostering communication networks that create mutually beneficial feedback loops. Exosomes primarily facilitate cell-to-cell dialogue by transferring various biomolecules, including proteins, DNA, mRNAs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs. The transfer of oncogenic miRNAs from cancer cells can modify the behavior of non-cancerous cells, whereas tumor-suppressing miRNAs can impede tumor development. Based on this, we proposed that exosomes originating from cancer stem cells (CSCs) might contribute to chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer through the delivery of miRNAs.
This study investigated if exosomes from GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer stem cells (BxR-CSCs) induce GEM resistance in GEM-sensitive cells (BxS). We further explored if these exosomes transfer drug resistance traits to BxS and PANC-1 cells via miR-210 delivery.
Gemcitabine (GEM)-sensitive (BxS) and GEM-resistant (BxR) human pancreatic cancer cell lines, both derived from BxPC-3, were obtained from Dr. Yiwei Li. The PANC-1 human pancreatic cancer cell line was acquired from the Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. All human pancreatic cancer cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 units/ml), and streptomycin (100 g/ml) at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The BxR cell line was previously established from the BxS cell line through prolonged exposure to escalating concentrations of GEM, reaching up to 200 nM. Furthermore, cancer stem cells (CSCs) expressing CD133 and CD44 were isolated from both BxS and BxR cells. These CSC populations, designated BxS-CSCs and BxR-CSCs, were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, penicillin (50 IU/ml), and streptomycin (50 mg/ml) under identical temperature and atmospheric conditions. Cells were collected using trypsinization and subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).
To characterize cancer stem cells (CSCs), they were cultured overnight in 6-well plates. The following day, cells were harvested, centrifuged for 5 minutes, and then resuspended in PBS with 10% FBS. These cell suspensions were incubated with Alexa Fluor®488-conjugated antibodies against SOX-2, OCT-4, and Nanog (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes in the dark at room temperature. Flow cytometry (Beckman) was used to quantify the percentage of positive cells. For sphere formation assays, CSCs were plated in ultralow-cluster 6-well plates (Corning Inc.). They were cultured for 10 days in serum-free DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen) supplemented with B27 (Invitrogen), epidermal growth factor, basic fibroblast growth factor (PeproTech), bovine serum albumin (Sigma), and insulin. The resulting spheres were then photographed using a bright-field microscope.
Exosomes were successfully isolated from CSC supernatants using an ExoQuick-TC Kit. Following the manufacturer's protocol, supernatants underwent centrifugation at 10,000×g to eliminate cell debris. An ExoQuick-TC Exosome Precipitation Solution was then added in a 1:5 ratio to the filtered solution and refrigerated for a minimum of 12 hours. Subsequent centrifugation at 1500×g for 30 minutes allowed for the aspiration of the supernatant. After a final centrifugation step at 1500×g for 5 minutes to remove any remaining liquid, the isolated exosomes, designated BxS-CSCs/Exo and BxR-CSCs/Exo, were dissolved in PBS and stored at -80 °C. Exosomal protein content was analyzed by Western blotting for markers CD81, CD63, and GM130 after extraction with radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer. Exosome morphology was also confirmed via transmission electron microscopy.
To confirm exosome uptake, BxS and PANC-1 cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled exosomes. Exosomes were first labeled using a PKH67 kit. Following dilution and incubation with the PKH67 solution, the dye was quenched with bovine serum albumin in PBS. The labeled exosomes were then pelleted through two centrifugation steps at 100,000×g and 4 °C for 70 minutes each. Subsequently, BxS and PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. After reaching 50–60% confluence, they were cultured with a determined concentration of exosomes (2 μg per well, based on bicinchoninic acid assay for exosomal protein concentration) for 24 hours. For visualization of uptake, BxS and PANC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and incubated overnight with 10 μg of PKH67-labeled exosomes in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere. Nuclei were then stained with DAPI, and cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope. The presence of green fluorescence indicated the endocytosis of PKH67-labeled exosomes by the cells.
To assess cell viability, an MTT assay was performed following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. Following experimental treatments, MTT reagent (5 mg/ml) was added and incubated for 4 hours at 37°C. Subsequently, the culture medium was removed, and 200 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to each well for 30 minutes at room temperature. Absorbance was then measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader.
To assess cell cycle progression, harvested cells underwent washing with PBS. Subsequently, they were incubated with RNase A (10 mg/ml), propidium iodide (PI) (400 mg/ml), and Triton-X (0.1%) in PBS at room temperature for 30 minutes. The stained cells were then subjected to flow cytometry analysis. Apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V/PI staining method, following the manufacturer's guidelines. Cells were initially plated in 6-well plates to achieve 50–60% confluence and incubated for 24 hours. Following treatment, cells were collected via trypsinization and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/ml. These cells were then labeled with Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate for 15 minutes at room temperature. After adding 5 μl of PI, the samples were analyzed by flow cytometry, acquiring at least 2 × 104 cells per sample. Each experiment was performed with three independent replicates.
To evaluate the impact of CSC-derived exosomes on cell invasion and tube formation, specific assays were conducted. For invasion assessment, BxS or PANC-1 cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were initially placed in the upper chamber of a Transwell system with serum-free medium. These cells were pretreated with CSC-derived exosomes at a concentration of 100 mg/ml. The lower chamber contained complete culture medium with a high serum concentration to attract invading cells. Following a 48-hour treatment with GEM at varying concentrations (0, 0.5, 1.5, or 15 μM), the cells that successfully migrated through the Matrigel-coated membrane into the lower chamber were stained with crystal violet and photographed. Invasion ability was quantified by counting these stained cells. Tube formation assays, following established protocols, were also performed. A 24-well plate was prepared by coating it with 200 μl of Matrigel and incubating it at 37 °C for 1 hour to allow gel formation. Subsequently, BxS or PANC-1 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded onto these Matrigel-coated wells. These cells were cultured in M199 medium supplemented with a low-serum growth supplement. The cells were then treated with conditioned media derived from BxS or PANC-1 cells that had been stimulated with either BxS- or BxR-CSC-derived exosomes (100 mg/ml). After a 48-hour exposure to different GEM concentrations (0, 0.5, 1.5, or 15 μM), the resulting tube formation was evaluated using a bright-field microscope.
To analyze protein expression, total protein was extracted from samples using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing a proteinase inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentrations were then determined via a bicinchoninic acid assay. Subsequently, approximately 30 μg of protein was subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. To minimize non-specific binding, these membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for one hour at room temperature. Following the blocking step, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting a comprehensive panel of proteins. This included antibodies for stemness markers (SOX-2, OCT-4, Nanog), drug resistance proteins (MDR1, YB-1, BCRP), exosome markers (CD63, CD81, GM130), apoptosis-related proteins (cleaved caspase-3, Bax, Bcl-2), cell cycle regulators (cyclin D1, cyclin E, p27kip1), and components of the mTOR signaling pathway (mTOR, p-mTOR, S6K1, p-S6K1). GAPDH served as a loading control. After washing, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for one hour before visualization using an enhanced chemiluminescence system.
Sangon Biotech Ltd. synthesized miR-210 mimics and inhibitors, with similar negative control sequences designated miR-NC. BxS cells, in logarithmic growth, were cultured in 12-well plates at 2 × 10⁴ cells/well for 24 hours. Subsequently, these cells underwent transfection with 20 nM miR-210 mimics and 25 nM miR-210 inhibitors using Lipofectamine 2000 for 48 hours, following the manufacturer's protocol.
To assess miRNA expression, total RNA was extracted from cells and exosomes using a mirVana™ miRNA Isolation Kit. Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was then employed to measure the levels of miR-145, miR-210, miR-509, and miR-1243, with U6 serving as the internal control. The 2-ΔΔCt method was used to analyze the qRT-PCR data based on Ct values.
To assess the in vivo effects, nude BALB/c mice, aged six weeks, were obtained from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Ethical approval for all animal experiments was granted by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University, adhering to Chinese legislation. Following subcutaneous inoculation with 5 × 10⁶ BxS cells, tumors were allowed to grow for seven days. Subsequently, the mice were divided into eight groups (eight mice per group). These groups included a control group receiving only BxS cells, groups treated with BxS cells plus BxR-CSCs/Exo, miR-210 mimics (20 nM), or miR-210 inhibitors (25 nM). Additionally, corresponding groups received 40 mg/kg GEM treatment. Exosomes, miR-210 mimics, and inhibitors were administered intravenously via tail vein injections, with PBS serving as a vehicle control. GEM was administered intraperitoneally three times weekly. Tumor growth was monitored every two days using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume (mm³) = 0.5 × (W)² × (L), where L is the length and W is the width.
Data from three independent replicates were averaged and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed Student's t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with SPSS 16.0, followed by a least significant difference post hoc test. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
To understand how gemcitabine (GEM) resistance develops in pancreatic cancer, researchers first created GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer cell lines. From these, they isolated cancer stem cells (CSCs) from both GEM-sensitive (BxS) and GEM-resistant (BxR) BxPC-3 cell lines. These isolated CSC populations were confirmed to be enriched for stemness markers, including SOX-2, OCT-4, and Nanog, as well as CD44 and CD133, as visualized through flow cytometry (Fig. 1a, b). The study then aimed to assess the role of these CSCs in mediating GEM resistance. BxS-CSCs and BxR-CSCs were exposed to varying concentrations of GEM for 48 hours. A subsequent MTT assay demonstrated that BxR-CSCs exhibited significantly higher resistance to GEM compared to BxS-CSCs, with an IC50 value for GEM in BxR-CSCs being over 50 times greater than in BxS-CSCs (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. S2). Further analysis using Western blotting revealed distinct patterns in the expression of stemness markers (SOX-2, OCT-4, Nanog) and drug resistance proteins (MDR1, YB-1, BCRP). In BxS-CSCs, the expression of these proteins decreased in a dose-dependent manner with increasing GEM concentrations. However, in BxR-CSCs, this reduction was not observed, indicating their inherent resistance to GEM (Fig. 1d). These findings collectively suggest that the GEM resistance observed in BxR-CSCs is a key factor contributing to the overall resistance of BxR cells to the drug.
Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the isolation of exosomes, measuring approximately 50–100 nm in diameter, from both BxS-CSCs and BxR-CSCs (Fig. 1e). Western blot analysis revealed robust expression of exosomal markers CD63, CD81, and GM130 within these isolated vesicles, while their presence in the original cell lysates was minimal (Fig. 1f), validating the successful extraction of exosomes. To ascertain the influence of these CSC-derived exosomes on pancreatic cancer cells, BxS and PANC-1 cells were cultured with exosomes originating from either BxR-CSCs (BxR-CSCs/Exo) or BxS-CSCs (BxS-CSCs/Exo). These co-cultures were then subjected to GEM treatment at varying concentrations (0, 0.5, 1.5, or 15 μM). The uptake of both exosome types by BxS and PANC-1 cells was confirmed using PKH67 labeling. As visualized by green fluorescence signals, BxS (Fig. 2a) and PANC-1 (Fig. 2b) cells successfully endocytosed these fluorescently tagged exosomes, both in the presence and absence of GEM (15 μM). Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity increased over time, indicating a greater accumulation of exosomes within the cells after 24 hours of culture compared to 2 hours.
The expression of drug resistance markers MDR1, YB-1, and BCRP was assessed in both BxS and PANC-1 cells. In BxS cells treated with exosomes from GEM-sensitive CSCs (BxS-CSCs/Exo), these resistance proteins decreased as GEM concentration increased, indicating a loss of drug resistance. Conversely, when BxS cells were exposed to exosomes from GEM-resistant CSCs (BxR-CSCs/Exo), the levels of these resistance proteins were significantly elevated with increasing GEM doses. This suggests that BxR-CSCs/Exo confer GEM resistance to BxS cells. A similar pattern was observed in PANC-1 cells treated with BxS-CSCs/Exo. However, BxR-CSCs/Exo did not substantially alter GEM resistance in PANC-1 cells. These findings, visualized through Western blot analysis (Fig. 3a), highlight the differential impact of CSC-derived exosomes on drug resistance in pancreatic cancer cells. The subsequent analysis also explored the effects of these exosomes on cell cycle progression, migration, and tube formation in the presence of GEM.
The study then investigated how these exosomes affected the survival and proliferation of BxS and PANC-1 cells. When treated with GEM, both cell types exhibited cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase after exposure to exosomes from BxR-CSCs. This effect was not observed with exosomes from BxS-CSCs. Furthermore, BxR-CSC-derived exosomes significantly inhibited GEM-induced apoptosis in both BxS and PANC-1 cells, a protective effect absent when using BxS-CSC-derived exosomes. The researchers also assessed the impact of these exosomes on cell migration and tube formation in the presence of GEM. Exosomes from BxR-CSCs promoted enhanced migration and tube formation in BxS and PANC-1 cells compared to those treated with BxS-CSC-derived exosomes, even at high GEM concentrations. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that exosomes from GEM-resistant CSCs can confer GEM resistance to GEM-sensitive pancreatic cancer cells by transferring drug-resistant properties across various drug concentrations.
The transfer of drug-resistant traits from GEM-resistant cancer stem cells (CSCs) to GEM-sensitive cells via exosomes may be mediated by specific microRNAs (miRNAs). To investigate this mechanism, we assessed the expression of several miRNAs previously linked to drug resistance, including miR-145, miR-210, miR-509, and miR-1243, within CSC-derived exosomes. Our analysis revealed that miR-210 expression in BxR-CSCs was significantly elevated in a dose-dependent manner when exposed to GEM, compared to BxS-CSCs. In contrast, no substantial differences in the expression of miR-145, miR-509, or miR-1243 were observed between BxR-CSCs and BxS-CSCs under varying GEM concentrations. Crucially, BxR-CSC-derived exosomes (BxR-CSCs/Exo) exhibited significantly higher levels of miR-210 compared to exosomes from BxS-CSCs (BxS-CSCs/Exo). Furthermore, when BxS and PANC-1 cells were treated with BxR-CSCs/Exo, a dose-dependent increase in miR-210 expression was observed, which was linked to GEM exposure. This finding suggests that exosomes facilitate the horizontal transfer of miR-210 between GEM-resistant (BxR) and GEM-sensitive (BxS and PANC-1) pancreatic cancer cells. The observed increase in miR-210 within recipient cells upon exosome treatment indicates a potential role for this miRNA in mediating GEM resistance. These results collectively point towards exosomes as key mediators in the intercellular communication that confers drug resistance, specifically through the delivery of miR-210.
To investigate miR-210's role in GEM resistance, BxS cells were transfected with miR-210 mimics or inhibitors before GEM exposure. qRT-PCR confirmed successful transfection, showing high miR-210 expression with mimics and minimal expression with inhibitors or the negative control. Following GEM treatment, cells exposed to BxR-CSCs/Exo or transfected with miR-210 mimics exhibited increased levels of drug resistance proteins MDR1, YB-1, and BCRP compared to cells treated with miR-210 inhibitors. Notably, the drug resistance conferred by miR-210 mimics appeared more potent than that induced by BxR-CSCs/Exo. Further analysis using flow cytometry assessed cell cycle progression in BxS and PANC-1 cells under similar treatment conditions, revealing distinct effects of BxR-CSCs/Exo and miR-210 modulation on cell cycle distribution when exposed to varying GEM concentrations.
To further investigate the influence of exosome-delivered miR-210 on pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and apoptosis, BxS and PANC-1 cells that had been transfected were exposed to increasing concentrations of GEM. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that in both BxS and PANC-1 cells, treatment with miR-210 inhibitors led to a dose-dependent cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase when exposed to GEM. Conversely, transfection with miR-210 mimics and treatment with BxR-CSCs/Exo did not alter cell cycle distribution (Fig. 5c and d, Supplementary Fig. S7 and S8). Furthermore, miR-210 overexpression resulted in a reduction of GEM-induced apoptosis in both cell types, mirroring the effects observed with BxR-CSCs/Exo treatment (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. S9 and S10). These findings were corroborated by Western blot analysis, which examined proteins involved in cell cycle progression, including cyclin D1, cyclin E1, and p27kip1, as well as apoptosis-related proteins such as pro-apoptotic cleaved caspase-3 and Bax, and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 (Fig. 6a).
The mTOR signaling pathway is recognized for its critical role in tumor cell proliferation, survival, and motility. Our investigation revealed that phosphorylation levels of mTOR and its downstream target, S6K1, were significantly increased in BxS cells exposed to BxR-CSCs/Exo or transfected with miR-210 mimics. This effect was less pronounced in cells treated with miR-210 inhibitors. Furthermore, in a mouse model, the administration of miR-210 inhibitors effectively limited tumor growth, outperforming treatments with BxR-CSCs/Exo or miR-210 mimics. While GEM treatment generally reduced tumor volumes across groups, the miR-210 inhibitor group showed a more substantial decrease. This highlights that GEM's efficacy was diminished in mice treated with BxR-CSCs/Exo or miR-210 mimics due to the induced drug resistance. We also confirmed the presence of miR-210 in tumor tissues. Mice receiving BxR-CSCs/Exo exhibited elevated miR-210 levels compared to controls. Conversely, miR-210 mimics increased miR-210 expression, while inhibitors reduced it, as expected. GEM administration consistently lowered miR-210 levels in tumor tissues. These findings collectively suggest that miR-210, transferred by BxR-CSCs/Exo, confers GEM resistance by activating the mTOR signaling pathway.
Gemcitabine (GEM) resistance poses a significant challenge in pancreatic cancer treatment. Understanding the mechanisms behind this resistance is crucial for enhancing GEM's therapeutic effectiveness. Our research demonstrates that exosomes originating from GEM-resistant cancer stem cells (BxR-CSCs) can transfer drug-resistant characteristics to GEM-sensitive pancreatic cancer cells (BxS and PANC-1) via the delivery of miR-210.
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are implicated in both the initiation of primary cancers and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Their contribution to drug resistance involves several mechanisms, such as reduced cell cycle progression, enhanced drug efflux, improved DNA repair, increased anti-apoptotic capabilities, and elevated detoxification enzyme activity. To explore this, CSCs were isolated from GEM-sensitive (BxS) and GEM-resistant (BxR) BxPC-3 cells. The BxR-derived CSCs demonstrated significantly higher resistance to GEM than their BxS counterparts. Furthermore, while drug resistance proteins like MDR, YB-1, and BCRP decreased in BxS-CSCs with increasing GEM concentrations, their expression remained stable in BxR-CSCs. This suggests that the inherent GEM resistance of BxR-CSCs is a primary driver of the overall resistance observed in BxR cells.
The interplay between cancer stem cells (CSCs) and their surrounding environment is crucial for developing drug resistance. Exosomes, recognized as key mediators of cell-to-cell communication, have been shown to facilitate the transfer of drug resistance. Specifically, exosomes have been documented to transfer drug-resistant characteristics from imatinib-resistant chronic myeloid leukemia cells to sensitive ones. CSCs themselves utilize exosomes to communicate with neighboring cancer cells and stromal cells. In this study, we isolated exosomes from both GEM-resistant (BxR) and GEM-sensitive (BxS) CSCs. Our findings demonstrate that exosomes originating from BxR-CSCs significantly bolster the GEM resistance of BxS and PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cells. Exposure to BxR-CSCs/Exo led to increased GEM resistance, attenuated GEM-induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and enhanced tube formation and cell migration.
The enhanced GEM resistance observed in BxS and PANC-1 cells following exposure to BxR-CSCs/Exo is likely due to the cargo delivered by these exosomes. Exosomes are known to facilitate intercellular communication by transferring various biomolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and miRNAs, which can alter recipient cell behavior. CSC-derived exosomes, in particular, are crucial for miRNA-mediated cell-to-cell signaling. Research has demonstrated that exosomes from drug-resistant cancer cells can confer chemoresistance to sensitive cells through miRNA transfer. Specifically, miRNAs like miR-145, miR-210, miR-509, and miR-1243 have been linked to CSC-mediated chemoresistance. Our study detected these relevant miRNAs in BxS-CSCs, BxR-CSCs, and their derived exosomes via qRT-PCR. We observed a significant, GEM dose-dependent increase in miR-210 expression within BxR-CSCs, which was also highly present in BxR-CSCs/Exo. Furthermore, treatment with BxR-CSCs/Exo led to elevated miR-210 levels in GEM-exposed BxS and PANC-1 cells. Given that miR-210 has previously been shown to enhance radio-resistance in lung cancer, these findings suggest that exosomes facilitate the horizontal transfer of miR-210, thereby conferring GEM resistance between BxR and BxS/PANC-1 cells.
MiR-210 is frequently detected in various cancers, with its target genes implicated in crucial cellular processes like cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, angiogenesis, and cancer stem cell (CSC) viability. Our study revealed that elevated miR-210 levels in BxS cells led to delayed G2/M phase cell cycle arrest and reduced GEM-induced apoptosis. Furthermore, increased miR-210 promoted BxS tumor growth in vivo, mirroring the effects observed with BxR-CSC-derived exosome treatment. These findings were corroborated by observing heightened phosphorylation of mTOR and its downstream target S6K1 in BxS cells exposed to BxR-CSCs/Exo or transfected with miR-210 mimics, compared to those treated with miR-210 inhibitors. Existing research supports miR-210's role in activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. For instance, miR-210 has been shown to protect against hypoxia-induced damage by targeting BNIP3, thereby activating the mTOR pathway. Additionally, miR-210 has been identified as a regulator of BTG3 in neural stem cells, where its overexpression enhances PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation. Our results align with these observations, suggesting that miR-210 enhances proliferation and suppresses apoptosis in BxS cells through mTOR pathway activation.
Exosomes from GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer stem cells (BxR-CSCs) transfer drug resistance to sensitive cells via miR-210. This mechanism explains GEM treatment failure in pancreatic cancer and could inform new therapeutic strategies for advanced disease.
