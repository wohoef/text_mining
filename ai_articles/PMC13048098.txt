This study aimed to assess the performance and short-term outcomes of three distinct transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) devices—the self-expanding Medtronic CoreValve (MCV), the mechanically expanded Lotus valve, and the balloon-expandable Edwards SAPIEN XT (SXT)—in treating severe aortic stenosis (AS). This research addressed a gap in existing literature regarding whether a single heart team could achieve comparable results across different TAVR devices and delivery methods. A retrospective review was conducted on 231 consecutive patients who underwent TAVR at a single high-volume center in Taiwan. The cohort comprised 112 patients receiving MCV, 18 receiving Lotus, and 101 receiving SXT. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using Valve Academic Research Consortium 2 (VARC-2) criteria. The primary endpoint, a composite of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction (MI), or disabling stroke, was assessed within 180 days. Results indicated similar device and procedural success rates across all three TAVR devices. At 30 days post-procedure, no significant differences were observed in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, periprocedural MI, stroke, major vascular complications, life-threatening bleeding, acute kidney injury (AKI, stage 2/3), or the VARC-2 composite early safety endpoints. Furthermore, the primary endpoint rate at 180 days did not differ significantly between the groups. Independent predictors of adverse outcomes included a lack of procedural success, acute coronary occlusion during TAVR, and stage 3 AKI after TAVR. In conclusion, TAVR performed with MCV, Lotus, or SXT demonstrated comparable clinical outcomes at both 30 and 180 days. The study highlighted that periprocedural complications were key determinants of short-term adverse events.

TAVR offers a robust and rapidly advancing treatment for severe aortic stenosis in high-risk individuals. Since 2010, three primary valve technologies have been available: the self-expanding Medtronic CoreValve (MCV), the mechanically expanded Lotus, and the balloon-expandable SAPIEN XT (SXT). These devices received approval from the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration for clinical application in 2012, 2015, and 2016, respectively. It remains an open question whether these earlier-generation TAVR prostheses possess distinct limitations that could influence patient outcomes. Such limitations may be specific to individual patients or inherent to the TAVR system itself.
Recognizing that a universal approach is insufficient for all patients, a "tailored TAVR approach" has been suggested. Our TAVR team aimed to master various TAVR devices and delivery methods. This proficiency allows us to treat a broader range of eligible patients, as centers with limited device options can only serve a fraction of the population. Nevertheless, there is limited evidence demonstrating that a single heart team, utilizing multiple TAVR devices and approaches, can achieve consistently positive outcomes.
This study assessed the performance and outcomes of three TAVR devices (MCV, Lotus, SXT) for severe AS since 2013. The goal was to determine if post-TAVR adverse events were linked to the patient, device, or procedure.
Between March 2013 and August 2017, Cheng-Hsin General Hospital's TAVR program treated 231 consecutive patients. These individuals, all suffering from severe AS and deemed high-risk for traditional cardiac surgery, were assessed by a multidisciplinary team including interventional cardiologists, imaging cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, radiologists, and anesthesiologists. The SXT valve was implanted in 101 patients (43.7%), the MCV device in 112 (48.5%), and the Lotus valve in 18 (7.8%).
At our institution, the MCV, Lotus, and SXT devices were introduced in 2013, 2015, and 2016, respectively. Pre-procedural imaging, including CT scans, angiography, and echocardiography, guided the selection of access for all patients. The transfemoral (TF) approach was the standard. However, if peripheral vessel disease made TF unfeasible, alternative approaches like trans-subclavian, transaortic, or transapical implantation were considered.
Implantation procedures for all study participants took place in a hybrid operating room, with most patients receiving general anesthesia. For transfemoral (TF) TAVR, percutaneous closure devices or surgical femoral artery cut-down were utilized, particularly in cases of severe obesity or vessel calcifications. The trans-subclavian method involved dissecting the subclavian artery via a 4-5 cm left infraclavicular incision. Transaortic access required an upper median mini-sternotomy. For the transapical approach, straight access to the left ventricular apex was achieved through an anterolateral mini-thoracotomy in the fifth or sixth intercostal space. Valve deployment, typically following balloon valvuloplasty during rapid ventricular pacing, was guided by fluoroscopy.
Following TAVR, patients were admitted to the intensive care unit for a minimum of one day, with continuous heart rate monitoring until discharge. All patients received daily 100 mg aspirin for platelet inhibition. Most also received 75 mg clopidogrel daily for three months post-procedure. For those requiring anticoagulation, clopidogrel was combined with warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant, omitting aspirin.
All patients underwent pre- and post-procedure echocardiography and clinical monitoring by the heart valve team. Follow-up involved office visits and telephone interviews. Patients were censored at 6 months or death, whichever came first.
The Society of Thoracic Surgeons' Online Risk Calculator was used to predict patient operative mortality post-TAVR. Echocardiographic studies, conducted at baseline and 30 days post-TAVR, were assessed using American Society of Echocardiography criteria.
The Valve Academic Research Consortium-2 (VARC-2) consensus document established specific criteria for defining successful outcomes. Device success required three conditions: no procedural mortality, accurate placement of a single prosthetic heart valve in the correct anatomical position, and the prosthetic valve performing as intended. This intended performance meant avoiding prosthesis-patient mismatch, maintaining a mean aortic valve gradient below 20 mmHg or peak velocity under 3 m/s, and preventing moderate or severe prosthetic valve regurgitation. Procedural success, on the other hand, was met if the TAVR device was successfully deployed and its delivery system retrieved, without any mortality, conversion to surgical aortic valve replacement, or myocardial infarction.
The study's main objective was to assess major cardiac and cerebral adverse events (MACCE) within 180 days post-TAVR, specifically combining all-cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and disabling stroke. Additionally, early safety endpoints at 30 days were evaluated. These included severe heart failure (NYHA functional class III/IV), life-threatening hemorrhage, significant acute kidney injury (AKI, stage 3), major vascular issues, paravalvular leakage, and the necessity for a permanent pacemaker due to complete heart block. AKI (stage 3) was defined by VARC-2 criteria as a serum creatinine increase of at least 3.0-fold from baseline or reaching ≥4.0 mg/dL (≥354 mmol/L) within 72 hours.
To compare patient demographics, procedural details, and outcomes across TAVR device groups, univariate analyses were performed. Continuous data, presented as mean ± SD, were assessed using Student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical data, shown as counts and percentages, were analyzed with Pearson's chi-square or Fisher's exact test.
To analyze survival, patients were categorized into two groups based on the occurrence of MACCE (the primary endpoint) within 180 days. Clinical characteristics and laboratory data were then compared between these groups using appropriate univariate tests. Subsequently, multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses identified independent predictors of 180-day MACCE, incorporating device types and factors significant in the univariate analysis.
Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of <0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA).
Table 1 provides an overview of the baseline demographic and clinical features across the three patient cohorts, categorized by their TAVR device. While the groups were largely comparable, some non-significant trends emerged. Patients receiving the MCV device showed a tendency towards older age, with a mean of 80±8 years, compared to 78±8 years for Lotus and 78±9 years for SXT (p=0.056). The Lotus group had a lower incidence of peripheral artery disease (17%) than the MCV (29%) and SXT (40%) groups (p=0.068). Similarly, end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis was less common in the MCV group (5%) compared to Lotus (11%) and SXT (13%) groups (p=0.078), though these differences did not reach statistical significance. No notable variations were observed in the mean STS score or the percentage of patients presenting with NYHA functional class III/IV heart failure among the three groups.
Baseline echocardiography and CT measurements were comparable across the three groups, despite individual prosthesis type and size being selected based on these imaging findings. Table 2 details these similar measurements.
Table 3 details the technical aspects and outcomes of the TAVR procedures. The range of valve sizes varied by device: MCV valves were 23 to 31 mm, Lotus valves 23 to 27 mm, and SXT valves 23 to 29 mm. Notably, the Lotus group utilized a significantly smaller mean valve size compared to the other two groups (p<0.001), with 94% of Lotus devices being ≤26 mm, compared to 55% for MCV and 84% for SXT (p<0.001). Regarding access routes, MCV procedures employed transfemoral (91%), trans-subclavian (4%), or transaortic (5%) approaches. For Lotus valve implantation, only the transfemoral approach was utilized. SXT valves were implanted via transfemoral (85%), transapical (10%), or transaortic (5%) access. Pre-dilatation with balloon valvuloplasty was less frequently required for Lotus implantations (50%) than for MCV (71%) and SXT (97%) procedures (p<0.001). Conversely, post-dilatation with balloon valvuloplasty was more common with SXT (29%) compared to MCV (13%) and Lotus (11%) (p=0.008). The mean final implantation depth below the annulus was significantly greater in the MCV group (5.3±2.5 mm) compared to Lotus (1.9±1.1 mm) and SXT (2.3±1.1 mm) (p<0.001). Implantation depth was routinely determined via fluoroscopy at the institution. This involved aligning the TAVR frame's bottom with the device's axis and then averaging the distances from the lower edges of the frame to the non-coronary and left coronary cusps, using Siemens software for measurement.
The overall device success rates were comparable across the three TAVR devices: 86% for MCV, 83% for Lotus, and 90% for SXT (p=NS). Issues like initial implant embolization or malpositioning necessitated a second valve implantation in 10 (9%) MCV patients and 4 (4%) SXT patients. Furthermore, moderate or severe paravalvular leakage occurred in 4% of MCV patients, 6% of Lotus patients, and 2% of SXT patients, with no significant differences. Similarly, a post-procedural transvalvular gradient exceeding 20 mmHg was observed in two patients from each group.
The three device groups exhibited comparable rates of major intraoperative complications, such as emergency open-heart surgery conversion, annular or left ventricular rupture, coronary occlusion, or the necessity for urgent hemodynamic support. Procedural success rates were also similar, with MCV at 99%, Lotus at 100%, and SXT at 98% (p=NS). While mean procedure and fluoroscopic times did not differ significantly, the MCV group utilized a notably lower mean contrast volume (112.1±40.2 mL) compared to Lotus (132.7±58.4 mL) and SXT (142.1±59.2 mL; p<0.001).
The hemodynamic performance of the three TAVR devices showed a universal improvement in patients who underwent successful procedures, with a notable decrease in prosthetic valvular pressure gradient (PG) and an increase in prosthetic aortic valve area (AVA) by 30 days. However, the Lotus valve group exhibited a significantly higher mean transaortic valve PG (14.0±6.7 mmHg) compared to MCV (8.1±3.5 mmHg) and SXT (8.2±3.6 mmHg; p<0.001). Concurrently, the Lotus valve also resulted in a smaller AVA (1.7±0.2 cm2) than MCV (1.8±0.3 cm2) and SXT (2.0±0.3 cm2; p<0.001). Echocardiography at 30 days post-TAVR confirmed substantial improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction and right ventricular systolic pressure across all groups, with no significant inter-group differences. The overall incidence of moderate or severe aortic regurgitation (AR) remained low and comparable among the MCV (7%), Lotus (6%), and SXT (3%) devices (p=0.352).
Table 5 details the clinical outcomes of the study participants. All patient groups experienced a notable improvement in their NYHA functional class. The average intensive care unit stay length remained consistent across all three device groups. At the 30-day mark following TAVR, several key outcomes showed no significant differences between the groups. These included all-cause mortality (MCV: 3%, Lotus: 0%, SXT: 3%; p=0.605), cardiovascular mortality (MCV: 2%, Lotus: 0%, SXT: 3%; p=0.564), periprocedural myocardial infarction (0% in all groups; p=NS), stroke (MCV: 2%, Lotus: 0%, SXT: 1%; p=0.691), major or life-threatening bleeding (MCV: 2%, Lotus: 0%, SXT: 5%; p=0.239), major vascular complications (MCV: 11%, Lotus: 17%, SXT: 5%; p=0.388), and acute kidney injury (stage 3) (MCV: 9%, Lotus: 0%, SXT: 3%; p=0.130). However, a significantly higher proportion of patients receiving the Lotus device required a permanent pacemaker (Lotus: 22%, MCV: 13%, SXT: 3%; p=0.004).
Within 180 days, independent predictors of the primary composite endpoint included a lack of procedural success (p=0.023), stage 3 acute kidney injury (p=0.037), and coronary obstruction (p=0.05). Across the device groups, there was no significant difference in the 180-day rate of MACCE, encompassing all-cause death, cardiovascular death, recurrent nonfatal MI, and stroke.
Our study revealed three key findings. First, all three TAVR devices showed similar positive safety and efficacy results at both 30 and 180 days in a real-world patient group. Second, the Lotus device was linked to a higher transaortic valve pressure gradient and a greater need for permanent pacemaker implantation after TAVR. Finally, acute coronary occlusion during TAVR, acute kidney injury, and ongoing refractory heart failure after the procedure independently predicted adverse outcomes within 180 days.
This study observed a device success rate of 86% for MCV, which was slightly lower than the other devices in the cohort, a finding consistent with the CHOICE trial. This reduced success for MCV was primarily linked to increased instances of embolization/migration and moderate to severe aortic regurgitation (AR). The Lotus valve also demonstrated a lower device success rate at 83% when compared to SXT's 90%. This 83% for Lotus was even below what was reported in the REPRISE III trial and was largely attributable to a higher rate of patient-prosthesis mismatch. Similar to the REPRISE III study, our findings indicated that the Lotus valve resulted in a higher mean post-TAVR transaortic valve pressure gradient (PG) of 12 mmHg and a smaller mean aortic valve area (AVA) of 1.59 cm² compared to the other devices. Despite comparable baseline annular dimensions, a greater number of smaller prostheses were implanted in the Lotus group, adhering to manufacturer guidelines for less oversizing with this particular device, which may be due to its design. This smaller average device size could potentially explain the elevated mean gradient observed. While severe patient-prosthesis mismatch post-TAVR is uncommon and can be predicted by a larger body surface area, it does not appear to impact mid-term mortality or overall clinical outcomes. Further research with larger cohorts is necessary to pinpoint independent predictors of patient-prosthesis mismatch and to understand its implications for device longevity and long-term clinical effectiveness. The lower device success rates for MCV and Lotus valves in this study might be coincidental, given the study's limited power for such events. Alternatively, they could reflect a learning curve associated with these specific prostheses. Earlier research suggested that MCV implantation, benefiting from supra-annular valve placement, led to a larger AVA and lower mean pressure gradient compared to SXT. However, in our study, MCV was linked to a smaller AVA, potentially due to deeper implantation, less frequent pre-dilatation, and a slightly smaller baseline annulus diameter.
This study observed a significantly higher requirement for permanent pacemaker implantation with MCV (13%) and Lotus valves (22%) compared to SXT (3%), a finding consistent with prior research. While the Lotus device demonstrated favorable rates for death, stroke, and paravalvular leakage in the REPRISE III study, its 30-day pacemaker implantation rate was nearly twice that of MCV-treated patients (35.5% vs. 19.6%; p<0.001). Although pacemaker implantation is known to increase ventricular dysfunction and hospitalization costs, it hasn't been linked to increased mortality in TAVR patients. The long-term implications of the Lotus device's higher post-TAVR pressure gradient and pacemaker rate, even with the upcoming Lotus Edge, remain unclear. For MCV, deeper implantation might contribute to the elevated pacemaker rate. However, the newer-generation Evolut-R valve, recently introduced in Taiwan, offers retrieval and repositioning capabilities. This advancement is anticipated to reduce pacemaker implantation rates and potentially enhance device success by mitigating embolization/migration and moderate to severe aortic regurgitation compared to the earlier MCV valve.
Coronary obstruction, a rare but significant complication of TAVR, can manifest during valve implantation or later in the post-procedural period (early or after 60 days). Our study identified coronary obstruction occurring within 30 days as a key factor influencing adverse outcomes at 180 days. Therefore, careful consideration of potential coronary obstruction is crucial when selecting a prosthetic valve. Some devices, like the Evolut-PRO, feature a longer frame extending past the coronary ostia, which may reduce obstruction risk. Conversely, valves with larger stent cells and shorter frames could improve future access to coronary arteries. Newer TAVR devices that anchor directly to calcified native or surgical leaflets can also lessen the chance of future valve tissue prolapse and subsequent coronary obstruction. Furthermore, achieving optimal implantation depth is vital for facilitating future coronary reaccess, particularly when the ostia are less than 10 mm.
Acute kidney injury (AKI) at stage 3 was identified as a significant predictor of negative outcomes in our study. The development of post-TAVR AKI is complex, stemming from multiple factors. Key procedural concerns include renal hypoperfusion due to intra-procedural hypotension and contrast-induced nephropathy. While the average contrast volumes in our study aligned with prior research, there remains potential for reduction. For patients with existing chronic renal issues, employing self-expanding or mechanically expanding valves, which offer repositioning and retrievability guided by echocardiography or fluoroscopy-echocardiography fusion, could facilitate more precise valve placement and avoid contrast aortography.
This study has several limitations. It was not a randomized trial, meaning selection bias and unmeasured confounders could influence the findings, despite similar patient characteristics across groups. The research was conducted at a single center with a relatively small patient cohort. Additionally, the follow-up period was limited to 180 days, suggesting a need for further investigation into the long-term outcomes of these devices.
This study, conducted at a single Asian center, evaluated the technical success, hemodynamic performance, and VARC-2 outcomes of three distinct TAVR devices. The results indicated no significant differences in early safety endpoints at 30 days or the primary endpoint at 180 days across patients treated with MCV, Lotus, or SXT devices. This suggests that, with a proficient heart team, adverse events following TAVR are primarily patient-dependent. These findings offer important benchmarks for future patient selection and evaluating newer TAVR technologies.
