Demographers traditionally focus on societal fertility ideals, which are presumed to be more stable than personal ideals. Investigating the stability of personal fertility ideals is crucial for refining models of population fertility trends and understanding well-being linked to fertility outcomes. This study utilized the two-wave National Survey of Fertility Barriers (NSFB) to examine fertility ideal stability and change among 879 women in persistent heterosexual unions. The findings reveal that while personal fertility ideals remain stable for the majority of women (69%), a significant portion, approximately one-third, adjust their ideal number over the study period. Among those who modified their ideals, the changes were evenly split between increases and decreases. Multinomial logistic regression analysis indicated that women with a higher fertility ideal at the first wave were more likely to increase their ideal and less likely to decrease it by the second wave. Furthermore, higher educational attainment was linked to a reduced likelihood of increasing fertility ideals, while full-time employment at the initial interview was associated with a greater probability of decreasing fertility ideals. These results underscore that individual characteristics, attitudes, life course events, and social influences are connected to shifts in personal fertility ideals. Notably, more factors were associated with decreases in ideals than with increases. By demonstrating that a considerable number of women alter their personal fertility ideals over a three-year span, this research enhances our comprehension of variations in fertility experiences and offers valuable insights for policies and interventions aimed at improving child and maternal health.

Despite the widespread availability of reproductive health services for nearly forty years, the existence of unmet fertility ideals continues to prompt inquiry into the individual, cultural, social, and policy influences on fertility at both personal and population levels. While many developed nations, such as Japan and Germany, have experienced significant declines in fertility rates, the United States stands out with its relatively higher fertility rate for a highly developed country. This trend of low fertility in developed countries has led demographers to question whether current reproductive behaviors stem from a genuine preference for fewer children or if a "fertility gap" exists, where achieved fertility falls short of desired fertility.
Demographers often presume that access to contraception correlates with the fulfillment of fertility desires. However, existing research on fertility desires typically examines short-term aspirations, not ideal circumstances. While fertility intentions have been extensively studied (Johnson-Hanks et al. 2011), the concept of fertility ideals, such as ideal family size, has only recently gained attention (Philipov and Bernardi 2012). Longitudinal data from Germany indicates that individuals do modify their fertility ideals, underscoring the importance of actively studying their stability and change rather than assuming it (Heiland, Prskawetz, and Sanderson 2008). This study addresses two key questions: First, what percentage of women in stable heterosexual relationships in the U.S. alter their personal fertility ideal over roughly three years? Second, what individual characteristics, attitudes, life course factors, and social cues are linked to these shifts in fertility ideals?
This research assesses personal fertility ideals by asking about the ideal number of children for individuals, irrespective of their current or intended family size. Our analysis focuses on women aged 25–45 in stable heterosexual relationships within the United States, utilizing data from two waves of the National Survey of Fertility Barriers (NSFB) collected between 2004–2006 and 2007–2010. The findings reveal that while personal fertility ideals remain stable for a majority of women (69%), a significant portion, approximately one-third, modify their ideal number of children over this three-year period. Among those who adjusted their ideals, the changes were evenly split between increases and decreases. Further investigation using multinomial logistic regression demonstrates that personal characteristics, attitudes, life course events, and social influences are linked to these shifts in fertility ideals.
The term "hypothetical fertility" was introduced by Philipov and Bernardi (2012) to encompass a variety of related concepts. These include fertility ideals, intentions, desires, expectations, and aspirations. A common thread among these concepts is their focus on fertility outcomes that are not yet realized and may never be. Given that fertility behavior typically involves planning, many demographers believe that fertility intentions, ideals, and desires are crucial elements in any theoretical framework of human fertility (Coale 1973; van de Walle 1992). However, the precise meaning and distinctions between these terms are not always clear. Fertility intentions are generally seen as direct precursors to fertility behavior (Ajzen and Klobas 2013; Trent 1980). In contrast, desires, preferences, and ideals are typically viewed as more distant influences (Hin et al. 2011; Miller 1994, 2011; Miller, Severy, and Pasta 2004). Fertility expectations and aspirations are often used as broader terms that can apply to either fertility ideals or intentions (Hayford 2009).
In the United States, the ongoing issue of unintended pregnancies has led to significant research into fertility intentions. While personal fertility ideals are linked to these intentions, they encompass distinct aspects of childbearing, including early aspirations and later adjustments based on actual family size. Fertility intentions are typically viewed as specific plans to have a child, considering the perceived advantages and disadvantages at a particular moment. Research indicates that fertility intentions are not static but rather dynamic, subject to rapid and significant shifts influenced by social contexts. Despite not consistently predicting fertility outcomes at individual or societal levels, fertility intentions do show a strong correlation with the likelihood of giving birth. While the imprecision in predicting fertility from intentions has raised questions about their usefulness, this imperfect correlation does not negate their predictive value. Instead, it offers insights into the obstacles that prevent individuals from achieving their fertility intentions and potential issues with how these intentions are measured.
While demographic surveys frequently inquire about fertility intentions, questions concerning fertility ideals are less prevalent due to conceptual challenges (Philipov and Bernardi 2012). Early attempts to measure ideal family size, such as the 1936 Gallup Poll's question about the "ideal number of children for the average American family," faced criticism for lacking face validity and a clear frame of reference, leaving respondents uncertain about the question's intent (Ryder and Westoff 1971; Blake 1966). Despite these early measurement issues, Philipov and Bernardi (2012) maintain that the concept of fertility ideals remains valuable. Research utilizing measures of fertility ideals offers insights into prevailing social norms (Basten and Gu 2013; Bacci 2001; De Santis and Bacci 2001). Furthermore, these measures contribute to testing theories of individual fertility decision-making (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980; Basten and Gu 2013; Goldstein, Lutz, and Testa 2003; Rindfuss, Morgan, and Swicegood 1988; Schoen et al. 1999) and evaluating theories of fertility decline (Goldstein, Lutz, and Testa 2003; Lesthaeghe and Surkyn 1988; van de Kaa 2001). Crucially, the factors influencing childbearing ideals may also correlate with actual fertility outcomes (Kohler 2001; van de Kaa 2001).
When fertility ideal measures include a specific reference point, they often reflect societal ideal family size. For instance, Eurobarometer surveys frequently ask, "Generally speaking, what do you think is the ideal number of children for a family to have?" This type of general question suggests that the ideal number of children reflects societal norms and the pronatalist stance of a society, rather than individual preferences. Demographers generally consider this measure of ideal children, as an indicator of social norms, to be relatively stable. In the United States, the prevailing two-child ideal norm in recent decades exerts pressure on individuals to conform to this standard.
Whether women personally adopt societal fertility norms remains uncertain. Given that fertility decision-making theories and policy suggestions often highlight individual contributions to broader societal shifts, assessing personal fertility ideals is crucial. For instance, the Eurobarometer surveys inquire, "And for you personally, what would be the ideal number of children you would like to have or would have liked to have had?" This measure of personal ideal family size, linked to fertility desires, is susceptible to shifts influenced by evolving attitudes, cognitive processes, and social contexts, as noted by Philipov and Bernardi (2012). Our current research employs social psychological frameworks, particularly those concerning desires and intentions, to inform our examination of personal fertility ideals.
Three prominent theoretical frameworks explain fertility decision-making: the Traits-Desires-Intentions-Behavior (TDIB) model, the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), and the Cognitive-Social Model (CSM). While compatible, these models differ in their primary focus. Developed by Miller and colleagues, the TDIB model, refined over two decades, outlines a sequential process. It suggests that unconscious motivational predispositions, or traits, regarding having children evolve into conscious desires. These desires then shape conscious intentions, ultimately leading to behaviors aimed at achieving or avoiding childbearing. This model emphasizes the progression from innate motivations to observable actions.
The TDIB model, while focusing on childbearing, doesn't explicitly measure fertility ideals. Within this framework, desires represent a conscious wish for children, bridging innate motivations with conscious intentions and behaviors. This model uniquely emphasizes the role of genetically based motivations in shaping fertility desires.
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) offers a framework for understanding intentional and reasoned actions, and it has been applied to reproductive decision-making. Within the TPB, the decision to have a child is viewed as a product of fertility decision-making influenced by intentions and perceived control. Fertility intentions, in turn, are shaped by three key elements: one's attitude towards having a child, the subjective norm surrounding childbearing, and the perceived control over one's ability to have a child. These three factors are further influenced by beliefs about the advantages and disadvantages of having a child, perceptions of social support for childbearing, and beliefs about factors that might facilitate or hinder the ability to have a child. Ultimately, these behavioral beliefs are rooted in individual, demographic, and societal background factors. According to the TPB, personal fertility ideals are likely to be unstable and are considered part of an individual's background factors. Consequently, the TPB explains the link between a personal ideal number of children and fertility intentions through attitudes and behavioral beliefs, emphasizing the cognitive structures underlying fertility decisions.
The Cognitive-Social Model (CSM) emerged to counter the Theory of Planned Behavior's (TPB) strong focus on deliberate intentions and immediate drivers of fertility choices. Drawing on contemporary cognitive and social science, the CSM proposes that fertility actions stem from both conscious, planned intentions and automatic, emotionally influenced thought processes. According to the CSM, these cognitions originate from social structures, encompassing both tangible elements and schemas. Schemas, defined as enduring abstract interpretations of objects or events, are molded by an individual's background, experiences, and transitions throughout their life. These schemas are dynamic and can be altered by life events such as a friend or family member having a child, shifts in religious observance, changes in employment, evolving media depictions of ideal family sizes, or modifications in social policy. While less detailed regarding the specific components of fertility decision-making compared to other models, the CSM is considered more reflective of how people actually think and behave. Within the CSM framework, fertility ideals can be understood as a type of schema, representing the perceived meanings associated with fertility. The CSM uniquely highlights potential catalysts for shifts in fertility ideals, including evolving attitudes and resources, individual experiences, and life course changes. Consequently, the CSM appears more appropriate for investigating the origins of both stability and change in fertility ideals than the Traits-Desires-Intentions-Behavior (TDIB) and TPB models.
Fertility ideals can differ among women due to several factors. Societal schemas concerning ideal family size, as proposed by Hayford (2009), are considered a significant influence, even within societies that adhere to a two-child norm, as individual schemas can still vary (Rackin and Bachrach 2016). Heiland, Prskawetz, and Sanderson (2008) identify three primary origins for these differences: family background, individual traits, and behavioral tendencies. Experiences during childhood, such as growing up in a larger family, tend to correlate with higher fertility ideals compared to those from smaller families (Axinn, Clarkberg, and Thornton 1994; Dey and Wasoff 2010; Gustavus and Nam 1970; Heiland, Prskawetz, and Sanderson 2008; Johnson and Freymeyer 1989). Consequently, early family experiences can mold one's perception of ideal family size (Basten and Gu 2013; Testa and Grilli 2006). Additionally, community fertility rates can shape these schemas (Goldstein, Lutz, and Testa 2003; Kohler 2001; Testa and Grilli 2006). Parental divorce has also been linked to lower fertility ideals (Axinn and Thornton 1996). While these early life experiences are acknowledged as influential in shaping adult fertility ideals, they were not directly measured in this study's dataset.
Individual characteristics that tend to remain constant also correlate with differences in fertility ideals. Research in the United States suggests that African Americans may hold lower fertility expectations compared to non-Hispanic whites. While findings are not always uniform, a significant body of evidence, primarily from European studies, indicates a link between higher educational attainment and increased fertility ideals. This inconsistency in the education-fertility ideal relationship might stem from variations between contexts with differing fertility rates. Although higher education is generally linked to higher fertility ideals, some research points to a correlation between more prestigious occupations and lower fertility ideals for women. Anticipated involvement in the workforce among women has also been associated with lower fertility ideals. The influence of economic resources and hardship on fertility ideals is less definitive; while higher incomes might elevate ideals, a greater focus on the quality of children could lead to lower ideals. For instance, a Scottish study found that higher income was associated with lower fertility ideals. Furthermore, societal policies designed to ease the tension between work and child-rearing likely play a role in shaping women's fertility ideals.
Research indicates a connection between attitudes, values, and behavioral tendencies and fertility ideals. Theories of fertility suggest that women prioritizing leisure or career achievement tend to have lower fertility preferences. Specifically, preference theory and other ideology-based frameworks emphasize the significance of values in shaping fertility intentions. For instance, one categorization of women identifies "work-focused" individuals as those most likely to hold the lowest fertility ideals, compared to those focused on home or a combination of both.
Religiosity is linked to fertility preferences. Both religious attendance and affiliation correlate with increased fertility desires. Research utilizing European surveys found that higher religiosity scores consistently corresponded with higher fertility ideals, a stronger association than observed with fertility intentions. This influence of religion on fertility ideals is attributed to factors such as religious socialization, institutional norms, enhanced social capital, and the certainty provided by religious beliefs.
Changes in fertility intentions are likely to correlate with shifts in fertility ideals. Research by Heiland, Prskawetz, and Sanderson (2008) indicated that women adhering to the standard two-child ideal were less prone to altering their fertility ideals compared to those with higher or lower ideals. Our inclusion of fertility intentions in this study aims to determine if intentions and ideals represent overlapping processes or distinct aspects of fertility experiences. Existing studies suggest a link between a greater emphasis on motherhood and higher fertility intentions (McQuillan et al. 2015), implying a similar connection with fertility ideals. Consequently, we have incorporated measures for initial intentions and alterations in the importance of motherhood.
Life course theory and empirical evidence indicate that life events and social cues, such as marriage, the number of children, or reaching a specific age, influence fertility preferences. According to Ní Bhrolcháin and Beaujouan’s Preference Construction Theory, individuals don't possess fixed fertility preferences. Instead, they construct these preferences in response to current schemas and circumstances when asked. This perspective suggests that fertility ideals are dynamic states, not immutable traits, and are therefore prone to change.
Fertility preferences are influenced by social networks and relationships within couples. Consequently, factors such as a partner's or parents' desire for children, and whether friends have children, are expected to correlate with fertility ideals. The number of children a woman currently has, known as parity, is also linked to higher fertility ideals. Research indicates that age negatively impacts fertility ideals, with younger individuals' higher ideals often decreasing as they age. Furthermore, studies have observed that individuals desiring zero children are more likely to remain childless.
Religion may influence shifts in fertility ideals. For instance, Catholic women have shown a greater propensity to alter their fertility desires compared to women of other faiths. Employment also correlates with reduced fertility desires, and periods of unemployment have been linked to a decline in fertility ideals. Furthermore, a European study indicated that while enrollment in education was associated with lower fertility ideals for women, it was linked to higher ideals for men.
This study examines personal fertility ideals among US women over a three-year period, focusing on both their initial levels and any changes that occur. Moving beyond the assumption that fertility ideals merely reflect stable societal norms (Naboodiri 1983), we analyze the distribution and shifts in these personal ideals. Our research investigates how individual characteristics, attitudes, and life course factors relate to both initial fertility ideals and subsequent changes. We developed five sets of expectations grounded in previous research and the Cognitive-Social Model (CSM). Firstly, we anticipated that women with backgrounds linked to higher fertility rates, such as religiosity and having friends with children, would report higher fertility ideals and that increases in these factors would correlate with rising ideals. Secondly, we hypothesized that perceptions of parental or partner desire for children would be associated with higher ideals or a reduced likelihood of decreasing them. Thirdly, we expected that higher education, full-time employment, and a strong emphasis on leisure and work would lead to a greater tendency to lower personal fertility ideals. Fourthly, we predicted that increases in parity or the importance of motherhood would correlate with a higher probability of increasing fertility ideals. Lastly, we posited that greater economic hardship would be associated with a decrease in fertility ideals.
The National Survey of Fertility Barriers (NSFB) provided the data for this study, utilizing two waves of interviews with 4,712 women aged 25–45. Wave 1 interviews took place between 2004 and 2006, followed by Wave 2 interviews approximately three years later, from 2008 to 2010. The survey employed a random-digit-dialing methodology. To ensure representation, areas with high minority populations were oversampled, necessitating the use of weighted analyses. Institutional Review Board approval was secured for the data collection. Further details and access to the data files are available through the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR).
The study achieved a 53% response rate for the initial screener and 37% for the completed survey. While these rates are relatively low, the sample generally reflects other random-digit-dialing telephone surveys from the era and, when compared to Census data, is largely representative of the population. A slight overrepresentation of women with higher education was noted. Of the 2,136 women who participated in Wave 2 (58% of Wave 1 participants), 158 declined the second interview. The remaining participants were difficult to recontact due to factors like the rise of cell-only households and increased mobility during the 2007 economic crisis. The final analytical sample comprises 879 women who completed interviews at both Wave 1 and Wave 2, provided information on their fertility ideals, and remained in the same heterosexual relationship throughout the study period.
The primary focus of our analysis is the personal fertility ideal, measured using a question identical to one employed in the 1995 National Survey of Fertility Growth. This question, posed as: "The next question asks about the number of children you consider ideal for yourself. This could be more or less than you already have or more or less than you expect to have. If you yourself could choose exactly the number of children to have in your whole life, how many would you choose?" allowed participants to specify any number from zero upwards. This crucial question was positioned early in the survey, around question 12 out of approximately 100, following inquiries about participants' pregnancy history, desires, and intentions. Specifically, desire for a child, including a partner's desire if applicable, was assessed in question 10. Fertility intentions were then explored in question 11, which asked about actual plans to have a baby. We opted not to include both desires and intentions in the model simultaneously due to their high correlation (r = .770), which could lead to multicollinearity issues. While fertility ideals showed only modest correlations with fertility desires (r = .238) and fertility intentions (r = .122), research by Schwarz and Strack (1990) suggests that placing questions on related concepts in close proximity within a survey aids participants in distinguishing between them. This supports our argument that personal fertility ideals can be considered distinct from desires and intentions. The data from Wave 1 on personal fertility ideals were also incorporated into our analyses. To mitigate potential floor and ceiling effects, the personal fertility ideal variable was categorized into three groups: 0–1 child, 2 children, and 3 or more children. The category of two children served as the reference group for our statistical models.
Education was measured as a continuous variable, representing years of schooling from 4 to 22. Consistent with the methodologies of Johnson (2005) and Su (2012), variables that varied over time were captured by two distinct measures: their value at Wave 1 and a change score calculated as the difference between Wave 2 and Wave 1. Employment status was categorized, with full-time employment defined as working over 35 hours per week, and part-time employment as working less than 35 hours per week. These groups were compared against those who were unemployed. Specifically, full-time employed individuals were assigned a value of 3, part-time employed a value of 2, and unemployed individuals a value of 1. Changes in employment status were quantified by subtracting the Wave 1 employment code from the Wave 2 code. Economic hardship was assessed using a composite scale derived from the average responses to three questions concerning difficulties in paying bills, affording food and clothing, and covering medical expenses over the preceding 12 months. Response options ranged from "never" to "very often," with higher scores indicating greater hardship. This scale demonstrated unidimensionality and strong internal reliability (α = .82).
The study measured several attitudinal and belief-based variables to understand their influence on fertility ideals. The importance of leisure was assessed by asking participants about the significance of having free time for personal interests, with responses ranging from "not important" to "very important." Similarly, the value placed on career success was gauged by a question about the importance of achieving in one's profession. Religiosity was captured through a four-item scale encompassing frequency of religious service attendance, prayer habits, perceived closeness to God, and the influence of religious beliefs on daily life. These measures were standardized and averaged to form a single religiosity scale, demonstrating high internal reliability (α = .78). Fertility intentions were assessed using two questions. The first asked directly about the intention to have a baby, while the second probed the certainty of these intentions, acknowledging that plans can change. Responses were coded on a scale from -2 ("very sure do not intend") to +2 ("very sure do intend"). Participants who expressed uncertainty, inability to have children, or a belief that fate would decide were assigned a neutral score of 0. The importance of motherhood was measured using five items. Four items, ranging from "strongly agree" to "strongly disagree," explored feelings of completeness as a woman, prior expectations of parenthood, the role of children in life fulfillment, and the general importance of having children. A fifth item assessed the importance of raising children in one's life, with responses from "not very important" to "very important." These items were combined into a single scale, also showing high reliability (α = .86), where higher scores indicated a greater importance of motherhood.
Parental and partner desires for children were assessed. Specifically, the statement "It is important to my parents that I have children" and "It is important to my partner that we have children" were used, with responses ranging up to "very important." The presence of children among one's social network was gauged by asking respondents to estimate the proportion of their friends and family who have children. Changes in parity were determined by analyzing reproductive histories, which tracked live births. This data was then categorized into dummy variables representing consistent childlessness, having always one child, always three or more children, becoming a parent for the first time, or adding another child to an existing family. Women with two children at both survey waves served as the reference group. Age was recorded in years.
Assessing individual changes in personal fertility ideals typically involves using a change score, calculated by subtracting Wave 1 scores from Wave 2 scores, as the dependent variable in an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. However, OLS regression primarily focuses on average trends and can fail to identify changes when individuals' ideals move in opposite directions. This can lead to an incorrect conclusion of no change when, in reality, individuals are adjusting their ideals, some upwards and others downwards. To address this limitation, we categorized change scores into three distinct groups: decreased, stable, or increased personal ideal number of children. Subsequently, we employed multinomial logistic regression to examine the associations between increases or decreases in fertility ideals and stability in these ideals. It's important to note that using conditional change scores as independent variables can introduce bias if the correlation between the initial measurement of an independent variable and its change score is not accounted for. Therefore, our analysis incorporated both initial and change score versions of the independent variables. Additionally, we included indicator variables for initial low (0-1) or high (3+) personal fertility ideals, relative to the normative ideal of two children. This allowed us to determine if the initial fertility ideal influenced the probability of increasing, decreasing, or maintaining a stable ideal. Similar to fixed effects and change score models, our multinomial logistic regression utilized two waves of data, thereby mitigating concerns about measurement error and omitted variables.
Analysis of personal fertility ideals revealed that 31% of women adjusted their ideal number of children between survey waves. Among those who changed their ideals, approximately half increased their desired number of children, while the other half decreased it. To account for these shifts, a three-category dependent variable was created, distinguishing between decreased, stable, and increased fertility ideals. Multinomial logistic regression was then employed to examine how individual characteristics, attitudes, life course factors, and social cues were associated with these decreases or increases, relative to maintaining stable ideals.

Despite 31% of women altering their fertility ideals, the average ideal number remained nearly constant between waves (2.59 in Wave 1 and 2.60 in Wave 2). This stability is partly due to the equal proportions of those who changed their ideals increasing and decreasing them. The study sample comprised women in stable heterosexual relationships across both survey periods. These women generally had high levels of education (mean 15.80 years), were mostly employed full-time, and experienced low economic hardship with minimal change over time.
Leisure and career importance remained high and stable for most women. Religiosity showed minimal average change over the study period. Fertility intentions were initially low and tended to decrease on average. The importance of motherhood was generally above average, with little change observed. Perceptions of partners' and parents' desires for children, along with the proportion of friends with children, were high, suggesting a pronatalist environment. Regarding parity, approximately 20% of women remained childless, while nearly 40% had two or more children throughout the study. Over a quarter of the women had a child between the two survey waves, with about a third of these experiencing childbirth for the first time.

Women with higher initial fertility ideals were more likely to decrease them and less likely to increase them compared to maintaining stability. Increased education was linked to a lower probability of increasing ideals, while greater initial economic hardship meant a lower chance of decreasing them. Full-time or part-time employment at Wave 1 also increased the likelihood of decreasing ideals compared to being unemployed.
Changes in fertility ideals were linked to three attitudinal measures. Women with higher initial religiosity, fertility intentions, or importance of motherhood were less likely to decrease their ideals compared to maintaining them. Likewise, increases in fertility intentions and the importance of motherhood were associated with a reduced likelihood of decreasing ideals. Notably, a rise in the importance of motherhood correlated with a lower probability of increasing ideals relative to maintaining stability.
Parity and age were the only life course and social cue measures linked to shifts in fertility ideals. Women with one child at both survey points were less likely to increase their ideal number of children compared to those with two. Similarly, those with three or more children at both waves showed a reduced likelihood of decreasing their ideal. Adding a child also lowered the odds of decreasing one's ideal, relative to having two children consistently. Furthermore, older women were less prone to decreasing their fertility ideals than maintaining them.
Over a three-year period, most women in stable heterosexual relationships maintained their personal fertility ideals (69%). However, a significant 31% adjusted their ideals, with roughly half increasing their desired number of children and the other half decreasing it. This demonstrates that personal fertility ideals are not static for a substantial portion of women, changing in both upward and downward directions. Such nuanced change patterns are difficult to capture with traditional OLS regression methods.
Our study began with five hypotheses regarding how independent variables would relate to shifts in fertility ideals. We initially hypothesized that women with backgrounds linked to higher fertility rates, such as greater religiosity or having friends with children, would hold higher fertility ideals and that increases in these factors would lead to higher ideals. While higher initial religiosity did correlate with a reduced likelihood of decreasing fertility ideals, changes in religiosity did not show a significant association with changes in ideals. Similarly, shifts in the number of friends with children did not impact fertility ideals, thus failing to support the notion that changes in friends' fertility experiences influence personal ideals. However, initial religiosity did appear to be linked to the stability of personal fertility ideals. The three-year timeframe of our study might have been too short for changes in social networks or religiosity to manifest their influence on fertility ideals.
We had anticipated that women who believed their parents or partners desired them to have children would hold higher fertility ideals and be less prone to reducing them. However, our findings did not support this expectation, as the perception of parental or partner desire for children was not linked to changes in fertility ideals. This outcome diverges from predictions made by the social cognitive model of fertility intentions. The discrepancy might stem from the limited three-year timeframe of our study, or it could be that individuals are most receptive to such social influences earlier in life. Further research could explore this by incorporating data on family background, such as sibling count and parental divorce, and by tracking participants over longer periods.
We anticipated that women with higher education, full-time employment, and a strong emphasis on leisure and career would be more prone to lowering their personal fertility ideals. Our findings offered partial confirmation of this expectation. Specifically, increased education correlated with a reduced likelihood of increasing fertility ideals. Furthermore, full-time employment at the outset of the study was linked to a greater probability of decreasing personal fertility ideals. Interestingly, the importance placed on leisure and career did not directly correlate with a decrease in fertility ideals. This suggests that practical time constraints, rather than the perceived importance of these activities, may drive downward adjustments in fertility ideals or reduce the likelihood of upward adjustments. It appears individuals are responding to the demands of their schedules, with their established preferences for career and leisure influencing their initial fertility ideals.
We anticipated that having more children or placing greater importance on motherhood would lead to an increased likelihood of raising one's fertility ideals. This expectation was largely supported by the data, as both parity and the importance of motherhood were indeed associated with changes in fertility ideals. While we initially focused on changes in parity, our findings revealed that women with one child at both survey waves were less prone to increasing their fertility ideals. Similarly, those with three or more children at both waves showed a reduced likelihood of decreasing their ideals. Notably, the addition of a child, but not the birth of a first child, was linked to a lower probability of decreasing one's fertility ideals.
The association between a higher importance of motherhood and fertility ideals was uncertain. This is because women who highly value motherhood might desire more children, or conversely, prefer to focus more resources on a smaller number of children. Our study found that women with a higher initial importance of motherhood were less likely to decrease their fertility ideals compared to maintaining them. Similarly, those who reported an increase in the importance of motherhood were less likely to decrease or increase their ideals, tending instead to remain stable. This outcome suggests a potential interplay of conflicting processes. One possibility is that an increased emphasis on motherhood could lead women to reduce their ideal number of children, opting to invest more resources per child rather than aiming for a larger family. Alternatively, a heightened sense of motherhood could foster a desire for more children to fulfill these intensified maternal aspirations.
Contrary to our expectation that increased economic hardship would lead to lower fertility ideals, we found no association between changes in economic hardship and shifts in fertility ideals. This stability can be understood in light of existing research showing mixed results regarding the relationship between economic hardship, income, and fertility. However, we did observe that women experiencing greater initial economic hardship were less likely to decrease their fertility ideals, tending instead to maintain them. This persistence might stem from findings suggesting that, within lower socioeconomic strata, children can contribute to a sense of achievement and success.
This study's findings are subject to several limitations. Primarily, the analysis of change in personal fertility ideals was confined to a relatively short three-year period, utilizing only two waves of data. The optimal timeframe for observing such shifts remains undetermined, and ideally, longitudinal data tracking women throughout their reproductive lives would offer a more comprehensive understanding of fertility ideal stability and revision. Moreover, the limited fluctuation in some independent variables over the three-year interval precluded a definitive assessment of their impact on ideal changes. The study also lacked data on crucial background characteristics, such as the number of siblings, community norms, and alterations in relationship status, which prior research indicates can influence fertility ideals. Additionally, the possibility of social desirability bias exists, as reporting a fertility ideal lower than one's current number of children might be perceived as taboo; however, this was observed in less than 3% of participants. Comparing the findings to existing literature is also challenging, as much previous research on fertility ideals has focused on European contexts. Despite these limitations, this study contributes valuable insights into fertility ideals within the U.S. context. Future research could enhance understanding by including male perspectives and conducting couple-level analyses to explore gender's role in fertility ideals.
Despite the study's limitations, the research highlights the significance of investigating personal fertility ideals. The findings demonstrate that these ideals are not static for all women, are distinct from desires and intentions, and are influenced by various factors. While many in the U.S. adhere to a two-child norm, societal challenges in supporting working parents may hinder the achievement of personal fertility goals. Future research should broaden its scope by including younger women, men, and examining partner influences on personal fertility ideals. Additionally, comparing individuals who achieve their fertility ideals with those who do not, in terms of psychosocial outcomes, would be valuable. The study's insights can refine theories of fertility behavior and inform policies aimed at ensuring children are wanted and that individuals can meet their fertility aspirations. Internationally, further investigation is needed to understand how fertility ideal malleability varies across different societal contexts. In countries with very low fertility rates, like Japan and Germany, or high rates, like some sub-Saharan African nations, the adaptability of an individual's ideal number of children could be a key area for policy intervention.
