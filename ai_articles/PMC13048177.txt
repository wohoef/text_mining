This study investigated the efficacy of traditional Chinese acupuncture for postpartum depression (PPD), a mood disorder whose treatment with acupuncture has remained largely unexamined. Acupuncture, a traditional Chinese medicine practice involving the insertion of thin needles into the body, is typically used for pain management. To assess its role in PPD, a comprehensive meta-analysis was conducted. The research team systematically searched major databases including PUBMED, EMBASE, and Cochrane Controlled Trials Register for studies published up to September 2018. Data analysis was performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis 2.0 software, calculating mean differences (MD) and risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for comparative evaluation. The meta-analysis incorporated data from 12 randomized controlled trials, involving a total of 887 PPD patients. Of these, 443 received acupuncture treatment, while 444 were in control groups. Results indicated that patients receiving acupuncture showed significantly improved outcomes on the Hamilton Depression Scale compared to controls, with a pooled MD of -1.27 (95% CI: -2.55 to 0.01; p = 0.05, I2 = 83%) under a random-effect model. Furthermore, the acupuncture group demonstrated a significantly higher effective rate, reflected by a pooled RR of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.09 to 1.33; p < 0.0001, I2 = 46%). However, when analyzing acupuncture therapy in isolation, only the effective rate showed a significant improvement. In conclusion, while traditional Chinese acupuncture appears to alleviate some PPD symptoms, the current evidence is not definitive. The study highlights the need for additional research, adhering to standardized guidelines and minimizing bias, to conclusively establish acupuncture's effectiveness in treating PPD.

Ancient Chinese medical experiences and the Yin-Yang and Five Elements theories form the basis of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). Its unique system is characterized by an overall concept and the differentiation of symptoms and signs, summarizing human life and disease changes.
Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) utilizes acupuncture as a treatment method. This therapy encompasses two main components: needle manipulation and moxibustion. Needle manipulation, often underestimated, involves inserting thin needles, like filiform needles, into specific points on the patient's body at a precise angle, guided by TCM principles. Techniques such as twisting, lifting, and inserting are then applied to stimulate these "acupoints" to treat various ailments. Moxibustion, conversely, is a heat-based therapy. It involves burning dried plant materials, known as "moxa," on the surface of certain acupoints. This process aims to enhance the body's Qi flow and eliminate harmful influences.
Postpartum depression (PPD) manifests as clear depressive symptoms or episodes in women during the postpartum period, potentially leading to conditions like puerperal psychiatric syndrome, restlessness, or psychosis. This condition affects 15% to 30% of new mothers. While typical PPD usually appears within six weeks of childbirth and often resolves naturally within three to six months, severe cases can persist for one to two years. For mothers experiencing a second pregnancy, the recurrence rate of PPD is between 20% and 30%. Clinically, PPD shares characteristics with other depressive episodes. Current diagnostic tools include the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale (EPDS) and the Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD). Standard treatments for PPD involve psychological intervention, medication, and physiotherapy. Although acupuncture has gained attention in Western medicine for its effectiveness in managing various conditions like postoperative pain, pregnancy-related nausea, chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, and toothache with minimal side effects, its utility in treating PPD is not yet established. This study therefore sought to determine if acupuncture offers benefits in alleviating depression in PPD patients compared to existing therapies, potentially introducing a valuable new treatment option.
The study adhered to the PRISMA statement for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
To identify relevant clinical studies on acupuncture for PPD, a comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure. This search, limited to English and Chinese publications before December 2018, utilized keywords such as "acupuncture," "moxibustion," "TCM," and "PPD." Additional references were also reviewed to ensure thoroughness.
To be included in this study, research had to meet specific eligibility criteria. First, all participants needed to have a confirmed diagnosis of Postpartum Depression (PPD). Given the lack of a single diagnostic standard for PPD, most studies employed a two-step process: initial screening with scales followed by formal diagnosis. Second, the treatment groups could involve various acupuncture techniques, including manual or electro-acupuncture, potentially combined with other therapies. Control groups, conversely, received placebos or other Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) interventions like herbs, drugs, or psychological support. Third, only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were considered, requiring a minimum of 30 cases and comparable baseline characteristics between the acupuncture and control groups. Finally, in instances of duplicate publications, the most recent or highest quality study from the same research team was chosen.
Studies were excluded if their full text or extractable data were unavailable. Non-randomized controlled trials, reviews, editorials, letters, comments, practice guidelines, and case reports were also not included.
To ensure accuracy, two independent reviewers meticulously screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of all identified publications. Any potential biases in data extraction and evaluation were rigorously cross-checked. Disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third party. When crucial data were missing, the original authors were contacted via email or telephone for clarification. The extracted information encompassed several key areas: fundamental publication details like author, year, sample size, and participant age; baseline characteristics and intervention methods for the subjects; study designs and critical aspects of bias analysis; and finally, outcome indicators along with their corresponding measurement data. The quality of each included study was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s "Risk of Bias" tool, as outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Intervention.
For each study, we extracted data on authors, countries, designs, sample sizes, interventions, treatment durations, and outcome measures.
Postpartum Depression (PPD) levels were quantitatively assessed using two main scales: the Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) and the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale (EPDS). The HAMD, a widely utilized clinical tool, evaluates depressive states through 24 items, each scored across five grades. The EPDS, a 10-item self-rating scale, is commonly employed for screening in primary healthcare, typically administered six weeks postpartum to identify depressive disorders. These diagnostic methods largely align with criteria from the American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders.
Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (Version 2.0) was employed to analyze effect values and conduct heterogeneity tests. Initial assessment of heterogeneity involved two tests, with a significance level (a) set at 0.1. Studies were considered homogeneous if p > 0.1 and I2 < 50%, in which case a fixed-effect model was applied for meta-analysis. If heterogeneity was apparent among studies, a random-effect model was utilized for meta-analysis, following the exclusion of significant heterogeneity's impact. For studies exhibiting substantial heterogeneity, further subgroup, sensitivity, or descriptive analyses were conducted. The combined statistics for continuous variables included weighted mean difference (MD), risk ratio (RR), and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI). The meta-analysis's test significance level (α) was established at 0.05. During sensitivity analysis, results from heterogeneity tests where p < 0.10 and I2 > 50% were examined by converting between fixed and random effect models.
Our initial database search, supplemented by seven manual retrievals, yielded 298 publications. After removing 163 duplicates, 142 unique titles and abstracts were screened, leading to the exclusion of 102 irrelevant or incomplete records. The remaining 40 full-text articles were then reviewed, with 26 failing to meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 14 studies were deemed suitable for inclusion in the meta-analysis.
The meta-analysis incorporated data from 14 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published between 2004 and 2015, involving a total of 934 PPD patients. Of these, 465 patients received treatment, while 469 were in the control group. Treatment interventions for PPD patients in the study group included acupuncture or electroacupuncture, often combined with other drug therapies and psychological treatments. Conversely, the control group received antidepressant drugs alongside other symptomatic and supportive therapies. The duration of these treatments varied across the RCTs, ranging from 4 to 12 weeks. Patient outcomes were assessed using the HAMD and EPDS scales, in addition to evaluating the effective outcome rate and estradiol levels.
Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration's "Risk of Bias" tool, detailed in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Intervention. This evaluation considered factors such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, personnel, and outcome assessors, incomplete data, selective reporting, and other potential biases. Notably, blinding was not implemented in any of the included studies. Overall, the quality of all studies was rated as medium to high.
Acupuncture demonstrated a notable improvement in Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) scores compared to control groups, with a pooled mean difference (MD) of -1.27 (95% CI: -2.55 to 0.01; p = 0.05, I2 = 83%) using a random-effect model. Conversely, the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale (EPDS) was utilized in only two randomized controlled trials, revealing a non-significant trend between the acupuncture and control groups (MD = -0.49; 95% CI: -1.01 to 0.02; p = 0.06). Furthermore, acupuncture significantly elevated estradiol levels, showing an MD of 63.99 (95% CI: 13.47 to 114.51; p = 0.01, I2 = 98) when compared to controls.
The study also examined the effective rate, defined as recovery to normal mental status from depressive outcomes. Acupuncture demonstrated a significantly superior effective rate compared to control groups, with a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.09 to 1.33; p < 0.0001, I2 = 46%) in the random-effect model (Fig. 3A). An asymmetric funnel plot (Fig. 3B) indicated a lower publication bias for these effective rate outcomes.
Our analysis specifically isolated the effects of acupuncture therapy, excluding studies where it was combined with other treatments like psychological intervention or traditional Chinese medicine. This subgroup analysis, encompassing eight randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (Table 1) that utilized either acupuncture or electroacupuncture exclusively, revealed mixed results. For the Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), acupuncture alone did not demonstrate a significant difference between the treatment and control groups (MD = -0.11, 95% CI: -1.09, 0.87; p = 0.83, I2 = 74%) (Figure 4A). Similarly, data from the single RCT reporting on the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale (EPDS) for acupuncture alone showed no discernible benefit (MD = -0.30, 95% CI: -4.64, 4.04; p = 0.89, I2 = 0%) (Figure 4B). Furthermore, no data on estradiol levels were available for this specific subgroup. However, when evaluating the effective rate, the acupuncture-only subgroup did show a significantly better performance, with a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 1.17 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.33; p = 0.01, I2 = 34%) (Figure 4C).
Postpartum depression (PPD) arises from a complex interplay of factors, posing significant risks to the mother, infant, family, and society. Its clinical presentation typically includes depression, anxiety, fear, irritability, low self-confidence, and hopelessness, with severe cases potentially involving suicidal ideation. Risk factors for PPD encompass psychological, genetic, and social elements. Affecting 15% to 30% of new mothers, PPD usually manifests within six weeks postpartum. While it often resolves spontaneously within three to six months, severe cases can persist for one to two years. Furthermore, the recurrence rate of PPD in subsequent pregnancies is between 20% and 30%.
Current PPD treatments, including psychotherapy, routine therapy, drug therapy, physical therapy, and electroconvulsive therapy, often lack efficacy and carry numerous side effects. This highlights the critical need for safer and more effective interventions. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) views PPD as linked to emotional and visceral imbalances. Acupuncture, a TCM approach originating in China, addresses this by stimulating specific acupoints. This stimulation is believed to enhance motor cortex signals and increase central nervous system activity, potentially alleviating depressive states. Our systematic review and meta-analysis support acupuncture's safety and effectiveness for PPD, evidenced by improvements in HAMD and EPDS scores, as well as treatment effective rates.
For moderate postpartum depression (PPD), drug therapy is the primary approach, utilizing first-line antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and 5-hydroxytryptamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, among others with varied mechanisms. While these conventional drugs can alleviate depressive states, their prolonged use may lead to drug resistance. Acupuncture offers a beneficial alternative or adjunct, demonstrating superiority when combined with conventional drug therapy. Its mechanism involves stimulating both the skin and subcutaneous muscle layers, which improves local blood circulation and tissue metabolism. Furthermore, acupuncture contributes to sedation and antidepressant effects by promoting the diffusion of inhibitory functions within the cerebral cortex, aligning with the treatment objectives for PPD.
Acupuncture's mechanism for alleviating PPD is explained by several theories. One perspective, the neuroendocrine disorder theory, suggests that acupuncture's antidepressant effects and improvement of depressive symptoms occur via its influence on the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal and hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid axes. Furthermore, the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonad axis theory proposes that acupuncture regulates PPD by enhancing sex hormone levels, a notion supported by this meta-analysis's findings on estradiol levels. Beyond these, other theories, such as neurotrophic and nerve regeneration dysfunction, hippocampal neuron alteration, and cell signal transduction, also contribute to understanding how acupuncture improves depressive symptoms. The immune function changes theory posits that proinflammatory cytokines like IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, TNFα, and interferon can trigger psychiatric symptoms including apathy and depression. Studies have shown that elevated serum levels of IL-2, IL-6, and TNFα in depressed rats decreased significantly after acupuncture at specific acupoints. Additionally, research indicates that combining acupuncture with medicine can reduce the overexpression of regulatory T cells, thereby improving depressive symptoms and the immunosuppressive state in depressed mice.
Several limitations were identified in this meta-analysis. The included studies often had a small patient count and did not extensively explore the effectiveness of combined therapies. Significant heterogeneity was also observed across the various documents. While this systematic review indicates that acupuncture appears safe and effective for treating PPD, the overall evidence remains uncertain. Consequently, more rigorous studies adhering to standardized guidelines and minimizing bias are essential to definitively confirm acupuncture's efficacy in PPD treatment.
