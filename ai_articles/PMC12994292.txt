Apatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2), has demonstrated promising therapeutic outcomes across various cancers. However, its specific role in ovarian cancer remained largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate apatinib's impact on the viability and proliferation of ovarian cancer cells, both in vitro and in vivo, and to assess its influence on cellular glucose metabolism. To achieve these objectives, a range of experimental techniques were employed. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and colony formation assays were utilized to evaluate the effects of apatinib on ovarian cancer cell viability and proliferation. Western blotting was performed to analyze the expression levels of proteins within the VEGFR2/AKT1/SOX5/GLUT4 pathway. Glycolysis was assessed through glucose uptake and lactate production assays. The role of SOX5 was further examined by its exogenous overexpression and silencing using expression vectors and shRNA, respectively. RNA expression analyses were conducted using RNA-seq and gene-chip-based methods. Additionally, GLUT4 promoter activity was investigated using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. In xenograft models, the expression of p-VEGFR2 (Tyr1175), p-AKT1 (Ser473), p-GSK3β (Ser9), SOX5, and GLUT4 in tumor tissues was determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC). The results revealed that apatinib significantly inhibited the viability and proliferation of Hey and OVCA433 ovarian cancer cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner, both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, apatinib effectively reduced glucose uptake and lactate production by downregulating GLUT4 expression. SOX5 was found to largely restore the inhibitory effect of apatinib on GLUT4 expression by activating its promoter. Mechanistically, apatinib was observed to regulate SOX5 expression through the suppression of the VEGFR2/AKT1/GSK3β signaling pathway. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that apatinib effectively suppresses the in vitro and in vivo viability and proliferation of ovarian cancer cells, while also inhibiting glycolysis. This is achieved by modulating the VEGFR2/AKT1/GSK3β/SOX5/GLUT4 signaling pathway. These findings suggest that apatinib holds potential as a promising therapeutic agent for ovarian cancer treatment.

Ovarian cancer ranks as the seventh most prevalent cancer globally and is the primary cause of mortality among gynecological malignancies. A significant majority, approximately 70%, are diagnosed at advanced stages, and a substantial proportion (75–85%) experience relapse despite treatment. Chemoresistance often develops following recurrent disease, leading to a 5-year survival rate below 50%. Consequently, there is a critical demand for innovative therapeutic strategies and the discovery of drugs targeting key molecular pathways.
The critical role of angiogenesis in tumor growth and metastasis necessitates the exploration of anti-angiogenic drugs for patients who have exhausted other chemotherapy options. Medications such as bevacizumab, ramucirumab, and apatinib fall into this category. Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2), primarily found on endothelial cells, is a key mediator of angiogenesis in various solid tumors. Evidence suggests that elevated VEGFR2 expression within tumor cells themselves may correlate with increased invasiveness and poorer clinical outcomes. Apatinib, a novel receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor, exhibits high specificity for VEGFR2. This orally administered drug has demonstrated promising anti-tumor efficacy with a manageable side effect profile. Clinical trials have indicated its potential to significantly improve survival in patients with advanced gastric cancer and benefit those with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer. Despite these findings, research into the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of apatinib in ovarian cancer remains limited. Nevertheless, case reports and phase II studies suggest its potential as a treatment option for chemotherapy-refractory, recurrent, or platinum-resistant epithelial ovarian cancer, with some studies exploring its combination with etoposide for platinum-refractory cases.
SOX5, a member of the SOX protein family, functions as a transcription factor crucial for embryonic development and cell fate. Its involvement extends to various cancers, where it has been observed to inhibit PDGFB-induced glioma development. Evidence suggests SOX5 can act as an oncogenic factor, promoting migration and proliferation in non-small cell lung cancer by interacting with YAP1. Additionally, SOX5 has been linked to enhanced glucose metabolism in individuals with type 2 diabetes.
This study investigated apatinib's impact on ovarian cancer cells. We observed that apatinib inhibits cell proliferation by influencing glucose metabolism. Furthermore, the drug suppresses this metabolic process through the VEGFR2/AKT1/GSK3β/SOX5 signaling pathway. These findings suggest apatinib holds potential as a therapeutic agent for ovarian cancer.
Human ovarian cancer cell lines, including Hey and OVCA433, along with a HUVEC line, were obtained from ATCC. These cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with fetal bovine serum, penicillin, and streptomycin. Incubation occurred at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 to maintain optimal growth conditions for subsequent experiments.
Apatinib, sourced from MCE (MedChemExpress), was prepared as a 10 mM solution by dissolving 10 mg of powder in 2.026 ml DMSO, stored at 4 °C. Ovarian cell cultures were subsequently treated with varying concentrations (μmol/L) of apatinib for 48 hours.
To assess cell viability, 5 × 10³ ovarian cancer cells were plated in 96-well plates with 100 μl of culture medium per well. Following an overnight incubation, the cells received varying concentrations of apatinib. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was then employed to measure cell viability at 24, 48, and 72 hours. Viable cell numbers were determined by absorbance readings at 450 nm after a two-hour incubation, using a Microplate Reader. The viability rate was subsequently calculated by comparing experimental optical density values to control values.
To manipulate gene expression, specific plasmids encoding SOX5, AKT1, and VEGFA were acquired. SOX5 expression was reduced using shRNAs, while VEGFR2 and AKT1 were silenced with siRNAs. Hey and OVCA433 cells were transfected with these constructs or control vectors using a liposomal reagent, following the manufacturer's guidelines.
To analyze gene expression, total RNA was extracted from Hey and OVCA433 cells using Trizol reagent. This RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix. Real-time PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems Prism 7900 system with TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II. The amplification protocol involved an initial 30-second denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 5-second denaturation at 95 °C, 30-second annealing at 55 °C, and 30-second extension at 72 °C. Melting curve analysis was conducted to confirm amplification specificity. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, with Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphatedehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the internal control. Final analyses were based on the 2-ΔΔCT relative quantification method across three independent experiments. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.
To assess glucose uptake, cells were pretreated for 24 hours, then seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Following this, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Glucose uptake was then quantified using a Glucose Uptake Colorimetric Assay kit from BioVision, following the manufacturer's instructions. For lactate measurement, cells were cultured in phenol red-free DMEM (Hyclone) for 15 hours. The collected culture medium was then used to quantify lactate levels with a Lactate Colorimetric Assay kit (BioVision). These lactate concentrations were subsequently normalized against the total protein level, determined using a BCA protein assay kit from Beyotime Biotechnology.
To assess protein expression levels, Western blot analyses were performed. Ovarian cancer cells were first treated with varying concentrations of apatinib or its diluent, DMSO, for 48 hours. Following treatment, cells were collected, washed with phosphate-buffered saline, and then lysed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. Subsequently, 24 μg of each protein sample underwent SDS-PAGE and transfer to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescent reagent. Antibodies targeting LDHA, HK2, SOX5, VEGFA, AKT1, and β-Actin were sourced from Proteintech. Antibodies for VEGFR2, p-AKT1(Ser473), p-GSK3β(Ser9), and p-VEGFR2(Tyr1175) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, while the GLUT4 antibody was from Signalway Antibody.
Following 48-hour treatment with apatinib or DMSO, cells were seeded at 500 cells per well in 6-well plates. After culturing in fresh medium for at least one week, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, they were stained with Crystal violet and the resulting colonies were counted.
To analyze gene expression profiles, total RNA was extracted from Hey cells. This RNA was then processed using VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads to enrich for polyA+ RNA, a crucial step before constructing RNA libraries. The library preparation utilized the VAHTS mRNA-seq v2 Library Prep kit from Illumina. Sequencing was conducted in a paired-end format on an Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform by RiboBio Co., Ltd. Computational analysis of the resulting RNA-seq data involved aligning sequencing reads with the HISAT2 spliced read aligner, using the Ensembl human genome assembly (GRCh38) as the reference. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads). Functional gene annotation was achieved through Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).
To investigate SOX5's regulation of GLUT4 transcription, the human GLUT4 gene promoter was cloned into a pGL3 basic vector. This construct, along with a Renilla luciferase control plasmid, was then transfected into ovarian cancer cells where SOX5 was either overexpressed or silenced. Following a 48-hour incubation, luciferase activity was measured. Reporter activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase and then rescaled relative to vector control signals.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on xenograft tumor sections, following previously established protocols [23, 24]. This analysis utilized antibodies targeting p-VEGFR2(Tyr1175), p-AKT1(Ser473), p-GSK3β(Ser9), CD31, GLUT4, and SOX5. Specific antibody details, including manufacturer and dilution, are provided. Staining intensity and the proportion of positive tumor cells were used to score the IHC results. A scoring system was employed where negative expression was defined as less than 2+, and positive expression ranged from greater than 2+ to less than 6+.
Prior to commencing animal studies, approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee at FUSCC. Female BALB/c nude mice, aged 4–6 weeks, were utilized for these experiments. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of Hey cells (5 × 10^6 cells in 0.1 ml PBS) to establish tumors. Once the tumors reached an average volume of 100 mm^3, the mice were divided into groups of five. These groups were then treated intraperitoneally with either apatinib (50 mg/kg) or a vehicle control. Tumor dimensions were recorded every three days using a digital caliper, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula V = L × W^2 × 0.52. One week following the final apatinib administration, the mice were euthanized, and their tumors were excised and weighed. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was subsequently performed on these xenografted tumor tissues, following the established protocol.
Results were analyzed in triplicate using Graph Pad Prism, presented as mean ± S.E. Statistical significance between control and treated groups was assessed via t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison tests, with p < 0.05 considered significant.
To investigate the role of apatinib in ovarian cancer, we first assessed VEGFR2 expression in various ovarian cancer cell lines and HUVEC cells using Western blotting. Our findings revealed significantly higher VEGFR2 expression in Hey and OVCA433 cells compared to SKOV3, IGROV1, OVCA420, OVCA429, OVCAR-8, and ES-2 cells, as depicted in Figure 1a. Consequently, Hey and OVCA433 cell lines were chosen for further experimentation. These selected cell lines were then subjected to treatment with varying concentrations of apatinib, ranging from 0 to 640 μM, for durations of 24, 48, and 72 hours (Figure 1b). The results demonstrated a dose- and time-dependent reduction in cell viability in both Hey and OVCA433 cells following apatinib treatment, when compared to untreated controls. The calculated half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values at 48 hours were 32.15 μM (95% CI 28.17–36.73) for Hey cells and 92.85 μM (95% CI 77.36–111.1) for OVCA433 cells. These dose-response data informed the selection of concentrations for subsequent experiments. Further analysis, including gene-chip-based pathway analysis after apatinib treatment (Figure 1c), indicated that pathways related to glycolysis were significantly altered. Specifically, RNA sequencing of Hey cells treated with 15 μM apatinib for 48 hours showed a downregulation of genes involved in glycolysis (qFDR < 0.001) (Figure 1d). Western blotting confirmed that GLUT4 expression was reduced, while LDHA and HK2 expression remained unchanged (Figure 1f). Functional assays demonstrated that apatinib treatment significantly repressed both glucose uptake and lactate production in these cells compared to controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 1g). Additionally, colony formation assays revealed a progressive decrease in the number of colonies formed by ovarian cancer cells upon exposure to different apatinib concentrations (p < 0.05) (Figure 1h). These findings collectively suggest that apatinib suppresses proliferation by inhibiting glycolysis in ovarian cancer cells.
Gene-chip assays were conducted on apatinib-treated Hey cells and control cells to evaluate the impact of apatinib on gene expression profiles in ovarian cancer cells. Pathway analysis following these assays indicated that glycolysis-related pathways were significantly altered by apatinib treatment (Fig. 1c-d). Both qRT-PCR and Western blotting results identified GLUT4 as a key factor whose expression was notably affected (Fig. 1e-f). To further investigate apatinib's role in glycolysis, glucose uptake and lactate production assays were performed on Hey and OVCA433 cells treated with varying concentrations of apatinib. These experiments revealed a significant repression of both glucose uptake and lactate production in apatinib-treated cell lines compared to controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1g). Additionally, colony formation assays demonstrated a reduction in the number of colonies formed by Hey and OVCA433 cells following apatinib treatment (Fig. 1h).
To understand how apatinib suppresses glycolysis, RNA sequencing data was analyzed, revealing SOX5 as a potential downstream target. Western blot analysis confirmed that apatinib treatment dose-dependently reduced SOX5 protein levels in Hey and OVCA433 ovarian cancer cells. To investigate SOX5's role in apatinib's effect on glycolysis, SOX5 was overexpressed in apatinib-treated cells. This exogenous SOX5 expression was found to effectively counteract apatinib's inhibitory effects on colony formation, glucose uptake, and lactate production. These findings suggest that SOX5 is a key mediator in apatinib's regulation of glycolysis in ovarian cancer.
The study demonstrated that introducing SOX5 into ovarian cancer cells counteracted apatinib's suppression of GLUT4 expression, at both the mRNA and protein levels. This finding, supported by Figures 3a-b and Table S1, aligns with SOX5's known role as a transcription factor. To investigate this further, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted, confirming that SOX5 overexpression partially restored GLUT4 promoter activity, which had been reduced by apatinib treatment (Figure 3c). Conversely, when SOX5 expression was reduced in Hey and OVCA433 cells using shRNA, a corresponding decrease in GLUT4 expression was observed (Figure 3d). This SOX5 knockdown also led to a significant reduction in GLUT4 promoter activity. Furthermore, the silencing of SOX5 resulted in diminished glucose uptake, lactate production, and colony formation in these ovarian cancer cells compared to control groups (Figures 3e-g). Collectively, these findings suggest that apatinib exerts its inhibitory effect on glycolysis in ovarian cancer cells by disrupting the SOX5/GLUT4 signaling pathway.
Our investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying apatinib's action revealed that it impacts cell proliferation and metabolism by downregulating AKT1 and its downstream targets, including MAPK and P53, as indicated by gene-chip-based expression assays (Fig. S1). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that apatinib treatment suppressed the phosphorylation of VEGFR2 (Tyr1175) and AKT1 (Ser473), as well as SOX5 expression, while concurrently increasing phospho-GSK3β (Ser9) levels compared to control groups (Fig. 4a). To clarify the role of VEGFR2, we employed specific siRNAs to silence its expression. This intervention resulted in protein alterations mirroring those observed after apatinib treatment (Fig. 4b), reinforcing VEGFR2's involvement. Furthermore, we observed that exogenous overexpression of VEGFA led to increased levels of phospho-VEGFR2, phospho-AKT1, SOX5, and GLUT4, accompanied by a reduction in phospho-GSK3β (Fig. 4c). Conversely, exogenous AKT1 overexpression attenuated phospho-GSK3β expression and enhanced SOX5 expression (Fig. 4d), findings consistent with the effects of AKT1 silencing (Fig. 4e). Collectively, these results strongly suggest that apatinib inhibits SOX5 expression through the VEGFR2/AKT1/GSK3β signaling pathway, thereby influencing glycolysis in ovarian cancer cells.
To evaluate the in vivo anti-tumor effects of apatinib, Hey ovarian cancer cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. Once tumors reached a volume of 100 mm³, mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of apatinib at a dosage of 50 mg/kg. The study observed a significant reduction in tumor growth speed in the apatinib-treated group compared to the control group (Fig. 5a-b). Furthermore, both tumor volumes and weights were markedly decreased in mice receiving apatinib treatment (p < 0.05, Fig. 5c-d). PET-CT imaging revealed that apatinib treatment significantly suppressed in vivo glucose uptake in Hey tumor cells, evidenced by a lower SUVmax value (Fig. 5e-f). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of xenografted tumor tissues provided further mechanistic insights. Staining for p-VEGFR2(Tyr1175), p-AKT1(Ser473), SOX5, and GLUT4 showed decreased expression following apatinib treatment. Conversely, the expression of p-GSK3β(Ser9) was increased in the apatinib-treated tumors (Fig. 5g). Additionally, IHC assessment of p-VEGFR2(Tyr1175) and CD31 in xenografted tumors indicated a reduction in microvascular density (MVD) in the apatinib treatment group (Fig. 5h). These findings collectively demonstrate apatinib's ability to inhibit in vivo tumor proliferation and glycolysis in ovarian cancer models.
Our findings reveal that apatinib effectively suppresses ovarian cancer cell proliferation, both in vitro and in vivo. This anti-proliferative effect is achieved by regulating glycolysis via the VEGFR2/AKT1/GSK3β/SOX5 signaling pathway. This discovery offers a novel perspective on apatinib's anti-tumor activity. Aerobic glycolysis, a recognized hallmark of cancer, fuels cellular growth by providing essential energy and nutrients. Consequently, targeting glycolysis is an increasingly important therapeutic strategy, with drugs targeting metabolic pathway enzymes, such as HK2 inhibitors, currently undergoing research and clinical trials.
Apatinib, an orally administered anti-angiogenic medication, exhibits selective targeting of VEGFR2. Its binding affinity for VEGFR2 is ten times greater than that of vatalanib or sorafenib. In vitro studies further suggest apatinib's superior selectivity as a VEGFR2 inhibitor compared to sunitinib, with reported IC50 values of 0.001 μM or 0.005 μM. Sunitinib, in contrast, is a multi-targeting agent with activity against VEGFR-1, -2, and -3, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), c-Kit, FMS-like tyrosine kinase-3, and RET, and has received FDA approval for renal cell cancer and gastrointestinal stromal tumors. Clinical investigations have indicated that apatinib may offer benefits to patients with recurrent, platinum-resistant epithelial ovarian cancer, demonstrating an acceptable safety profile. Furthermore, combining apatinib with oral etoposide has shown promising efficacy and manageable toxicity in platinum-resistant and platinum-refractory ovarian cancers. Preclinical research suggests apatinib's potential to overcome multidrug resistance by inhibiting the transport functions of multidrug resistance protein 1 (ABCB1), multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1, ABCC1), and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP, ABCG2). This is particularly relevant given the established link between cellular metabolic dysregulation and drug resistance in cancer therapy. Beyond its direct anti-angiogenic effects, apatinib has been observed to induce autophagy and apoptosis in osteosarcoma via the VEGFR2 signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3)/B cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) signaling pathway. Enhancing apatinib's anti-tumor effects through co-administration with autophagy inhibitors has been demonstrated in osteosarcoma, and similarly, inhibiting autophagy has been shown to boost apatinib's efficacy in colorectal cancer, both in vitro and in vivo. These findings led to the hypothesis that apatinib might reverse multidrug resistance by targeting specific cellular functions, including glucose metabolism. Collectively, evidence suggests that apatinib can exert its anti-tumor effects and potentially achieve enhanced efficacy when used in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents.
SOX5, a transcription factor belonging to the SOX family, is crucial in the advancement of numerous cancers. Research indicates SOX5 can influence epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by modifying twist-related protein 1 (Twist1) expression, thereby promoting metastasis in prostate cancer. Furthermore, SOX5 overexpression has been observed to counteract apoptosis induced by specific microRNAs in pituitary tumor cells, and its levels are elevated in invasive pituitary tumor tissues. These findings suggest SOX5 functions as a versatile oncogene, though its precise role in tumor initiation and progression warrants further investigation. Our study demonstrates that SOX5 regulates GLUT4 promoter activity, impacting glycolysis.
Apatinib suppresses ovarian cancer cell growth and proliferation both in vitro and in vivo by inhibiting glycolysis. This action is mediated through the VEGFR2/AKT1/GSK3β/SOX5 signaling pathway. These findings offer new insights into apatinib's mechanism of action and suggest potential strategies for treating recurrent, platinum-refractory ovarian cancer.
