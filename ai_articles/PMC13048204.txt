This study investigated differences in golf club control and clubface orientation during downswing and impact between elite and intermediate golfers. Using an eight-camera VICON motion capture system, researchers recorded seven elite and thirteen intermediate golfers as they putted with a pitch club. To facilitate biomechanical analysis of swinging movements, six retro-reflective markers were strategically placed on each club. The study focused on comparing several key outcome variables between the two groups: the turning point, the sweet spot, the elevation angle (EA), and the azimuth angle (AA) of the club head. Significant group differences were observed in the standard deviations (SDs) of the club tail's location at the turning point across all three axes (x, p = 0.004; y, p = 0.015; z, p = 0.035). Furthermore, significant differences were found in the minimum distance between the sweet spot's center and the ball at impact (p = 0.007), the EA (p = 0.001), and the AA (p = 0.001) of the club head. Elite golfers consistently demonstrated more converged turning point locations, shorter distances between the sweet spot and the ball at impact, higher EAs, and lower AAs when compared to their intermediate counterparts. These results highlight a biomechanical approach for understanding golf swing behaviors, suggesting that the consistency of turning point locations serves as a crucial indicator for distinguishing golf performance levels.

The golf swing, a rapid chain-reaction movement, is typically broken down into five phases: take-away, backswing, downswing, acceleration, and follow-through. The downswing, completed within 300 milliseconds and comprising four critical periods (starting, acceleration, impact, and follow-through), has been a primary focus of numerous biomechanical studies since 1969. That year, Cochran and Stobbs pioneered the use of high-speed filming to analyze golf swing mechanics, ball aerodynamics, and equipment dynamics. The downswing's initiation ultimately dictates the ball's trajectory.
Previous research has highlighted the importance of minimal variability in torso and pelvic rotation during the downswing for skilled golfers. However, other studies, like that by Tuck et al., observed highly skilled golfers achieving consistent ball speed despite variations in their body movements. This suggests that club trajectory and club head-to-ball contact variability might be more critical than body movement variability. Further investigation by Choi et al. revealed that skilled golfers exhibit less positional variability of the club head just before the downswing, unlike unskilled golfers who show greater variability at the top of the downswing. This reduced variability in the club's top-of-downswing position is a significant hurdle for intermediate golfers aiming to reach a zero handicap.
Achieving a sweet-spot impact is a hallmark of skilled golfers, allowing for ideal downswing motions. This "sweet spot" is a small area on the clubface, roughly two grooves above the leading edge. When the club head's center of mass aligns with the golf ball's center of mass during impact, it creates a collinear collision, minimizing twisting motion. Conversely, even minor body sway during the downswing can lead to missing this optimal contact point. Consequently, an intermediate golfer's inability to consistently orient the clubface for a sweet-spot impact during the downswing represents a significant obstacle to reaching a zero handicap.
This study aimed to investigate differences in club control variability during the downswing's initial phase and clubface orientation at impact between golfers of varying skill levels. Our hypothesis proposed that elite players would exhibit more consistent club positioning at the turning point. Additionally, we anticipated that elite golfers would demonstrate superior clubface orientation, leading to a smaller distance between the sweet spot and the ball upon impact, compared to intermediate players. The findings are intended to assist coaches and golfers in improving performance to achieve a handicap below zero.
For this study, twenty right-handed golfers were recruited, none having a history of musculoskeletal issues. They were divided into two groups based on skill level: seven elite players (handicap of +4 or lower) and thirteen intermediate players (handicap between +5 and 18). Before participating, all individuals provided informed consent, a process approved by the National Yang-Ming University Institutional Review Board.
A VICON computer-assisted video motion analysis system, operating at 125 Hz, was employed in an 8 × 8 × 2.6 m3 gait laboratory for the experiments. To track the pitching clubs' trajectories, six 14-mm diameter reflective markers were utilized. These markers were strategically placed, with three on the club's surface and three on its body.
Participants warmed up with practice swings until ready for the six formal experimental trials. A Titleist pitch wedge, a type of pitching club, was the equipment used for testing all movements.
Kinematic data, captured in three dimensions, was exported via Nexus software and subsequently analyzed in Matlab. To ensure data quality, a 4th order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 6 Hz cutoff frequency was applied to all coordinates, removing artifact-induced noise before further processing and variable calculation.
To assess differences between elite and intermediate golfers, the three-dimensional position of the club tail was measured at the turning point—the transition from backswing to downswing. The standard deviation of these coordinates was then calculated for analysis.
Optimal energy transfer from the club head to the golf ball occurs when their centers of gravity (CoG) align at impact. This specific collision point is termed the sweet spot, located on the clubface, extending from its center towards the top. For this study, the sweet spot was identified as being on the lower third of the clubface, near the bottom edge. To quantify impact coherence for each player, researchers measured the minimum distance between this sweet spot and the golf ball. The sweet spot's relative position was determined using three reflective markers placed on the club tail, while the golf ball's position was fixed within the laboratory's coordinate system. Consistently shorter distances between the sweet spot and the ball across a player's swings indicated greater impact coherence.
To assess a player's ability to control ball distance, the elevation angle (EA) was measured. This angle represents the orientation of the clubface at impact, specifically the angle between the club head's face and a horizontal line.
To assess a player's ability to control the ball's direction, the azimuth angle (AA) was measured. This angle is formed between the clubface's normal vector and the target line, which is a horizontal line aligned with the ball's intended flight path and parallel to the player's feet. The AA thus indicates how effectively the clubface is oriented at impact for optimal ball direction.
To compare elite and intermediate players, Welch's t-test was employed due to unequal variances and sample sizes. Statistical analyses, performed with SPSS 20.0, considered a significance level of α < 0.05.

Significant differences were observed in the standard deviations (SDs) of the club tail's position at the top of the swing between elite and intermediate golfers. Specifically, the elite group exhibited considerably smaller SDs across all three spatial axes (x, y, and z) compared to the intermediate group. For the x-axis, elite golfers had an SD of 17.70 ± 5.11 mm versus 33.52 ± 15.18 mm for intermediate golfers (p = 0.004). Similar disparities were found for the y-axis (elite: 14.52 ± 4.32 mm; intermediate: 23.17 ± 10.01 mm; p = 0.015) and z-axis (elite: 18.97 ± 7.745 mm; intermediate: 27.77 ± 8.38 mm; p = 0.035).
Elite golfers achieved significantly shorter distances between the sweet spot and the ball at impact compared to intermediate golfers (p = 0.007). Specifically, the elite group averaged 35.45 ± 17.75 mm, while the intermediate group averaged 45.58 ± 19.94 mm. This indicates a more precise impact for elite players.
Significant differences in both azimuth (AA) and elevation angles (EA) at impact were observed between elite and intermediate golfers. Elite players demonstrated smaller AAs (4.98° ± 3.99°) and larger EAs (44.83° ± 6.70°) compared to intermediate players (AA: 8.61° ± 5.92°; EA: 39.38° ± 8.62°), with both differences being statistically significant (p = 0.001).
This study offers empirical biomechanical evidence distinguishing elite and intermediate golfers during a swing. The subsequent sections explore how these findings can be practically applied to refine coaching and practice methodologies.
Elite golfers demonstrated a more consistent club tail location at the turning point compared to intermediate players. Across each axis, the average difference between these groups was approximately 10 mm. This quantitative observation reinforces the long-held belief among professional golfers that the turning point is crucial for successful play, a concept previously lacking empirical investigation. The club's position at the end of the backswing, marking the transition to the downswing, is defined by this turning point. Minimizing the variability of this location across multiple shots establishes a stable and precise downswing trajectory.
Elite golfers demonstrated a sweet spot to ball impact distance approximately 10 mm shorter than intermediate players, a difference mirroring the turning point findings. This suggests a strong connection between the consistency of the turning point's location and the precision of the sweet spot impact. While not directly compared, the data implies that a more stable turning point leads to a more accurate strike on the clubface's sweet spot. This consistency in the turning point appears to directly correlate with a more convergent impact between the club's sweet spot and the golf ball.
The concept that an effective turning point leads to a superior downswing has been acknowledged by world golf champions. However, the general applicability of this observation, particularly for non-elite players, has been questioned. This study offers quantitative data supporting the idea that a consistent turning point facilitates optimal sweet spot impact. The sweet spot, located on the club head, is crucial for maximizing energy transfer from the club to the ball during impact. From an energy conversion perspective, the potential energy accumulated at the turning point during the downswing is most efficiently transformed into kinetic energy when the ball is struck precisely on the sweet spot.
Our research suggests that the turning point acts as a "blind aiming point" in golf. This concept is based on the idea that, similar to aiming in shooting, a critical aiming process occurs in golf. Once the club's position at the end of the backswing, known as the turning point, is set, the subsequent downswing trajectory and the club's impact point on the ball become predetermined and unchangeable. Despite golfers focusing their gaze on the ball, the consistent control of the turning point's dynamic characteristics is crucial for executing a flawless shot, given the continuous and reactive nature of the golf swing.
The elevation angle (EA) immediately following impact is a crucial determinant of a golf ball's trajectory. The inherent loft angle of a club, which differs across various clubs, also dictates the distance a ball will travel. For this study, a P club with a standard loft angle of 52° was employed. Our findings indicated that elite golfers achieved higher EAs compared to intermediate players. Specifically, the elite group's average EA of 44° was only 8° shy of the P club's standard loft, whereas the intermediate group's average of 39° was 13° lower. This suggests that elite players effectively utilized the P club's characteristics to generate a more favorable EA at impact, leading to an improved ball trajectory. Conversely, the intermediate group's EA deviated significantly from the club's standard loft, potentially resulting in a flattened and less effective shot. Ultimately, a greater consistency in turning point locations correlated with an EA closer to the club's loft angle.
The direction of the golf ball is primarily determined by the azimuth angle (AA) immediately following impact. An ideal scenario involves an AA of 0°, ensuring the ball travels precisely along the intended target line. This study revealed that elite golfers exhibited notably smaller AAs compared to intermediate players. The average AA for the elite group was 4°, which is half the 8° average observed in the intermediate group. Even minor deviations from the target line at impact can lead to significant directional errors, amplified over the ball's flight distance. Consequently, the observed slight difference in AA between the groups has a substantial effect on achieving the desired shot outcome. Furthermore, our findings suggest a correlation: more consistent turning point locations are associated with a smaller AA.
The study challenges the traditional belief that the most crucial moment for a successful golf shot is immediately before impact. This common misconception in training suggests that achieving the ideal elevation angle (EA) primarily involves adjusting the clubface as it approaches the ball. However, such last-minute adjustments can disrupt the natural flow of the swing and potentially lead to injuries. The downswing is characterized by an irreversible sequence of movements, meaning any abrupt changes or additional upper extremity joint movements during this phase will impede the efficient transfer of energy. Consequently, the research proposes that golf training should shift its focus away from the downswing phase or the moments just prior to impact, and instead prioritize the turning point.
The study's findings offer two key applications. Firstly, the data on turning points, the minimum distance between the sweet spot and the ball, and the club head's Elevation Angle (EA) and Azimuth Angle (AA) serve as crucial metrics for identifying promising new golf talent. The research demonstrates a significant impact of turning point variability on the outcome at impact, influencing factors like sweet spot contact, the EA's alignment with the loft angle, and the AA's relation to the target line. Consequently, the consistency of turning point locations emerges as a strong indicator of a player's inherent skill. A player exhibiting more stable turning points across multiple swings likely possesses greater ability and potential. Golf inherently requires precision, and consistent performance at the turning point may signify a player's natural aptitude for executing correct movements. This foundational ability can then be refined through targeted coaching and practice that emphasizes the turning point.
This research offers a practical, scientific framework for consistently tracking a golfer's performance and development throughout their career. Unlike traditional coaching, which often depends on a coach's subjective observations and personal experience, this methodology allows for objective and quantitative monitoring of a player's learning trajectory. Golfers, unlike athletes in many other sports, typically have extended careers, yet even elite players experience fluctuations in their performance. Previously, there was no systematic, long-term method to document and analyze these changes. This meant that golfers might achieve an ideal turning point and a fluid swing, leading to excellent results, without fully understanding the reasons for their success. Conversely, when performance declined, they lacked the tools to pinpoint the core issues and develop effective recovery strategies. This study addresses this gap by proposing a viable solution, advocating for a shift in analytical focus from the impact moment to the pre-downswing phase.
This research utilized biomechanical principles and motion analysis to examine golf swing characteristics. The study's findings indicate that several factors are crucial for distinguishing golf skill levels: the consistency of turning point locations, the proximity of the impact site to the sweet spot's center, and both the elevation and azimuth angles of the club head at impact. This quantitative methodology is validated as a practical tool for diagnosing golfers' swing behaviors. A key takeaway is the emphasized importance of stable turning point locations. Consequently, golf swing training should prioritize achieving consistent and ideal turning points. Prior research has identified a link between an optimal turning point and the shifting of the center of gravity to the leading leg. This suggests that the interplay between the turning point, ball placement, and target line is vital for successful performance, warranting further investigation.
