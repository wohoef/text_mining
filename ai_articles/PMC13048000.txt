This study aimed to assess the Prostate Health Index's (PHI) effectiveness in predicting prostate cancer (PCa) within Asian populations, a demographic previously underrepresented in such prospective research. We compared PHI's performance against conventional prostate-specific antigen (PSA) tests. Between February 2017 and September 2018, we recruited patients suspected of PCa. This included individuals with total PSA (tPSA) levels between 4 and 10 ng/mL, or those with tPSA below 4 ng/mL but presenting a suspicious digital rectal examination. Each participant underwent a 12-core transrectal ultrasound-guided prostate biopsy. Before the biopsy, blood samples were collected to measure tPSA, free PSA (fPSA), percentage of fPSA (%fPSA), [-2]proPSA (p2PSA), and percentage of p2PSA (%p2PSA). The PHI was determined using the formula: (p2PSA/fPSA) × √tPSA. To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of these PSA derivatives, we calculated their areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) and their specificities at a predefined sensitivity of 90%. Out of 307 participants, 95 (30.9%) were diagnosed with PCa via biopsy. All PSA derivatives, except fPSA, exhibited significant differences between patients with positive and negative biopsy results. PHI demonstrated superior predictive capability for initial biopsy outcomes (AUC: 0.783) compared to tPSA (AUC: 0.611). At a 90% sensitivity, PHI achieved the highest specificity at 46.7%, significantly outperforming tPSA's 23.2%. A PHI cutoff of 35.15 could have potentially spared 108 patients (35.2%) from unnecessary biopsies. For detecting Gleason score ≥ 7 disease, a PHI threshold of 36.96 yielded a 90% sensitivity and 52.1% specificity. In conclusion, PHI proved to be the most effective biomarker among the PSA derivatives for predicting PCa in men with tPSA below 10 ng/mL. Furthermore, the likelihood of a Gleason score ≥ 7 diagnosis increased proportionally with higher PHI values.

Prostate cancer (PCa) presents a significant public health challenge in Taiwan. While historically less prevalent in Asia than in Europe and North America, its incidence in the region, including Taiwan, has seen a rapid rise over the past two decades. Globally, PCa ranked as the fifth most common cancer among men in 2014, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 29.1 per 100,000 person-years. Notably, Taiwan's incidence rate is considerably lower than that of Europe (59.3 per 100,000) and the United States (123.2 per 100,000). However, a concerning 44% of PCa diagnoses in Taiwan occur at a late stage, contrasting sharply with the 80-92% early-stage diagnoses observed in the United States.
Despite its widespread use in PCa screening and disease monitoring, PSA's low specificity is a significant drawback. While PSA has improved early detection and reduced PCa mortality, its inability to differentiate between aggressive and non-aggressive cancers often results in unnecessary biopsies and overtreatment, particularly in men with tPSA levels below 10 ng/mL.
P2PSA, a specific form of proPSA, is recognized as the most cancer-specific variant, with elevated levels observed in the blood of men with prostate cancer. ProPSA itself is a subform of free PSA and exists in four different serum isoforms: [-2]proPSA, [-4]proPSA, [-5]proPSA, and [-7]proPSA, distinguished by their proleader peptide sequence lengths (two, four, five, or seven amino acids). Two derivatives derived from p2PSA, the Prostate Health Index (PHI = [p2PSA/fPSA] × √tPSA) and %p2PSA ([p2PSA/fPSA] × 100), have demonstrated superior ability in differentiating prostate cancer from benign prostatic conditions compared to tPSA or fPSA, and are found in higher concentrations in PCa patients.
Despite existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses, such as those by Abrate et al. and Wang et al., consistently showing that %p2PSA and PHI outperform standard PSA in predicting prostate biopsy results and guiding decisions, most of this research originates from Caucasian populations, which have a higher prostate cancer incidence. Consequently, there's a scarcity of prospective studies examining %p2PSA and PHI performance in Asian populations. Our study, therefore, sought to assess how well %p2PSA and PHI predict prostate cancer compared to standard PSA tests in a Taiwanese cohort undergoing their initial biopsy for a tPSA level below 10 ng/mL.
From February 2017 to September 2018, a prospective observational study was conducted. Its aim was to assess if %p2PSA and PHI tests were superior to other PSA derivatives for detecting PCa during initial biopsies in patients with tPSA levels under 10 ng/mL.
All patients provided written consent after being fully informed about the procedure and potential complications. The Ethics Committee of Taipei Veterans General Hospital approved the study.
Men were included if their tPSA was between 4 and 10 ng/mL (regardless of DRE findings) or if their tPSA was below 4 ng/mL but accompanied by a suspicious DRE. Excluded were individuals with acute prostatitis, urinary tract infections, prior prostate cancer, recent 5-α reductase inhibitor use (within 3 months), previous prostate biopsies, or a history of transurethral prostate resection.
The study's main goal was to assess PHI's effectiveness in predicting prostate cancer (PCa) during biopsy, contrasting it with standard PSA tests. This involved evaluating its sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, researchers quantified how many biopsies could be prevented and how many aggressive cancers (Gleason score ≥7) might be overlooked.
Blood samples taken before biopsy were analyzed for tPSA, fPSA, and p2PSA using a Beckman Coulter DxI800 Unicel Immunoassay system. Prostate volume, measured by transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) using the formula width × height × length × 0.52, allowed for the calculation of PSA density (PSAD) as tPSA/prostate volume. The Prostate Health Index (PHI) was derived from the formula (p2PSA/fPSA) × √PSA. Additionally, %fPSA was calculated as the fPSA to tPSA ratio, and %p2PSA as the p2PSA to fPSA ratio.
Each patient received a minimum 12-core TRUS biopsy, following a standard template and additional sampling of any echogenic lesions. Histological analysis and grading of these specimens adhered to the 2005 International Society of Urological Pathology Consensus Conference guidelines.
To predict PCa detection, logistic regression models were employed. Statistical analyses, including basic participant characteristics, utilized the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data and the χ2 test for categorical data. The performance of PSA derivatives was assessed by estimating Areas Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves (AUCs) and specificity at a 90% sensitivity. All computations were performed using SPSS Statistics version 22, with a 5% significance level for two-sided tests.
Out of 307 patients, 95 (30.9%) had a positive biopsy. Within this group, 43 (45.3%) were diagnosed with Gleason score 6 prostate cancer, 33 (34.7%) with Gleason score 7, and 19 (20.0%) with Gleason scores 8-10. For patients with tPSA below 4 ng/mL and a suspicious digital rectal examination, the positive biopsy rate was 13.7% (7 out of 51). All PSA derivatives, except for fPSA, showed significant differences when comparing positive and negative biopsy groups. The Prostate Health Index (PHI) demonstrated superior performance in predicting initial biopsy outcomes (AUC: 0.783; p < 0.001) when compared to total PSA (AUC: 0.611; p = 0.002).
An analysis at a 90% sensitivity level further evaluated PSA derivative performance. The PHI demonstrated superior specificity at 46.7%, significantly higher than tPSA's 23.2%. This meant a PHI cutoff of 35.15 could have prevented 108 (35.2%) biopsies. For detecting Gleason score ≥ 7 disease, a PHI threshold of 36.96 achieved 52.1% specificity at the same sensitivity.
An increasing PHI level correlated with a higher percentage of Gleason Score (GS) ≥ 7 disease. Evaluating PHI performance using manufacturer-defined bands, 17.8% of GS ≥ 7 cancers were detected when PHI was between 36 and 54.9, rising to 53.7% for PHI levels ≥ 55.
To predict prostate cancer (PCa), logistic regression analysis was conducted using cutoff values from various PSA derivatives and clinical characteristics, all determined by ROC curves at a 90% sensitivity level. Univariate analysis revealed that prostate volume, PSAD, %fPSA, and all p2PSA-related parameters were statistically significant predictors of PCa, with PHI and %p2PSA showing particular strength (both p < 0.001, Table 6). For multivariate analysis, age and prostate volume formed the baseline prediction model. Subsequently, tPSA, fPSA, %fPSA, p2PSA, %p2PSA, and PHI were individually incorporated and tested. PSAD was omitted from the multivariate model to prevent multicollinearity. The multivariate analysis ultimately identified only tPSA and p2PSA-related parameters as significant independent predictors of a positive biopsy, with PHI ≥ 35.15 demonstrating the highest odds ratio of 7.02 (95% CI: 6.63-7.40; p < 0.001).
In 2012, the FDA approved the PHI test to help differentiate prostate cancer (PCa) from benign prostate conditions. This approval applied to men aged 50 or older, with total serum PSA levels between 2 and 10 ng/mL, and a non-suspicious digital rectal examination (DRE). A key clinical trial supporting this approval involved 658 men (324 with PCa, 334 without). In this trial, the PHI achieved an AUC of 0.708, significantly outperforming tPSA, which had an AUC of 0.516. When sensitivity was fixed at 90%, PHI demonstrated a specificity of 31.1%, nearly tripling tPSA's specificity of 10.8% (p < 0.001), indicating a substantial improvement in PCa detection. Further supporting PHI's utility, a meta-analysis by Wang et al. reported pooled AUCs of 0.86 for %p2PSA and 0.70 for PHI in PCa prediction. Additionally, Filella et al.'s meta-analysis found a pooled clinical specificity of 31.6% for PHI at the 90% sensitivity threshold.
For men with tPSA < 10 ng/mL, PHI emerged as the superior biomarker for predicting PCa at biopsy. With a cutoff of ≥35.15, PHI achieved a specificity of 46.7% at 90% sensitivity, which was double the 23.2% specificity of tPSA. This could prevent 35.2% of unnecessary biopsies. The AUC for PHI was 0.783, compared to tPSA's 0.611. Additionally, multivariate analysis showed PHI ≥ 35.15 had the highest odds ratio of 7.02 for a positive biopsy.
Tan et al. conducted the first prospective Asian study on %p2PSA and PHI for PCa detection. Their research in Singapore involved 157 men with tPSA levels between 4 and 10 ng/mL and normal DRE. The PHI test demonstrated superior performance in predicting initial biopsy outcomes, achieving an AUC of 0.794, significantly higher than tPSA's 0.479. At 90% sensitivity, PHI's specificity was 58.3%, over three times greater than tPSA's 17.3%. This could have prevented 49% of biopsies using a PHI cutoff of ≥26.75.
In a Hong Kong study, Chiu et al. assessed PHI in 569 men with tPSA between 4 and 10 ng/mL and no suspicious DRE. They reported AUCs of 0.76 for PHI and 0.54 for tPSA. The PHI model could have prevented 38.4% and 55.4% of biopsies at 10% and 20% PCa risk thresholds, respectively.
Na et al.'s multicenter Shanghai study of 660 patients (tPSA 2-10 ng/mL) demonstrated PHI's superior ability over tPSA to predict biopsy outcomes. PHI achieved an AUC of 0.87, compared to tPSA's 0.60. Utilizing a PHI threshold of ≥32, 52.0% of biopsies could have been averted at 88.2% sensitivity.
In a study of Taiwanese men with PSA levels between 4 and 10 ng/mL, Hsieh et al. found PHI superior to tPSA for predicting prostate biopsy results (AUC 0.77 vs. 0.57). A PHI threshold of 20.7 could have prevented 26.6% of biopsies while maintaining 90% sensitivity.
In a study of Taiwanese men with tPSA ≤10 ng/mL, Cheng et al. found PHI outperformed tPSA for PCa prediction at biopsy (AUC 0.772 vs. 0.544). A PHI cutoff of ≥21.62 achieved 27.27% specificity at 90% sensitivity, potentially preventing 22.3% of unnecessary biopsies.
Asian prospective studies consistently demonstrate PHI's superiority over tPSA for PCa detection. However, these studies exhibit considerable variation in PHI cutoff values used to distinguish PCa from benign prostatic conditions, as well as in specificities and the proportion of biopsies that could be avoided. Our study's PHI cutoff value is notably higher than those reported in other Asian cohorts, possibly reflecting our higher PCa detection rate of 30.9% within Asian series. Currently, a universally agreed-upon optimal PHI value remains elusive, suggesting that PHI reference ranges might be population-specific.
NCCN guidelines (version 2.2015) incorporate PHI for early PCa detection, noting that a PHI over 35 suggests a greater likelihood of high-grade PCa in patients, whether they are biopsy-naive or have had a previous negative biopsy. Our study observed a direct correlation: as PHI levels rose, so did the incidence of Gleason score ≥ 7 cancers. Within our cohort, applying a PHI threshold of 36.0 for high-grade PCa (GS ≥ 7) at 90% sensitivity could have prevented 120 biopsies (39.1%), though it would have missed four (7.7%) such cancers.
In a study of 658 U.S. men with tPSA between 4 and 10 ng/mL, Loeb et al. found that a PHI cutoff of 28.6 could prevent about 30% of biopsies, though 10% of high-grade PCa cases (GS ≥ 7) would be missed. Conversely, Tan et al. observed in a Singaporean cohort with similar tPSA levels (4-10 ng/mL) that a PHI cutoff of 27.0 could avert roughly 51% of biopsies, with only 2.5% of GS ≥ 7 PCa cases being overlooked.
In this study, prostate volume was identified as a predictor of PCa, with smaller volumes correlating to higher detection rates in TRUS-guided biopsies. The average prostate volume in the negative biopsy group (72.20 ± 39.00 mL) was significantly larger than in the positive biopsy group (50.54 ± 35.46 mL), with a p-value of 0.001 and an AUC of 0.546. Consequently, age and prostate volume formed the foundational prediction model in multivariate logistic analysis. When individual PSA derivatives were subsequently incorporated, a PHI of 35.15 or greater demonstrated the highest adjusted odds ratio of 7.02 (95% CI: 6.63-7.40; p < 0.001).
This study represents Taiwan's most extensive prospective cohort investigation into PHI's predictive power for PCa versus standard PSA tests. The substantial sample size likely enhanced the statistical robustness of the results. Furthermore, the research exclusively included men suspected of PCa who had not undergone previous biopsies. This approach was crucial because the criteria for an initial biopsy differ significantly from those for a repeat biopsy, and including men with prior biopsies could introduce selection bias.
Several limitations affect this study. A key concern is the generalizability of the findings, as all participants were recruited from tertiary care clinics, potentially limiting their applicability to primary care settings or the broader population. Additionally, the study did not exclude individuals with an abnormal Digital Rectal Examination (DRE), with 25.4% of the cohort presenting with a suspicious DRE. While a meta-analysis by Naji et al. indicated poor performance of DRE in PCa screening (pooled sensitivity 0.51, specificity 0.59), and Chiu et al. suggested PHI's effectiveness remains consistent with abnormal DRE, including all clinically indicated TRUS biopsy patients means the PHI cutoff is more relevant to real-world practice. Finally, the study did not account for potential false-negative prostate biopsies or changes in PCa grading between biopsy and radical prostatectomy.
For men with tPSA levels under 10 ng/mL, PHI proved superior to standard PSA tests for predicting PCa at biopsy. Achieving 90% sensitivity, PHI demonstrated a 46.7% specificity for positive biopsies.
