The tumor suppressor p53 is frequently altered in human cancers. While p53 alterations are known in breast cancer, specific investigations into TP53 mutations within invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) have been limited. Recent conditional mouse models suggest that functional p53 inactivation might contribute to ILC development and progression. However, existing reports on TP53 mutation detection in the classic and more aggressive pleomorphic variants of ILC (PILC) are scarce and unclear. To address this, we undertook a thorough analysis to establish the TP53 mutation status in these breast cancer subtypes. Our methods involved examining TP53 mutations and p53 accumulation in 22 classic ILC and 19 PILC cases using direct DNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry. This aimed to deepen our understanding of p53-mediated pathways in the etiology of classic ILC and PILC. We identified 11 potentially pathogenic TP53 mutations; 3 in classic ILC (13.6%) and 8 in PILC (42.1%), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.04). Although overall p53 protein accumulation was similar between classic and pleomorphic ILC, mutations impacting protein structure and function showed a significant correlation with p53 protein levels. In conclusion, TP53 mutations are more prevalent in PILC compared to classic ILC.

Initially identified in 1979, the p53 tumor suppressor protein functions as a crucial cell cycle regulator. When cells experience stress, the p53 signaling pathway activates genes responsible for inhibiting cell division, repairing DNA, and inducing programmed cell death [1, 2]. Disruptions to the p53 gene that render it inactive are frequently found in human cancers. These alterations impair p53's regulatory capabilities, thereby contributing to cellular transformation. While p53 mutations are present in breast cancer, their occurrence is less frequent (approximately 20%) than in other solid tumors [2]. It is well-established that these mutations are linked to high-grade and triple-negative breast cancers [3, 4]; however, the specific range of p53 mutations across different histological subtypes of breast cancer remains largely uncharacterized.
Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) represents about 15% of all breast cancers, with the majority classified as luminal-type based on their molecular characteristics. This type of cancer encompasses several distinct subtypes. The classic variant is characterized by small, uniform cells, often containing intracytoplasmic vacuoles and small nuclei. It typically displays a trabecular infiltration pattern, with cells appearing dissociated or in single files, frequently forming targetoid arrangements around unaffected ducts, and exhibiting a low mitotic rate. Other well-defined subtypes include the alveolar type, which forms small, round aggregates of 20 or more cells with typical lobular features, and the solid variant, presenting as more cohesive sheets of cells with minimal intervening stroma, increased mitotic activity, and often some degree of atypia. A particularly aggressive subtype is the pleomorphic variant of ILC (PILC). While it shares the overall growth pattern of classic lobular carcinoma, PILC is distinguished by polygonal, eccentrically positioned pleomorphic nuclei and a higher frequency of mitoses. This variant is relatively rare, constituting less than 1% of all breast carcinomas and no more than 10% of all ILC cases. PILC is associated with a less favorable prognosis compared to classic ILC. Research has explored the molecular and histological features of PILC as a distinct entity. Immunohistochemical studies have shown that PILCs commonly express estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors, are predominantly E-cadherin negative, often Her2 positive, and occasionally p53 positive. While gross cystic disease fluid protein (GDCFP) expression has been utilized for differentiating classic ILC from PILC, its reliability varies. Despite the clinical significance of PILC, limited information exists regarding its p53 mutation status. Given its elevated nuclear grade and poorer prognosis, a higher incidence of p53 mutations in PILC would be anticipated. Furthermore, conditional knock-out mouse models have suggested that functional inactivation of p53 might contribute to the development and progression of mouse PILC. To expand the understanding of these ILC variants, our current study examined the p53 mutation status in a cohort of 41 ILC cases, comprising 22 classic and 19 pleomorphic subtypes.
Tissue samples from 41 breast cancer patients, specifically those with lobular carcinoma, were sourced from pathology departments in Utrecht and Nijmegen, The Netherlands, and Paderborn, Germany. An expert pathologist, PJvD, classified these cases as either ILC or PILC by examining H&E stained slides derived from formaldehyde-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. Cases exhibiting a nuclear atypia score of 3 were designated as PILC. The use of anonymized or coded surplus material for research adhered to standard patient treatment agreements in these institutions.
To prepare samples, slides underwent de-paraffinization. Using a scalpel, the specific tissue area (previously marked on H&E slides) was removed and placed into a Tris/HCl buffer (pH 8.0 with Tween). Proteinase K was then introduced, followed by a 1-hour incubation at 56°C. Finally, the samples were heated to 95°C for 10 minutes to deactivate the proteinase K.
To identify mutations, the genomic DNA underwent amplification using primers designed to target exons 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 of the TP53 gene, as detailed in Table 1. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) process involved an initial denaturation phase at 94°C for 3 minutes. This was followed by 35 cycles, each consisting of a 1-minute denaturation step at 94°C, a 30-second annealing step at 60°C, and a 45-second extension step at 72°C. A final extension phase at 72°C for 5 minutes concluded the PCR. Subsequently, the resulting PCR products were subjected to sequencing in both forward and reverse directions. This was performed using the BigDye Terminator v1.1 sequencing kit on an ABI 3130 instrument, adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines from Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA. The analysis of these sequences was carried out with Mutation Surveyor software, provided by SoftGenetics, LLC., State College, PA, USA.
For immunohistochemical analysis, 4 μm sections from paraffin blocks were prepared on Superfrost + slides. Antigen retrieval was carried out using citrate buffer. An automated immunostainer (Bond-MaX, Leica) applied a mouse monoclonal p53 antibody (clone BP53-12-1, 10 mg/mL stock, MU 195-UC, BioGenex; 1:100 dilution). Following this, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and cover-slipped. To ensure accuracy, negative controls omitted the primary antibody, while a colon cancer tissue with a confirmed p53 mutation served as a positive control. An experienced pathologist (PJvD) then scored the stained nuclei. P53 was considered accumulated if 5% or more of nuclei stained, whereas approximately 1% nuclear expression indicated wild-type p53.
Frequencies between groups were compared using the Chi-Squared test or Fischer’s Exact test, as suitable, with SPSS 15.0 software. A p-value under 0.05 indicated significance.
Our identification of pleomorphic ILC relied on H&E staining, characterized by a classic trabecular growth pattern alongside polygonal, eccentric, pleomorphic nuclei and increased mitotic activity (nuclear atypia score 3). Notably, our samples did not show a significant rise in HER2 expression in PILC compared to classic ILC.
Our study aimed to understand the unique genetic changes causing classic and pleomorphic ILC subtypes by investigating p53, thereby gaining new insights into their distinct etiologies.
To understand the frequency of TP53 mutations in classic and pleomorphic invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), we analyzed 41 ILC cases, comprising 22 classic ILC and 19 pleomorphic ILC (PILC) samples. Our methodology involved polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting exons 4 through 9 of the TP53 gene, a region known for its conservation. Following PCR, the amplified DNA products underwent direct sequencing. Our analysis identified a total of 11 mutations and 2 previously validated polymorphisms across the 41 ILC cases. Among the 11 mutations, one was a novel finding, while the remaining ten had been reported previously. Regarding their genomic location, one mutation was situated within an intron, and the other ten were found in coding regions of the gene. To assess the potential impact of these mutations, we utilized the IARC TP53 database, a publicly accessible resource (version R15, November 2010). This database allowed us to thoroughly examine several critical aspects for each mutation, as detailed in Table 2. Specifically, we investigated the functional roles of the protein domains where the mutated residues were located, the established functions of the wild-type residues, and the specific effect of each mutation (e.g., missense or silent). Furthermore, we evaluated the predicted consequences of these mutations on splicing, used computer models to forecast their functional impact based on structural changes, and noted any tumor sites where these mutations had been previously observed. The comprehensive data compiled in Table 2 provided the basis for predicting the pathogenicity of each identified mutation. Based on these established criteria, we concluded that all 11 mutations discovered in our study held the potential to be pathogenic. Table 3 further details the two validated polymorphisms, including their genomic descriptions, amino acid changes, domain functions, effects, predicted splicing impacts, and the number of classic ILC and PILC cases in which they were observed. One notable polymorphism, a P72R amino acid change, was found in a significant number of both PILC (11 cases) and ILC (18 cases) samples and has been previously linked to breast cancer susceptibility in various populations.
Our analysis then focused on how these potentially disease-causing mutations were distributed between classic and pleomorphic ILC subtypes. A significantly higher proportion of pleomorphic ILC cases (8 out of 19, or 42.1%) displayed a potentially pathogenic mutation. In contrast, only 3 out of 22 classic ILC cases (13.6%) harbored such mutations, a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05). Additionally, two known and validated polymorphisms were identified across our samples. These polymorphisms showed no preferential distribution between the classic and pleomorphic ILC variants.
To understand the relationship between p53 mutations and protein expression, p53 immunostaining was conducted, with results detailed in Table 2. Variability in p53 immunohistochemistry scores was noted across both pleomorphic and classic cases. A threshold of 5% positivity was established to differentiate between normal, wild-type p53 expression and mutated p53 overexpression, given that normal breast tissue typically exhibits about 1% p53 staining. Out of 41 cases, 9 (21.9%) displayed p53 accumulation, while 6 cases showed no expression. These 15 cases were split between 6 classic ILC and 9 PILC (p = 0.157). This indicates that p53 accumulation was not uniquely linked to either the classic or pleomorphic ILC variant. Our investigation explored the relationship between p53 protein accumulation and the presence of mutations, a connection frequently highlighted in existing research. Among the cases exhibiting p53 accumulation as determined by immunohistochemistry, 5 out of 9 also possessed mutations (Table 5). Conversely, in cases where p53 accumulation was absent (wild-type or 0% staining), 4 out of 32 cases still presented with potentially pathogenic mutations (p = 0.01). Furthermore, one of the six cases showing no p53 staining had a pathogenic mutation. It is worth noting that the other five cases with 0% staining displayed one of the two identified polymorphisms (Table 3, Number 2). Overall, our findings revealed a significant association between p53 accumulation and potentially pathogenic mutations (Table 5).
First identified in 1982 as a form of infiltrating lobular carcinoma, pleomorphic ILC (PILC) presents with a distinct morphology characterized by significant nuclear atypia. Despite these unique morphological features, PILC shares similarities with classic ILC, particularly in its high incidence of multicentricity and bilaterality. Studies, such as the one by Weidner and Semple, have consistently shown that PILC is associated with more aggressive clinical behavior and a less favorable prognosis compared to classic ILC. Given these distinctions, the precise genetic mechanisms driving the evolution of each ILC variant remain unclear. The TP53 tumor suppressor gene, frequently altered in various human cancers, represents a compelling area of investigation in invasive breast cancer. While numerous studies have explored p53 mutation distribution and its correlation with immunohistochemistry in invasive carcinomas, there is a scarcity of data specifically addressing different ILC variants. Our research, therefore, aimed to analyze the mutational status of p53 in both classic and pleomorphic ILC. This investigation seeks to enhance our understanding of the molecular alterations within this gene that may contribute to the development of these subtypes and to explore its potential as a diagnostic marker for differentiating ILC from PILC.
Our investigation focused on 41 cases of invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), examining both classic and pleomorphic variants for p53 mutations and protein accumulation. Across these cases, we identified a total of eleven mutations, representing 26% of the samples. This frequency aligns with existing literature, which estimates the overall incidence of p53 mutations in breast cancer to be around 20% [28]. A notable finding was that nearly all these mutations were situated within the highly conserved DNA-binding domain of the p53 protein [18] (Table 2). A key distinction emerged when comparing the two ILC subtypes: pleomorphic ILC (PILC) exhibited a significantly higher frequency of potentially pathogenic p53 mutations (42.1%) compared to classic ILC (13.6%). Among these potentially pathogenic mutations, four out of eleven did not result in a change to the amino acid sequence, yet they have been previously documented in various solid tumors, including breast cancer [18]. These "silent" mutations warrant particular attention. It has long been understood that mutations not directly altering protein sequence can still impact protein production and function by disrupting transcription and translation processes [29]. For instance, silent mutations within codons containing splicing enhancers can interfere with the proper removal of introns from gene transcripts. Alternatively, they might compromise mRNA stability by preventing correct folding, thereby affecting both the rate of protein translation and mRNA degradation. Recent research by Lamolle et al. further underscores the significance of these non-transforming mutations, demonstrating that all such mutations recorded in the p53 somatic mutation database are predominantly found at conserved amino acid positions, suggesting their functional importance [30]. These mutations were typically solitary, not co-occurring with other p53 gene mutations, and often located within suspected splicing enhancers. Our study's silent mutations similarly presented as single events and were found either in an exposed residue of the DNA-binding domain or were predicted to induce a change in splicing. Moreover, silent p53 mutations can elicit a functional response by interfering with MDM2 binding. Specifically, single silent point mutations in p53 mRNA can disrupt its interaction with MDM2, leading to abnormal p53 synthesis and degradation [31]. This impaired binding has been shown to diminish p53 activity, reinforcing the idea that silent p53 mutations can indeed impact p53 function. Consequently, we believe our observations are significant, and in light of these recent discoveries, assumptions regarding non-transforming mutations should be approached with caution. Additionally, we identified two previously reported and validated polymorphisms present in a substantial number of our cases, without a preferential association with either ILC variant. These polymorphisms have been linked to cancer susceptibility [18]. Notably, the 72P > R amino acid change (Table 3) has been specifically associated with an increased susceptibility to breast cancer [32–36].
Our findings show a significantly higher incidence of pathogenic mutations in PILC, aligning with previous mouse model research where the inactivation of p53 and E-cadherin in mammary glands led to PILC development. Over half of these identified mutations are predicted to result in non-functional proteins, according to computational models and existing literature (Table 2). While functional predictions were unavailable for certain potentially pathogenic mutations, the presence of activating mutations would not be surprising. This is because mutated p53 can acquire novel functions in cancer, including the promotion of invasion and metastasis.
This study found a significant link between the buildup of p53 protein and pathogenic mutations, a correlation that held true across all ILC variants. While some research groups have also noted this positive relationship between p53 accumulation and mutations, others have not. The strength of this correlation is influenced by how a mutation affects the protein's half-life. Many mutated p53 forms persist longer in cells, making them detectable by antibodies, unlike some other mutations. This variability helps explain why p53 accumulation isn't consistently observed for all mutations. Specifically, a mutation could lead to a misfolded protein structure, such as silent mutations impacting splicing enhancers or mRNA stability, or a truncation that alters the antibody-binding site. We also encountered a case where p53 accumulated without any detected mutations, which could be due to mutations located in regions of the gene not examined in this study. Furthermore, a notable number of cases lacking p53 expression exhibited a polymorphism at residue 72. This particular mutation has been linked to increased protein degradation, potentially explaining the absence of p53 in these instances. Consequently, our findings, alongside existing literature, suggest that p53 immunostaining doesn't always precisely mirror genetic alterations, and conversely, a mutation doesn't invariably result in either p53 accumulation or its complete absence.
The pleomorphic variant of ILC, which is clinically more aggressive, exhibits a significantly greater incidence of p53 mutations than the classic variant. Given that p53 and E-cadherin inactivation in mice results in PILC development, it is plausible that p53 mutations contribute to PILC carcinogenesis.
