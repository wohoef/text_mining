A recent addition to risk stratification for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients is the EPICOR score, developed from the EPICOR registry to predict long-term mortality. This study aimed to assess the EPICOR score's ability to predict 2-year mortality in ACS patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The study enrolled 6087 consecutive ACS patients scheduled for PCI between January and December 2013. The simplified EPICOR calculator was used to determine their expected 2-year risk of death. Over the 2-year follow-up period, 68 patients (1.1%) died. The EPICOR score demonstrated moderate discriminatory power for mortality in the overall cohort, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.712. Its predictive performance was notably better in the ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) subgroup (AUROC 0.790) compared to non-ST-segment elevation ACS (NSTE-ACS) (AUROC 0.683). The EPICOR score was found to be noninferior to the updated Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) risk score. Patients were categorized into three risk groups: low (n=3382), medium (n=2547), and high (n=158). Kaplan-Meier curves revealed significant differences in mortality rates across these groups (0.6% vs. 1.3% vs. 9.5%, p < 0.001). Similar divergences were observed for major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) (11.8% vs. 12.3% vs. 19.6%, p = 0.014). Multivariate Cox analysis indicated that the medium- and high-risk groups had a 2-fold and 12-fold increased hazard of death, respectively, compared to the low-risk group. However, the EPICOR score was not a significant independent predictor of MACCEs after adjusting for confounding factors. In conclusion, the simplified EPICOR score exhibits fair discriminatory power for 2-year mortality in ACS patients undergoing PCI, with enhanced performance in the STEMI subgroup. It can serve as an independent predictor, aiding in the risk stratification of ACS patients.

Effective secondary prevention of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), a prevalent condition marked by significant global mortality and morbidity, has been a long-standing clinical challenge. Despite a shared underlying pathophysiology, individual patient responses to treatment and long-term outcomes exhibit considerable heterogeneity. To optimize the prevention of adverse events, current guidelines advocate for prompt risk stratification upon admission. This involves employing clinical prediction algorithms that integrate patient history, physical examination findings, electrocardiography (ECG), and cardiac troponin levels. Several established risk scores, including the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) scores, have been developed from extensive studies to predict both in-hospital and post-discharge ischemic risks.
The GRACE score is a widely recognized measure used in numerous studies for risk stratification in acute coronary syndrome (ACS). However, its predictive accuracy varies depending on the population and clinical context. To address these limitations, research has focused on enhancing the GRACE model, either by incorporating additional biomarkers or by developing entirely new models that include a broader range of clinical, laboratory, and angiographic factors. More recently, the EPICOR and EPICOR Asia studies introduced a novel risk-scoring model. This model, derived from data collected at admission, during hospitalization, and at discharge, demonstrated strong predictive capabilities for 2-year postdischarge survival in ACS patients, achieving a c-statistic of 0.80.
The predictive capability of the EPICOR score for 2-year mortality in Chinese patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains uninvestigated. This study therefore aimed to assess the EPICOR score's predictive value for 2-year mortality within a substantial cohort of Chinese patients.
A prospective observational study was conducted at a major cardiovascular center in China from January to December 2013. The study enrolled 6431 consecutive patients admitted with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), encompassing unstable angina, non-ST-elevated myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-elevated myocardial infarction (STEMI), all of whom were scheduled for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Within 24 hours of admission, comprehensive data including ECG, echocardiography, X-ray, blood tests, and baseline clinical information were collected. Patients were then followed for a period of two years. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to their intervention. Patients with missing data, in-hospital mortality, or those who did not complete the 2-year follow-up were excluded from the analysis.
Prior to the percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), patients were administered aspirin 300 mg and a loading dose of clopidogrel (300 or 600 mg) promptly. During the procedure, all patients received unfractionated heparin at a dose of 100 U/kg, with the use of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors based on the operator's discretion. Additional heparin sodium (1000 U) was given if the PCI extended beyond one hour. Experienced cardiologists interpreted coronary angiography findings, and stent implantation was indicated for vessels with over 70% stenosis. Following the procedure, patients were advised to take aspirin 100 mg daily indefinitely, along with either clopidogrel 75 mg daily or ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily for a minimum of one year post-PCI.
The EPICOR risk score originated from prospective international cohort studies, the EPICOR and EPICOR Asia registries, which included unselected consecutive patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS). An online calculator at www.acsrisk.org offers a 2-year mortality risk prediction. For this study, we employed a simplified version of this algorithm, which incorporates 11 independent variables. These include patient demographics (age, gender), ACS subtype, cardiac function (ejection fraction), laboratory values (serum creatinine, hemoglobin, fasting blood glucose), treatment details (interventions during admission), health status at discharge (EuroQol-5D score), and comorbidities (previous cardiac disease, COPD). It is important to note that the EPICOR calculator allows for "Unknown" entries for certain items. Since the EuroQol-5D score was not collected in our study, we utilized the "Unknown" option for all participants.
The updated GRACE score 2.0 utilizes nonlinear functions for improved long-term prediction of death or death/MI. This score incorporates eight variables: ST segment deviation, age, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, creatinine/renal failure, Killip class/diuretic usage, cardiac arrest at admission, and elevated necrosis biomarkers.
Follow-up evaluations were conducted for all patients at 30 days, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months via telephone, messages, or clinic visits managed by the hospital's follow-up center. The primary endpoint of the study was 2-year all-cause mortality. A secondary outcome was the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs), which comprised a composite of all-cause death, myocardial infarction (MI), revascularization, stent thrombosis, and stroke. Myocardial infarction was defined according to the Third Universal Definition. Revascularization encompassed both coronary artery bypass grafting and percutaneous coronary intervention. Stent thrombosis was categorized as definite, probable, or possible based on Academic Research Consortium criteria. Two independent cardiologists centrally assessed all adverse events, with any disagreements resolved through consensus.
Statistical analysis involved comparing continuous variables, presented as mean ± SD or median (P25, P75), using Student's t-test or ANOVA. Categorical variables, expressed as percentages, were analyzed with chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. The discriminatory power of the EPICOR and GRACE scores in predicting events was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs), with comparisons made via the Z-test. The EPICOR calculator stratifies patients into six risk groups, from low to very high. For this study, these were consolidated into three categories: low-risk (groups 1 and 2), medium-risk (groups 3 and 4), and high-risk (groups 5 and 6). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were employed to assess differences between these risk strata. Furthermore, Cox regression analysis identified independent predictors of long-term outcomes. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant for all analyses, which were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 20.0.
After excluding incomplete data, in-hospital deaths, and lost follow-ups, 6087 ACS patients were analyzed. The cohort's average age was 58 years, with 77% being male and 22.7% presenting with STEMI. Over the 2-year follow-up period, 1.1% of patients died post-discharge, and 12.2% experienced major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events.
Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants, categorized by death or survival status. Patients who died were older and more likely to have a history of cerebrovascular disease (p < 0.001) and prior PCI (p = 0.016). Other comorbidities and the type of ACS at admission showed no significant differences between the groups. Laboratory parameters at admission revealed some distinctions between the groups in markers of inflammation, such as white blood cell count and C-reactive protein, and in indicators of renal function, including blood urea nitrogen and creatinine. Nearly all patients, 98.3% (5887), received dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), with 97.1% continuing for at least one year and 29.4% still on DAPT at the two-year mark. Differences were also noted in the use of DAPT, calcium-channel blockers, and diuretics between the death and survival groups.
The EPICOR system estimated risks between 0.4% and 26.6%. Most patients fell into low- and intermediate-risk categories, as shown in Figure 1.
Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, presented in Figure 2, revealed a moderate predictive value for the EPICOR score concerning 2-year all-cause mortality, with an area under the curve (AUROC) of 0.712 (95% CI, 0.65-0.772; p < 0.001). This discriminatory power was enhanced when analyzing the STEMI subgroup, achieving an AUROC of 0.790 (95% CI, 0.676-0.903; p < 0.001) with a cut-off value of 4.45% offering 68.4% sensitivity and 81.3% specificity. Conversely, the score's performance was less adequate for NSTE-ACS patients, showing an AUROC of 0.683 (95% CI, 0.615-0.751; p < 0.001). Across all ACS patients and their subgroups, the EPICOR score demonstrated noninferior results when compared to the revised GRACE score.
Patients were stratified into low-risk (n=3382, 55.6%), medium-risk (n=2547, 41.8%), and high-risk (n=158, 2.6%) groups. Kaplan-Meier curves showed significant differences in mortality rates across these groups (0.6% vs. 1.3% vs. 9.5%, p<0.001). Major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) also diverged significantly (11.8% vs. 12.3% vs. 19.6%, p=0.014). Cox regression analysis identified the estimated percentage risk as an independent predictor of all-cause death, but not MACCEs.
This study evaluated the simplified EPICOR score's ability to predict long-term mortality in Chinese patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Key findings revealed that patients who died within two years post-discharge typically had more risk factors and comorbidities. The EPICOR score demonstrated moderate predictive power for 2-year mortality in the overall ACS population, with enhanced accuracy in the ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) subgroup. Furthermore, the EPICOR score performed comparably to the GRACE score. Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that the EPICOR score can serve as an independent predictor, aiding in the risk stratification of ACS patients.
The Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) risk score, in its initial version, was developed using data from a large international cohort of patients hospitalized with acute coronary syndrome (ACS). This score incorporates clinical history, physical examination findings, electrocardiogram (ECG) changes, and cardiac biomarkers to predict the likelihood of in-hospital death and mortality at six months post-discharge. Its widespread validation across diverse populations, ethnicities, genders, ACS subtypes, and follow-up durations, extending up to two years, led to its recommendation in clinical guidelines for early risk stratification upon admission. However, the GRACE score has limitations affecting its accuracy and applicability. The original registry predominantly comprised white patients of European descent, and subsequent advancements in medical and procedural management have surpassed the clinical context of its development. Our prior research indicated that the GRACE score demonstrated limited ability to discriminate long-term outcomes in patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) for death and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) of 0.661 and 0.520, respectively. A more recent iteration, GRACE score 2.0, has been introduced online, offering improved prediction for 1- and 3-year prognoses, and has undergone preliminary evaluation. In this study, we assessed the updated GRACE score and compared it with the EPICOR score. Despite incorporating different variables, the EPICOR score proved to be noninferior to GRACE score 2.0 across all ACS patient groups, including STEMI and NSTE-ACS.
The EPICOR and EPICOR Asia studies, drawing from extensive registry patient cohorts across diverse geographic and healthcare settings, aimed to develop a novel model for predicting long-term mortality. This model incorporated a broader range of variables than the GRACE model, including hemoglobin, fasting glucose, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interventional strategy, and importantly, educational attainment and quality of life. While the GRACE model does not include sex as a factor, and some research suggests higher mortality in females may be linked to elevated baseline risk profiles, the role of sex as a predictor remains a subject of ongoing discussion. In contrast to many prior findings, the EPICOR study identified male sex as an independent predictor of a 32% increased risk. Beyond established risk factors like ejection fraction and prior cardiac disease, certain parameters not directly indicative of cardiac status have also been found to contribute to ACS patient risk. For instance, studies by Stefan et al. and Su et al. have highlighted that patients with COPD experiencing acute myocardial infarction face heightened risks of in-hospital and post-discharge mortality, along with a reduced likelihood of receiving optimal treatments, leading to increased 1-year mortality. Furthermore, elevated blood glucose levels at admission have been consistently linked to adverse short- and long-term outcomes, with proposed mechanisms involving oxidative stress, platelet activation, and enhanced thrombin formation. Similarly, reduced hemoglobin levels at admission, another component of the EPICOR model, have been identified as a significant predictor of adverse events, potentially due to compromised oxygen delivery and demand balance.
This single-center study in China, utilizing unselected clinical patients and reflecting consistent real-world practice, found the EPICOR score to have moderate discriminatory ability for long-term mortality in the overall cohort. However, its accuracy improved significantly within the STEMI subgroup. When risk was categorized, the model effectively identified patients with a higher likelihood of adverse outcomes. The identified two-fold and 12-fold increased hazards of death in medium- and high-risk groups highlight the need for intensified medical management and more frequent follow-up to enhance patient prognoses.
The simplified EPICOR score demonstrated a comparatively poorer performance in our patient cohort than in the original EPICOR studies. This difference can likely be attributed to variations in baseline characteristics, clinical practices, and overall mortality rates. Specifically, our study included a lower proportion of STEMI patients (22.5%) compared to the EPICOR studies (49.2%). However, when the EPICOR score was applied to the STEMI subgroup, its performance was comparable to that observed in the original studies. Furthermore, while COPD was identified as a significant mortality predictor in the EPICOR model, its prevalence was lower in our cohort (2.3% vs. 4.5%), and univariate Cox regression did not reveal a significant association with mortality in our patients. Our study population also differed in treatment approaches; all participants underwent PCI, with most receiving drug-eluting stents, a figure higher than the less than 70% reported in the EPICOR studies. These factors collectively suggest that our study population was generally at lower risk, which aligns with the observed lower mortality rates. An additional consideration is the absence of EQ-5D data in our database. This limitation, where "Unknown" was used in the algorithm, may have led to an underestimation of mortality risk. Despite this, the EPICOR and GRACE scores showed no significant differences, even without EQ-5D data. Consequently, further research utilizing more comprehensive data is warranted to fully validate the EPICOR score.
Multivariate Cox regression analysis, similar to findings in the EPICOR studies, identified diuretic use at discharge as an independent predictor of 2-year mortality. Furthermore, patients with a prior history of cerebrovascular disease faced a twofold increased hazard of death. These observations may be partly attributed to inadequate evidence-based treatment or intervention, though underlying pathophysiological mechanisms require further investigation. This highlights that even with a comprehensive set of variables, not all prognostic factors are captured. For instance, recent studies suggest that angiographic data could offer additional predictive value for long-term outcomes after PCI.
Our study has several limitations. As a single-center observational study, it focused solely on patients undergoing PCI, which may not reflect broader national clinical practices. Furthermore, the mortality rate observed in our cohort was lower than that reported in the EPICOR and EPICOR Asia studies. This difference could be attributed to factors such as the widespread use of drug-eluting stents, a lower proportion of acute myocardial infarction cases, and preserved cardiac function indicated by normal ejection fractions. Additionally, the inability to perform full model calculations due to the unsystematic collection of certain required variables, and the absence of EQ-5D data, likely led to an underestimation of the simplified model's accuracy.
The simplified EPICOR score effectively predicts long-term mortality in ACS patients after PCI, particularly excelling at identifying high-risk individuals.
