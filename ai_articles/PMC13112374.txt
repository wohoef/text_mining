This study investigated the neural mechanisms of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression, a topic not well-understood despite CBT's widespread clinical use. We utilized graph theory and network science to examine transdiagnostic brain network changes. Our sample comprised 64 individuals: 33 patients (15 with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and 18 with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)) and 31 healthy controls. Patients underwent longitudinal fMRI resting-state scans before and after 12 weeks of CBT. Depression severity was assessed using the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS). Healthy controls provided baseline network data. We analyzed the normalized changes in within- and between-system connectivity, correlating these with normalized MADRS change scores using both univariate and multivariate regression. Penalized regression identified key predictors in a data-driven approach. Univariate analyses revealed that greater symptom reduction correlated with an increased functional role of the Ventral Attention (VA) system, characterized as an incohesive provincial system (decreased both between- and within-system connectivity). Multivariate analyses further highlighted between-system connectivity of the VA system as the most significant factor linked to depression improvement. These VA system changes align with brain controllability theories, which suggest attentional control systems like the VA system mediate integration or segregation among cognitive systems. Therefore, increased segregation of the VA system post-CBT (reduced between-network connectivity) might lessen emotional attention's influence on cognitive processes, potentially enhancing cognitive control.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) effectively treats both Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), offering comparable efficacy to antidepressants but with more lasting benefits. This broad effectiveness across depression-related mental disorders implies shared neural mechanisms underpin treatment response. However, research has not yet explored the common network mechanisms of transdiagnostic CBT response in both MDD and PTSD. Prior investigations into the brain mechanisms of CBT-induced improvement have typically focused on changes within cognitive control regions, examining MDD and PTSD independently after treatment. While studies have shown CBT alters cognitive control regions across various treatment forms, a comprehensive understanding of its network-level effects remains elusive.
Despite growing recognition of neuropsychiatric disorders as large-scale functional network dysfunctions, the network-level effects of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) remain largely unexplored. Previous research into CBT's neurobiological impact has typically focused on either broad network features or specific individual networks. Consequently, the precise mechanisms by which functional interactions among dynamic brain systems contribute to CBT treatment response are still unclear. This gap in understanding highlights the need for more comprehensive investigations using tools like graph theory and network science to elucidate these complex neural underpinnings of depression treatment.
Prior research has extensively documented baseline network differences in individuals with depression. Specifically, studies have identified distinctions within and between key brain networks, such as the affective, cognitive control, and default mode networks. Investigations have also focused on abnormalities in the interconnections forming these systems. However, much of this earlier work relied on *a priori* regions of interest rather than employing data-driven analytical approaches. More recent data-driven studies have begun to explore baseline network characteristics in depression. These investigations have revealed various alterations, including a loss of small-world network structure and substantial reorganization of community structure. Nevertheless, the application of basic graph theory metrics in these studies has yielded inconsistent findings. For instance, some research indicated reduced path length without changes in the clustering coefficient, while other studies reported significant shifts in community structure but no differences in path length or clustering coefficient. Further conflicting results include observations of increased local efficiency and modularity, or heightened local efficiency and modularity alongside disruptions in nodal centralities across numerous brain regions, particularly within the default mode and cognitive control systems. Crucially, while these studies illuminated baseline brain network differences in untreated depression, they did not investigate changes associated with therapeutic interventions. The present study addresses this gap by employing network science tools to pinpoint alterations in network architecture and function throughout the course of treatment.
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) alleviates depressive symptoms by modifying negative thought patterns and behaviors. This therapeutic effect is thought to involve rebalancing communication within functional brain networks. Recent advancements in network neuroscience offer novel tools for a more profound understanding of intricate brain functions. For instance, methods originally developed for analyzing complex systems like airline transportation networks and the internet have revealed how system roles emerge during typical neurodevelopment. These studies indicate that the development of functional brain organization is shaped by shifts in the balance between within-module and between-module (or system) connectivity. Such tools enable the definition of network roles based on a module's placement within a two-dimensional space, determined by its within- and between-system connectivity. For clarity in this paper, "network" refers to a graph, while "system" denotes a collection of brain regions, avoiding ambiguity from dual use of "network."
This study investigated the neural mechanisms of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression. Using network neuroscience tools on resting-state fMRI data, we analyzed a transdiagnostic sample of patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) or posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) undergoing 12 weeks of CBT. Our primary aim was to identify neural correlates of depressive symptom improvement across these diagnoses. We hypothesized that changes in higher-order cognitive network functions, specifically those reducing emotional signal salience, would correlate with symptom reduction. A data-driven analysis was also employed to pinpoint network features most predictive of improvement.
This study initially recruited 95 participants. This cohort comprised 64 patients, specifically 21 with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and 43 with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), all of whom began Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). Additionally, 31 healthy controls were included to establish baseline brain network functional roles. All participants were right-handed, English-speaking females between 18 and 55 years old. Demographic details are provided in Table 1. MDD and PTSD diagnoses were confirmed using the DSM-IV-TR and the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS), respectively. All PTSD patients had PTSD as their primary diagnosis, with a mean lifetime CAPS score of 78.16 ± 18.51, and reported trauma stemming from interpersonal violence, often involving multiple, long-duration episodes. Exclusion criteria for all participants included neurological disorders, current substance abuse, a history of psychotic, bipolar, or obsessive-compulsive disorders, current suicide risk, or recent use of psychotropic or CNS-active medications (within 3 weeks, or 5 weeks for fluoxetine). All study procedures were approved by the Human Subjects Committees of Washington University and the University of Missouri-St. Louis, and all participants provided informed written consent.
To evaluate changes in depressive symptoms and treatment response across diagnoses, the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) was utilized. This clinician-administered scale is recognized for its sensitivity to symptom fluctuations. Notably, 21.95% of participants with PTSD presented with a MADRS score of 18 or higher at baseline. To account for potential overlap between anxiety and depression and to specifically assess depression-related outcomes, the Anxious Arousal (AA) subscale of the self-report Mood and Anxiety Symptoms Questionnaire (MASQ) was also administered. All symptom and brain imaging data were collected concurrently on the same day. Patients underwent 12 weeks of manualized psychotherapy as part of the longitudinal treatment. Participants diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) received Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), while those with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) received Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT). A single, highly-trained CBT therapist (SEB) either delivered or supervised all treatment sessions. CPT, as outlined by the APA Clinical Practice Guidelines, is a specialized form of CBT proven effective in mitigating symptoms arising from various traumatic experiences.
After initial data quality checks and exclusions (detailed in the Supplement), our longitudinal imaging sample comprised 33 patients (15 with MDD, 18 with PTSD) who provided usable MRI scans at two time points. These patients, along with 31 healthy controls, underwent MRI scanning at Washington University. While patients and controls showed no significant age difference (p > 0.20), patients had notably lower education levels (15.39 ± 1.62 years) compared to controls (16.79 ± 2.19 years; p = 0.01). All patients were free of current psychotropic medication. PTSD participants received treatment at the University of Missouri-St. Louis's Center for Trauma Recovery, while MDD participants were treated at Washington University. Further demographic information is provided in the Supplement.
All subjects underwent imaging on a Siemens 3T Trio scanner, employing a consistent acquisition protocol. T1 image preprocessing specifics are documented elsewhere. Resting-state fMRI data were processed using a validated confound regression method, minimizing motion artifacts. Further details on fMRI preprocessing and motion correction are available in the Supplement.
Our analysis utilized 220 brain regions, or nodes, organized into 10 distinct systems. These nodes were derived from a larger, pre-existing parcellation of 264 regions, originally defined in a separate adult cohort. To optimize data quality and ensure robust network measures, we systematically refined this initial set. Specifically, 7 regions with inadequate coverage and 28 regions lacking clear network assignments were excluded. Furthermore, to enhance the stability of our network analyses, we removed small systems comprising five or fewer regions, which included the cerebellum (4 regions) and memory retrieval system (5 regions). The somatomotor mouth (5 regions) and somatomotor hand regions were merged to form a single motor network. Functional connectivity between these 220 selected regions was then assessed using wavelet coherence. This method, applied to resting-state fMRI data within the standard low-frequency range (0.01–0.08 Hz), was chosen for its advantages over Pearson's correlation coefficient, particularly in defining module roles within the network, building on prior research.
We represented each subject's functional brain network as a fully weighted adjacency matrix. In this matrix, brain regions were depicted as network nodes, and their functional connections were shown as network edges. To comprehensively examine connectivity patterns relevant to mood and anxiety disorders, we avoided applying arbitrary thresholds to this functional connectivity matrix. To mitigate residual motion and global effects not addressed during preprocessing, each subject's functional connectivity matrix was normalized by dividing it by its average value. This normalized matrix then served as the basis for all subsequent analyses.
A network's functional role is determined by two characteristics: its within-system and between-system connectivity. These are quantified mathematically. Within-system connectivity, denoted as Ri, is calculated as the sum of weighted adjacencies (Ãij) between nodes i and j within community Ci, divided by the square of the community's size (|Ci|). Between-system connectivity, Ii, is the sum of weighted adjacencies between nodes i within community Ci and nodes j outside Ci, divided by the product of the community's size and the total number of nodes (N) minus the community's size. Ã represents the mean-normalized weighted adjacency matrix. These two metrics establish a two-dimensional space, which is then divided into four categories: cohesive connector, cohesive provincial, incohesive connector, and incohesive provincial. The mean within-system connectivity across all systems separates cohesive from incohesive systems, while the mean between-system connectivity distinguishes connector from provincial systems. Each system is assigned to one of these four unique categories based on these definitions.
To investigate the neural underpinnings of depressive symptom reduction following CBT, we analyzed the relationship between changes in depressive symptoms and alterations in the network roles of various brain systems. Symptom improvement, measured by the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), and changes in network features (both within- and between-system connectivity) were expressed as normalized percentage changes: 100 × (postCBT-preCBT)/preCBT. Partial correlation analyses were conducted, controlling for age and head motion (quantified by mean framewise displacement). A system's network role was conceptualized within a two-dimensional space, encompassing both its within- and between-system connectivity. The statistical significance of these two-dimensional partial correlations was assessed using a two-dimensional permutation test. For individual dimensions, a non-parametric bootstrap approach was employed. This involved generating 1000 bootstrap samples by resampling, with replacement, 15 MDD and 18 PTSD patients from the original dataset for each system. Partial correlations were then re-calculated on these bootstrapped samples to establish 95% confidence intervals. To account for multiple comparisons, a false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied at a threshold of q = 0.05. This correction was applied to 10 tests for two-dimensional analyses (one per system) and 20 tests for one-dimensional analyses (10 for within-system and 10 for between-system connectivity).
Given the significant correlation between depression and anxiety symptom changes (r = 0.68, p < 0.001), we re-analyzed the data. To ensure the specificity of depression-related findings, we incorporated changes in anxious arousal (MASQ-AA score) as a covariate, alongside age and head motion.
To confirm the findings from univariate analyses regarding brain-symptom associations, a multivariate regression analysis was performed. This analysis aimed to identify the most influential predictors of change in depression severity, as measured by the normalized change in MADRS score. The predictors included normalized change scores for both within- and between-system connectivity across all ten identified brain systems, alongside normalized change scores for in-scanner head motion. This resulted in a total of 21 predictors. Prior to model fitting, age was linearly regressed out from both the predictors and the dependent variable to control for its potential confounding effects. The regression model employed was a linear regression with an elastic net penalty. This penalty combines the LASSO penalty, which promotes sparsity by selecting a subset of the most relevant predictors, and a ridge regression penalty, which addresses multicollinearity among predictors. Optimal tuning parameters for this elastic net model were determined through a grid search and subsequent cross-validation, as detailed in the supplementary materials.
To establish a baseline, we first characterized the average functional roles of each system in healthy controls. This delineation of baseline functional roles, detailed in Figure 2b and Table S1, showed strong consistency with prior research employing identical methodologies. This foundational step allowed us to subsequently investigate how alterations in system roles post-CBT correlated with improvements in depression.
After 12 weeks of CBT, patients showed significant improvement in depressive symptoms, with a 64.15% average reduction as measured by the MADRS scale [t(32) = -7.45; p < 0.001, d= 1.68]. Anxious arousal symptoms, assessed by MASQ-AA, also decreased by an average of 18.02% [t(23) = -2.98, p = 0.007, d = 0.88]. Analysis of functional brain network changes revealed a strong correlation between greater depression improvement and an increased functional role of the Ventral Attention (VA) system, specifically as an incohesive provincial system (two-dimensional permutation test: p = 0.005, FDR corrected). This effect was primarily driven by a significant decrease in the VA system's between-system connectivity (partial r = 0.71; p < 0.001, FDR corrected). A decrease in within-system connectivity also approached significance (partial r = 0.49; p = 0.005). Further specificity analyses confirmed this brain-symptom association was unique to depressive symptoms, as no other brain systems exhibited significant links with symptom changes.
To further understand the relationship between changes in depressive symptoms and the functional role of the Ventral Attention (VA) system in patients, we investigated baseline connectivity differences between patients and controls. A two-dimensional significance test revealed no significant differences in baseline connectivity between these groups across any system after FDR correction. Furthermore, at the group level, the overall functional role of the VA system did not significantly change following CBT (p > 0.1). However, a notable distinction emerged when examining specific connectivity changes: the between-system connectivity of the VA system showed a significant difference between patients and controls [t(52) = 2.25, p = 0.03, d = 0.62]. Additionally, VA within-system connectivity exhibited a marginal difference [t(52) = 1.71, p = 0.09, d = 0.49]. These findings suggest that the observed connectivity alterations in patients post-CBT are indeed associated with the treatment.
Elastic net regression coefficients, presented in Figure 4, were analyzed using the Covariance test to determine their significance, accounting for variable selection. This analysis revealed that only decreased between-system connectivity within the Ventral Attention (VA) system significantly correlated with improved symptoms after CBT. Other findings, detailed in the Supplement (Figures S5, S6, S7), did not show significant associations with CBT outcomes. Furthermore, the Covariance test indicated that the coefficient for motion was negligible and non-significant, confirming that in-scanner motion did not compromise the study's results. This emphasizes the specific role of VA system connectivity changes in therapeutic response.
Our study investigated how functional interactions within brain networks contribute to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) treatment response, focusing on network organization. We employed an integrative approach that mapped the functional role of brain systems in a two-dimensional space, based on the balance of within- and between-module connectivity. This method allowed for system-level analysis, enhancing biological interpretability and significantly reducing data dimensionality compared to region-level analyses. At baseline, we observed that the Ventral Attention (VA) system typically functions as an incohesive provincial system, with no discernible difference in its functional role between patients and healthy controls. However, individual variations in the VA system's functional role change were found to be behaviorally significant, correlating with the extent of symptom improvement. Through a data-driven analysis, we identified a primary link between reduced between-system connectivity within the VA system and decreased scores on the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) following CBT. This finding was consistent across both univariate and multivariate analyses, underscoring the critical importance of diminished VA between-system connectivity in CBT's therapeutic effects. These results align with the VA system's established role in processing salient stimuli, suggesting that CBT treatment may alter what is perceived as "salient." The increased segregation of the VA network from other systems, indicated by decreased between-system connectivity, appears to be a crucial component of the treatment's efficacy. Given the absence of baseline differences, this relationship likely represents a compensatory mechanism rather than a restorative one.
Resting-state fMRI functional connectivity (rs-FC) offers a means to uncover the brain's inherent network organization, which persists across diverse brain states. Research has demonstrated a strong relationship between task activation patterns and rs-FC. Specifically, studies analyzing various individual task states have revealed that the underlying task architectures, once task-specific effects are removed, closely resemble the rs-FC architecture. Although around 25% of connections may alter during task performance, rs-FC fundamentally represents the brain's default functional network state. This consistency across different tasks provides crucial insights into the brain's functional organization across a broad spectrum of mental activities and states. Understanding this intrinsic architecture is key to comprehending brain function.
The observed changes in the Ventral Attention (VA) system align with research on brain structural controllability. A previous study [43] established that attentional control systems, including the VA system, are positioned at the interface of network communities, where they regulate the integration or segregation of various cognitive systems. Our current findings indicate that a key system reorganization after treatment involved the VA system becoming more segregated, specifically demonstrating decreased between-system connectivity. This heightened segregation could lead to network interactions, such as those with salience and subcortical systems, becoming more disengaged from emotional attention. Such a shift might alleviate symptoms like low mood, anhedonia, irritability, and rumination. This interpretation is consistent with the notion of an overactive VA system at baseline [53], a characteristic linked to anxiety disorders and heightened attention to emotional stimuli. Furthermore, the strength of VA system resting-state functional connectivity has been correlated with threat-related attention bias in children with anxiety or depression history [54]. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has been shown to effectively reduce this threat bias [55], suggesting it addresses an overactive VA system.
Elastic net penalized regression, a technique increasingly applied in neuroimaging and psychiatry, was employed for multivariate analysis. This method, useful for identifying brain regions affected by depression and predicting clinical outcomes, allowed for simultaneous consideration of contributions from multiple brain systems to symptom changes. The analysis revealed that Ventral Attention (VA) between-system connectivity was the most prominent predictor of symptom improvement, and the sole significant predictor after applying the Covariance Test. This finding corroborates the univariate analysis results, underscoring the critical role of decreased VA between-system connectivity in the therapeutic response to CBT.
The study's findings, derived from PTSD participants who were all survivors of intimate partner violence, may not extend to other PTSD populations. Future research is needed to investigate how diverse populations influence network changes in brain-behavior relationships. Additionally, the absence of an active control group restricts the interpretation of observed network alterations as solely attributable to CBT. Factors like general clinician interaction or disease progression could also contribute to changes in Ventral Attention (VA) network connectivity post-treatment. Nevertheless, the significant difference in VA system between-system connectivity changes between patients and controls suggests a potential link to CBT's effects.
Our study has several limitations. We did not record illness duration or prior psychotropic medication use for patients, though none were on medication during the study. Future research should incorporate these factors to assess their influence on treatment efficacy. Furthermore, our sample size was relatively small and comprised heterogeneous patients. This limited our ability to perform diagnosis-specific analyses, particularly for detecting subtle effects. However, our transdiagnostic methodology allowed us to explore common neural mechanisms of CBT across MDD and PTSD. Replicating these findings with a larger, more homogeneous sample is crucial for future work.
This study revealed a shared transdiagnostic network restructuring following CBT across MDD and PTSD. These findings highlight common changes across diagnostic categories, supporting a dimensional approach to understanding treatment-related brain-behavior connections.
