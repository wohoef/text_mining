This study investigates the impact of CD147 phosphorylation on the aggressive characteristics of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells and the mechanisms involved. CD147, a tumor-associated antigen, is known to regulate tumor invasion and metastasis, but the specific role of its phosphorylation, which is altered in cancer, remained unclear. To address this, researchers utilized an in situ Duolink-proximity ligation assay (PLA) to detect CD147 phosphorylation and tandem mass spectrometry to pinpoint the phosphorylation sites. The influence of CD147 phosphorylation on HCC cell malignancy was assessed through scratch wound healing, transwell invasion, and cell cycle assays. RNA sequencing was employed to identify genes affected by CD147 phosphorylation. The findings revealed that serine-246 in the C-terminus of CD147 was phosphorylated in primary HCC tissues. In HCC-derived Huh-7 cells, however, serine-252 was identified as the primary phosphorylation site through serine to alanine substitution mutation analysis. When S246A/S252A mutants were re-expressed in CD147 knockout cells, a significant increase in migration and invasion capabilities was observed compared to cells expressing wildtype CD147. Cyclophilin A (CyPA) treatment led to a reduction in CD147 phosphorylation, while NIMA-related kinase 6 (NEK6) increased it. Furthermore, a substantial decrease in CD147 phosphorylation was noted in HCC tissues from patients with distant metastases. A low level of CD147 phosphorylation was also linked to elevated serum AFP levels, disease recurrence, and reduced overall survival. These results suggest that hypo-phosphorylated CD147 enhances the migration and invasion of HCC cells and is associated with a poor prognosis in HCC patients. Therefore, targeting this aberrantly hypo-phosphorylated form of CD147 could be a promising strategy for developing new therapies against HCC metastasis.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, ranking among the most prevalent malignancies [1]. Despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment, metastasis remains a primary driver of treatment failure and patient death [2–4]. The ability of cancer cells to spread to distant locations is governed by intricate cellular mechanisms, including microenvironmental shifts, enhanced cell migration and invasion, and various genetic changes and regulatory factor modifications [5]. Although significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular basis of HCC metastasis [6, 7], high relapse rates persist post-resection, with nearly all relapses stemming from metastases [8]. Overcoming HCC invasion and metastasis is crucial for improving patient survival and quality of life [9].
CD147, also referred to as EMMPRIN, is a versatile cell adhesion molecule. It is crucial for various normal physiological and pathological processes, such as reproduction, development, immune responses, infectious diseases, and malignant tumors [10]. Its overexpression is observed across numerous human malignant tumors, including HCC [11], where it is linked to different stages of tumor progression, especially metastasis [12]. Research, including our own, indicates that CD147 enhances tumor cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and survival [13–17]. High levels of CD147 in tumor cells and serum are considered a negative prognostic indicator [18, 19].
CD147 employs diverse molecular mechanisms in its function. Its extracellular Ig domain facilitates its role as a receptor for various molecules via trans-recognition. For instance, Cyclophilin A (CyPA), a common ligand, is secreted by bone marrow endothelial cells and draws in myeloma cells that highly express CD147. Beyond its receptor function, CD147 is recognized as a powerful inducer of extracellular matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), stimulating their production in both tumor and mesenchymal cells. Furthermore, CD147 contributes to epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), the reorganization of the cytoskeleton, and the formation of cellular structures like lamellipodia and invadopodia. However, the precise signal transduction pathway following trans-recognition and the involvement of CD147's intracellular domain remain unclear.
Proteins undergo post-translational modifications (PTMs) like glycosylation and phosphorylation, which are essential for precisely regulating their cellular functions. A thorough understanding of how PTMs relate to functional alterations is vital for deciphering the molecular mechanisms of specific cellular processes. For instance, prior research demonstrated that CD147 isolated from human lung cancer tissue was N-glycosylated, exhibiting both high-mannose and complex-type N-linked glycan structures. Further mutation analysis confirmed N-glycosylation's critical role in CD147 protein folding and the induction of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [24]. Phosphorylation is a key component of cellular signaling, allowing cells to temporarily modify protein localization, conformation, and interactions. Its dysregulation can contribute to disease, particularly cancer. Phosphoproteome analysis has identified that CD147 can be phosphorylated at serine residues 246 and/or 252 across diverse human tissues and cell lines, including those from muscle [25], liver [26, 27], B cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma [28], and lung cancer [29]. This highlights phosphorylation as a significant PTM for CD147. However, the exact function of CD147 phosphorylation in both healthy and diseased states remains to be elucidated.
Our findings indicate that CD147 undergoes phosphorylation in both primary HCC tissues and derived cell lines. Specifically, the primary phosphorylation sites were identified as S246 in HCC tissues and S252 in Huh-7 cells. When CD147 phosphorylation was eliminated through S246A/S252A mutations, we observed changes in the expression of genes linked to extracellular matrix remodeling, alongside increased cell migration and invasion, mediated by STAT3 and Akt signaling. Furthermore, a significant reduction in CD147 phosphorylation was noted in HCC cases with distant metastases. Low CD147 phosphorylation levels correlated with elevated serum AFP, disease recurrence, and diminished overall survival, suggesting that this hypo-phosphorylated CD147 could be a crucial biomarker for prognosis and a target for new HCC metastasis treatments.

The study utilized several hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines. Huh-7 cells were acquired from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB, Osaka, Japan), while HepG2 and SMMC-7721 cells came from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Shanghai, China). A Huh-7 CD147-KO cell line was developed using a CRISPR/Cas9 system. All cell lines, authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling, were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2.
From 2008 to 2012, 76 HCC tissue samples were obtained from the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital, affiliated with the Second Military Medical University. These specimens were histologically confirmed using hematoxylin and eosin staining. All patients gave written informed consent, and the hospital's Ethics Committee approved the study.
For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, 5 μm tissue sections underwent a series of preparatory steps. Initially, paraffin was removed from the sections, followed by antigen retrieval using a 10 μM citrate buffer at pH 6.0. The deparaffinized sections were then exposed to methanol containing 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 minutes. Subsequent to a PBS wash, the sections were blocked with serum for 30 minutes. An overnight incubation at 4 °C with an anti-CD147 antibody then occurred. Immunoperoxidase staining was performed next, utilizing a streptavidin-peroxidase kit and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine from Zhongshan Jinqiao Co., Beijing, China, to visualize the target proteins. Nuclei were counterstained with Hematoxylin. Two senior pathologists independently assessed CD147 expression levels based on the intensity and proportion of positive cells. A scoring system was applied: 0 for no staining, 1 for weak intensity or less than 30% intermediate intensity, 2 for over 30% intermediate intensity or less than 50% strong intensity, and 3 for over 50% strong intensity.
Cells were prepared for immunofluorescence by first harvesting them and allowing them to adhere for 24 hours to fibronectin-coated glass-bottomed dishes. Following two washes with PBS, the cells were fixed using paraformaldehyde in PBS. Permeabilization was achieved with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocking was performed with 1% BSA in PBS for one hour. Subsequently, cells were incubated with primary antibodies for one hour, washed twice with PBS, and then treated with Alexa 488-phalloidin and FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 minutes in the dark. DAPI was used to stain cell nuclei. After a final wash, an A1R-A1 confocal laser microscope was used for visualization.
The in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) was conducted following the manufacturer's instructions, utilizing the Duolink In Situ Detection Reagents Red kit from Sigma-Aldrich. This method allowed for the detection of CD147 phosphorylation. The experimental procedure began with seeding cells into glass-bottom dishes. After two washes with PBS, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature, then blocked for one hour at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were rinsed twice with PBS/0.1% Tween 20 (PBST). Primary antibodies, specifically mouse anti-CD147 (HAb18, 1:1000, developed in-house) and rabbit anti-phospho-serine (Abcam, ab9392, 1:100), were diluted in Duolink In Situ Antibody Diluent and incubated with the cells overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with Wash Buffer A, secondary probes (anti-mouse-PLUS and anti-rabbit-MINUS, oligonucleotide-conjugated) were diluted to a 1:5 concentration in antibody diluent. This secondary probe mixture was applied to each sample and incubated for an hour at 37 °C, followed by another wash with Wash Buffer A. Next, 40 μl of ligation solution was added, and the dishes were incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C. After a further wash with Wash Buffer A, 40 μl of amplification solution was introduced and incubated for 100 minutes at 37 °C. The cells were then rinsed three times with Wash Buffer B. For visualization, 50 μl of Duolink In Situ Mounting Medium containing DAPI was added to each sample. A fluorescence microscope was used to capture images, and the resulting PLA signals were analyzed with Duolink ImageTool software from Sigma-Aldrich. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) HCC tissue samples were processed as detailed in section 2.3 before the blocking step, and the remainder of the assay followed the manufacturer's protocol.
Cells were prepared for transfection by seeding 4 × 105 cells per well in a 12-well plate with complete medium the day before. Following transfection with Lipofectamine 2000, cells underwent a two-week selection period in 100 μg/ml G418. Resistant colonies were then isolated, combined, and grown under selective conditions for subsequent analysis.
Following the manufacturer's protocol, co-immunoprecipitation was conducted using a Pierce™ Co-IP Kit. Cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with 10 μg of affinity-purified antibody. Subsequently, Western blotting with specified antibodies analyzed the eluted samples.
Protein samples were first separated by denaturing SDS-PAGE, ensuring equal amounts were loaded. These separated proteins were then transferred onto PVDF microporous membranes. To prevent non-specific binding, the membranes were blocked using 5% nonfat milk in TBS-T. Subsequently, primary antibodies, diluted in TBS-T, were applied and incubated with the blots overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBS-T, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for one hour at room temperature. Finally, a ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System, coupled with Image Lab™ Software, was used for signal detection and analysis.
To assess cell migration, scratch wound healing assays were conducted. Cells were seeded into 12-well plates and grown to confluence after a 24-hour treatment period. A scratch was then made in the cell monolayer, which was subsequently washed with serum-free medium. Photomicrographs were captured at different time intervals to monitor wound closure.
To evaluate cell invasiveness, cells were prepared by trypsinization and resuspended in serum-free medium. These cells were then seeded at a density of 1 x 10^5 cells per well into the upper compartments of 24-well plates. These upper compartments consisted of polycarbonate filters (8 μm pore size, Millipore) pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Bioscience). The lower compartments of the plates were filled with 500 μl of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Following a 24-hour incubation, the chambers were transferred to new 24-well plates and stained with 0.2% crystal violet for 20 minutes. The number of cells that had invaded to the lower surface was subsequently quantified using a light microscope and subjected to statistical analysis.
NEK6-targeting siRNAs, designed and synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma Co., were introduced into cells using Lipofectamine 2000, following the manufacturer's protocol. The specific siRNA sequences are detailed in Table S1.
To evaluate the impact of CD147 phosphorylation on cell death and proliferation, Huh-7 CD147-KO cells expressing either wildtype or S246A/S252A mutant CD147 were analyzed. Apoptosis and cell cycle progression were measured using specific detection kits and quantified via flow cytometry (FACSAria system).
For quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagents. Reverse transcription employed a PrimeScript RT reagent kit. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was conducted with a SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit. Primers, synthesized by BGI, are detailed in Table S1.
For RNA sequencing, total RNA was initially isolated using Trizol reagent (Tiangen, Beijing). Its quality and quantity were then verified with an Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Only RNA samples exhibiting an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 7.0 and a 28S:18S ratio exceeding 1.8 were deemed suitable for further processing. Subsequently, CapitalBio Technology (Beijing, China) generated and sequenced the libraries. The NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) facilitated library construction. Specifically, poly(A) mRNA molecules were enriched from 1 μg of total RNA using a NEB Next Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). This mRNA was then fragmented into approximately 200 base pair segments. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed from these fragments using reverse transcriptase and random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis with DNA polymerase I and RNase H. The cDNA fragment ends underwent repair, including the addition of a single "A" base, before adapter ligation. The resulting library DNA was purified, amplified via PCR, and quantified using a KAPA Library Quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems, South Africa) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Finally, after qRT-PCR validation, these libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing, yielding 150-base pair reads on an Illumina HiSeq sequencer.
Following RNA extraction, sequencing quality was initially evaluated using FastQC (Version 0.11.5). Low-quality data were then removed via NGSQC (v0.4). The remaining high-quality reads were aligned to the human reference genome using HISAT2 and subsequently HISAT, both from Johns Hopkins University, USA, employing default parameters. Gene expression was quantified using Cuffquant and Cuffnorm (Cufflinks 2.2.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between samples were identified and analyzed by Cuffdiff, which utilized a geometric standardization method with per-condition and pooled discrete models. To determine statistical significance, thousands of independent hypothesis tests were performed on the DEGs, generating p-values that were then corrected using the FDR method. Genes were classified as significantly differentially expressed if their transcript abundance showed a ≥ 2-fold difference (|log2FC| ≥ 1). Functional annotation of these DEGs was achieved by performing BLAST alignment against ENSEMBL, NCBI, UniProt, GO, and KEGG databases, selecting the best matches. Finally, KOBAS 3.0 software was used for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs.
Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism V7.0 software, with all experiments performed in triplicate and results presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used for multiple groups, while Student's t-test was applied for two groups. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance, with specific thresholds denoted by asterisks (*** p < 0.001, ** p < 0.01, * p < 0.05) and '#' for p > 0.05.
Previous phosphoproteome analyses, utilizing mass spectrometry, have indicated that CD147 can undergo phosphorylation at Ser246 and/or Ser252 in various human tissues and cell lines. Building on this, our initial investigation focused on confirming CD147 phosphorylation within HCC tissues and cell lines. To achieve this, we first purified total CD147 from both HCC tissue and cell lysates through immunoprecipitation. Subsequently, we used an anti-phospho-serine antibody to detect phosphorylation modifications. Our findings revealed that CD147 was indeed phosphorylated in HCC tissues (Fig. 1a) as well as in Huh-7, HepG2, and SMMC-7721 cell lines (Fig. 1b). Crucially, a mouse IgG (mIgG) isotype control group, also tested with the anti-phospho-serine antibody, yielded no positive results, confirming the specificity of our detection. To further characterize CD147 phosphorylation in its natural cellular context, we employed an in situ Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA). This technique is designed to detect post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, directly within fixed biological samples. The PLA method relies on two paired antibodies: an anti-phospho-serine antibody and an anti-CD147 antibody (Fig. 1c). We validated this in situ approach by examining CD147 phosphorylation in FFPE HCC specimens. As a negative control, we analyzed an HCC tissue that lacked CD147 expression, a fact confirmed by IHC staining. In this control, minimal background staining was observed. In contrast, distinct red spots, indicative of phosphorylation, were detected in an HCC tissue that showed positive CD147 expression (Fig. 1d). We observed a similar pattern of red spots in Huh-7, HepG2, and SMMC-7721 cells. Conversely, Huh-7 CD147-KO cells exhibited very little staining (Fig. 1e). Collectively, these results definitively demonstrate that CD147 undergoes serine phosphorylation in both HCC tissues and cell lines.
The investigation into CD147 phosphorylation in HCC tissues began with the observation that serine phosphorylation was present. To pinpoint the exact phosphorylation sites, native CD147 was first isolated from HCC tissue using an anti-CD147 antibody (Fig. 2a). This isolated protein then underwent mass spectrometry analysis. This process revealed an approximate 80 Da mass shift on serine-246, indicating that CD147 is phosphorylated at this specific serine residue within HCC tissue (Fig. 2b). Prior research had suggested that CD147 could be phosphorylated at serine-246 and/or serine-252 across various tissues. Further sequence analysis confirmed that both serine-246 and serine-252 are evolutionarily conserved in CD147 across multiple species, including human, mouse, rat, chicken, rabbit, and Chinese hamster (Fig. 2c). To further evaluate the phosphorylation levels of these serines, specifically in Huh-7 cells, a series of CD147 mutants were engineered. These included S246A, S252A, and S246A/S252A mutations. The steady-state expression of these mutants was then assessed in Huh-7 CD147 knockout (KO) cells (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, both wild-type CD147 and the S246A/S252A mutants demonstrated comparable turnover rates (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, these mutations did not alter the membrane distribution pattern of CD147 (Fig. 2f), suggesting that the mutations did not significantly impact protein stability or localization. The phosphorylation level of each mutant was subsequently analyzed through immunoprecipitation using an anti-phospho-serine antibody. This analysis showed that wild-type CD147 displayed a higher degree of phosphorylation modification compared to the mutants. Notably, no phosphorylation was detectable for the S252A and the S246A/S252A mutants (Fig. 2g). To independently confirm these findings, an in situ Duolink-Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA) was conducted. As a control, Huh-7 CD147-KO cells exhibited a PLA signal below the detection threshold, confirming the specificity of the assay. When compared to wild-type CD147, the PLA signal for S246A phosphorylation was observed to be reduced. Moreover, the PLA signals for S252A and the S246A/S252A mutants were found to be low (Fig. 2h). Collectively, these experimental results strongly suggest that the primary phosphorylation sites of CD147 in both HCC tissues and cells are serine-246 and serine-252.
To investigate the biological implications of CD147 phosphorylation, experiments were conducted using Huh-7 CD147-KO (Fig. 2d) and HepG2 shCD147 (Fig. 3a) cells. These cells were transfected with either a wildtype CD147 construct or a CD147 mutant where serines 246 and 252 were replaced with alanine (S246A/S252A). The impact on cell migration was assessed using scratch wound healing assays. The results showed that restoring CD147 expression with the S246A/S252A mutant significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of both HepG2 shCD147 (Fig. 3b) and Huh-7 CD147-KO (Fig. 3c) cells when compared to their respective wildtype CD147 expressing controls. Similarly, transwell invasion assays were performed to evaluate cell invasiveness. These assays consistently demonstrated that the S246A/S252A mutants led to increased cell invasion in both HepG2 shCD147 (Fig. 3d) and Huh-7 CD147-KO cells (Fig. 3e) compared to the wildtype control. These findings collectively suggest that the phosphorylation of CD147 hinders its ability to promote migration and invasion in HCC cells. Furthermore, the study explored whether CD147 phosphorylation influences cytoskeleton rearrangements, a known regulatory function of CD147. It was observed that both the CD147 mutants and wildtype CD147 primarily localized to the cell membrane, with no notable changes in the cytoskeleton (Fig. 2f). The effects of CD147 phosphorylation on cell death and cell cycle progression were also examined. HCC cells expressing the S246A/S252A mutants exhibited similar proportions of apoptotic cells (Fig. 3f) and comparable cell cycle progression parameters (Fig. 3g) when contrasted with wildtype controls. This indicates that CD147 phosphorylation does not play a role in regulating CD147-mediated cytoskeleton rearrangements, apoptosis, or cell cycle progression.
To investigate the mechanisms by which non-phosphorylated CD147 enhances the migration and invasion of HCC cells, gene expression profiling was conducted. This involved using Huh-7 KO cells that were transfected with either wildtype CD147 or the S246A/S252A mutant CD147. The analysis identified 632 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 358 showing upregulation and 274 showing downregulation (Fig. 4a). A subset of fifteen of these DEGs was subsequently validated through qRT-PCR (Fig. 4b). Further analysis, specifically KEGG pathway analysis, revealed that extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and ECM degradation were the two most prominent pathways affected (Fig. 4c). This finding supports the established role of CD147 in ECM remodeling and its contribution to tumor invasion during cancer progression. Considering that CD147 is known to regulate various signaling pathways, including STAT3, Akt, and c-Jun, the researchers explored whether this regulatory function was dependent on CD147's phosphorylation state. It was observed that the expression of the S246A/S252A mutant CD147 led to a significant increase in the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 and Akt, a contrast to the effects seen with wildtype CD147 (Fig. 4d). This suggests that CD147 primarily promotes STAT3 and Akt signaling when it is in its non-phosphorylated form. To determine if the observed changes in gene expression profiles were indeed a consequence of activated STAT3/Akt signaling, Huh-7 CD147-KO cells, transfected with either the S246A/S252A mutant or wildtype CD147, were treated with specific inhibitors: Niclocide for STAT3 and LY294002 for Akt. The upregulation of LABM3 and COL12A1 induced by the S246A/S252A mutant was partially reversed when STAT3 was inhibited in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4e). Similarly, the downregulation of SPP1 caused by the S246A/S252A mutant could be reversed by dose-dependent inhibition of Akt (Fig. 4f). These results imply that while STAT3 and Akt signaling are involved, other pathways might also contribute to the gene expression alterations induced by non-phosphorylated CD147 in HCC cells.
Our investigation explored the involvement of STAT3 and Akt signaling in the increased cell migration and invasion observed with hypo-phosphorylated CD147. We had previously established that the S246A/S252A mutant of CD147, representing a hypo-phosphorylated form, enhanced both migration and invasion in HCC cells, alongside an increase in STAT3 and Akt activation. To determine if these signaling pathways were indeed responsible for the observed malignant behaviors, we conducted experiments using Huh-7 CD147-KO cells expressing the S246A/S252A mutant. When these cells were treated with Niclocide, a STAT3 inhibitor, or LY294002, an Akt inhibitor, the enhanced cell migration promoted by the S246A/S252A mutants was effectively reversed. Similarly, the heightened invasive capabilities of the S246A/S252A mutant-expressing cells were also abolished by treatment with either the STAT3 or Akt inhibitors. These findings collectively indicate that the S246A/S252A mutant CD147 facilitates cell migration and invasion, at least in part, by influencing STAT3 and Akt signaling pathways.
The study investigated whether CD147 phosphorylation is involved in signal transduction and how it is regulated. Serine-252, a key phosphorylation site, is situated within a conserved sequence, APLKSSGQ in human CD147 and APLKGSGT in murine CD147. This specific peptide sequence aligns with the typical consensus motif (L-X-X-S/T), a motif also present in TPP1, a protein known to be phosphorylated by NEK6. Given this structural similarity, the researchers hypothesized that NEK6 might be the kinase responsible for phosphorylating CD147. To test this, Huh-7 cells were transfected with siRNAs designed to target and silence NEK6. The results showed that reducing NEK6 expression through siRNA significantly decreased the level of CD147 phosphorylation. Further experiments were conducted to determine if there was a direct physical interaction between CD147 and NEK6. Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in Huh-7 cells revealed that NEK6 specifically co-purified with CD147. These findings collectively suggest that NEK6 directly interacts with CD147 and is responsible for phosphorylating the protein at serine-252 in Huh-7 cells. This establishes a regulatory link between NEK6 and CD147 phosphorylation.
We investigated the potential involvement of CD147's intracellular domain in signal transduction triggered by CyPA, given that CD147 functions as a cell surface receptor for extracellular CyPA and interacts with its own ectodomain. Our analysis of CD147 phosphorylation in Huh-7 cell lysates, following stimulation with CyPA for varying durations, revealed a distinct down-shift in the phospho-CD147 band after 10 minutes. Concurrently, we observed phosphorylation of p38, a known target of CyPA regulation, within the same timeframe. These findings suggest that CyPA stimulation leads to the dephosphorylation of serine-252 on CD147, implying that reversible phosphorylation of CD147 could play a role in CyPA-mediated signal transduction.
Given the observation that hypo-phosphorylated CD147 enhances cell migration and invasion, a study was conducted to investigate the relationship between CD147 phosphorylation and the prognosis and clinical characteristics of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The study involved a random sample of 76 HCC tumor tissue specimens, each accompanied by comprehensive clinical and follow-up data (Table 1). The expression of CD147 was evaluated using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, while the phosphorylation status of CD147 was determined through in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) analysis (Fig. 7a). A notable finding was that the group of patients with distant metastasis exhibited a lower PLA signal, indicative of reduced CD147 phosphorylation, compared to the group without metastasis (Fig. 7b). Interestingly, despite this difference in phosphorylation levels, no significant variation in overall CD147 expression was observed between these two groups (Table 2). Further analysis revealed that patients with lower serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels (defined as AFP ≤ 87 μg/l) displayed higher PLA signals, suggesting greater CD147 phosphorylation (Fig. 7c). Conversely, the group of patients who experienced HCC recurrence showed lower PLA signals for CD147 phosphorylation when compared to those without recurrence (Fig. 7d). A Pearson correlation analysis was performed to quantify these relationships. The results indicated a significant negative correlation between high levels of phosphorylated CD147 expression and several adverse clinical indicators: serum AFP levels (p = 0.0016), distant metastasis (p = 0.0004), and disease recurrence (p = 0.0461). However, no significant correlation was found between CD147 phosphorylation levels and other factors such as patient age (Table 1). To assess the impact on patient survival, a Kaplan-Meier analysis was conducted. This analysis demonstrated that low PLA signals of CD147 phosphorylation were associated with a poorer overall survival rate (Fig. 7e). In conclusion, these findings collectively suggest that reduced CD147 phosphorylation levels are linked to the occurrence of distant metastasis and an unfavorable prognosis in HCC patients.
Our research identified a previously unrecognized functional phosphorylation of the CD147 protein, which is crucial for regulating its activity. This finding aligns with prior phosphoproteome studies in other human tissues and cell lines, demonstrating that CD147 undergoes varying degrees of phosphorylation in primary HCC tissues and cell lines. Therefore, phosphorylation, alongside glycosylation, is a significant post-translational modification for CD147 in these contexts. Subsequent analysis showed that patients lacking distant metastases exhibited higher levels of phosphorylated CD147 compared to those with metastases, suggesting that reduced CD147 phosphorylation is more effective at driving HCC metastasis.
CD147 undergoes two post-translational modifications: glycosylation and phosphorylation. Previous research, using mass spectrometry on CD147 from human lung cancer tissue, identified N-glycosylation in native eukaryotic CD147, featuring high-mannose and complex N-linked glycan structures. This glycosylated form was more effective at stimulating MMP secretion than its non-glycosylated counterpart. Furthermore, a mutation at asparagine-152 highlighted its importance for CD147's correct folding in the ER, its stability, and its role in promoting HCC metastasis. In contrast, our study found that phosphorylation of CD147 does not impact its protein folding, stability, or membrane translocation. Instead, phosphorylation acts as a dominant negative regulator, diminishing CD147's pro-metastatic effects on HCC cell migration and invasion. This suggests that the non-phosphorylated state of CD147 is the active form driving tumor progression.
CD147 is a transmembrane protein characterized by a lengthy extracellular domain and a concise intracellular domain. While the ectodomain of CD147 has been extensively studied for its crucial role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis, primarily through interactions with other transmembrane proteins like integrins, CD44, and CD98, our prior research indicated that both the ectodomain and intracellular domain are essential for CD147's influence on MMP induction and metastasis-related processes. Specifically, the ability of CD147 to affect adhesion and invasion in HCC cells was lost when the C-terminal fragment was truncated. Our current findings demonstrate that when the intracellular domain of CD147 is dephosphorylated, it boosts Akt and STAT3 signaling, potentially accelerating HCC cell migration and invasion. This highlights the significant functional role of CD147's intracellular domain, although the precise molecular mechanisms by which it impacts HCC metastasis warrant further investigation.
The serine/threonine kinase NEK6, a homologue of the *Aspergillus nidulans* NIMA protein, was investigated for its role in CD147 phosphorylation. While NEK6's abundance and activity increase during mitosis, with activation dependent on Serine-206 phosphorylation, its substrates are largely unknown despite its importance in anaphase progression. Our findings demonstrate that NEK6 directly interacts with and phosphorylates CD147. Specifically, silencing NEK6 in Huh-7 cells resulted in a reduction of CD147 phosphorylation, suggesting that CD147 is a substrate for NEK6. Conversely, CyPA, a known ligand for CD147, was observed to decrease CD147 phosphorylation upon binding to its extracellular domain. This evidence points to CD147 phosphorylation as a regulatory mechanism potentially involved in downstream signal transduction, though its precise role in NEK6 or CyPA-mediated cellular functions requires further exploration.
Our findings highlight the significant role of hypo-phosphorylated CD147 in HCC progression. This suggests that hypo-phosphorylated CD147 could be a useful prognostic biomarker and a target for new therapies against HCC metastasis.
