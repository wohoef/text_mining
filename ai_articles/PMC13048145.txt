This meta-analysis investigated the impact of HbA1c control on anti-tuberculosis (TB) treatment outcomes in patients co-infected with diabetes mellitus (DM). The study was prompted by existing research suggesting poorer outcomes for diabetic TB patients. To gather relevant data, a comprehensive search was conducted across both English and Chinese academic databases, including PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, WanFang, SinoMed, and VIP. The analysis focused on studies examining the link between HbA1c levels and specific anti-TB treatment effects: sputum conversion rate (SCR) and TB focus absorption. Data analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 software. A total of 12 studies met the inclusion criteria. Among these, five reported on SCR at 2 months, seven on SCR at 3 months, and seven on TB focus absorption. For 2-month SCR, five studies indicated an odds ratio (OR) of 2.14 (95% CI: 0.84-5.43) when comparing controlled (HbA1c <7.0) and uncontrolled (HbA1c ≥7.0) diabetes groups. However, a more significant finding emerged from seven studies focusing on 3-month SCR, where controlled diabetics exhibited a higher SCR (OR 3.39, 95% CI: 2.12-5.43) compared to their uncontrolled counterparts. Furthermore, seven of the included studies revealed a notable difference in TB focus absorption between the controlled and uncontrolled diabetes groups, with an OR of 2.69 (95% CI: 1.91-3.79). In conclusion, the findings suggest that HbA1c control levels significantly affect the 3-month SCR and TB focus absorption by the end of the intensive anti-TB treatment phase. This underscores the critical importance of managing HbA1c and glucose levels in patients suffering from both TB and DM.

The global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) is increasing, while tuberculosis (TB) continues to be a major global health concern, contributing significantly to illness and death. Research has established a link between DM and the onset of TB. A systematic review of cohort studies, for instance, revealed that individuals with DM face roughly three times the risk of developing TB compared to those without DM. Poorly managed glucose levels can heighten susceptibility to various infectious diseases, including TB, and can also diminish the efficacy of anti-TB treatments in patients with both conditions. Therefore, glucose monitoring is crucial for DM-TB patients. While TB symptoms in these patients correlate with glucose control, glucose levels are highly variable and easily affected by numerous factors. Consequently, TB manifestations, though reflecting immediate glucose levels, are inadequate for assessing long-term diabetes control. HbA1c, formed by the gradual and irreversible binding of glucose to hemoglobin in red blood cells, offers a more reliable indicator of blood glucose management. Unlike fluctuating random or fasting glucose readings, HbA1c provides an average blood glucose level over two to three months. This makes HbA1c testing valuable for DM diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis. Previous studies have offered conflicting findings regarding the impact of HbA1c control on anti-TB treatment outcomes. Some research suggests that diabetics with well-controlled HbA1c exhibit lower positive smear rates and improved culture conversion after two months of anti-TB therapy. Conversely, other studies have found no significant difference in treatment duration or culture conversion rates between controlled and uncontrolled diabetic patients. Given this lack of consensus on whether HbA1c levels are the primary determinant of anti-TB treatment effectiveness, this meta-analysis aimed to investigate the influence of diabetes control status, as measured by HbA1c, on anti-TB treatment outcomes.
To be included, studies had to be randomized controlled trials, case-controlled, or cohort designs investigating HbA1c control, sputum smear positivity, and TB focus absorption. Participants needed a dual diagnosis of both diabetes mellitus and tuberculosis, with available data on HbA1c, sputum outcomes, and TB focus absorption. The primary outcomes focused on sputum conversion rates at 2 and 3 months of anti-TB treatment, with controlled DM defined as HbA1c <7.0% and uncontrolled as HbA1c ≥7.0%. A secondary outcome was TB focus absorption after the intensive treatment phase. Additionally, all included studies had to utilize a standard TB therapy regimen.
Given that HbA1c reflects blood glucose levels over two to three months, and intensive anti-TB regimens also span two to three months, sputum conversion typically occurs after two months of treatment. Therefore, sputum conversion data were collected at both two and three months.
Studies were excluded if they involved animal experiments, systematic reviews, lectures, or comments. We also omitted duplicate publications and those with incomplete medical records, specifically if they lacked reporting on at least one specified TB outcome. Patients with congenital or secondary immunodeficiency (e.g., HIV), conditions requiring long-term glucocorticoid use (e.g., autoimmune diseases, organ transplantation), drug-resistant TB, or extrapulmonary TB were excluded. Additionally, pregnant and lactating women were not included, nor were reports with fewer than 10 cases.
Each study adhered to institutional, national, or comparable ethical standards, including the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. All individual participants provided informed consent for their involvement in the research.
A comprehensive search strategy, combining both electronic and manual methods, was employed. This involved searching major databases like PubMed, Embase, and CNKI, along with their citations, for studies published up to April 1, 2016. The retrieved articles and their references were then thoroughly reviewed.
To identify relevant studies, a comprehensive search was conducted using keywords such as "tuberculosis," "Mycobacterium tuberculosis," "tuberculosis infection," "tuberculosis disease," "diabet*," "HbA1c," and "glycated hemoglobin." The search was limited to English and Chinese publications. Titles and abstracts were reviewed to select studies investigating HbA1c's influence on TB outcomes. Unpublished data were excluded, given the extensive existing research on DM-TB, which suggests a low likelihood of publication bias. All specified keywords were combined with free-text terms during the search.
Prior to study commencement, two independent reviewers extracted data using a pre-designed form and assessed methodological quality. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.
To evaluate study quality, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were assessed using the Cochrane systematic review's risk of bias tools. This assessment covered six areas: randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential biases. For cohort and case-control studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was employed. The NOS evaluates four aspects of the research, with one entry for group comparability and three for outcome measures. Studies scoring less than six out of a possible nine points on the NOS were classified as B-class, while those with higher scores were designated A-class.
The meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 software from the Cochrane Collaboration. To quantify the effect size for count data, such as sputum conversion rate (SCR) or TB focus absorption, the odds ratio (OR) was employed, along with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance for OR values was determined using the Z (U) test, with a p-value below 0.05 indicating significance. Statistical heterogeneity between studies was evaluated with the Chi-square-based Q test. If the Q test yielded a p-value greater than 0.05 or an I2 value less than 50%, a fixed-effect model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was applied due to good homogeneity. Conversely, if heterogeneity was present, a random-effect model (DerSimonian–Laird method) was utilized. Potential sources of homogeneity were further explored through sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Sensitivity analysis involved sequentially removing studies to assess result stability, while publication bias was examined using funnel plots.
Following a predefined search strategy, 1106 studies were initially identified. After removing duplicates and reviewing abstracts, 12 studies were ultimately selected for inclusion (Fig. 1). These studies, all originating from Asia, comprised nine randomized controlled trials (RCTs), two cohort studies, and one case-control study (Tables 1, 2, and 3). Quality assessment revealed that eight of the nine RCTs carried a moderate risk of bias, with one exhibiting a high risk. Among the non-RCTs, two were rated as A-grade, and one as B-grade.
This study evaluated the impact of HbA1c control levels on anti-tuberculosis (TB) treatment outcomes, specifically sputum conversion rates (SCR) and TB focus absorption. The overall effect size was quantified using Odds Ratios (ORs), with individual study weights depicted in Figures 2 and 3. For the 2-month SCR, five studies were analyzed, encompassing 348 cases of uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c ≥7.0) and 698 cases of controlled diabetes (HbA1c <7.0). All patients received the 3HRZE/6HR treatment regimen. Due to significant heterogeneity (p < 0.05), a random-effects model was employed. The pooled random-effects OR for SCR between controlled and uncontrolled diabetes was 2.14 (95% CI: 0.84-5.43). Since the 95% CI included an OR of 1, and the Z-value was 1.6 with p = 0.11 (> 0.05), no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups. This suggests that after 2 months of anti-TB therapy, there was no conclusive evidence that the SCRs in the well-controlled group were higher than in the uncontrolled group (77.64% vs 73.63%, respectively), as illustrated in Figure 2. Further investigation into the literature characteristics involved cumulative analyses based on quality assessment. This revealed instability and significant fluctuations in the OR point estimates and 95% CIs. Despite the overall non-significance, two of the five studies individually demonstrated a statistically significant difference between the groups. Sensitivity analysis indicated that factors such as small sample size and ambiguous treatment regimen descriptions did not correlate with the observed SCRs. Successive exclusion of these two articles did not alter the pooled results, which remained consistent with the initial findings (pooled OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 0.44-3.87), thus affirming the reliability of the overall result. However, one particular study among the five reported a lower 2-month SCR in the uncontrolled group compared to the controlled group (OR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.31-1.03), though this difference was not statistically significant. A subsequent sensitivity analysis, excluding this specific study, revealed that the remaining four studies showed statistically significant results (pooled OR: 3.17, 95% CI: 2.01-4.99). These four studies exhibited homogeneity, and their findings contradicted the broader meta-analysis. The discrepancy was attributed to several factors in the excluded study, including a substantial disparity in sample size (82 controlled vs. 428 uncontrolled cases), incomplete sputum results for some patients during follow-up, and the fact that the uncontrolled group in this study comprised over half of the total uncontrolled participants across all five studies (428/698). These issues, particularly the sample size imbalance and incomplete data, were identified as potential sources of heterogeneity. Moving to the 3-month SCR, seven articles met the inclusion criteria, involving 205 controlled and 201 uncontrolled patients. A heterogeneity test (p = 0.99) indicated homogeneity, leading to the selection of a fixed-effect model for the meta-analysis. The controlled group exhibited a higher SCR than the uncontrolled group (82.93% vs 58.71%). The fixed pooled OR was 3.39 (95% CI: 2.12-5.43), indicating a statistically significant difference in 3-month SCRs between the two groups (p < 0.05). While all seven studies showed higher SCRs in the controlled groups, four of them individually did not reach statistical significance. Sensitivity analysis, involving sequential exclusion of studies, consistently yielded the same results, reinforcing the reliability of this finding (Figure 3). Regarding TB focus absorption at the end of the intensive anti-TB treatment phase (2-3 months), seven studies compared outcomes between controlled and uncontrolled groups, involving 275 controlled and 360 uncontrolled patients. With no statistical heterogeneity (p = 0.99), a fixed-effect model was applied. The fixed pooled OR was 2.69 (95% CI: 1.91-3.79), which was statistically significant (Figure 4). This result demonstrated that TB focus absorption was superior in the HbA1c controlled group compared to the uncontrolled group. Further breakdown of TB focus absorption revealed specific findings for different treatment durations. For the 2-month anti-TB treatment, three studies were analyzed, comprising 170 controlled and 242 uncontrolled patients. These studies showed no heterogeneity (p = 0.59), and the fixed-effect model yielded a pooled OR of 2.64 (95% CI: 1.71-4.08), indicating a statistically significant difference in TB focus absorption between the groups (Figure 5). For the 3-month anti-TB therapy, four studies were included, with 105 controlled and 118 uncontrolled patients. Again, no statistical heterogeneity was found (p = 0.77), and the fixed-effect model resulted in a pooled OR of 2.76 (95% CI: 1.57-4.86) (Figure 6). Sensitivity analyses for all these TB focus absorption results consistently showed no changes, confirming their reliability. Finally, an assessment of publication bias using a funnel plot for the 3-month SCR did not reveal any evidence of bias (Figure 7).
Diabetes Mellitus (DM) and Tuberculosis (TB) are both chronic debilitating diseases that mutually exacerbate each other. Research conducted globally has identified several mechanisms through which diabetes negatively impacts TB patients. Firstly, elevated blood glucose levels or insufficient insulin production create an environment conducive to the survival and colonization of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, thereby hindering TB control. Secondly, compromised immune function in diabetic patients, characterized by impaired monocyte chemotaxis, reduced alveolar macrophage activation, decreased interferon-gamma levels, and altered type I cell hormone expression, diminishes the body's capacity to resist and eliminate *M. tuberculosis*. This allows the bacteria to spread more easily, further impeding TB management. Thirdly, an interaction between rifampicin, a key anti-TB drug, and hypoglycemic medications can reduce rifampicin absorption in DM patients. Evidence suggests that rifampicin's efficacy is dependent on its peak concentration and exposure time, which are often lower in TB patients with DM compared to those without. This directly compromises the effectiveness of TB treatment and can contribute to the development of acquired drug resistance. Fourthly, the higher incidence of drug-resistant TB in patients with co-existing DM complicates treatment, making standard short-course chemotherapy less effective. These factors highlight a vicious cycle where immune system dysregulation and a decline in respiratory immunity, often seen in combined DM and TB cases, are compounded by negative interactions between the therapeutic drugs for both conditions. This study specifically investigates the role of HbA1c, a key indicator of long-term blood glucose control, in influencing TB treatment outcomes. While the initial two months of anti-TB treatment for DM-TB patients did not show a clear correlation between HbA1c levels and the sputum conversion rate (SCR) or TB severity, a direct influence emerged as treatment progressed. For anti-TB therapy extending beyond two months, patients with well-controlled HbA1c levels demonstrated better focal absorption of TB lesions compared to those with poorly controlled HbA1c. This suggests that maintaining a low HbA1c level is beneficial for therapeutic effects and overall outcomes. Therefore, in the context of pulmonary TB complicated by diabetes, effective diabetes management, particularly HbA1c control, is crucial. It aids in improving patient metabolism, regulating immune system functions, and bolstering patient immunity. Furthermore, an appropriate extension of anti-TB therapy duration may be necessary in these cases. In essence, controlling blood glucose levels is paramount for diabetic patients, as it directly impacts the onset, progression, treatment, and prognosis of TB. HbA1c serves as a vital indicator for monitoring diabetes control and guiding the development of therapeutic strategies for both diabetes and TB. Regular HbA1c monitoring (every 1-3 months) during anti-TB therapy allows for timely assessment of diabetes control and necessary adjustments to blood glucose management plans.
Several limitations impacted this study's findings. A primary concern was the limited sample size, with the majority of included research originating from a single country, leading to inconsistencies in study quality. Furthermore, the methodologies regarding randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding were often inadequately described. The extended duration of TB treatment introduced a susceptibility to numerous confounding variables, and patient adherence to medication, which was not reported in any of the 12 included studies, is a critical factor influencing treatment outcomes. For instance, one survey indicated that only 54% of TB patients maintained compliance over a year. Although the initial research plan aimed for broader geographical representation, ten of the twelve studies ultimately came from China, with the remaining two from Taiwan and South Korea. These regional biases could compromise the generalizability and reliability of the research. Collectively, these issues impede the widespread applicability and acceptance of this study's conclusions.
Ultimately, the current understanding emphasizes a concurrent treatment approach for both tuberculosis and diabetes. While standard anti-TB therapy is essential, consistent management of blood glucose, particularly through HbA1c monitoring, is equally crucial for diabetic patients. The goal is to achieve long-term stability in blood glucose and HbA1c levels, leading to effective control of both conditions. Nevertheless, methodological constraints within this research temper the confidence in the meta-analysis findings. Future, more robust studies are needed to definitively confirm the influence of HbA1c levels on pulmonary TB treatment outcomes.
