An application was submitted by Syngenta Switzerland to modify existing maximum residue levels (MRLs) for the active substance azoxystrobin in chervil, rhubarb, linseed, safflower, and borage seeds. Greece, acting as the evaluating Member State (EMS), received this application under Article 6 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. Following Article 8 of the same regulation, Greece prepared an evaluation report, which was then sent to the European Commission and subsequently to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). EFSA's assessment found the provided data sufficient to propose an MRL of 0.4 mg/kg for linseed, safflower, and borage seeds, supporting their use in both northern and southern Europe. For chervil, EFSA determined that the submitted residue data indicated a need for a lower MRL than currently established in EU legislation, thus no change was proposed. An MRL was not proposed for rhubarb because the residue trials submitted did not align with the intended good agricultural practices (GAP). Based on its risk assessment, EFSA concluded that the proposed uses of azoxystrobin on chervil, linseed, safflower, and borage seeds are unlikely to pose a risk to consumer health, as the anticipated exposure levels would not exceed established toxicological reference values.

Greece, acting as the evaluating Member State, received an application from Syngenta Switzerland to amend existing maximum residue levels (MRLs) for azoxystrobin. This request, made under Article 6 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, concerned chervil, rhubarb, linseed, safflower, and borage seeds. Following this, Greece prepared an evaluation report, as stipulated by Article 8 of the same regulation. This report was subsequently submitted to the European Commission and then forwarded to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on November 26, 2015.
EFSA's assessment relies on the evaluation report from the EMS, the DAR from Directive 91/414/EEC, and peer review conclusions on pesticide risk. It also incorporates previous reasoned opinions from Article 12 MRL reviews and Article 10 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005.
Azoxystrobin's toxicological profile was reviewed under Directive 91/414/EEC. The available data supported establishing an acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0.2 mg/kg body weight per day. An acute reference dose (ARfD) was not considered necessary.
EFSA confirmed that azoxystrobin metabolism in fruit, cereal/grass, and pulses/oilseeds crops, following foliar application, has been adequately studied. The residue definition for both enforcement and risk assessment was established as azoxystrobin. Therefore, the derived residue definitions are considered applicable for the crops in question.
EFSA has determined that the supervised residue trials submitted are adequate to propose a Maximum Residue Level (MRL) of 0.4 mg/kg for linseed, safflower, and borage seeds. For chervil, the existing MRL does not require modification based on the trial data. However, residue trials for rhubarb were insufficient to support the intended Good Agricultural Practice (GAP). The availability of adequate analytical enforcement methods, capable of detecting azoxystrobin residues at a Limit of Quantification (LOQ) of 0.01 mg/kg, has also been confirmed.
Studies on azoxystrobin residues in processed commodities were not submitted. They are not required because the residue contribution from the crops in question to the total theoretical maximum daily intake is less than 10% of the acceptable daily intake.
Residue levels of azoxystrobin in rotational crops were examined during the peer review. Available data suggest that significant residues are improbable in subsequent crops, provided the compound is applied according to the proposed good agricultural practices.
EFSA determined that azoxystrobin residues in linseed, safflower, and borage insignificantly contribute to the livestock dietary burden. Therefore, no amendment to MRLs for animal products is deemed necessary.
Using EFSA's PRIMo model (revision 2), the consumer risk assessment found no long-term or acute intake concerns. The highest chronic intake for azoxystrobin was 21% of the ADI, observed in a Dutch child. Residues from the crops in question contributed negligibly, less than 0.1% of the ADI, to overall consumer exposure.
EFSA has concluded that the proposed use of azoxystrobin on chervil, linseed, safflower, and borage seeds will not lead to consumer exposure exceeding toxicological reference values, thus posing no public health risk. Consequently, EFSA recommends amending the existing Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) as detailed in the summary table, which includes the enforcement residue definition for azoxystrobin.
For chervil, no change to the existing MRL is proposed as submitted data suggest a lower value. Rhubarb has no proposed MRL due to lack of GAP-compliant data. Linseed's MRL is proposed at 0.4 mg/kg.

Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 governs pesticide Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) within the EU. Article 6 mandates that any party with a legitimate interest or seeking authorization for plant protection products, under the now repealed Council Directive 91/414/EEC, must submit an application to a Member State to modify an MRL. This modification request must adhere to the procedures outlined in Article 7 of the Regulation.
An application to modify existing maximum residue levels (MRLs) for azoxystrobin in chervil, linseed, rhubarb, safflower, and borage seeds was submitted to Greece, the evaluating Member State (EMS), by Syngenta Switzerland. Following notification to the European Commission and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Greece conducted an evaluation in accordance with Article 8 of the relevant Regulation. The completed evaluation report was sent to the European Commission and EFSA on November 26, 2015. This application was subsequently registered by EFSA under reference number EFSA-Q-2015-00812.
Greece proposed increasing azoxystrobin MRLs for chervil (3 to 10 mg/kg) and linseed, safflower, and borage seeds (0.05 to 0.5 mg/kg). The EMS did not propose an MRL for rhubarb.
EFSA assessed the application and evaluation report, fulfilling Article 10 requirements.
Based on the EMS's evaluation report, EFSA will provide a reasoned opinion on consumer risks related to the application, as per Article 10 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005.
As per Article 11 of the Regulation, EFSA must issue its reasoned opinion promptly, within three months of receiving the application. This timeframe can be extended to six months if a more in-depth evaluation is necessary. Any request for supplementary information from EFSA will suspend this deadline until the required data is supplied.
Azoxystrobin, (2E)‐2‐{2‐[6‐(2‐cyanophenoxy)pyrimidin‐4‐yloxy]phenyl}‐3‐methoxyacrylate (IUPAC), is its ISO common name. Its chemical structure is in Appendix C.
Azoxystrobin underwent evaluation under Directive 91/414/EEC, with Germany serving as the rapporteur Member State. The peer review process considered outdoor foliar applications on crops such as wheat, barley, rye, triticale, and vines. Subsequently, Directive 1998/47/EC, effective from July 1, 1998, led to azoxystrobin's inclusion in Annex I of Directive 91/414/EEC. A subsequent re-evaluation for the renewal of this Annex I inclusion involved the United Kingdom and the Czech Republic as the rapporteur and co-rapporteur Member States, respectively. This re-evaluation focused on representative uses involving foliar applications on leafy crops and cereals. Following EFSA's peer review in 2010, azoxystrobin received approval under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, which replaced Directive 91/414/EEC. This approval, effective from January 1, 2012, via Regulation (EU) No 703/2011, specifically limits its use to fungicide applications.
Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 establishes EU Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) for azoxystrobin, detailed in Annexes II and IIIB. Following its implementation, EFSA has provided multiple reasoned opinions regarding azoxystrobin MRL modifications, which have been incorporated into EU legislation. During azoxystrobin's authorization renewal and MRL review, analytical methods for plant residue determination were assessed. These assessments occurred under Directive 91/414/EEC and the Article 12 MRL review.
A validated analytical method using HPLC-MS/MS can determine azoxystrobin residues. This method is suitable for various matrices, including high starch, acid, water, and oil content foods, as well as hops. The limit of quantification is 0.01 mg/kg.
EFSA concluded that validated analytical methods are available for azoxystrobin residues in chervil, linseed, safflower, and borage seeds, as these crops fall into high water and high oil content categories.
An analytical method using GC-NPD was validated for determining azoxystrobin residues in milk and animal products during the Directive 91/414/EEC peer review. This method achieved a limit of quantification of 0.001 mg/kg for milk and 0.01 mg/kg for eggs, liver, fat, and muscle. However, a confirmatory method was not available at that time.

A total of 31 residue trials were submitted by the applicant, conducted on lettuce varieties across Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom between 2000 and 2012. These trials were intended to reflect the proposed Good Agricultural Practices (GAPs), with 12 conducted indoors and 8 and 11 in Northern and Southern Europe, respectively. While the trials were generally on open-leaf lettuce, two Southern European trials were excluded because they used iceberg lettuce, a head-forming variety. Where different formulations were tested at the same location, the highest residue level was used for Maximum Residue Level (MRL) calculations.
Indoor trials showed significantly higher residue levels, leading to a proposed MRL of 10 mg/kg for chervil.
For chervil, an MRL of 70 mg/kg was recommended during the Article 12 MRL review and implemented in EU legislation. Therefore, no change is recommended. For rhubarb, the GAP involves 2 applications of 200 g/ha with a 21-day PHI.
No maximum residue level (MRL) is proposed for rhubarb. While the applicant suggested extrapolating data from celery residue trials, these trials did not adhere to the intended Good Agricultural Practice (GAP) for rhubarb. Specifically, the celery trials involved three applications instead of the permitted two, and residue data were only available up to 14 days post-application, not the required 21 days. Despite the theoretical possibility of extrapolation from celery to rhubarb, the absence of GAP-compliant residue trials means no MRL can be established. For linseed, safflower, and borage seeds, the GAP involves two applications of 250 g/ha with a 21-day pre-harvest interval.
To support the intended Good Agricultural Practice (GAP), eight Northern Europe (NEU) and four Southern Europe (SEU) residue trials on rapeseed, conducted in France and the UK between 1996 and 1997, were submitted. The applicant proposed extrapolating this rapeseed residue data to linseed, safflower, and borage seeds, following EU guideline 7525/VI/95. For MRL calculations, the highest residue level per location was used, considering two different formulations tested at some sites.
Residue trials from Northern and Southern Europe showed similar results, allowing the datasets to be combined. This enabled the derivation of a proposed MRL of 0.4 mg/kg, which was then extrapolated to linseed, safflower, and borage seeds.

Storage stability of azoxystrobin was established during the peer review process. Studies demonstrated its stability for 24 months at -18°C across various commodity types, including high water, starch, acid, and oil-content matrices, as well as cereal grain and straw. The residue data for lettuce and rapeseed samples, stored for 10 and 12 months respectively under validated conditions, were deemed valid concerning storage stability.
The EMS confirmed that the analytical methods used for residue trial samples were sufficiently validated and fit for purpose.
EFSA has determined that sufficient data exist to propose a maximum residue level (MRL) of 0.4 mg/kg for linseed, safflower, and borage seeds, based on extrapolations from rapeseed trials in Northern and Southern Europe. For chervil, no MRL modification is recommended, as the submitted data suggest a lower level than the current EU MRL of 70 mg/kg. An MRL is not proposed for rhubarb due to a lack of trials that comply with the intended good agricultural practices.
Azoxystrobin was found to be hydrolytically stable under processing conditions like pasteurisation, boiling, and sterilisation, according to peer review assessments. This stability means the same residue definition applies to both processed and raw agricultural commodities.
Processing studies for azoxystrobin residues were previously evaluated during the peer review and Article 12 MRL review, with processing factors established for various crops. No further studies were submitted for the crops in question. This is deemed unnecessary because the theoretical maximum daily intake (TMDI) was found to be less than 10% of the acceptable daily intake (ADI).
Residue investigations in succeeding crops are necessary due to the proposed use of azoxystrobin on crops that may be grown in rotation.
During the peer review, rotational crop studies involving lettuce, radish, and wheat were conducted. These studies, using a maximum dose of 2,200 g/ha, indicated that azoxystrobin's metabolism in rotational crops mirrors that in primary crops. Consequently, residue levels exceeding 0.05 mg/kg are not anticipated in succeeding crops.
EFSA's conclusion is that azoxystrobin residues are unlikely in rotational crops. This is based on limited application rates of 500 g/ha (2 x 250 g/ha) for chervil, linseed, safflower, and borage seeds, provided the substance is used according to proposed Good Agricultural Practices.
Assessing azoxystrobin residues in linseed and safflower by-products was necessary as they can be fed to livestock.
Livestock dietary burdens, both median and maximum, were calculated during the Article 12 MRL review. This calculation utilized the animal feedstuff table from EU guideline 7031/VI/95 and accounted for livestock consumption of all feed products with azoxystrobin residues from authorized European uses. No further EFSA opinions impacting these dietary burdens were released following the Article 12 review.

Estimated animal intakes exceeded 0.1 mg/kg dry matter feed, necessitating consideration for setting MRLs in animal products.
Compared to previous estimates from the Article 12 MRL review, animal intake shows no significant increase, with citrus pulp, rye straw, and swede roots remaining the primary contributors to the animal burden. However, the updated OECD feedstuff table indicates a substantial reduction in the maximum calculated intake for beef cattle, decreasing from 0.925 to 0.185 mg/kg body weight.
Based on cow and poultry feeding studies, where residues remained below the limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.01 mg/kg, it is concluded that azoxystrobin residues in animal matrices are unlikely to exceed this level. Consequently, considering all authorized uses of azoxystrobin in Europe, maximum residue levels (MRLs) for animal products do not need to be set above the LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg.
The Joint Meeting on Pesticides Residues (JMPR) established Codex Maximum Residue Limits (CXLs) for mammalian matrices, including liver, kidney, offal at 0.07 mg/kg and fat at 0.05 mg/kg. These CXLs were incorporated into EU legislation via Regulation (EU) No 459/2010. However, following the Article 12 MRL review, these limits were designated as 'tentative' in Regulation (EU) No 2015/1040. This designation is pending further data on metabolite toxicity and a definitive conclusion on the risk assessment residue definition for animal products.
