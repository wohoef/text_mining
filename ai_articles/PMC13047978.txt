This study aimed to establish reference values and understand age-related changes in oxidative stress markers, specifically derivatives of reactive oxygen metabolites (d-ROMs) and biological antioxidant potential (BAP), in healthy children. While these markers, measurable from a single sample using the same equipment, allow for a simultaneous assessment of oxidation degree and antioxidant capacity, established reference values exist for healthy adults but not for children. To address this knowledge gap, 77 healthy children aged 2 to 15 years, confirmed by a pediatrician, along with seven healthy adult volunteers, participated in the study. Serum samples were collected from all participants. These samples were then used to measure d-ROMs and BAP levels, and the researchers analyzed how these measurements correlated with age. The results indicated a clear trend: d-ROMs levels decreased as age increased. Conversely, BAP showed no significant correlation with age. Notably, the youngest group of children (2-6 years) exhibited significantly higher d-ROMs levels compared to older children (7-11 and 12-15 years) and the adult group. Furthermore, the BAP/d-ROMs ratio, serving as an indicator of antioxidant capacity, demonstrated a significant increase with advancing age. In conclusion, this research, the first of its kind to assess oxidative stress in healthy children using d-ROMs and BAP, found that younger children (2-6 years) had higher d-ROMs and a lower BAP/d-ROMs ratio. These findings underscore the importance of considering age when interpreting oxidative stress assessments using d-ROMs and BAP in pediatric populations.

Oxidative stress occurs when oxidative reactions outweigh antioxidant ones, leading to an undesirable physical state. This imbalance is heightened in conditions like smoking, hemodialysis, rheumatoid arthritis, and mental illnesses. At a molecular level, oxidative stress contributes to the development of many diseases that cause cellular damage.
Numerous markers exist for assessing oxidative stress, including acrolein-lysine (lipid oxidation), protein carbonyls (protein oxidation), 8-hydroxy-deoxy-guanosine (8-OHdG, nucleic acid oxidation), and pentosidine (carbohydrate oxidation). The widespread availability of assay kits has made these markers more easily measurable, facilitating various oxidative stress studies. However, a comprehensive evaluation of oxidative stress necessitates considering both the degree of oxidation and the body's intrinsic antioxidant capacity. This dual assessment is challenging with commonly used markers like 8-OHdG, which do not effectively measure antioxidant capacity. In contrast, measuring plasma levels of derivatives of reactive oxygen metabolites (d-ROMs) and biological antioxidant potential (BAP) allows for a simultaneous evaluation of both oxidation and antioxidant capacity using a single sample and testing apparatus. This method is also simple and rapid, making d-ROMs and BAP valuable oxidative stress markers.
While reference values for adult d-ROMs and BAP exist, and some urine 8-OHdG data for healthy children is available, d-ROM and BAP measurements in healthy children remain unreported. This lack of data hinders direct comparison across age-stratified pediatric groups. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate age-related changes and establish reference values for d-ROMs and BAP in healthy children.
This study included 77 healthy Japanese children, aged 2 to 15 years, with 39 males and 38 females. Parental informed consent was obtained for all participants. A control group of seven healthy adults, aged 23 to 35 years (three male, four female), was also part of the study. Pediatricians confirmed the mental and physical health of all child subjects. To ensure the health status of participants, several exclusion criteria were applied. These included individuals with underlying diseases, those currently ill, anyone receiving medication for treatment or prevention, subjects with any health disorder in the month prior to the study, and those exhibiting abnormal C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, an indicator of inflammation.
Hydroperoxides, by-products of free radicals reflecting peroxidized lipids, proteins, amino acids, and nucleic acids, were measured in blood. Their levels indicate the amount of free radicals present in the body.
The capacity to halt free radical-induced peroxidative chain reactions was assessed by measuring the reducing power of iron (III) oxide to iron (II) oxide. A higher reducing power indicates a greater ability to eliminate free radicals from the body. Therefore, this reducing power of blood is used to evaluate biological antioxidant potential.
Previous reports show a link between CRP and d-ROMs levels. By measuring CRP to exclude inflammation, we incorporated objective indicators alongside clinical exams and symptoms for a more comprehensive health diagnosis, removing factors that could skew oxidative stress measurements.
From May 2017 to April 2018, blood samples were collected between 10 AM and 5 PM. Following collection, samples were immediately centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes to isolate the serum. These serum samples were then stored at -30°C until they were ready for analysis. To measure d-ROMs and BAP, the stored serum was thawed and analyzed promptly using an automatic free radical analyzer, the "FREE CARRIO DUO." For CRP measurements, collected blood samples were tested without freezing using an immunoanalyzer.
For d-ROMs measurement, 20 μL of each sample was added to a cuvette containing a pH 4.8 buffer. Agitation after inverting the cuvette facilitated the release of Fe2+ and Fe3+ from blood proteins. These ions acted as catalysts, breaking down blood hydroperoxides into alkoxy and peroxy radicals. Subsequently, 20 μL of a color-developing chromogen, N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine, was introduced. Free radicals then oxidized this chromogen, producing a red-colored radical cation. The cuvette was inverted and agitated again before optical measurement in a photometer. The results are reported in U.CARR units, where 1 U.CARR corresponds to 0.08 mg/dL of H2O2.
To measure BAP, a chromogen containing trivalent iron (50 μL) was added to the cuvette, producing a red color. After inverting and agitating the cuvette, the color development was measured photometrically. Subsequently, 10 μL of plasma sample was added, agitated, and incubated in a thermostat for 5 minutes. Finally, the cuvette was placed in a photometer for optical measurement, yielding results in μmol/L.
The relationship between age and d-ROMs, BAP, and the BAP/d-ROMs ratio was statistically analyzed using correlation coefficients (r) and p-values. For analysis, children were divided into age groups: Group A (2-6 years), Group B (7-11 years), and Group C (12-15 years), with healthy adults forming Group D. Statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics version 21) was employed, utilizing the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons between these four groups. Additionally, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to examine sex differences in measurement values within each age group.
The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Red Cross Ethics Committee. Informed consent was obtained from the participating children and their families through written documentation, with clear explanations that participation was voluntary and could be withdrawn at any time without consequence. To ensure privacy, all collected assay data were anonymized, preventing individual identification.
The pediatric participants, averaging 8.49 ± 3.75 years with a median age of 8, comprised 39 males and 38 females. Age group breakdowns revealed 15 males and 13 females in Group A (2-6 years), 15 males and 13 females in Group B (7-11 years), and 9 males and 12 females in Group C (12-15 years). The adult reference group, Group D, consisted of 3 males and 4 females aged 23-35. Across all subjects, the average CRP value was 0.08 ± 0.05 mg/dL.
Figure 1A illustrates the comparison of d-ROMs levels across the four age groups. Notably, Group A, comprising children aged 2-6 years, exhibited significantly higher d-ROMs levels compared to Group B (7-11 years), Group C (12-15 years), and the healthy adult Group D. These differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for all comparisons involving Group A). However, no significant differences in d-ROMs levels were observed between Group B and Group C, Group B and Group D, or Group C and Group D. Further analysis of the correlation between age and d-ROMs revealed a significant inverse relationship: as age increased, d-ROMs levels gradually decreased (r = −0.809, p < 0.001). In contrast, Figure 1B presents the intergroup comparison of BAP levels. The BAP levels did not show any significant differences when comparing all the age groups. Similarly, the correlation analysis between age and BAP yielded no significant association (r = −0.189, p = 0.105).
Figure 2 illustrates the comparison of the BAP/d-ROMs ratio across the four age groups. This ratio, representing antioxidant capacity, was found to be significantly lower in the youngest group (2-6 years) compared to the older children's groups (7-11 and 12-15 years) and the healthy adult group. Analysis of the correlation between age and this ratio revealed a significant positive relationship. Specifically, as age increased, the BAP/d-ROMs ratio also increased, indicating a rise in antioxidant capacity with age.

Quantifying free radicals in vivo is challenging due to their extremely short half-lives, making methods like electron paramagnetic resonance (ESR) impractical. In contrast, d-ROMs offer a stable and quantifiable measure of oxidative stress. This parameter specifically tracks hydroperoxides (ROOH), which are direct precursors to peroxyradicals and alkoxy radicals. The d-ROMs assay is advantageous as it requires only a small blood sample, as little as 20 μL of serum obtained via fingertip puncture. The measurement process is also efficient, taking only 5 minutes for optical analysis, making it minimally invasive and time-saving. The reproducibility of d-ROMs as an oxidative stress marker has been established by Nojima et al. Furthermore, research by Alberti et al. has validated the d-ROMs test by demonstrating a correlation between its results and ESR assay data. Consequently, d-ROMs measurement has gained traction as a screening tool for various diseases and for assessing treatment efficacy. Its clinical application now extends to a broad range of conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, renal failure, pulmonary diseases, hypertension, and Alzheimer's dementia. While some studies have reported sporadic findings in pediatric patients, there remains a lack of data on d-ROM and BAP values in healthy children. This study aimed to address this gap, demonstrating that d-ROM levels correlate with age, whereas BAP does not. These findings align with previous adult studies by Nojima et al., which also found age-related changes in d-ROMs but not in BAP. This highlights the significance of considering age when interpreting these markers, even in pediatric populations.
Previous research by Tamura et al. indicated that 8-OHdG, another oxidative stress marker, also declines with age in healthy children. Tsukahara et al. further noted that 8-OHdG levels stabilize between 10 and 12 years old. The age-related shifts in d-ROMs observed in this study align with these findings, suggesting a potential correlation between d-ROMs and 8-OHdG in healthy children.
To ensure the "healthy condition" of study participants, several exclusion criteria were implemented, including physician examinations and CRP measurements, as CRP correlates with d-ROMs. The reason for the observed decrease in d-ROM levels with increasing age remains unclear. Elevated d-ROM levels are generally attributed to cellular, mitochondrial, and DNA damage. It is hypothesized that the rapid growth and development characteristic of younger children, involving frequent cell death and regeneration, may contribute to higher d-ROMs. Furthermore, psychological stress, such as fear and anxiety during blood collection, could potentially influence results in infants. However, these explanations are speculative and require further investigation.
The findings indicate a correlation between lower age and elevated oxidative stress levels, alongside increased antioxidant capacity. This suggests that the human body's biological homeostasis, which aims to adapt to environmental changes, may be at play. It is hypothesized that an increase in oxidation markers, such as d-ROMs in blood and 8-OHdG in urine, could stimulate antioxidant capacity, thereby maintaining a state of equilibrium through homeostatic mechanisms. Consequently, while oxidation levels alone can serve as an indicator, a more precise assessment of in vivo oxidative stress can be achieved by evaluating the ratio of oxidation degree to antioxidant capacity. Ultimately, a comprehensive understanding of true oxidative stress status is best obtained by examining both opposing parameters, rather than relying on either in isolation. Furthermore, when employing biomarkers in a clinical context, particularly with children, it is crucial to consider age as a factor in the interpretation of results.
Despite stringent exclusion criteria to ensure subject health, undetected factors might still influence the measured levels. To enhance the study's clinical credibility, future research should involve larger sample sizes and collect assay data for more specific age strata.
This study successfully characterized the trends of d-ROMs and BAP in healthy children. The findings indicate that measuring both d-ROMs and BAP allows for a comprehensive assessment of oxidative stress by analyzing the opposing forces of oxidation degree and antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, the established d-ROMs and BAP values for healthy children across different ages can facilitate direct age-matched comparisons for pediatric patients with diseases. This information could serve as a valuable objective index for diagnosing and evaluating the treatment of such conditions.
