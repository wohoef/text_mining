This study investigated the impact of volume guarantee (VG) when used with high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) compared to HFOV alone in preterm infants suffering from hypoxic respiratory failure (HRF). A total of 52 preterm infants with HRF, unresponsive to conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV), were included. From June 2012 to February 2016, 34 infants received HFOV as a rescue therapy after CMV failure. Subsequently, from March 2016 to December 2017, 18 infants were treated with HFOV combined with VG as a rescue measure. The findings indicated that the combination of HFOV and VG led to a significant decrease in the combined outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), with a p-value of 0.017. Furthermore, this approach also resulted in a significant reduction in episodes of hypercarbia, as evidenced by a p-value of 0.010, when compared to HFOV used alone. In conclusion, preterm infants with HRF treated with HFOV and VG demonstrated improved outcomes, specifically a lower incidence of death or BPD and fewer instances of hypercarbia, compared to those receiving HFOV without VG.

Ventilator-induced lung injury is primarily determined by tidal volume (VT), not inflation pressure. Previous research indicates that volume-target ventilation (VTV) modes, when compared to pressure-limited ventilation in preterm infants, can decrease the combined outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), as well as reduce pneumothorax, hypocarbia, and the duration of mechanical ventilation.
Volume guarantee (VG) is a type of volume-target ventilation (VTV). In VG, a microprocessor monitors exhaled tidal volume (VT) from the preceding breath and modifies the working pressure to achieve a predetermined VT. VG ventilation is known to offer several benefits. High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) is a recognized treatment for preterm infants experiencing respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) and other acute lung issues. Previous research by Cools et al. indicated that HFOV might slightly decrease the risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) when compared to conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV). More recently, studies have explored the combined use of VG and HFOV in both preterm infants and animal models. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of VG when used with HFOV, as opposed to HFOV alone, in preterm infants suffering from hypoxic respiratory failure (HRF).
A retrospective analysis of data from intubated preterm infants requiring mechanical ventilation at our level III NICU was conducted from June 2012 to December 2017.
This study enrolled 52 preterm infants diagnosed with hypoxic respiratory failure (HRF), characterized by an oxygenation index (OI) exceeding 15, which was unresponsive to conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV). Infants who were full-term or had congenital malformations were not included. The OI was determined by the formula: mean airway pressure × FiO2 × 100/PaO2. Following the failure of CMV, high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) was administered as a rescue therapy to all 52 infants. From June 2012 to February 2016, 34 infants received HFOV alone (using the SLE 5000 ventilator). Subsequently, from March 2016 to December 2017, the remaining 18 infants were treated with HFOV in combination with volume guarantee (VG) (using the Dräger Babylog VN 500 ventilator).
Table 1 details the patient characteristics for both the HFOV alone and HFOV with volume guarantee (VG) groups. The mean birth weights were 1113.1 ± 639.6 g (range: 512-2485 g) for the HFOV alone group and 1319.5 ± 776.5 g (range: 450-2460 g) for the HFOV with VG group. Gestational ages averaged 27.7 ± 4.1 weeks (range: 23 weeks 1 day to 36 weeks 2 days) in the HFOV alone group and 29.7 ± 4.6 weeks (range: 23 weeks 2 days to 36 weeks 4 days) in the HFOV with VG group. Infants in the HFOV alone group were initiated on HFOV therapy at a mean age of 7.15 ± 7.83 days (range: 1-29 days), while those in the HFOV with VG group began at 6.17 ± 6.21 days (range: 1-22 days). The primary underlying conditions varied between the groups. In the HFOV alone cohort, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) was present in 29 infants, neonatal pneumonia in three, and idiopathic persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) in two. For the HFOV with VG group, RDS was diagnosed in 13 infants, neonatal pneumonia in four, and idiopathic PPHN in one. Notably, the three infants with idiopathic PPHN (two in the HFOV alone group and one in the HFOV with VG group) did not have concurrent RDS, neonatal pneumonia, or other underlying conditions. Pulmonary hypertension, confirmed by echocardiography, was a co-existing condition with RDS or neonatal pneumonia in fourteen infants in the HFOV alone group and eight in the HFOV with VG group. Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) therapy was administered to 16 patients in the HFOV alone group and nine in the HFOV with VG group due to evidence of pulmonary hypertension. All infants receiving iNO underwent comprehensive echocardiographic and Doppler assessments, including evaluation of tricuspid regurgitation jet, patent ductus arteriosus shunt direction, and atrial shunt direction. Echocardiographic findings indicative of pulmonary hypertension, such as elevated pulmonary artery pressure and abnormal shunting through the patent ductus arteriosus or patent foramen ovale, were present in all patients receiving iNO therapy.
Initially, all infants in the study received conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV). High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) was then employed as a rescue therapy for those infants who subsequently developed hypoxemia, defined as a PaO2 below 50 mmHg or SpO2 below 80%, or hypercarbia, indicated by a PaCO2 exceeding 60 mmHg, alongside an oxygenation index (OI) greater than 15. Upon initiating HFOV, a lung volume recruitment maneuver was performed in accordance with standard clinical practice. This began with a mean airway pressure 1 to 2 cmH2O higher than that used during CMV. Following this, the mean airway pressure was incrementally increased by 1 to 2 cmH2O until the critical lung opening pressure was achieved. Both the opening and optimal pressures were determined through the previously described lung recruitment maneuver. Chest radiography was used to ensure ideal lung inflation, with the right diaphragm positioned at the level of the 9th rib. Pressure amplitude was adjusted to produce noticeable chest wall movement, with further modifications made to optimize ventilation. The respiratory frequency was set based on birth weight: 13 to 15 Hz for infants weighing less than 1000 g, 12 to 13 Hz for those between 1000 g and 1500 g, and 10 to 12 Hz for infants exceeding 1500 g. An inspiratory/expiratory (I:E) ratio of 1:1 was used with the SLE 5000 ventilator, while a 1:2 ratio was employed with the Dräger VN 500 ventilator. The high-frequency tidal volume (VThf) was set between 1.5 and 2.5 mL/kg, with an initial setting of 2 mL/kg. Arterial blood gas measurements were taken every 12 hours for the first 72 hours after HFOV initiation, and subsequently every 24 hours for the remainder of the study. The target ranges for arterial blood gases were a pH of 7.25 to 7.45, PaO2 of 50 to 70 mmHg, PaCO2 of 35 to 55 mmHg, and SpO2 of 90% to 95%.
Definitions were established for several clinical conditions. Hypocarbia was identified as a PaCO2 below 35 mmHg, while hypercarbia was defined as a PaCO2 exceeding 60 mmHg. Hypoxemia was characterized by a PaO2 less than 50 mmHg or an SpO2 below 80%. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) was diagnosed if oxygen treatment or respiratory support was required at 36 weeks of corrected gestational age. Sepsis was confirmed by a positive blood culture and at least 7 days of antibiotic treatment. Intraventricular hemorrhage encompassed intraventricular hemorrhage with or without ventricular dilatation, or intracerebral hemorrhage, as determined by cranial ultrasound. The Ethical Committee at Chung Shan Medical University Hospital provided approval for the study.
Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 22. Student's t-test was used for means, and Fisher's exact test for categorical data. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
When comparing the HFOV alone and HFOV with VG groups, no significant differences were observed in several key patient characteristics. These included birth body weight, gestational age, sex, age at the start of HFOV therapy, and the number of patients requiring iNO therapy, along with its maximal concentration and duration. Regarding outcomes, Table 2 details the results for the study infants. Neither the duration of ventilation nor the incidence of hypoxemia, death, BPD, intraventricular hemorrhage, sepsis, or air leak showed significant variation between the two groups. However, a notable difference emerged in gas exchange. The HFOV alone group experienced 61 episodes of hypocarbia, contrasted with 16 episodes in the HFOV with VG group. While this trend towards fewer hypocarbia episodes with VG did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.075), the incidence of hypercarbia episodes was significantly lower in the HFOV with VG group (p = 0.01). Furthermore, the combined outcome of death or BPD was also significantly reduced in the HFOV with VG group compared to HFOV alone (p = 0.017).
Studies in animal models indicate that HFOV promotes uniform lung inflation, enhances oxygenation, and lessens lung injury from intermittent positive pressure ventilation. Elective HFOV has also been associated with a slight decrease in chronic lung disease risk compared to conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV). In contrast, this study utilized HFOV as a rescue therapy for all infants after CMV failure.
In this study, 16 infants in the HFOV alone group and nine in the HFOV with VG group received iNO therapy due to positive echocardiographic findings of pulmonary hypertension. Previous research by Dobyns et al. indicates that combining HFOV with iNO leads to greater oxygenation improvement in children with acute HRF compared to either therapy alone. Similarly, Golfar et al. found that many very low birth weight infants with severe HRF treated with HFOV and iNO survived without neurodevelopmental issues. Despite the American Academy of Pediatrics' (AAP) stance against routine iNO use in preterm infants born before 34 weeks gestation, numerous studies demonstrate its efficacy in improving oxygenation for preterm infants with HRF and pulmonary hypertension. In this specific study, iNO was exclusively administered to infants with HRF who exhibited positive echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension.
Volume-target ventilation (VTV) modes aim to deliver a specific tidal volume (VT) by automatically adjusting peak positive pressure or ventilator cycle duration. These modes are suggested to mitigate ventilator-induced lung injury, which can arise from excessive or insufficient VT during conventional pressure-controlled ventilation. Research by Pen et al. indicated that preterm infants managed with VTV experienced fewer instances of BPD, hypocarbia, pneumothorax, grade 3/4 intraventricular hemorrhage, and periventricular leukomalacia, alongside a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation and reduced primary mode failure. Volume Guarantee (VG) is a prevalent VTV mode in neonates. It functions by modifying the peak pressure of each breath to ensure a target VT, guided by previously measured exhaled VT. VG ventilation is noted for preventing both excessively high and low VT, and for consistently lowering peak pressure. Studies have demonstrated VG's effectiveness in reducing hypocarbia and preventing excessively large VT. While one study by Jain et al. found VG led to a modest decrease in the duration of hypoxemia episodes under routine conditions compared to pressure control ventilation, it did not reduce the occurrence of more severe hypoxemia. The impact of VG on high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) in preterm infants has been explored in several investigations. For instance, Iscan et al. observed that HFOV with VG could decrease fluctuations in high-frequency VT and lower the incidence of CO2 levels deviating from the target, when contrasted with HFOV alone. Similarly, Enomoto et al. reported that combining VG with HFOV stabilized SpO2 and CO2 clearance, thereby preventing hypoxemia and hypocarbia.
Our findings indicate that high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) with volume guarantee (VG) significantly lowered the incidence of hypercarbia and the combined outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) when compared to HFOV alone. While not statistically significant (p = 0.075), a trend towards fewer episodes of hypocarbia was observed in the HFOV with VG group. Both hypocarbia and hypercarbia are known to be linked to brain damage in preterm infants. The observed reduction in the combined outcome of death or BPD with HFOV and VG aligns with prior research by Wheeler et al., which demonstrated a similar benefit for volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) compared to pressure-limited ventilation.
This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective, single-center analysis. Additionally, different ventilators were used: the SLE 5000 for HFOV alone (June 2012-February 2016) and the Dräger VN 500 with VG for HFOV combined with VG (March 2016-December 2017).
HFOV with volume guarantee (VG) demonstrated reduced hypercarbia and a lower combined outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia compared to HFOV alone. A trend towards decreased hypocarbia was also observed with VG. This lung protection strategy may mitigate ventilator-induced lung injury in preterm infants.
