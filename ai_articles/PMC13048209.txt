This study investigated the effectiveness of different bubble continuous positive airway pressure (BCPAP) settings in protecting lungs and facilitating gas exchange. While BCPAP has been a long-standing treatment for respiratory distress in newborns, the most effective circuit configuration is still unclear. Our research focused on rats experiencing ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI). Following 90 minutes of mechanical ventilation, these rats were assigned to one of four groups: an immediate euthanasia control group (n=3), a spontaneous breathing (SB) group (n=8), a conventional BCPAP group (expiratory limb at 0°, n=8), or a high-amplitude BCPAP group (expiratory limb at 135°, n=7). The BCPAP and SB groups received their respective treatments for 90 minutes. We assessed gas exchange, lung volume, and the extent of pulmonary inflammation. Our findings revealed that the high-amplitude BCPAP group showed significantly reduced alveolar protein, lung volume, and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels compared to the SB group. Furthermore, IL-6 levels were notably lower in the high-amplitude BCPAP group than in the conventional BCPAP group. Interestingly, both BCPAP groups demonstrated comparable gas exchange efficiency. In conclusion, high-amplitude BCPAP effectively mitigated lung inflammation and alveolar overdistension in VILI-affected rats after mechanical ventilation ceased, suggesting its potential for superior lung protection compared to conventional BCPAP.

Ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), caused by high tidal volume mechanical ventilation, can trigger or worsen acute lung injury. Since the 1970s, a link between mechanical ventilation, lung inflammation, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) has been recognized. BPD is a severe neonatal complication with high rates of illness and death. While treatments like vitamin A, steroids, and caffeine can decrease severe BPD incidence, the overall occurrence of BPD has not changed.
For over four decades, Bubble Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (BCPAP), a type of Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP), has been employed in the management of neonatal respiratory conditions. This method, along with Nasal Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (NCPAP), has shown benefits such as facilitating extubation and lowering the risk of extended ventilation, as well as contributing to a reduced incidence of Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD). The unique "bubbling" aspect of BCPAP, characterized by varying pressure oscillation frequencies and amplitudes, is thought to aid in opening collapsed lung units and enhancing air exchange. Evidence suggests BCPAP's superiority over infant flow driver CPAP, leading to higher successful extubation rates and shorter CPAP support durations in infants ventilated for less than 14 days. Studies, including one by Pillow et al. on a preterm ovine model, indicate that BCPAP improves gas exchange, lung mechanics, gas mixing efficiency, and lung volume compared to constant-pressure CPAP. This implies that BCPAP's benefits extend beyond simple CPAP delivery and bulk flow ventilation. Furthermore, a randomized study by Wu et al. demonstrated that rats with ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) treated with bubble CPAP exhibited lower alveolar protein levels and reduced lung injury scores compared to those on spontaneous breathing. Despite these known advantages, the ideal BCPAP circuit settings for maximizing gas exchange efficiency and lung protection are yet to be determined.
The concept of stochastic resonance, where noise superimposed on driving pressure aids alveolar recruitment, was proposed by Suki et al. They suggested that optimizing the amplitude and frequency of this superimposed noise could lead to the most effective amplification. In vitro research has since revealed several factors within the BCPAP circuit that impact the intensity and frequency of noise reaching the lungs. These include the bias flow, lung mechanics, the diameter of the bubble generator bottle, the angle of the expiratory limb, and the depth and size of the underwater seal. Specifically, Diblasi et al. investigated different expiratory limb angles in BCPAP circuits. Their findings indicated that a 135° expiratory limb angle generated the greatest pressure and volume oscillations, leading to its designation as "high-amplitude BCPAP." Building on this, our animal study aimed to determine if different BCPAP configurations—conventional (0° expiratory limb) versus high-amplitude (135° expiratory limb)—would differentially affect gas exchange and lung protection in rats with VILI after mechanical ventilation was stopped.
The Taipei Medical University's Animal Care and Use Committee granted approval for this investigation. Twenty-six adult male Sprague Dawley rats, free of pathogens and weighing between 250 and 300 grams, were housed under a 12-hour light-dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water. For the experiment, the rats were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg; Abbott, North Chicago, IL), weighed, and positioned on a heating pad. To facilitate blood gas analysis, a PE-50 polyethylene catheter (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD) filled with heparinized isotonic saline was inserted into a carotid artery, with measurements taken using a model 1620 blood gas analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratories, Lexington, MA). Following a tracheostomy, a 14-gauge plastic cannula was placed in the trachea. This cannula was then connected to a SAR-830/AP volume-cycled small animal ventilator (CWE, Ardmore, PA). All rats underwent 90 minutes of ventilation with a tidal volume of 40 mL/kg, zero positive end-expiratory pressure, a respiratory rate of 30 breaths/min, an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:1, and an inspired oxygen fraction of 0.21.
To assess the in vivo pressure oscillations generated by the two BCPAP devices, a model rat, subjected to the previously described VILI procedure, was utilized. This rat, euthanized immediately post-measurement, received alternating 3-second applications of conventional and high-amplitude BCPAP. A fiberoptic probe from OpSens Technologies, Quebec, Canada, was employed to measure the airway pressure oscillations within the rat. The sensor's tip was placed at the distal opening of the channel, and its cable was linked to a LifeSens signal-reading device from OpSens, Canada. Data collection was performed via an iWorx 404 digitizer connected to a computer. Both BCPAP devices, distinguished solely by their expiratory limb angles (0° for conventional, 135° for high-amplitude), shared identical circuit designs and settings. A gas flow of 1 L/min was supplied by an SAR-830/AP animal ventilator (CWE, Ardmore, PA). To achieve a 5 cmH2O CPAP, the expiratory limbs of both devices were submerged 5 cm deep in water.
Following 90 minutes of high tidal volume ventilation, arterial blood gas levels were evaluated. The rats were then randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups. Three rats formed a control group and were euthanized immediately. Eight rats received spontaneous breathing (SB) treatment. The remaining rats were divided into two BCPAP groups, both receiving treatment for 90 minutes: one group (n=8) utilized a BCPAP device with an expiratory limb set at 0° (BCPAP 0), while the other (n=7) used a BCPAP device with an expiratory limb at 135° (BCPAP 135). Throughout the experiment, pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally maintained anesthesia. The underwater seal in the BCPAP circuits was consistently set to a depth of 5 cm. Respiratory rates and arterial blood gas levels were monitored at 0, 30, 60, and 90 minutes after BCPAP initiation.
Following the experimental interventions, the rats were euthanized. Control animals were sacrificed immediately after 90 minutes of high tidal volume ventilation. For the spontaneous breathing (SB) and BCPAP groups, euthanasia occurred 90 minutes post-intervention. Before organ removal, the tracheal tube was clamped for three minutes to promote oxygen absorption and lung collapse. To assess lung mechanics, a static deflation pressure-volume curve was generated. This involved inflating the lungs with air to 25 cmH2O for one minute, recording the maximum lung volume. Subsequently, the pressure was incrementally reduced, holding at 20, 15, 10, 5, and 0 cmH2O for 30 seconds each, with lung volume measurements taken at every step. These volumes were adjusted for system compression and reported as milliliters per kilogram of body weight. After these measurements, the thorax was opened, and the lungs were extracted. The right lung tissue was preserved for histological examination. The left lung underwent three saline lavages, and the combined bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected and stored for subsequent analysis.
To assess lung injury, total protein concentration in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was quantified using a BioRad Laboratories protein assay kit, following the Bradford method with bovine serum albumin as the reference. Additionally, levels of inflammatory markers—Interleukin-6 (IL-6), macrophage inflammatory protein-2 (MIP-2), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)—in the BALF were measured via ELISA, utilizing kits from Cloud-Clone Corp. and Invitrogen Life Technologies. Results were reported in picograms per milliliter.
For histological examination, lung tissue was fixed for 48 hours at room temperature. Samples underwent dehydration in alcohol, paraffin embedding, and xylene deparaffinization. Rehydrated 5-μm sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin for observation and photography using a Nikon Eclipse E600.
Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for analyzing significant differences, with between-group comparisons conducted via the Mann–Whitney U test. SPSS (version 17.0) facilitated the analysis.
Figure 2 illustrates the airway pressure oscillations in the model rat lung. While mean airway pressures were similar between conventional (5.08 cmH2O) and high-amplitude BCPAP (5.35 cmH2O), the latter generated noticeably larger pressure oscillation amplitudes (0.96 cmH2O) compared to conventional BCPAP (0.70 cmH2O).
The study analyzed changes in pH, PaO2, PaCO2, and respiratory rate (RR) from the start of high tidal volume ventilation through the end of BCPAP treatment, comparing the combined BCPAP group (BCPAP 0 + BCPAP 135) with the spontaneous breathing (SB) group. Initially, arterial blood gas levels were similar between the BCPAP and SB groups. At 120 minutes, the BCPAP group showed a significantly lower pH compared to the SB group. While PaO2 and RR were comparable between these two groups, the BCPAP group had significantly higher PaCO2 levels at both 120 and 180 minutes. When comparing the two BCPAP subgroups, their arterial blood gas levels were similar, though the BCPAP 135 group displayed a non-significant trend toward lower RRs than the BCPAP 0 group.
Analysis of inflammatory markers revealed distinct patterns across the groups. The high-amplitude BCPAP (BCPAP 135) group showed a significant reduction in both BALF protein and IL-6 levels compared to the spontaneous breathing (SB) group. Furthermore, BCPAP 135 also exhibited significantly lower IL-6 levels than the conventional BCPAP (BCPAP 0) group. While BCPAP 0 showed a trend towards reduced BALF protein compared to SB, this difference was not significant, nor was the difference in BALF protein between BCPAP 135 and BCPAP 0. Regarding MIP-2, both BCPAP groups had non-significantly lower levels than the SB group, which itself had significantly higher MIP-2 than the control group. No notable differences in TNF-α levels were observed among any of the groups.
At 0-cmH2O lung pressure, the BCPAP 135 group showed significantly less lung volume compared to the control group. When considering both BCPAP groups (0° and 135°), their combined lung volume was significantly lower than the control group's at 5- and 10-cmH2O pressures. Furthermore, at 20-cmH2O, the BCPAP 135 group had a significantly reduced lung volume compared to the spontaneous breathing (SB) group. While not statistically significant, the BCPAP 135 group consistently exhibited lower lung volume than the BCPAP 0 group.

Clinical evidence suggests that BCPAP offers superior lung protection compared to conventional CPAP, a finding consistent with our prior research. This may explain why preterm infants receiving CPAP experience a lower incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) than those on mechanical ventilation. Specifically, a meta-analysis by Fischer and Buhrer, which evaluated trials comparing CPAP with mechanical ventilation (with or without prophylactic surfactant), CPAP with the INSURE method, and other less invasive surfactant delivery techniques, concluded that strategies designed to prevent intubation were more effective. They reported an odds ratio for death or BPD of 0.83 (95% CI, 0.71-0.96) for these intubation-avoiding approaches. Further supporting BCPAP's benefits, a cohort study by Narendran et al. observed that BCPAP, when used for respiratory distress syndrome in preterm infants, led to a significant decrease in postnatal steroid administration and a trend towards reduced chronic lung disease incidence. Similarly, an observational study by Pelligra et al. in Canada, analyzing 821 infants under 32 weeks, found that BCPAP use significantly lowered the need for exogenous surfactant and postnatal steroids, as well as the duration of mechanical ventilation, when compared to ventilator-derived CPAP. These clinical observations, indicating a trend towards lower BPD incidence and reduced intervention requirements with BCPAP, suggest its enhanced lung protective capabilities and more uniform alveolar recruitment compared to conventional CPAP. An ovine model of preterm lung disease by Pillow et al. further corroborates these findings, showing lower alveolar protein levels in the BCPAP group compared to a conventional CPAP group. While our current study did not directly compare BCPAP with conventional CPAP, it focused on different BCPAP amplitudes. We found that only the high-amplitude BCPAP (BCPAP 135) group exhibited significantly lower alveolar protein levels than the spontaneous breathing (SB) group and significantly lower BALF IL-6 levels than the conventional BCPAP (BCPAP 0) group. This suggests that high-amplitude BCPAP may offer a lung protective effect superior to that of conventional BCPAP.
While no significant differences in TNF-α and MIP-2 levels were observed across the SB, BCPAP 0, and BCPAP 135 groups, the combined BCPAP group (BCPAP 0 + BCPAP 135) did show lower average levels of these markers compared to the SB group. IL-6 appears to be the most responsive cytokine in this VILI rat model. Further research with more animals is needed to fully understand the contributions of TNF-α and MIP-2 in this acute lung injury model.
Stochastic resonance, a defining characteristic of BCPAP distinct from constant-pressure CPAP, suggests that an optimal amplitude can maximize lung volume recruitment. Previous research by Diblasi et al. in lavaged juvenile rabbits showed that high-amplitude BCPAP offered greater respiratory support with reduced breathing effort compared to conventional BCPAP. Our current study supports this, demonstrating that high-amplitude BCPAP (with a 135° expiratory limb) provided superior lung protection. Specifically, the high-amplitude BCPAP group exhibited significantly lower alveolar protein and BALF IL-6 levels than the spontaneous breathing and conventional BCPAP groups, respectively. Interestingly, despite similar arterial blood gas and respiratory rates to the BCPAP group, the spontaneous breathing group displayed elevated MIP-2 levels, indicating a subtle inflammatory response.
Contrary to expectations, the BCPAP group in this study did not show improved oxygenation compared to the spontaneous breathing (SB) group. While BCPAP was hypothesized to lead to higher PaO2, lower PaCO2, and increased pH, the results indicated the opposite for carbon dioxide and pH. Specifically, the BCPAP group exhibited higher PaCO2 and lower pH than the SB group. This unexpected outcome might be due to the relatively healthy lung compliance in our VILI model, differing from prior studies using lavaged lungs. Additionally, we hypothesize that the 5 cmH2O CPAP level applied to the BCPAP group may have been excessively high, contributing to the elevated PaCO2.
Alveolar damage during ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) primarily stems from two factors: alveolar overdistension and repetitive collapse (cyclic atelectasis). Our investigation, using pressure-volume curves, revealed that the control group displayed the greatest lung volume during the entire lung deflation phase. This was particularly evident at 0 and 5 cmH2O lung pressure, pointing to overdistended alveoli following mechanical ventilation and significant air trapping at the end of deflation in this group. After mechanical ventilation ceased, BCPAP treatment effectively reduced alveolar overdistension resulting from VILI. Specifically, the combined BCPAP group (BCPAP 0 + BCPAP 135) showed significantly lower lung volumes than the control group at 5 and 10 cmH2O lung pressure. The BCPAP 135 group, in particular, demonstrated a significantly reduced lung volume compared to the control group at 0 cmH2O lung pressure. Furthermore, the BCPAP 135 group appeared to offer a more pronounced reduction in post-VILI alveolar overdistension compared to the BCPAP 0 group. This was supported by the observation that only the BCPAP 135 group had a significantly lower lung volume than the spontaneous breathing (SB) group at 20 cmH2O lung pressure. While not statistically significant, the BCPAP 135 group consistently maintained a lower average lung volume than the BCPAP 0 group throughout the deflation process, suggesting less alveolar overdistension. These findings align with histological observations, where the BCPAP 135 group exhibited the most uniform alveolar size, contrasting with the highly varied alveolar sizes seen in the control group.
Welty et al.'s preliminary human research explored BCPAP with varied expiratory limb angles. Their findings indicated that infants receiving Seattle PAP (BCPAP with a 135° expiratory limb) experienced reduced breathing effort, as evidenced by a lower average force per breath, compared to those on standard BCPAP (0° expiratory limb). This positive outcome suggests the potential benefits of employing BCPAP circuits with diverse expiratory limb configurations.
Our animal study provides strong preclinical evidence that high-amplitude BCPAP offers better lung protection than conventional BCPAP in respiratory diseases after extubation. Further research is needed to determine if this reduced breathing effort translates to improved clinical outcomes.
A limitation of this research was the use of adult rats instead of neonates. While we hypothesized that the biochemical and genetic responses of pulmonary endothelial cells to mechanical stretching would be similar in both adult and newborn rat lungs, a newborn lung's inherent vulnerability suggests that pathological overdistension could lead to more severe injury in neonates. This is supported by findings from Kuebler et al., who showed that circumferential stretch triggers nitric oxide production in pulmonary endothelial cells via a specific signaling pathway in mouse lungs.
High-amplitude BCPAP demonstrated superior lung protection compared to conventional BCPAP in rats with VILI, reducing both inflammation and alveolar overdistension after mechanical ventilation ceased. This suggests a need for further research into its short- and long-term efficacy for neonatal respiratory diseases post-extubation.
