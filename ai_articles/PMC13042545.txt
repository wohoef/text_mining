Congenital heart disease (CHD) affects approximately 13.08 out of every 1000 live births in Taiwan. Given that the majority of children with CHD do not survive past age five, finding ways to extend their lives is a critical clinical concern. This study aimed to investigate the involvement of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), endothelin-1 (ET-1), and CD34 in autopsy cases of CHD, comparing them to cases without CHD. The research involved 19 CHD autopsy cases, categorized into four groups: acyanotic CHD (11 cases), cyanotic CHD (3 cases), CHD with chromosomal abnormalities (3 cases), and complex CHD (2 cases). Ten autopsy cases without CHD served as controls. The findings revealed significant, high expression levels of HIF-1α (100%), VEGF (89.5%), iNOS (78.9%), and ET-1 (84.2%) in CHD autopsy cases. Hypoxia-induced HIF-1α appears to influence downstream gene expression in CHD patients. Specifically, HIF-1α's upregulation of VEGF may be crucial for promoting angiogenesis, thereby aiding myocardial cell survival in hypoxic conditions. Consequently, HIF-1α emerges as a potential prognostic marker for CHD and a promising target for future cardiovascular disease therapies.

Congenital heart disease (CHD), the most prevalent cardiac defect arising from fetal cardiac malformation, is particularly common in Asia. In Taiwan, CHD occurs at an average rate of 13.08 per 1000 live births. Common forms include ventricular septal defect (VSD), atrial septal defect (ASD), and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), alongside pulmonary stenosis, tetralogy of Fallot, and coarctation of the aorta. CHD represents the primary cause of mortality in children, with approximately 1% affected, and a significant majority succumbing before their fifth birthday. Consequently, developing targeted cardiovascular therapies holds promise for extending the lifespan of CHD patients.
Hypoxia is recognized as a significant contributor to congenital heart disease (CHD) and other prevalent conditions from early life. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which are transcription factors, become stabilized primarily when oxygen levels decrease. These HIFs consist of two subunits: HIF-1α, which is oxygen-sensitive, and HIF-1β, which is not. In normoxic conditions, HIF-1α binds to the von Hippel-Lindau protein, leading to its degradation. However, during hypoxia, HIF-1α bypasses this degradation pathway, rapidly accumulating. It then forms a heterodimer with HIF-1β. This complex subsequently activates downstream genes, including those for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and endothelin-1 (ET-1). This activation, occurring in cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, promotes angiogenesis.
VEGF, an endothelial cell mitogen, is crucial for vascular development, angiogenesis, and collateral growth. Hypoxia triggers increased VEGF expression as HIF-1α binds to its 5' flanking region. This binding then stimulates endothelial cell proliferation, sprouting, migration, and tube formation, contributing to various physiological and pathological processes.
Nitric oxide (NO) is a crucial regulator across nervous, immune, and cardiovascular systems. Within the heart, its basal production by endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) influences coronary circulation, heart rate, and contractility. Conversely, NO produced by inducible NO synthase (iNOS) is linked to various pathological conditions, including myocardial dysfunction and ischemia/reperfusion injury.
Endothelin-1 (ET-1), a powerful vasoconstrictor produced by vascular endothelial cells, is found in elevated plasma concentrations in heart failure patients. Increased ET-1 levels promote cardiac fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix recombination, leading to fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction. ET-1 receptor antagonists are employed to treat pulmonary hypertension by preventing pulmonary vasculature constriction and reducing vascular resistance.
This study employed immunohistochemistry to investigate the expression of HIF-1α, VEGF, iNOS, and ET-1 in autopsy cases with and without CHD. Semiautomated image analysis software was used for scoring. The pathological findings of these markers and their diagnostic specificity for CHD were discussed.
Autopsy cases of congenital heart disease (CHD) patients from Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital, Taiwan, spanning January 1975 to October 2014, were examined. These cases, preserved in formalin and paraffin, were sourced from the hospital's Department of Pathology archives.
This research utilized 19 congenital heart disease (CHD) autopsy specimens, specifically focusing on abnormal cardiac regions from the left ventricle. These specimens were categorized into four distinct groups for examination. The largest group comprised 11 cases of acyanotic CHD (ACHD), encompassing conditions such as patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), ventricular septal defect (VSD), and atrial septal defect (ASD). A smaller group of three cyanotic CHD (CCHD) cases was also included, consisting of two instances of left heart hypoplastic syndrome and one case of Eisenmenger syndrome. Additionally, three cases of CHD associated with chromosomal abnormalities (Trisomy 13, 18, and 21) and two cases of complex CHD were analyzed. For comparative purposes, ten autopsy cases without CHD served as controls, as detailed in Table 1. Prior to immunohistochemistry (IHC), histological examination of the ventricle tissues revealed no fibrosis in the sampled areas, which were relevant to potential ischemic regions. This prompted the use of IHC to detect any ischemic changes not discernible through standard hematoxylin and eosin staining. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Kaohsiung Medical Hospital (IRB KMUH‐IRB‐2013287) for the use of these human tissue samples.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was conducted on 4-μm thick paraffin sections. These sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration before being subjected to an autoclave treatment at 121°C for 10 minutes in a pH 9.0 Dako target retrieval solution to facilitate antigen retrieval. To block endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 5 minutes. Subsequently, primary antibodies for HIF-1α (1:200), VEGF (1:400), CD34 (1:200), iNOS (1:100), and ET-1 (1:200) were applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Following this, the Dako REAL Envision Detection kit was used for 30 minutes. The staining process concluded with a 5-minute incubation in 3,3′-diaminobenzidine, followed by Mayer's hematoxylin counterstaining and mounting. Negative controls were prepared by substituting primary antibodies with nonimmune serum.
Immunohistochemically stained slides were scanned and digitalized. Image analysis software (Quan Center v.1.16.2) was used for unbiased quantification of immunostaining results. HIF-1α, VEGF, iNOS, and ET-1 expressions were assessed using the Allred scoring system, with scores determined by proportion and intensity of staining.
The scoring system for immunostaining involved a proportion score, ranging from 0 (no staining) to 5 (≥67% positive staining), and an intensity score, from 0 (negative) to 3 (strong staining). The total score (TS) was the sum of these two. A TS less than 5 indicated a low score, while a TS of 5 or greater signified a high score.
Microvessel density (MVD) was determined by counting CD34-positive staining vessels in areas of highest vascular density. This involved point counting individual stained microvessels at 400× magnification. Three randomly selected fields with the most blood vessels were analyzed. The mean blood vessel density was then calculated by dividing the average number of blood vessels by the field's area, expressed as blood vessels per square millimeter.
To analyze differences in HIF-1α, VEGF, iNOS, and ET-1 levels between CHD patients and healthy individuals, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. This nonparametric method is suitable for comparing multiple independent samples, especially with small or asymmetric datasets. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 19.

All 19 congenital heart disease (CHD) cases in our study showed high HIF-1α expression. Statistical analysis revealed a significant correlation between high HIF-1α expression and CHD patient survival (p = 0.013).
VEGF expression was high in 89.5% of the 19 CHD cases, encompassing acyanotic, cyanotic, chromosomally abnormal, and complex forms. Only two cases (10.5%) exhibited low VEGF expression, one with PDA and another with intraventricular hemorrhage. Despite its prevalence in CHD cases, VEGF expression did not significantly differ between CHD and non-CHD autopsy samples.
In fifteen of the nineteen congenital heart disease (CHD) cases studied, representing 78.9%, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression was high. These included nine acyanotic CHD, three cyanotic CHD, one CHD with chromosomal abnormality (Trisomy 13), and two complex CHD cases. Conversely, four CHD cases (21.1%) exhibited low iNOS expression. These four cases included one with patent ductus arteriosus and atrial septal defect, one with congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and two with chromosomal abnormalities (Trisomy 18 and Trisomy 21).
Endothelin-1 (ET-1) showed high expression in 84.2% (16 cases) of CHD autopsy samples. Conversely, three CHD cases exhibited low ET-1 expression, including one with PDA and ASD, another with PDA, VSD, and a diaphragm defect, and a third with Trisomy 21.
In our study, 84.2% of CHD cases exhibited high microvessel density (MVD). MVD was found to significantly correlate with VEGF expression.
Hypoxia is a well-established characteristic of congenital heart disease (CHD), as indicated by previous research. In response to hypoxia, cells activate the expression of HIF-1α, a key transcription factor. HIF-1α orchestrates the regulation of various genes involved in processes such as glucose metabolism, cell survival and proliferation, angiogenesis, energy metabolism, and erythropoiesis. This study investigated the expression of HIF-1α and its downstream target genes, VEGF, iNOS, and ET-1, in autopsy samples from infants with CHD using immunohistochemistry (IHC). The findings revealed significantly elevated levels of HIF-1α in CHD patients compared to non-CHD controls. Furthermore, the downstream genes VEGF, iNOS, and ET-1 were found to be significantly upregulated in the majority of CHD cases. IHC staining performed on different cardiac regions, including the right ventricle, left atrium, and septum, yielded similar marker scores across these sites, mirroring the results observed in the left ventricle (data not shown). Supplementary Figures S1A-S1E, S1F-S1J, and S1K-S1O provide illustrative IHC staining for HIF-1α, VEGF, iNOS, ET-1, and CD34 in the right ventricle, left atrium, and septum, respectively.
HIF-1α is crucial in the development of persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) and cyanotic congenital heart disease (CCHD), as supported by prior research. Its presence has also been noted in autopsy samples from individuals who experienced acute myocardial infarction. Studies have shown that HIF-1α expression is elevated in the heart cells of ischemic rat hearts. Furthermore, chronic low oxygen levels are linked to the activation and stabilization of HIF-1α, along with its downstream targets VEGF and eNOS, in infants with CCHD. In animal models, continuous high levels of HIF-1α in the heart led to reduced infarct size and better cardiac function, indicating its role in cardiac remodeling. Our immunohistochemistry findings align with these observations, revealing high HIF-1α expression across all examined CHD autopsy groups, underscoring its significance as a regulator in CHD patients.
Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a powerful driver of angiogenesis, promoting the extensive formation of collateral blood vessels. This process serves as a compensatory mechanism to ensure adequate oxygen supply to tissues, particularly in cases of cyanotic congenital heart disease (CCHD) [20]. VEGF also fosters angiogenesis and neovascularization within the myocardium, especially under conditions of hypoxia and ischemia [21]. Furthermore, it has been implicated in cardiac remodeling in response to mechanical stress [22]. Persistently elevated VEGF levels may be linked to the development of collateral arteries connecting the systemic and pulmonary circulation in CCHD patients [23]. Abnormal blood vessel proliferation is a common occurrence in individuals with CHD, potentially indicating anomalous angiogenesis, particularly in the lungs or myocardium [24]. In this study, the majority of CHD cases exhibited high VEGF expression. However, a small number of cases showed low VEGF expression. Previous research suggests that intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) in premature infants can involve compromised blood-brain barrier tight junctions, leading to disruption of the choroid plexus [25]. This disruption can impair VEGF's ability to reach its receptors, resulting in reduced VEGF expression. The brain also contains stem cells capable of differentiating into VEGF-producing glial cells. IVH-induced brain tissue damage could therefore diminish VEGF production by these cells. This mechanism may explain the low VEGF expression observed in the CHD case with a brain lesion (Case No. 10). Interestingly, VEGF expression levels did not significantly differ between the CHD and non-CHD groups in our study. Existing research indicates that VEGF is widely expressed in embryonic and fetal tissues, with peak levels in the lung, kidney, and heart during development [26]. Additionally, VEGF expression is elevated in normal infants during the neonatal period, declining a few months after birth [23]. These developmental patterns might account for the lack of a significant difference in VEGF expression between the CHD and non-CHD groups in our cohort.
Previous research has established that hypoxia elevates both iNOS messenger RNA and protein levels, without impacting neuronal NOS levels [27]. Studies have also reported significant increases in eNOS and iNOS expression within the pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of CHD patients when compared to control infants [28]. Furthermore, hypoxia is known to induce iNOS expression in cardiac myocytes, with HIF-1 playing a crucial role in this hypoxic regulation of iNOS gene expression [29]. Our investigation revealed a high percentage of iNOS expression in CHD cases, a finding that significantly differed from non-CHD cases. This immunohistochemistry result aligns with findings from other research groups employing different methodologies. While some studies have noted significantly higher iNOS expression in cyanotic CHD (CCHD) compared to acyanotic CHD (ACHD) [30], our study did not replicate this specific observation. This discrepancy may be attributed to the limited number of CCHD autopsy samples in our cohort. The observation of low iNOS expression in certain CHD cases (Cases 6 and 7) suggests that skeletal muscle immaturity and dysfunction could influence iNOS expression induction [31]. Additionally, low iNOS expression was noted in cases of Trisomy 18 (Case 16) and Trisomy 21 (Case 17), indicating that chromosomal abnormalities, such as Trisomy 18 and 21, might affect iNOS production via cardiac abnormalities [32].
Endothelin-1 (ET-1) has been implicated in various cardiovascular conditions, particularly in the context of congenital heart disease (CHD). Research has shown a connection between ET-1 receptor dysregulation and structural changes in pulmonary arteries of newborns with congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH) and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), suggesting a role for ET-1 receptor dysfunction in PPHN associated with CDH. Furthermore, elevated immunoreactive ET-1 levels are frequently observed in children with pulmonary hypertension, correlating strongly with pulmonary vasoreactivity during acute hypoxia. However, it remains speculative whether these elevated levels are a direct cause or merely markers of altered pulmonary vascular tone and reactivity. Specific genetic conditions, such as Down syndrome, may also be linked to ET-1 injury, potentially leading to sustained increases in ET-1 concentration and prolonged artificial respiration. Hypoxia is a significant trigger for ET-1 gene expression in endothelial cells and is considered a primary driver of increased ET-1 production during myocardial ischemia. Hypoxia's induction of ET-1 synthesis and secretion in isolated endothelial cells is influenced by nitric oxide (NO) and carbon monoxide, and can be mimicked by transition metals. Additionally, a potential positive feedback loop between ET-1 and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) has been suggested, which could continuously promote smooth muscle cell contraction and proliferation, thereby contributing to the development of pulmonary hypertension. The emergence of ET receptor antagonists as therapeutic agents highlights the need to explore their utility in treating both chronic and acute pulmonary hypertension secondary to CHD. Our own findings, consistent with other studies using different methodologies, revealed high ET-1 expression in the majority of CHD cases, with a significant difference compared to non-CHD cases. This supports the notion that ET-1 may act as a pathophysiological regulator in patients with CHD.
Previous research suggests that chronic hypoxia in cyanotic congenital heart disease (CCHD) may exclusively stimulate angiogenesis within the heart's subendocardial network. However, this study found that microvessel density (MVD), assessed via CD34 staining, could not predict ventricular dysfunction in CCHD patients. Another study indicated that hypoxia promotes myocardial microvessel development and that elevated VEGF levels in CCHD patients contribute to this process. Our findings align with these observations, showing significantly higher MVD in CHD cases compared to non-CHD controls, with a notable correlation between CD34 expression and VEGF. We also observed a higher mean MVD in CCHD than in acyanotic CHD, consistent with prior research. Nevertheless, the potential of VEGF-mediated MVD as a predictor of ventricular dysfunction in CHD warrants further investigation.
Our study found significantly elevated levels of HIF-1α, VEGF, iNOS, and ET-1 in CHD autopsy cases. Hypoxia-induced HIF-1α appears to influence downstream gene expression in these patients. Specifically, HIF-1α's upregulation of VEGF may promote angiogenesis, aiding myocardial cell survival under hypoxic conditions. Consequently, HIF-1α shows promise as a significant prognostic marker for CHD and a potential target for future cardiovascular therapies.
