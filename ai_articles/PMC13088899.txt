This EFSA report details the 2015 surveillance findings for transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs) across bovine animals, sheep, and goats within the EU and three non-Member States, alongside sheep genotyping data. Regarding bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE), approximately 114 million cattle in the EU have undergone testing since 2001, adhering to Regulation (EC) 999/2001. In 2015 alone, 1.4 million bovine animals were tested, revealing five cases across four Member States: Ireland (one), Slovenia (one), Spain (one), and the United Kingdom (two). Additionally, Norway reported one case. Of these, two cases (in Ireland and the UK) were identified as classical BSE, notably born after the 2001 EU-wide feed ban. The remaining four cases were atypical BSE, comprising three H-BSE types and one L-BSE type. For scrapie, EU-wide surveillance has tested around 8.4 million small ruminants since 2002. In 2015, 319,638 sheep and 135,857 goats were tested. This led to the detection of 641 scrapie cases in sheep across 18 Member States and 1,052 scrapie cases in goats across nine Member States. Furthermore, Iceland and Norway, two non-Member States, reported 40 scrapie cases in sheep. While some Member States show a clear reduction in classical scrapie, an overall decreasing trend for scrapie in small ruminants is not evident at the EU level. Sheep genotyping data indicates that classical scrapie cases are concentrated in specific genotypes, which represent less than 20% of the randomly sampled European sheep population. Finally, 580 samples from non-domestic ruminant species were tested for TSE in three Member States, all yielding negative results.

EFSA's report details 2015 monitoring of TSEs in cattle, sheep, and goats across the EU and three non-Member States. It also provides an overview and trends of surveillance activities conducted between 2001 and 2015.
Countries submit monthly TSE monitoring data for bovine animals and small ruminants, adhering to Regulation (EC) 999/2001. This data includes both testing results and information on confirmed positive animals. Additionally, annual reports from Member States and non-Member States, as per Article 6.4 and Annex III Chapter B.I of the TSE Regulation, provide genotyping data for ovine TSE cases and randomly selected sheep.
Data analysis employed descriptive statistics. Stratification of descriptions and calculations, where feasible, was based on database variables. These included surveillance target groups (e.g., healthy slaughtered, animals culled for BSE/TSE control), surveillance activity (passive vs. active), country, and year (2001 for bovine, 2002 for small ruminants). Further stratification considered case type (classical/atypical BSE, classical/atypical scrapie), flock status (infected/non-infected), and age class.
In 2015, the EU detected five BSE cases across four Member States (Ireland, Slovenia, Spain, UK) and one H-BSE case in Norway. All EU cases were found in fallen stock. Two of these were classical BSE (C-BSE), notably in animals born after the 2001 EU feed ban, while the other three were atypical (two H-BSE, one L-BSE). This occurred within a broader trend of decreasing BSE testing, with approximately 1.4 million bovine animals screened in the EU in 2015, a significant drop from the 114 million tested since 2001. The overall number of positive BSE cases and their proportion among tested animals have also declined over this period, influenced by changes to Regulation (EC) 999/2001.
Throughout the EU's BSE monitoring from 2001 to 2015, a distinct decrease in the annual case rate per million tests was evident. Concurrently, affected animals showed an increased age, with a concentration of cases among those born in the mid-1990s.
Between 2002 and 2015, roughly 8.4 million small ruminants underwent scrapie testing across the EU. In 2015 alone, testing involved around 300,000 sheep and 135,000 goats. That year, 641 cases of scrapie were identified in sheep within the EU, alongside 1,052 cases in goats. These sheep scrapie cases were found in 18 Member States, while goat cases were reported by nine Member States, with approximately 90% originating from Cyprus. Furthermore, 40 sheep scrapie cases were detected in two of the three non-Member States providing data. Overall, the epidemiological status of small ruminant TSEs in the reporting countries shows no distinct improvement, whether considering the total number of cases or the proportion in tested animals. Nevertheless, certain Member States have observed a decrease in classical scrapie. Genotyping results corroborate that classical scrapie in sheep predominantly affects genetically susceptible animals, which constitute under 20% of the randomly sampled sheep population.
In 2015, Estonia, Finland, and Hungary tested 580 samples from non-domestic animals for TSEs; all results were negative.
Member States (MS) are required to submit annual reports detailing Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathy (TSE) surveillance data, as stipulated by Article 6(4) of Regulation (EC) 999/2001 (the TSE Regulation), specifically Part I.A, Chapter B.I Annex III. These reports must encompass several key data points. For all animal species, MS need to report the count of suspected cases placed under official movement restrictions, as outlined in Article 12(1). Furthermore, the number of suspected cases undergoing laboratory examination under Article 12(2) must be provided, including the outcomes of both rapid and confirmatory tests (positive and negative results). For bovine animals specifically, the age distribution of all tested animals is crucial, categorized into 'below 24 months', 12-month intervals between 24 and 155 months, and 'above 155 months'. The reports must also include the number of ovine and caprine flocks where suspected cases have been identified and investigated according to Article 12(1) and (2). For bovine animals, data on testing within specific subpopulations (Chapter A, Part I, points 2.1, 2.2, 3.1, and 5) is required, along with details on sample selection methods, rapid and confirmatory test results, and the aforementioned age distribution. Similarly, for ovine and caprine animals and their flocks, testing data from subpopulations (Chapter A, Part II, points 2, 3, 5, and 6) must be reported, including sample selection methods and test results. Regarding positive TSE cases, the geographical distribution of BSE and scrapie cases is mandatory, noting the country of origin if different from the reporting country. The year, and ideally the month, of birth for each bovine, ovine, and caprine TSE case must be provided, with atypical cases clearly marked. For scrapie cases, the results of primary and secondary molecular testing, as per Annex X, Chapter C, point 3.2(c), are to be included where applicable. Beyond domestic ruminants, MS must report the number of samples and confirmed TSE cases for other animal species. Finally, for ovine animals, the genotype and, if possible, the breed of each TSE-positive animal (sampled under Chapter A, Part II, point 8.1) or randomly sampled animal (under Chapter A, Part II, point 8.2) must be documented.
The annual report mandated by Article 6(4) can be fulfilled by compiling reports that contain the information outlined in Chapter B.I. These reports are submitted electronically to the Commission, which then forwards them to the European Food Safety Authority. This submission occurs monthly, except for information specified in point 8, which is submitted quarterly. Crucially, all information must be updated as new data emerges.
The EU summary report must be tabled, including, at minimum, the information for each Member State as specified in Part I.A Chapter B.I.
Starting January 1, 2016, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) is mandated to analyze data from Part I Chapter B.I. By the end of November, EFSA will release a summary report detailing Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSEs) trends and origins within the EU.
The legal basis for active TSE surveillance in ruminants is established in Article 6 and Annex III Chapter A of the TSE Regulation. Additionally, Article 12.2 of the same regulation mandates the examination of animals suspected of having a TSE.
In 2009, Commission Decision 2009/719/EC enabled 25 of the 27 Member States, excluding Bulgaria and Romania, to implement an updated BSE monitoring program. Subsequently, Commission Implementing Decision 2013/76/EU, issued in February 2013, granted these 25 Member States the option to discontinue testing healthy slaughtered cattle.
Mandatory discriminatory testing for BSE in small ruminant TSE cases was introduced in 2005, as stipulated by amendments to Annex X of the TSE Regulation (Commission Regulation (EC) No 36/20054). The foundational legal framework for sample collection and test methodologies is detailed in Chapter C of Annex X of the same regulation.
Prion protein genotype testing is legally mandated by the TSE Regulation. Specifically, Chapter A, Annex III, points 8.1 and 8.2, outline the basis for determining these genotypes.
Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) surveillance in cattle relies on testing samples from several distinct categories. These include "BSE suspect" (SU) animals, which are live, slaughtered, or deceased bovines exhibiting neurological or behavioral abnormalities, or a general decline in health linked to central nervous system impairment. If clinical examination, treatment response, post-mortem analysis, or laboratory tests fail to provide an alternative diagnosis, BSE is suspected. Additionally, a positive rapid test for BSE, as outlined in Article 3 1(h) of the TSE Regulation, also triggers suspicion and subsequent measures under Articles 12 and 13. Another surveillance group comprises animals "culled under BSE eradication measures" (EM). This category encompasses "birth cohorts," meaning bovines born in the same herd as an affected animal within 12 months before or after its birth. It also includes "rearing cohorts," which are bovines that were raised alongside an affected animal during its first year of life (point 2 Annex I). Furthermore, if a female animal is confirmed with BSE, its offspring born within two years before or after the clinical onset of the disease are also included (point 1(a) Chapter B Annex VII). Other target groups for BSE surveillance are "animals with clinical signs at ante mortem" (AM), referring to bovines showing abnormal observations during ante-mortem inspections (Article 6 1a(a)). "Emergency slaughtered" (ES) bovines, sent for emergency slaughter, also fall under surveillance (Article 6 1a(a)). "Fallen stock" (FS) includes bovines not intended for human consumption that have died or been killed on a farm, during transport, or in an abattoir (Article 6 1a(c)). Member States may opt out of this provision for remote, low-density areas lacking dead animal collection, provided this exemption doesn't exceed 10% of their bovine population. Finally, "healthy slaughtered" (HS) bovines, slaughtered for human consumption, are also part of the surveillance program (Article 6 1a(b)).

For general TSE examination, samples must be gathered following the methods and protocols detailed in the most recent edition of the World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE) Manual for diagnostic tests and vaccines for Terrestrial Animals.
Bovine samples, collected under Annex III, Chapter A, Part I, undergo initial rapid testing. Should a rapid test yield inconclusive or positive results, confirmatory examinations are immediately performed. These confirmations utilize at least one method or protocol detailed in the most recent Manual edition (Figure 1).
For bovine animals with suspected TSE, immediate confirmatory testing is required. This involves at least one method from a set including immunohistochemistry, western blot, electron microscopy for scrapie-associated fibrils, or histopathological examination combined with rapid tests. Rapid tests can also serve as a primary screen and, if results are inconclusive or positive, for subsequent confirmation, following EURL guidelines.
If a primary rapid test result is unconfirmed by a subsequent rapid test, alternative confirmatory methods must be employed, as per Chapter C Annex X, point 3.1(b). Should histopathological examination be inconclusive or negative in such cases, further testing with other confirmatory methods and protocols is required.
To classify bovine TSE isolates, all confirmed BSE cases must undergo discriminatory testing at a laboratory. This is mandated by point 3.1(a)(c) Chapter C Annex X of the TSE Regulation, as updated by Commission Regulation 630/2013.
TSE surveillance in sheep and goats involves testing samples from several distinct target groups. These groups are defined by the animal's health status, the circumstances of their death or slaughter, and their role in disease eradication efforts. One such group is **Animals Clinically Suspected of being infected by a TSE (SU)**. This category includes live, slaughtered, or dead sheep and goats exhibiting neurological or behavioral disorders, or a progressive decline in general health linked to central nervous system impairment. For these animals, a TSE diagnosis is considered when clinical examination, treatment response, post-mortem examination, or ante- or post-mortem laboratory analysis fail to establish an alternative diagnosis, as per Article 3 1(h) of the TSE Regulation. Such suspected cases are then subject to the specific measures outlined in Articles 12 and 13 of the same regulation. Another target group is **Animals Culled under TSE Eradication Measures (EM)**. In situations where a flock or herd is infected with TSE and BSE cannot be definitively ruled out, all identified animals, embryos, and ova must be immediately killed and completely destroyed. If BSE and atypical scrapie (AS) can be excluded, and "option 1" has been implemented (which involves the killing and complete destruction or slaughter for human consumption of all animals), then sheep and goats over 18 months of age that are killed for destruction must be tested for TSE. This testing is based on a simple random sample, adhering to the sample size specified in Annex III Chapter A, Section II Point 5. If derogations for killing and destruction are applied, all animals over 18 months of age slaughtered for human consumption must be tested. When "option 2" is applied (killing and complete destruction or slaughter for human consumption of only susceptible animals), all ovine animals are genotyped first. Following genotyping, a simple random sample of animals over 18 months of age selected for killing and destruction must be tested for TSE, again according to the sample size in Annex III Chapter A, Section II Point 5. Similar to option 1, if derogations for killing and destruction are applied under option 2, all animals over 18 months of age slaughtered for human consumption must be tested. The **Animals Not Slaughtered for Human Consumption (NSHC)** group comprises sheep and goats that have died or been killed, but not as part of a disease eradication campaign or for human consumption. Minimum sample sizes for this group are established to align with the size of ovine populations in individual Member States (MS), ensuring achievable targets (refer to Table 4). Furthermore, in an infected flock or herd where options 1, 2, 3, or specific derogations (point 2.2.2 b ii and iii) have been applied, an intensified TSE testing program is initiated. For a period of two years from the last scrapie case detection, or for two years if the program is ongoing, all animals over 18 months of age (excluding ARR ovine animals) that have died or been killed on the holding, but not as part of a disease eradication campaign, must be tested for TSE. Finally, **Healthy Animals Slaughtered for Human Consumption (SHC)** form a surveillance target group. Member States with a sheep population (ewes and ewe lambs put to the ram) or a goat population (goats that have already kidded and mated goats) exceeding 750,000 animals are mandated to test a minimum annual sample of 10,000 ovine and/or caprine animals slaughtered for human consumption (as per point 2(a) Section II Chapter A Annex III of the TSE Regulation; see Table 4). MS have the flexibility to substitute up to 50% of this minimum sample size by testing dead ovine or caprine animals over 18 months of age. Additionally, up to 10% of the combined minimum sample size for SHC and NSHC can be replaced by testing ovine or caprine animals killed in a disease eradication campaign, provided they are over 18 months of age, at a one-to-one ratio. In an infected flock or herd where option 2 or specific derogations (point 2.2.2 b ii and iii) have been applied, and during the intensified TSE testing program (for two years or from the last scrapie case detection), animals present on the holding when the TSE case was confirmed and subsequently slaughtered for human consumption must be tested for TSE. Similarly, in an infected flock where option 3 (no mandatory killing and complete destruction of animals) or derogations (point 2.2.2 b ii and iii) have been applied, all animals over 18 months of age (excluding ARR/ARR animals) slaughtered for human consumption must be tested for TSE.
When an atypical scrapie (AS) case is confirmed on a farm, an intensified TSE surveillance protocol is implemented for two years following the last AS detection. During this period, all sheep and goats on the holding that are over 18 months old and either slaughtered for human consumption or found dead/culled must be tested for TSE.
The report uses this surveillance target group classification to present both current and historical data.
For ovine and caprine animals, samples submitted for laboratory testing under Annex III, Chapter A, Part II (Monitoring in ovine and caprine animals) must first undergo a rapid test. This initial screening aims to identify all known strains of Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSEs). Should the rapid test yield an inconclusive or positive result, the tissue samples are then immediately forwarded to an authorized laboratory for confirmatory analysis. These confirmatory examinations involve methods such as histopathology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), Western blot (WB), or the characterization of scrapie-associated fibrils (SAF) using electron microscopy, as detailed in point (a). If a confirmatory examination is negative or inconclusive, further testing using IHC or WB is required. A positive result from any of these confirmatory examinations classifies the animal as a positive TSE case, necessitating additional procedures outlined in point (c) (Figure 2).
For suspected ovine or caprine TSE cases, immediate confirmatory testing is required, utilizing methods like IHC, WB, SAF, or histopathological examination. Conversely, routine monitoring samples from these animals undergo rapid testing to detect all known TSE strains.
Should a rapid test yield inconclusive or positive results, tissues must promptly go to an official lab for confirmatory tests like histopathology, IHC, WB, or SAF. If these confirmatory tests are negative or inconclusive, further examination using IHC or WB is required.
For TSE cases requiring further investigation, particularly those not classified as atypical scrapie (AS) or exhibiting unusual characteristics, a discriminatory Western Blot (WB) method, as outlined by the TSE EURL, must be employed by an authorized diagnostic laboratory. Additionally, if initial molecular tests cannot definitively rule out Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) in any TSE case, subsequent confirmation is mandatory using at least one alternative immunochemical method.
For all sheep testing positive for TSE, the prion protein genotype at codons 136, 154, and 171 must be identified. Additionally, a representative sample of the overall sheep population needs genotyping. Member States with over 750,000 adult sheep are required to genotype a minimum of 600 animals, while other Member States must genotype at least 100. These samples can be sourced from live animals, those that have died on farms, or those slaughtered for human consumption, ensuring they reflect the entire ovine population.

This report details the 2015 Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSE) surveillance program in the EU, presenting key findings through figures, tables, and narrative. It organizes data into three primary sections: cattle, small ruminants (sheep and goats), and other species. Both national-level and aggregated data are included. Historical surveillance data are provided for bovine animals (2001–2015), small ruminants (2002–2015), and other species (2015).
For cattle, the analysis involved calculating the total number of tests conducted in 2015 by Member States and surveillance groups, as well as historical data back to 2001. The absolute number of confirmed BSE cases for both the reporting year and previous years was also considered. To understand the BSE epidemic's progression and contextualize current findings, various epidemiological parameters were estimated. These included the absolute number of cases or their proportions (cases per million tests) categorized by case type (e.g., C-BSE, H-BSE, L-BSE) and/or surveillance target group, along with proportions based on age class and year.
To gather specific epidemiological data on 2015 BSE cases, EFSA emailed a brief questionnaire to relevant Member States. This sought supplementary information beyond what reporting countries typically submit.
To conduct an age-period analysis, the study calculated age-specific case rates per million tests over time for both classical BSE (C-BSE) and atypical BSE (H- and L-type). Determining the appropriate denominators (number of tests over time by age group) presented a challenge due to inconsistencies in the EU database's age information for tested cattle. While most data provided closed age classes (e.g., '24–35' months), the initial ('< 24 months') and final ('> 156 months') categories were exceptions. Furthermore, a significant portion of the data contained either missing, unspecific, or open-ended age classes (e.g., '> 24' or '> 48' months). To address this and ensure complete age-specific denominators, an imputation technique was applied to all records lacking meaningful age classifications. This process involved three steps. First, the dataset was divided into two sub-datasets: one for animals with missing or open age classes, and another exclusively for animals with 1-year closed age classes. Second, both sub-datasets were categorized into three periods based on major changes in surveillance legislation (as detailed in Table 2). Period 1 (2001–2008) mandated testing for healthy slaughtered (HS) cattle aged ≥ 30 months and at-risk animals (fallen stock, emergency slaughtered, ante mortem) aged ≥ 24 months, as per Regulations (EC) No 999/2001 and 1248/2001. Period 2 (2009–2012) adjusted these requirements, with HS cattle tested at ≥ 48 months (2009–2010) and ≥ 72 months (2011–2012), and at-risk animals at ≥ 48 months, according to Decisions 2008/908/CE and 2011/358/CE. Period 3 (2013–2015), under Decision 2013/76/CE, eliminated HS testing and maintained at-risk animal testing at ≥ 48 months. Finally, for each of these surveillance periods, the observed age distribution from animals with closed age classes was utilized to assign age classes to cattle with previously missing or open age information.
Atypical BSE cases were analyzed using different age classifications due to their lower annual incidence compared to C-BSE. Cases and tests were grouped into two-year age bands and testing campaigns. To achieve these two-year campaigns, the 2001 surveillance data was excluded, which also meant two atypical cases identified retrospectively were not included.
For small ruminants, data analysis employed descriptive statistics. Stratification of descriptions and calculations was performed when feasible, utilizing database variables such as surveillance target group (SHC, NSHC, SU, EM), flock status (infected, other, unknown, non-infected), surveillance activity (passive for SU, active for SHC/NSHC in non-infected flocks), country, year (from 2002), case type (classical or atypical scrapie), and index case status.
To track the trend of classical scrapie (CS) cases versus atypical scrapie (AS) cases from 2002 to 2015, an annual CS/AS ratio was generated for each Member State. A correction factor was applied when either the numerator or denominator was zero, using the formula (CS + 0.5)/(AS + 0.5) to replace the ratio. Years without any detected CS or AS cases were excluded from this calculation. A logarithmic scale was employed for the Y-axis to accommodate all surveillance target groups within a single figure.
Genotyping data were analyzed using the Great Britain's National Scrapie Plan (NSP) classification. To track the impact of resistance breeding in some Member States, the five NSP categories were condensed into three: resistant (homozygous ARR), semiresistant (heterozygous ARR excluding ARR/VRQ), and susceptible (non-ARR/VRQ).
Assumptions were necessary to describe and plot the data for bovine animals, sheep, and goats, as presented in the report.
For visualizing the historical trend of BSE cases (C-BSE, L-BSE, and H-BSE), cases with unknown or missing types were categorized as C-BSE. This assumption was made because the majority of such cases occurred prior to 2005.
For small ruminants, several assumptions guided the analysis. When plotting scrapie cases based on flock status, flocks with an 'unknown', 'other', or blank status were categorized as 'not-infected'. To track the progression of initial scrapie cases, all reported 'yes' index cases were presumed to originate from non-infected flocks. Conversely, if an index case was marked as 'no' or 'unknown', it was attributed to an 'infected flock'. Furthermore, for discriminatory testing results in TSE, it was assumed that all scrapie cases showing 'BSE-like', 'non-BSE-like', or 'inconclusive' outcomes in initial or secondary molecular tests had undergone discriminatory testing.

Furthermore, evolving EU legislation and its national implementation contributed to a lesser, but still notable, decrease in testing across other surveillance target groups over the years.

In 2015, a total of five BSE cases were identified across four EU Member States: Ireland, Slovenia, Spain, and the UK. These cases were all found within the fallen stock (FS) surveillance group, which involved testing 866,208 animals. Two of these were classical BSE (C-BSE), originating from animals born in 2009 and 2010, after the 2001 EU feed ban. The other three cases were atypical BSE, comprising two H-BSE types and one L-BSE type. Notably, the atypical cases occurred in older animals (over 144 months), while the C-BSE cases were in animals aged 60-71 and 72-83 months.
Norway, a non-Member State, reported one atypical H-type BSE case in 2015, identified within the fallen stock surveillance group. Details on BSE cases, including type, age, and country, are presented in Table 7.

The global and EU trend of classical BSE (C-BSE) has declined to a very low level of occurrence. Compared to 2013 (two cases) and 2014 (three cases), 2015 showed no significant change in either the total number of cases or the proportion of cases per million tests. Over the past three years, a total of eight C-BSE cases were reported.
Atypical BSE, encompassing both H-type and L-type, has consistently shown a low incidence. The period between 2003 and 2015 saw 96.6% of all confirmed cases undergo discriminatory testing, a result of both mandatory and retrospective testing protocols. Despite this, 621 cases, representing 18% of the total, still lack a definitive classification. Across 31 reporting nations, 100 atypical BSE cases were identified from 2003 to 2015, making up 2.8% of all cases subjected to discriminatory testing. These included 45 H-type and 55 L-type cases. In 2015 alone, three atypical cases were reported: two H-type in Slovenia and the UK, and one L-type in Spain.
The peak number of confirmed classical BSE cases occurred in the 1995 birth cohort, as shown in Figure 6. This suggests that exposure to the BSE agent in the EU began to decrease in the mid-1990s, assuming primary exposure happens within the first year of an animal's life.

Since 2002, approximately 8.4 million small ruminants in the EU have undergone TSE testing. The total number of sheep and goats tested for TSE has consistently declined since a peak in 2006, which was attributed to revisions in the TSE Regulation (Figures 9 and 10). In 2015, testing involved 319,638 sheep and 135,857 goats. Within non-infected flocks of both species, a downward trend is evident in the number of sheep and goats identified as clinical suspects and subsequently tested for TSE (SU target group). This trend suggests a reduction in clinical cases and a diminished awareness of scrapie among relevant parties. A similar pattern is observed for sheep and goats tested within the EM target group. However, potential misclassification in the database complicates this observation, given the significant number of flocks with "unknown" or "other" status, and past instances of animals from non-infected flocks being reported under eradication measures.

In 2015, the TSE surveillance program identified 641 scrapie cases in sheep and 1,052 in goats across the 28 EU Member States. An additional 40 sheep scrapie cases were reported by two non-Member States: Iceland (30 cases) and Norway (10 cases). Among the sheep cases, 124 (19.4%) were atypical scrapie (AS), 498 (77.7%) were classical scrapie (CS), and 19 (2.9%) were of an unknown type. Only 14 (1.3%) of the goat cases were atypical.
Classical scrapie is the predominant type reported in the EU. Excluding Cyprus's data, the ratio of atypical to classical scrapie in sheep (0.21) is twice that observed in goats (0.10).
In 2015, classical scrapie (CS) was identified in sheep across 10 EU Member States and Iceland, and in goats within eight Member States. Atypical scrapie (AS) was more widespread, reported in sheep from 16 EU Member States, Iceland, and Norway, and in goats from six Member States. Notably, Greece, Italy, Romania, and Spain collectively accounted for 80.5% (516 cases) of all sheep scrapie cases in the EU.
Cyprus reported a significant majority of goat scrapie cases in 2015, accounting for 924 of the 1,052 total, with only 17 being index cases. This substantial contribution from Cyprus clarifies the higher incidence in goats compared to sheep. However, these figures represent a decrease from Cyprus's 2014 report of 1,364 goat scrapie cases, which included 26 index cases.
At the EU level, the overall number of scrapie cases, for both classical (CS) and atypical (AS) types, has not shown a distinct decrease over the past six years, following a peak in reported cases in 2006 and 2007, which coincided with a peak in testing. This trend, particularly for goats, requires careful interpretation due to the significant impact of Cyprus, where the highest number of cases was observed in 2013. When examining cases by type and surveillance group, CS cases are more commonly identified within the "not slaughtered for human consumption" (NSHC) target group compared to other surveillance categories.

The proportion of classical scrapie (CS) cases per 10,000 tested goats has consistently exceeded that of atypical scrapie (AS) cases. This enduring trend is likely attributable to the significant and ongoing scrapie epidemics in Cyprus.
Classical scrapie cases are more prevalent in non-slaughtered animals than in those slaughtered for human consumption. This trend is not as pronounced for atypical scrapie cases.

To analyze the trend in classical versus atypical scrapie cases from 2002 to 2015, an annual ratio of classical scrapie (CS) to atypical scrapie (AS) was generated for each Member State (MS) and visualized in Figure 14. This analysis assumes that AS is uniformly distributed across the EU and is not affected by breeding initiatives, as per EFSA BIOHAZ Panel findings (2010, 2014). The plot reveals varied detection patterns among MS; some exclusively identified classical cases, while others found only atypical ones. A declining CS/AS ratio over time was observed in several MS, including Germany, France, Ireland, the Netherlands, and the UK, indicating a shift towards a more balanced detection of both types or an increase in AS relative to CS. Conversely, Greece, Italy, and Romania consistently reported a higher number of CS cases than AS cases, maintaining a ratio above 1. Another group of countries, comprising Austria, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Croatia, Poland, Portugal, and Sweden, showed a consistent prevalence of AS cases over CS cases, with their ratios remaining below 1. It's important to note that variations in the CS/AS ratio might also be influenced by differences in the proportion of animals tested within the "not slaughtered for human consumption" (NSHC) versus "slaughtered for human consumption" (SHC) target groups, both across countries and over time, given that CS is more frequently detected in NSHC animals.
A comparison of age in atypical scrapie (AS) and classical scrapie (CS) cases reveals a notable difference. AS cases in both sheep and goats present at an older age, with mean ages of 79.3 months (SE: 0.86) and 82.2 months (SE: 3.53) respectively. In contrast, CS cases are observed in younger animals, averaging 45.6 months (SE: 0.18) in sheep and 49.9 months (SE: 0.22) in goats. This age disparity holds true across all surveillance target groups, though it is less pronounced for AS cases in sheep within the eradication measures (EM) and suspect (SU) groups, likely due to fewer AS detections in those categories between 2011 and 2013.

Of the 404 sheep cases in the EU28 and three non-MS countries, 386 (95.5%) were confirmed as classical scrapie (BSE excluded), with only 4.5% yielding inconclusive results. In contrast, only 12% of goat scrapie cases were subjected to discriminatory testing. Among the 124 goat cases tested, 117 (94.3%) were also confirmed as classical scrapie, excluding BSE.

In 2015, four countries—Estonia, Finland, Hungary (EU Member States), and Norway (a non-Member State)—submitted data on TSE testing in non-ruminant species. A total of 597 samples were analyzed, with 580 from the EU and 17 from Norway, all yielding negative results. Cats accounted for over 90% of these samples, followed by mink and fox.
In 2015, the EU and Norway detected six bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) cases, despite a significant decrease in testing. The total number of bovine samples tested fell by nearly 40% from 2.3 million in the previous year to 1.4 million. This decline in testing was primarily due to a shift towards exhaustive testing of "risk animals" over 48 months old, rather than random sampling of slaughtered populations, a method that enhances case detection probability. The five EU cases were reported by four Member States: Ireland and the UK each reported one classical BSE (C-BSE) case, while Slovenia and the UK each had one H-type atypical BSE case, and Spain reported one L-type atypical BSE case. Norway also identified one H-type atypical case.
Countries detecting BSE cases have enhanced the accuracy of BSE classification over time. This improvement is attributed to a significant number of samples undergoing discriminatory testing, both retrospectively and prospectively.
The European BSE epidemic shows a declining trend, as indicated by surveillance data on both the absolute number and prevalence of reported cases. However, 2015 did not differ significantly from previous years in terms of case numbers. Two classical BSE cases were reported in 2015, originating from Ireland (born 2010) and the UK (born 2009), 14 and 13 years after their respective feed ban enforcements. This is comparable to the two and three classical BSE cases in 2013 and 2014, respectively, and five and eight atypical BSE cases in those same years.
Surveillance data, analyzed through age-period analysis, demonstrates a decline in C-BSE cases over time. This trend is further supported by an observed increase in the age group exhibiting the highest disease prevalence in later years. The peak detection rate in animals born in 1995, marking the onset of the decline, reinforces the conclusion that control measures, such as the feed ban, effectively reduced the disease's impact.
While no BSE cases have been found in cattle under 60 months for seven years, ongoing monitoring of risk animals over 48 months can still detect them. Identifying cases in younger animals is crucial, as it could signal a re-emergence of the BSE epidemic.
EU-wide TSE surveillance has tested approximately 8.4 million small ruminants since 2002. In 2015, 319,638 sheep and 135,857 goats underwent testing. A decline is observed in the number of sheep and goats from non-infected flocks tested as TSE suspects (SU target group). This indicates a decreasing trend in suspected cases within these flocks. The implementation of surveillance activities varies significantly between countries, reflecting high heterogeneity. Nevertheless, 24 Member States met the sampling requirements for sheep, and 25 for goats, within the SHC and NSHC surveillance groups.
In 2015, EU surveillance detected 641 scrapie cases in sheep and 1,052 in goats. Eighteen Member States reported ovine scrapie, while nine reported caprine scrapie. Notably, Cyprus accounted for 90% of the goat cases.
The epidemiological status of scrapie in small ruminants shows no clear overall improvement, whether considering the total number or proportion of cases. Nevertheless, several countries did exhibit a declining trend in the yearly ratios of classical to atypical scrapie.
Genotyping data repeatedly show a link between genotype and classical scrapie, with fewer susceptible animals observed in the sampled population in recent years.
