The Wnt signaling pathway's activation is implicated in the development of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC), and inhibiting this pathway presents a promising therapeutic avenue. This study aimed to pinpoint molecular targets for down-regulating canonical Wnt signaling specifically within HNSCC cells. To achieve this, a selection of candidate genes involved in Wnt signaling (including PORCN, WNT3A, FZD2, FZD5, LRP5, DVL1, CIP2A, SET, KDM1A, KDM4C, KDM6A, CBP, CARM1, KMT2A, TCF7, LEF1, PYGO1, and XIAP) were silenced using siRNA. Subsequently, promising targets were inhibited with small molecule inhibitors. The impact of these treatments on the expression of β-catenin-dependent genes was quantified via qRT-PCR. Cell viability was assessed using a resazurin assay in HNSCC cell lines. Further confirmation of Wnt-dependent gene expression inhibition was obtained through a luciferase reporter assay. Additionally, cell migration was evaluated using a scratch wound healing assay. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed via flow cytometry of propidium iodide-stained cells, and apoptosis induction was assessed by measuring caspase 3/7 activity using cytometric analysis. The results indicated that inhibiting Porcupine and the CBP/β-catenin interaction, using IWP-2 and PRI-724 respectively, most significantly impacted β-catenin-dependent gene expression in HNSCC cells. These inhibitors also demonstrated the ability to induce apoptosis and influence HNSCC cell migration. In conclusion, targeting Porcupine or the CBP/β-catenin interaction appears to be an effective strategy for inhibiting canonical Wnt signaling in HNSCC cells. Further research is necessary to validate the potential therapeutic efficacy of IWP-2 and PRI-724 in HNSCC.

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) ranks as the sixth most prevalent cancer globally. Frequent recurrence after standard treatments like surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy leads to poor prognoses. Enhancing patient survival necessitates a deeper comprehension of HNSCC's underlying molecular pathogenesis.
Activation of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway has been recognized as a significant factor in the development of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC). This pathway's activation can stem from various sources, including mutations in genes integral to the pathway, such as FAT1. Furthermore, aberrant methylation of genes responsible for encoding antagonists of Wnt signaling, both extracellularly and intracellularly, can also contribute to pathway dysregulation. Examples of such antagonists include SFRP1–5, WIF1, DKK1–3, DACH1, and PPP2R2B. Evidence of Wnt pathway activation in HNSCC is further supported by observations of increased expression levels for key pathway components. This includes Wnt ligands, Frizzled receptors, Dishevelled, and critically, β-catenin, within HNSCC cells. Additional indicators of this activation include the frequent detection of β-catenin within the cell nucleus and a heightened expression of genes regulated by β-catenin. These target genes, such as c-MYC, CCND1, MMP7, and survivin (BIRC5), play crucial roles in cellular processes. The clinical significance of this pathway's activation is underscored by findings that elevated β-catenin expression correlates with a poorer survival rate in patients with oral carcinomas. This observation is further substantiated by studies demonstrating that reducing β-catenin levels through knockdown significantly inhibits the growth of HNSCC cells and tumors. Animal models of oral tumor induction also show enhanced Wnt signaling, leading to the proposal that Wnt pathway inhibition could be a valuable preventive and therapeutic strategy for oral cancers. Notably, cigarette smoke has been identified as a factor that can induce Wnt signaling. Beyond proliferation, activated β-catenin signaling can also enhance cellular invasiveness and migratory capabilities. This suggests a role for β-catenin signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) within HNSCC cells, potentially contributing to local recurrence and lymph node metastasis. Moreover, β-catenin appears to impede keratinocyte differentiation, with higher expression levels observed in poorly differentiated tumors. The presence of nuclear β-catenin, typically absent in normal oral mucosa, is a distinguishing feature compared to oral leukoplakia. Dysplastic leukoplakia exhibits more pronounced nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, and enhanced Wnt signaling is hypothesized to drive increased cell proliferation in these pre-cancerous lesions. Collectively, these findings strongly indicate that the Wnt signaling pathway is implicated in the initiation and progression of head and neck cancers. Consequently, inhibiting this pathway holds therapeutic promise, not only for controlling HNSCC cell proliferation and invasion but also for targeting cancer stem cells, which are believed to drive tumor recurrence. The inhibition of the Wnt pathway may prove particularly effective against high-grade, invasive tumors that are currently challenging to treat.
The therapeutic potential of Wnt pathway inhibition in HNSCC remains largely unexplored, with limited strategies tested for blocking Wnt signaling in these cells. One approach, blocking Porcupine to attenuate Wnt ligand palmitoylation with LGK974, has shown Wnt signaling inhibition in a third of HNSCC cell lines. Additionally, immunotherapy targeting Wnt ligands has demonstrated an ability to inhibit the proliferation of specific HNSCC cell lines. Furthermore, all-trans retinoic acid's capacity to induce differentiation and suppress HNSCC cell growth may be partially attributed to its Wnt signaling inhibitory effects.
This study sought to identify molecular targets for inhibiting Wnt signaling in HNSCC. We selected eighteen candidate genes involved in Wnt pathway regulation, including PORCN, WNT3A, FZD2, FZD5, LRP5, DVL1, CIP2A, SET, KDM1A, KDM4C, KDM6A, CBP, CARM1, KMT2A, TCF7, LEF1, PYGO1, and XIAP. Our approach involved identifying targets whose downregulation effectively suppressed Wnt signaling in HNSCC cells. Subsequently, we evaluated how modulating these targets impacted HNSCC cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis.
For this study, HNSCC cell lines originating from various anatomical sites, including tongue (CAL27, SCC-25), hypopharynx (BICR6, FaDu), and floor of mouth (H314, UM-SCC-1), were utilized. A normal immortalized keratinocyte cell line, OKF4/TERT-1, also from the floor of mouth, served as a comparison. These cell lines were acquired from commercial sources: ATCC (CAL27, FaDu, SCC-25), ECACC (BICR6, H314), and Merck (UM-SCC-1). The OKF4/TERT-1 cell line was obtained from Professor James Rheinwald's laboratory at Harvard Medical School.
HNSCC cell lines CAL27, FaDu, BICR6, and H314 were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and antibiotics. The immortalized keratinocyte line, OKF4/TERT-1, used for comparison, was maintained in Keratinocyte-SFM supplemented with growth factors and CaCl2. SCC-25 and UM-SCC-1 cells utilized a combined DMEM and Keratinocyte-SFM medium. All cell lines were incubated under standard conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity.
CAL27 and FaDu cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. Immediately following seeding, cells were transfected with 40 nM siRNA using the jetPRIME transfection agent, following the manufacturer's instructions. Two distinct siRNA duplexes, targeting different regions of the transcript sequence for each gene, were employed. Non-targeting siRNA served as a control. RNA was isolated 48 hours post-transfection, and the experiments were conducted twice.
To quantitatively analyze gene expression at the transcript level, total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using a Universal RNA Purification Kit (EURx, Poland). RNA quality and quantity were assessed via spectrophotometry with a NanoDrop apparatus (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized according to the manufacturer's protocol using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Real-time PCR reactions were then performed using Hot FIREPol EvaGreen qPCR Mix Plus (Solis Biodyne, Estonia) on a LightCycler 96 apparatus (Roche, Germany). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate across two independent experiments. Previously reported primer sequences for β-catenin target genes (Axin2, BIRC5, CCND1, c-MYC, and MMP7) were utilized, with sequences for siRNA knockdown efficiency analysis available upon request. Transcript level changes were quantified using the ΔΔCt method, with normalization performed using the mean expression of two reference genes, TBP and PBGD.
To evaluate the impact of various small molecule inhibitors on HNSCC cell viability, a resazurin assay was employed. Eight specific inhibitors were utilized, targeting KDM6A/B (GSK-J4), KDM1A (GSK-LSD1), Porcupine (IWP-2), KDM4C (ML324), CARM1 (MS049), Dishevelled (Dvl-PDZ Domain Inhibitor II), CBP/β-catenin (PRI-724), and KMT2A (MM-102). Stock solutions of these compounds were prepared in DMSO at concentrations of 20 mM, with IWP-2 at 8.75 mM, and stored at -20 °C. The resazurin assay relies on the conversion of resazurin to fluorescent resorufin by metabolically active cells, allowing for a fluorescence-based measurement of cell viability. For this assay, HNSCC cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, the growth medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different concentrations of the inhibitors and incubated for 24 hours. Following this incubation, cells were washed, and fresh medium with resazurin was added. After a 2-hour incubation, fluorescence was measured. This assay was conducted at least twice, with each experiment including four replicates.
To assess protein levels, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 10^5 cells per well. Following overnight pre-incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the test compounds, and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 hours. Subsequently, cells were collected via trypsinization. Protein extraction was performed using Laemmli buffer, and the samples were immediately heated at 96 °C for 15 minutes. Protein concentrations were determined using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Western blotting was employed to analyze Axin2 and survivin protein levels. For this, 50 μg of protein extract was separated by 7.5% SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, USA) and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 10% skimmed milk. Primary antibodies, a rabbit polyclonal antibody against Axin2 and a mouse monoclonal antibody against survivin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), were used for incubation. β-actin was utilized as a loading control. After washing, the membranes were incubated with alkaline phosphatase-labeled anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary IgG antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). Visualization was achieved using a BCIP/NBT AP Conjugate Substrate Kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using Quantity One software, and values were calculated as relative absorbance units (RQ) per mg of protein.
To confirm the inhibitory effects of PRI-724 and IWP-2 on Wnt-dependent gene expression, a luciferase reporter assay was conducted. CAL27 and FaDu cells were cultured in 96-well plates and co-transfected with reporter and control plasmids using ViaFect transfection agent, following manufacturer guidelines. The following day, cells were treated with varying concentrations of either IWP-2 or PRI-724, with vehicle-treated cells serving as controls. Half of the replicates for each compound were also supplemented with Wnt3a ligand. After a 24-hour incubation period, chemiluminescence was measured using a Nano-Glo® Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System and a GloMax microplate reader. The experiment was performed twice, and relative luminescence was calculated to assess the impact of the compounds on TCF/LEF-dependent luciferase production.
To evaluate the impact of the selected compounds on cell migration, a scratch wound healing assay was employed. Cells were initially seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight in serum-reduced medium. The following day, confluent cell layers were mechanically wounded with a pipette tip. After rinsing with warm PBS to remove detached cells, fresh serum-reduced medium containing the specified concentrations of test compounds was added. Each well was then photographed using a JuLI FL microscope. Subsequent photographs of the same areas were captured at 7, 13, or 18 hours post-treatment (tx). The incubation duration varied for each cell line to accommodate differences in their migration dynamics, ensuring that the wound area did not fully close in vehicle-treated control cells. Cell coverage area was quantified at both the initial (t0) and final (tx) time points using JuLI FL software, and the difference in area was calculated. These experiments were conducted twice, with three independent replicates per assay. Vehicle-treated cells (DMSO) served as controls. The relative effect on cell migration (RM) was determined using the formula: RM = [test compound (area tx - area t0) / control (area tx - area t0)] x 100%.
To assess cell cycle distribution, cells (1 × 10^5/well) were plated in 12-well plates. Following overnight pre-incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the test compounds, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 hours. Vehicle-treated cells served as negative controls, while topotecan-treated cells acted as positive controls. Cells were then harvested via trypsinization, washed with PBS buffer, and fixed in 70% ethanol. After storage at -20 °C for at least overnight, fixed cells were centrifuged. Cell cycle distributions were analyzed using a Muse Cell Cycle Kit (Merck, Germany) as per the manufacturer's instructions. This involved washing cell pellets with PBS buffer and incubating them with propidium iodide in the presence of RNase A. Flow cytometric analysis of the stained samples was conducted using a Muse Cell Analyzer (Merck, Germany), with data from two independent experiments analyzed using Muse 1.5 Analysis software.
To assess the impact of the compounds on apoptosis, cells were cultured in 12-well plates. Following an overnight pre-incubation, the medium was exchanged with fresh medium containing the test compounds, and cells were incubated for an additional 48 hours. Vehicle-treated cells served as negative controls, while topotecan-treated cells acted as positive controls. Subsequently, cells were harvested via trypsinization. Caspase-3/7 activity was then quantified using a Muse Caspase-3/7 Kit from Merck, following the manufacturer's instructions. This assay utilizes a fluorescently labeled peptide as a substrate for caspases. Upon cleavage, the released dye interacts with DNA. The inclusion of 7-AAD in the staining procedure enabled the classification of cells into four distinct populations: live, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and dead. The cell pellets were washed with PBS buffer, incubated with the substrate peptide and 7-AAD, and then analyzed by flow cytometry using a Muse Cell Analyzer. Data from two independent experiments were processed using Muse 1.5 Analysis software.
Statistical significance was determined using Student's t test (p ≤ 0.05), with analyses conducted via STATISTICA 10 software.
The canonical Wnt signaling pathway is a complex, multi-step process that can be targeted for inhibition at various points, from the initial secretion of Wnt ligands to the final activation of transcription by TCF/LEF factors. To identify effective targets for Wnt pathway inhibition in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cells, we employed a siRNA screening approach. This screen focused on a comprehensive list of 18 druggable targets, including those involved in pathway initiation (e.g., PORCN, WNT3A, FZD2, FZD5, LRP5, DVL1) and those regulating the nuclear transcriptional activity of β-catenin (e.g., KDM1A, KDM4C, KDM6A, CBP, CARM1, KMT2A, TCF7, LEF1, PYGO1, XIAP). Our primary method for assessing Wnt pathway inhibition involved measuring the expression levels of key TCF/LEF target genes: Axin2, CCND1, MMP7, c-MYC, and BIRC5. For each gene, we utilized two distinct siRNA duplexes to ensure efficient knockdown. Initially, we confirmed the efficacy of our transfection protocol by evaluating gene silencing efficiency. In the majority of cases, siRNA transfection in CAL27 and FaDu cells resulted in a substantial reduction (at least 70%) of the targeted gene transcript levels, as depicted in Figure 1a. To validate our experimental system's ability to identify effective Wnt inhibition targets, we assessed the impact of siRNAs targeting CTNNB1, the gene encoding β-catenin, a central mediator of Wnt pathway-dependent gene expression. Knockdown of CTNNB1 led to a decrease in the expression of Axin2, MMP7, and c-MYC, confirming the suitability of our setup for the screening process. Subsequently, we analyzed the expression of Wnt pathway-dependent genes 48 hours post-transfection. We observed that Axin2 and MMP7 expression were most frequently affected by the siRNA treatments compared to other Wnt target genes (Figure 1, b-f). Notably, siRNAs targeting histone demethylases (KDM1A, KDM4C, KDM6A), methyltransferases (KMT2A, CARM1), and acetyltransferases (CBP) significantly inhibited TCF/LEF-dependent gene expression. Similar inhibitory effects were observed following transfection with siRNAs against Porcupine, Dishevelled, Wnt3a, and Frizzled 2. Based on these findings, we selected eight targets for further investigation into their potential to inhibit canonical Wnt signaling: Porcupine, Dvl, KDM1A, KDM4C, KDM6A, KMT2A, CARM1, and CBP. Commercially available small molecule inhibitors exist for all these identified proteins.
To determine suitable concentrations for inhibitor testing, a resazurin assay was employed to evaluate their impact on the viability of both HNSCC and immortalized keratinocyte cell lines (Fig. 2). Notably, inhibitors targeting KDM6 (GSK-J4), KDM4 (ML324), and the CBP/β-catenin interaction (PRI-724) demonstrated the most substantial reductions in cell viability. Higher concentrations of the KMT2A inhibitor (MM-102) also significantly decreased cell viability. In contrast, inhibitors of Dishevelled (Dvl-PDZ Domain Inhibitor II), Porcupine (IWP-2), CARM1 (MS049), and KDM1A (GSK-LSD1) exhibited only low to moderate effects on cell viability across the tested concentration range. For subsequent experiments, sub-toxic concentrations, defined as those causing a reduction in cell viability of 50% or less, were selected for each compound.
The selection of drug targets with the potential to inhibit Wnt-dependent transcription was refined through a three-step process. Initially, the impact of various compounds on the expression levels of Wnt target genes was evaluated in CAL27 and FaDu cells, which had previously been utilized for the siRNA screening. During this phase, inhibitors targeting KDM4 (ML324) and KDM6 (GSK-J4) were found to be ineffective. These compounds did not yield the desired outcome, as they either left the expression of Axin2, a key indicator of Wnt pathway activity, unchanged or even increased it. Consequently, these inhibitors were excluded from further investigation. The inhibitor of KDM1A (GSK-LSD1) also demonstrated a lack of significant activity. While it showed a tendency to slightly reduce Axin2 expression in FaDu cells, this effect was not considered substantial. In contrast, all other tested compounds exhibited some capacity to decrease the transcript levels of Wnt target genes in at least one of the cell lines. Among these, the inhibitor targeting the interaction between CBP and β-catenin, PRI-724, displayed the most potent activity. It significantly reduced the expression of Axin2, MMP7, and BIRC5. However, it also led to an increase in the expression of CCND1 and c-MYC. Similar, though less pronounced, effects were observed with IWP-2. The inhibitors of KMT2A (MM-102), Dishevelled (Dvl-PDZ Domain Inhibitor II), and CARM1 (MS049) were found to be effective only in FaDu cells, not in CAL27 cells. These six compounds were then subjected to further testing in three additional HNSCC cell lines: BICR6, H314, and SCC-25. This step was crucial to determine the generalizability of their Wnt pathway-inhibitory effects across different HNSCC subtypes (Fig. 3c–e). Based on their insufficient activity in these cell lines, the inhibitors of KMT2A (MM-102) and KDM1A (GSK-LSD1) were ultimately discarded. The remaining four compounds underwent verification in another carcinoma cell line, UM-SCC-1, as well as in normal immortalized keratinocytes, OKF4/TERT-1 (Fig. 3f, g). Across these evaluations, the inhibitors of CBP/β-catenin (PRI-724) and Porcupine (IWP-2) consistently demonstrated the most significant reductions in the expression levels of three critical Wnt target genes: Axin2, MMP7, and BIRC5. Notably, the UM-SCC-1 cell line exhibited the highest resistance to the inhibitory effects of the tested compounds. Conversely, the inhibition of TCF/LEF-dependent gene expression was not confined to carcinoma cells, as similar effects were observed in OKF4/TERT-1 keratinocytes. Furthermore, these compounds were found to reduce the protein levels of Axin2 and survivin (Fig. 4). To further validate the efficacy of the two most potent modulators of Wnt pathway-dependent transcription, PRI-724 and IWP-2, a TCF/LEF luciferase reporter assay was conducted in CAL27 and FaDu cells (Fig. 3h). In this assay, cells were treated with either PRI-724 or IWP-2 for 24 hours, with or without the addition of Wnt3a ligand. The results indicated that IWP-2 was more effective than PRI-724 in significantly reducing TCF/LEF-dependent luciferase production. This inhibitory effect was even more pronounced when the cells were additionally stimulated with Wnt3a.
To further investigate the biological impact of four selected inhibitors—PRI-724, IWP-2, MS049, and Dvl-PDZ Domain Inhibitor II—we conducted assays to evaluate their effects on cell migration, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis. Cell migration was assessed using a scratch wound healing assay. This method revealed that both PRI-724 and IWP-2 demonstrated an ability to inhibit the migration of several head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cell lines, including BICR6, CAL27, FaDu, and SCC-25. Notably, IWP-2 was the sole inhibitor to show a significant effect on the migration of the UM-SCC-1 cell line. Regarding cell cycle distribution, the tested compounds exhibited a low to moderate capacity to alter the phase distribution of cells. PRI-724 was observed to have the most pronounced effects, leading to a reduction in the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase in the CAL27, SCC-25, and UM-SCC-1 cell lines. Conversely, it caused a slight increase in the G0/G1 population in the FaDu cell line. Importantly, with the exception of the highest concentration of PRI-724, none of the compounds significantly impacted cell cycle progression in the control OKF4/TERT-1 keratinocytes. As anticipated, the positive control, topotecan, induced a G2/M phase arrest across all tested cell lines. The impact of these four compounds on the induction of apoptosis was subsequently evaluated through flow cytometric measurement of caspase-3/7 activity. Topotecan, serving as a positive control, significantly increased the number of apoptotic cells in most cell lines, with the exception of BICR6. In line with their effects on other cellular processes, PRI-724 and IWP-2 demonstrated the most potent apoptotic activity among the tested inhibitors. Specific sensitivities were observed: BICR6 and CAL27 cells were more susceptible to the pro-apoptotic effects of IWP-2, whereas FaDu, SCC-25, and UM-SCC-1 cells exhibited higher rates of apoptosis when treated with PRI-724. Furthermore, the induction of apoptosis by PRI-724 and IWP-2 was comparable to, and in some cases even surpassed, that of topotecan in CAL27, SCC-25, and UM-SCC-1 cells. In contrast, only IWP-2 significantly induced apoptosis in the control OKF4/TERT-1 keratinocytes, and this effect was similar in magnitude to that observed with topotecan.
Canonical Wnt signaling aberrations are a recognized feature of colorectal cancer development, often stemming from mutations in APC and CTNNB1. This pathway's activation is also evident in other cancers, notably head and neck cancer, where it's emerging as a promising therapeutic target. The core of canonical Wnt signaling involves β-catenin, which drives the transcription of genes like CCND1, c-MYC, MMP7, and survivin. These target genes promote cell cycle progression and migration while suppressing apoptosis. Furthermore, the transcriptional upregulation initiated by β-catenin may depend on epigenetic modifiers such as histone methyltransferases, acetyltransferases, and demethylases. Specifically, CBP acetyltransferase has been implicated in the β-catenin-mediated expression of genes crucial for cell proliferation.
Our research focused on identifying optimal targets for inhibiting canonical Wnt signaling within head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cells. To achieve this, we initially screened a comprehensive panel of potential targets using siRNA-mediated gene silencing. This panel included genes involved in the initiation of the Wnt pathway, such as PORCN, WNT3A, FZD2, FZD5, LRP5, DVL1, CIP2A, and SET, as well as those regulating the nuclear activity of β-catenin, including KDM1A, KDM4C, KDM6A, CBP, CARM1, KMT2A, TCF7, LEF1, PYGO1, and XIAP. Given the diverse mechanisms that can activate Wnt signaling in HNSCC, targeting proteins both upstream and downstream of key pathway components offered potential for cascade down-regulation. To evaluate pathway inhibition, we monitored the expression levels of Wnt target genes like Axin2, MMP7, BIRC5, c-MYC, and CCND1, employing these as pharmacodynamic indicators, consistent with prior studies. Our findings highlighted Porcupine, a cell membrane-bound O-acyltransferase essential for Wnt ligand secretion, and CBP, a critical component of the β-catenin transcriptional complex, as the most effective targets for reducing Wnt-dependent gene expression in the tested HNSCC cells. Previous research has demonstrated that LGK974, a Porcupine inhibitor, significantly decreased β-catenin-dependent Axin2 expression in a subset of head and neck cancer cell lines. This compound also reduced Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in certain squamous cell carcinomas. In our study, we investigated IWP-2, another Porcupine inhibitor with a distinct chemical structure. IWP-2 has previously shown efficacy in reducing Wnt/β-catenin signaling in gastric cancer cells. Our results confirmed that IWP-2 substantially lowered the transcript levels of Axin2, MMP7, and BIRC5 across most cell lines. Furthermore, its inhibitory activity was validated through a reporter assay in FaDu and CAL27 cells. Notably, FaDu cells, which were previously considered unresponsive to LGK974 based on a strict reduction threshold, exhibited moderate decreases in Axin2, MMP7, and BIRC5 expression with IWP-2 treatment. The reporter assay revealed a more pronounced inhibitory effect in FaDu cells, suggesting their responsiveness to Wnt signaling suppression via Porcupine inhibition.
PRI-724, a second-generation antagonist targeting the CBP/β-catenin interaction, has advanced to clinical trials. It is a more potent enantiomer of ICG-001, which demonstrated a reduction in head and neck cancer growth in preclinical studies. In our research, PRI-724 decreased the expression of β-catenin-dependent genes. However, its impact on TCF/LEF reporter-based luciferase production was less pronounced than that of IWP-2. While ICG-001 was previously noted to affect stem-like tumor cells and was considered cytostatic rather than inducing cell death, our findings indicate that PRI-724 exhibits both cytostatic and cytotoxic effects on HNSCC cells. This was evidenced by a significant reduction in cell viability across all tested cell lines and the induction of apoptosis in all carcinoma cell lines, though not in immortalized oral keratinocytes.
Contrary to our initial hypothesis, inhibiting other target proteins had a limited impact on canonical Wnt signaling in HNSCC cells. Only the inhibition of Dishevelled and CARM1 showed some effects. While the PDZ domain of Dishevelled engages in weak interactions with Frizzled receptors, its inhibition has been proposed to influence Wnt signaling. However, this effect might be diminished by elevated Wnt ligand levels. CARM1 has been implicated in the formation of the β-catenin transcriptional complex and has also been shown to antagonize CBP through methylation. Consequently, the impact of Dvl-PDZ Domain Inhibitor II and MS049 on β-catenin-dependent gene expression was minimal. Blocking the PDZ domain did result in slight alterations in cell cycle distribution and induced apoptosis in HNSCC cells, suggesting these strategies may lack significant therapeutic potential for this cancer type. The low inhibitory activity of the PDZ domain might be insufficient to substantially reduce Wnt signaling. Similarly, the limited effects observed with MS049 suggest CARM1 may play a too minor role in the canonical Wnt pathway in HNSCC for therapeutic efficacy.
Epigenetic modifiers such as CBP, MLL, and KDM6 have been implicated in the initiation and progression of HNSCC, potentially through mutations [41]. These proteins also play a role in β-catenin-dependent transcriptional control. For instance, CBP collaborates with MLL (KMT2) to promote H3K4 trimethylation at the promoters of genes crucial for β-catenin-dependent self-renewal in salivary gland tumors [36]. However, MM-102, an inhibitor of MLL, did not affect Wnt target gene expression in our study. Similarly, while KDM4 demethylases are necessary for β-catenin transcriptional activity in other cancers [42, 43], their inhibition with ML324 yielded no results in HNSCC cells. LSD1/KDM1A histone demethylase has also been linked to head and neck carcinogenesis [44], and previous research indicates that GSK-LSD1 inhibitors only reduce β-catenin signaling in myoepithelial cells, not in tonsil cancer cells [45]. These findings align with our own observations.
Our findings collectively indicate that targeting Porcupine or the CBP/β-catenin interaction presents a viable strategy for inhibiting canonical Wnt signaling in HNSCC cells. The compounds IWP-2 and PRI-724 demonstrated efficacy not only in suppressing cell proliferation but also in inducing apoptosis. Furthermore, both agents impacted cell migration to some degree, aligning with the known association between Wnt signaling and cancer cell invasion. However, it remains a possibility that the observed effects of IWP-2 and PRI-724 on HNSCC cells could involve the modulation of pathways beyond the canonical Wnt pathway. Additional research is necessary to thoroughly understand the mechanisms of action of these compounds and to confirm their anti-cancer effects in other preclinical models.
