Uganda frequently experiences outbreaks of dangerous infectious diseases and other public health crises. Despite considerable advancements in emergency response over the last two decades, systemic vulnerabilities persist. A key weakness is the absence of a strong workforce equipped to detect, investigate, and manage disease outbreaks at their origin. This shortage of skilled personnel hinders global health security efforts. To cultivate a highly capable workforce for combating infectious diseases, the Uganda Ministry of Health initiated the Public Health Fellowship Program (PHFP). This advanced Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP) draws inspiration from the CDC's Epidemic Intelligence Service (EIS) program. As Africa's inaugural post-master's FETP, this two-year, full-time, non-degree program targets mid-career public health professionals. Participants acquire expertise across seven core domains, evidenced by their project deliverables, with 80% of their learning occurring through practical service delivery within the Ministry of Health. Between 2015 and 2017, the PHFP admitted three cohorts, totaling 31 fellows. By January 2018, 19 fellows from the 2015 and 2016 cohorts had successfully completed the program. These fellows were strategically assigned to 17 critical areas within the Ministry of Health. Their work encompassed 153 projects, including 60 outbreak investigations, 12 refugee assessments, 40 surveillance projects, and 31 applied epidemiologic studies. Notably, 49 of these projects focused on potential bioterrorism agents or diseases prone to epidemics. Fellows also contributed significantly to knowledge dissemination, delivering 132 conference presentations, preparing 40 manuscripts for peer-reviewed publication (17 of which were published by December 2017), and developing three case studies. Many of these initiatives directly led to public health interventions that enhanced disease control and surveillance systems. The program has also been instrumental in publishing 19 editions of Ministry of Health bulletins. A year after graduating, all program alumni have secured influential public health decision-making roles. Within its initial three years, the PHFP has bolstered global health security by improving public health emergency response, facilitating the identification, investigation, and control of outbreaks at their source, and ensuring that findings are documented and shared to inform stakeholder decisions.

Public health systems in Sub-Saharan Africa have long suffered from persistent deficiencies. These include inadequate infrastructure, limited diagnostic capabilities, and underdeveloped information systems. A critical shortage of skilled public health professionals, such as field epidemiologists and laboratory staff, has further hampered the effective management of disease outbreaks. The demand for proficient public health workers has outstripped supply, largely due to the escalating challenges posed by the HIV/AIDS pandemic, the continuous emergence and reemergence of novel microbes, increasing antimicrobial resistance, and the rise in zoonotic diseases stemming from more frequent human-wildlife contact. The devastating 2013-2016 Ebola epidemic in West Africa underscored these systemic weaknesses, particularly the lack of capacity for outbreak detection and control. Insufficient preparedness for communicable disease outbreaks exacerbated the crisis. Research indicates that major viral hemorrhagic fever epidemics, like Ebola, could be averted through swift national responses, transmission interruption, and effective containment before they escalate.
In February 2014, global nations initiated the Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA), a response to lessons learned from epidemics like Ebola. This initiative aimed to enhance national capabilities against infectious disease threats and prioritize global health security. A key component of GHSA is workforce development, which suggests a requirement of one trained field epidemiologist per 200,000 people. Consequently, Uganda, with its 41 million population, needs 205 trained field epidemiologists.
Uganda faces significant public health challenges, including a high burden of endemic diseases, rising noncommunicable diseases, and frequent outbreaks of severe pathogens like Ebola, Marburg, yellow fever, anthrax, and plague. Despite Makerere University's Master of Public Health (MPH) program, which has trained 173 field epidemiology graduates since 1994, the country's public sector still lacks sufficient skilled personnel. Most MPH graduates find employment in the private sector, NGOs, international agencies, or academia, leaving a critical gap in public health human resources at both national and subnational levels. This shortage is particularly concerning given Uganda's low life expectancy and healthy life expectancy, highlighting the inadequacy of current capacities to address its diverse health crises.
To address the significant shortage of field epidemiologists in Uganda's public sector, the Ministry of Health launched the Uganda Public Health Fellowship Program (PHFP) in 2015. This initiative, Africa's inaugural post-master's field epidemiology training program, received crucial backing from partners like Makerere University School of Public Health (MakSPH) and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The PHFP aims to bolster the nation's capacity to tackle various public health issues by training and integrating epidemiologists directly into service. This paper details the program's establishment, structure, and its impact over its initial three years, focusing on its role in enhancing global health security and managing other public health concerns within Uganda.
Modeled after the US Epidemic Intelligence Service (EIS), the PHFP is a full-time, 2-year fellowship program that does not grant a degree. It focuses on developing competencies in mid-career health professionals aspiring to public health leadership. Applicants must hold a master's degree in a health-related field like public health, veterinary public health, epidemiology, or nutrition. Over two years, fellows achieve proficiency in six competency domains by completing a project portfolio for each.
The PHFP benefits from a robust organizational structure, with a steering committee comprising crucial internal and external stakeholders offering overall guidance. Its leadership is shared among three primary entities: the Ministry of Health, MakSPH, and the CDC. Daily operations are managed by a dedicated program secretariat, which includes a field coordinator, two field supervisors, a scientific writer, a training manager, and an administrative assistant. A key figure within the secretariat is the full-time CDC resident advisor, an EIS program graduate. This advisor, possessing extensive expertise in medicine, field epidemiology, and biostatistics, and prior experience in establishing FETPs, was instrumental in the PHFP's development. Their contributions spanned curriculum design, didactic instruction, direct mentorship during complex investigations, advising on methodologies, ensuring compliance with requirements like human-subject protection, and overseeing the funding cooperative agreement.
The PHFP, a component of Uganda's Ministry of Health, collaborates closely with its Epidemiology and Surveillance Division, Public Health Emergency Operations Centre, and other key public health initiatives. Its primary financial backing comes from the U.S. government, specifically through the President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, the President's Malaria Initiative, and the Global Health Security Agenda.
To ensure fellows' full commitment to the intensive two-year program, they must resign from prior employment, receiving a monthly stipend as compensation. Annually, around ten fellows are chosen after a rigorous selection process. This process involves an interview with Q&A, an oral presentation, and an essay. Applications are solicited through various channels, including media, the program's website, and professional and alumni associations.
The program's educational framework incorporates two intensive six-week didactic courses. The initial course, delivered at the program's outset, focuses on applied field epidemiology, statistical methods, quality improvement, outbreak cost analysis, effective communication, public health programming (translating evidence into practice), and leadership skills. A more advanced course, offered late in the first year, delves into sophisticated epidemiological and statistical methods, public health in complex emergencies, and disease burden assessment. Instruction for these courses is provided by a diverse group, including Makerere University School of Public Health academics, Ministry of Health technical personnel, WHO Uganda staff, PHFP secretariat members, program graduates (after the first cohort), and CDC resident and technical staff. Furthermore, a one-day mentorship workshop is held immediately after fellows are placed, to equip academic and host institution mentors with necessary guidance and skills.
Following initial coursework, fellows spend the majority of their two-year program in practical, field-based training at various host sites. These placements are carefully selected, considering Ministry of Health priorities, opportunities for applied epidemiology learning, the fellow's career aspirations, and the presence of skilled mentors and accessible surveillance data. During this period, fellows engage in critical public health projects, guided by experienced professionals.
Fellows' progress is continuously monitored and evaluated quarterly and annually, with achievements documented. This evaluation involves the fellow, their host and academic mentors, and PHFP secretariat technical staff, utilizing a checklist against established criteria. Additionally, program staff visit host sites as needed to assist fellows with objectives and problem-solving.
Upon completing the two-year fellowship, fellows compile a portfolio detailing their accomplishments. They then participate in a final defense session, where they present a significant project contributing to disease control and public health. Successful completion of this defense earns them a certificate.
The Public Health Fellowship Program (PHFP) began in January 2015 with its inaugural cohort of 10 fellows. By the close of 2017, a total of 31 fellows had been recruited and trained across three cohorts: 10 each in 2015 and 2016, and 11 in 2017. An additional 11 fellows for the 2018 cohort were recruited and were scheduled to commence their fellowship in January 2018. The program has seen a significant increase in interest, with applications rising from 44 in 2015 to 195 in 2018. Fellows admitted to the program possess a diverse range of advanced degrees. The majority (14) hold an MPH or MScPH in general public health. Other qualifications include an MMed (1), MSc in epidemiology or clinical epidemiology (4), MSc in entomology, parasitology, or zoology (2), MSc in international or public health (2), and MSc in health services research and management (2). Single fellows also hold degrees in veterinary preventive medicine (BVM), medical microbiology (MSc), infectious disease management (MSc), and health policy (MSc). These fellows have been strategically assigned to 17 priority areas within the Ministry of Health. As of January 2018, the first cohort had successfully graduated, with the second cohort slated for graduation that same month.
In its initial three years, the PHFP's 31 fellows undertook 153 applied epidemiology projects, averaging 10 per fellow. These projects included 60 outbreak investigations and 12 emergency refugee health assessments. Spanning the entire country, these investigations focused on critical health concerns, such as high-priority diseases like viral hemorrhagic fevers, malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV/AIDS. They also addressed diseases targeted for eradication, including Guinea worm disease, human African trypanosomiasis, and measles, alongside pathogens with bioterrorism potential, such as cholera and anthrax.
Over the last five years, the Ministry of Health has developed three foundational components: PHEOC, ESD, and PHFP. These pillars ensure timely reporting, response, and investigation of disease outbreaks. Their work facilitates the recommendation of cost-effective interventions and the generation of evidence to prevent future public health crises.
The Public Health Fellowship Program (PHFP) serves as the primary investigative unit for outbreak response within Uganda's Ministry of Health. Over its initial three years, PHFP fellows undertook 153 projects, with a significant portion (78, or 51%) focusing on high-priority diseases, including those with bioterrorism potential, epidemic-prone illnesses, and other critical health concerns for Uganda. The findings from these investigations, documented in outbreak reports, are regularly submitted to the Public Health Emergency Operations Centre (PHEOC) and presented to the National Task Force for Epidemic Preparedness and Response, thus informing strategies for prevention and control. PHFP's swift epidemiological investigations have demonstrably reduced the time needed to identify hazardous pathogens and effectively curtailed the spread of outbreaks. A notable example is the 2016 yellow fever outbreak, which was confirmed within 12 days and brought under control within three weeks of its report, a significant improvement compared to the 2010 yellow fever outbreak that required 40 days for confirmation and three months for control. Recognizing these achievements, PHFP was honored with the CDC Director's Award for Excellence in Public Health and Response at the 2017 Epidemic Intelligence Service (EIS) conference.
Beyond the PHFP, Uganda's Ministry of Health and CDC launched a Frontline FETP in 2016 for district-level public health workers, focusing on outbreak detection and control. This complements MakSPH's MPH program, which has trained field epidemiologists since 1994.
The Ministry of Health is effectively developing a skilled workforce of field epidemiologists, enhancing Uganda's capabilities in epidemiology, surveillance, and outbreak response. This strategic development allows the nation to tackle critical public health threats. These initiatives are fundamental to Uganda's success in managing public health challenges that impact global health security, achieved through strong collaborations with various ministries, agencies, and partners focused on emergency preparedness and response.
PHFP fellows undertook numerous projects focusing on HIV/AIDS epidemiology, generating crucial evidence to inform "90-90-90" target programming. Their work explored various aspects, including service uptake among youth, virological nonsuppression, and care quality for high-risk groups. Additionally, they investigated HIV knowledge and behavior in fishing communities, mother-to-child transmission services, Option B+ reporting quality, and HIV services within refugee populations.
Fellows assigned to the National Malaria Control Program or focused on malaria control have significantly aided the Ministry of Health's elimination plan and PMI targets. Their contributions include creating the Uganda National Malaria Quarterly Bulletin, mapping malaria incidence with QGIS, and setting surveillance thresholds using Health Management Information System data. Additional projects explored the link between vector control and malaria incidence, malaria morbidity in young children, and mosquito behavior and pyrethroid susceptibility. Crucially, their analysis of malaria incidence data informed the decision to distribute long-lasting insecticide nets across Uganda.
Between 2015 and 2017, PHFP produced 40 manuscripts for peer-reviewed journals, with 17 published by the end of 2017. These published findings garnered significant international media attention. For instance, an investigation into podoconiosis, featured in the American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene, was highlighted by 29 media outlets, including major international news organizations like CNN, The New York Times, BBC, VOA, Newsweek, and NPR, by May 2, 2017.
PHFP fellows have presented 132 accepted abstracts at various national and international conferences. Since 2015, they have consistently presented at the highly competitive annual EIS conference's international night, including an oral presentation in 2015 and both oral and poster presentations in 2016 and 2017. These presentations have garnered five international awards, notably the distinguished Jeffrey P. Koplan Award for Scientific Poster Presentation in 2016.
PHFP has significantly contributed to public health communication by assisting the Ministry of Health in revitalizing three existing epidemiology bulletins and launching three new ones: the Neglected Tropical Diseases Bulletin, the National TB and Leprosy Program Bulletin, and the Non-communicable Diseases Bulletin. These publications serve as platforms for fellows and other Ministry of Health epidemiologists to disseminate crucial public health data. The quarterly Ministry of Health Epidemiological Bulletin has featured 95 articles derived from fellows' projects, while the Weekly Epidemiological Bulletin has been consistently produced since August 2015. Additionally, fellows have engaged the public by publishing numerous articles in national newspapers, addressing current health challenges like outbreaks and offering disease prevention advice.
PHFP investigations have been instrumental in guiding public health authorities to implement effective interventions. For instance, in 2015, the identification and investigation of a typhoid fever outbreak in Kampala led to a multisectoral control effort. Similarly, investigations into a cholera outbreak in Kasese District in 2015 prompted repairs to the public water system. In 2016, an investigation in Kamwenge District resulted in health worker training and community education focused on podoconiosis prevention. Following the identification of a yellow fever outbreak in southern and southwestern Uganda in 2016, the Ministry of Health initiated a mass vaccination campaign. Also in 2016, a meningitis (serogroup W) outbreak in an institutionalized population triggered multifaceted control efforts. Throughout 2016 and 2017, epidemiologic evidence from nationwide cholera, malaria, and measles outbreaks informed the Ministry of Health's control strategies. Furthermore, assessments and evaluations of surveillance systems in six refugee settlements during 2016 and 2017 led to the establishment and strengthening of these systems. These interventions have successfully identified and controlled outbreaks at their origin, such as the yellow fever outbreaks in 2016, thereby preventing the spread of communicable diseases across communities and borders, enhancing global health security, and potentially reducing morbidity and mortality.
A year after completing the program, all ten graduates from the 2015 cohort secured employment, utilizing the field epidemiology and public health leadership expertise gained during their fellowship for outbreak responses and other public health initiatives. Four of these graduates are now engaged in critical Ministry of Health programs, while three have been retained by PHFP as field supervisors. Additionally, one graduate joined WHO's NSTOP program in South Sudan, and two others are serving as field epidemiologists for AFENET. All alumni contribute to the National Rapid Response Team for epidemic preparedness. Efforts are also underway with the Ministry of Health to integrate more PHFP graduates into permanent field epidemiologist roles across various priority programs.
Over the last 50 years, epidemics and pandemics like HIV, influenza, Ebola, and Zika have consistently shown that in our interconnected world, a communicable disease issue in one nation affects all. Effective containment and control of outbreaks depend on early detection and swift response. Consequently, building strong capabilities for detecting and controlling outbreaks must be a global priority.
The Uganda PHFP was created to tackle existing public health challenges. Over its first three years, the program has effectively addressed identified deficiencies, as shown by the high quality of its outputs, national and international accolades, and the implementation of its project recommendations. Beyond training fellows in practical skills and knowledge, the PHFP has delivered significant services to the Ministry of Health, fostering the development of Uganda's future public health leaders, particularly in communicable disease control and prevention. This initiative has enhanced Uganda's ability to respond promptly and effectively to disease outbreaks and other public health emergencies, containing them at their origin. Furthermore, the PHFP has built essential capacities for adhering to International Health Regulations (IHR) and promoted evidence-based public health practices in critical areas, thereby contributing to the Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA).
The PHFP has rapidly transformed the approach to public health emergency responses. Previously, comprehensive epidemiologic investigations were typically reserved for outbreaks involving high-priority pathogens like viral hemorrhagic fevers. Less severe outbreaks, such as cholera and measles, were managed with general control measures, including WASH and vaccination, often without thorough investigation. However, the PHFP has highlighted the necessity of investigating all outbreaks, emphasizing that each presents unique characteristics. The epidemiological data gathered from these investigations proves crucial for developing more precise and effective interventions, thereby preventing future outbreaks and shaping public health policies. As a result, the Ministry of Health now consistently requests PHFP investigations for all outbreaks, regardless of the pathogen's priority level.
The Uganda Public Health Fellowship Program (PHFP) stands out globally for its high productivity, particularly in surveillance and outbreak investigations. This success stems from several key factors. Fellows are strategically assigned to priority areas within the Ministry of Health, fostering a strong sense of program ownership by the ministry. Close collaboration between PHFP and the Emergency Operations Centre (EOC), along with fellows' integration into the National Rapid Response Team, further enhances effectiveness. A 2014 CDC evaluation of field epidemiology training programs noted that strong national ownership, characterized by extensive support and reliance on FETPs for crucial public health tasks, correlates with high productivity. Additionally, the PHFP's smaller cohort sizes facilitate greater interaction between fellows and their mentors, while the full-time nature of the fellowship allows fellows to fully dedicate themselves to their assignments, contributing significantly to the program's notable achievements.
The PHFP fellowship model has proven highly effective in meeting Uganda's public health demands, exhibiting greater productivity than traditional MPH degree programs. This is largely because PHFP fellows, already holding master's degrees upon entry, dedicate more time to practical projects and less to classroom instruction. Their focus is squarely on addressing real-world public health issues within the country. In contrast to MPH students, who typically complete 2 to 4 projects over two years, PHFP fellows average over 9 projects, highlighting the program's hands-on, impact-driven approach.
While the PHFP incurs higher costs compared to MPH degree programs due to its extensive field-based training, this investment yields significant returns. The program immediately benefits public health by facilitating the prompt detection, investigation, and control of outbreaks, enhancing surveillance systems, and supplying critical data for various public health initiatives. The substantial economic impact of past epidemics, such as the $3.6 billion spent on the West African Ebola outbreak response and an additional $2.2 billion in GDP losses for affected countries, underscores the value of such proactive measures. A fraction of these costs, if invested in robust systems for early outbreak management, could have prevented such widespread economic devastation. Future accreditation and growing recognition are expected to further boost the PHFP's appeal, attracting even more skilled applicants, mirroring the success of programs like the US CDC's EIS and ECDC's EPIET.
PHFP's long-term viability is bolstered by its graduates quickly moving into leadership roles. Four alumni have joined key Ministry of Health programs, three are working with AFENET and WHO, and three have been retained by PHFP itself. This integration provides superior training and learning for current fellows, as alumni within the program secretariat offer valuable expertise. Furthermore, graduates placed in Ministry of Health departments enhance mentorship for new fellows. The program's sustainability is also strengthened by the swift public health interventions resulting from investigation findings.
The PHFP, despite its achievements, encounters several obstacles. A primary concern is its insufficient recognition. To counter this, the program has collaborated with the Ministry of Health to establish a dedicated "field epidemiologist" job category within its departments, making field epidemiology training a prerequisite for specific roles. This initiative could serve as a blueprint for the Africa CDC to advocate for similar established positions in other national health ministries. Furthermore, the PHFP is actively pursuing official recognition as a fellowship under the Uganda College of Medicine, with advanced discussions underway involving key bodies like the Uganda Medical and Dental Practitioner's Council and the Uganda National Council for Higher Education. The program is also employing a diverse strategy to enhance its visibility among a broader range of internal and external stakeholders. Concurrently, the PHFP is preparing for accreditation from the Training Programs in Epidemiology and Public Health Interventions Network (TEPHINET).
A significant hurdle for PHFP is the implementation of public health actions based on its generated evidence, primarily due to insufficient resources for recommended interventions, particularly for outbreak prevention. Repeated cost analyses by PHFP fellows have consistently shown that preventing outbreaks is considerably more cost-effective than responding to them. This crucial data will be compiled and disseminated to government officials, international bodies, NGOs, and other collaborators, aiming to secure increased funding for outbreak prevention initiatives.
To ensure its long-term viability, the Public Health Fellowship Program (PHFP), currently supported by CDC funding, requires complete integration into Uganda's Ministry of Health. Acknowledging this necessity, the Ministry has positioned PHFP as a crucial element within the envisioned Uganda National Institute of Public Health (UNIPH), structuring it as one of UNIPH's directorates. Upon parliamentary establishment, UNIPH will serve as a comprehensive disease control framework for the nation, drawing financial support from diverse channels including government, philanthropic organizations, the private sector, and various grants and cooperative agreements. Within this structure, PHFP is slated to function as the capacity-building arm, responsible for cultivating a skilled workforce of field epidemiologists and other health professionals vital for addressing the country's public health demands. Furthermore, the PHFP alumni have established "Field Epidemiologists without Borders," an association committed to collaborating with UNIPH to bolster the program's sustainability.
In its initial three years, the PHFP has become a strong and effective advanced FETP. It has enhanced Uganda's public health workforce and bolstered global health security by improving emergency responses and providing data for Ministry of Health decisions.
