The EFSA Plant Health (PLH) Panel conducted a risk assessment for Ceratocystis platani in the EU, at the European Commission's request. The primary goal was to evaluate the efficacy of various risk reduction options (RROs) by examining the pathogen's spread mechanisms, which include natural and human-assisted methods like waterways, root anastomosis, contaminated tools, machinery, insects, and sawdust. Ceratocystis platani, a destructive pathogen affecting Platanus trees, is currently found in France, Greece, and Italy within the EU. The assessment involved three distinct scenarios: A0 representing the current situation, A1 depicting a scenario without any RROs, and A2 incorporating additional RROs. The risk of new introductions of C. platani into the EU via primary entry pathways such as plants for planting, wood, and machinery (including construction and pruning tools) was found to be relatively low. However, this risk was approximately 250 times greater in the A1 scenario compared to the A2 scenario, based on median numbers of established populations. The risk of the pathogen spreading from already affected EU regions was higher, with variations depending on the scenario. Machinery was identified as the most significant factor in long-distance spread. Implementing additional RROs in the A2 scenario, specifically targeting machinery—a pathway not currently regulated—would effectively decrease the likelihood of further spread and mitigate the impact of C. platani in unaffected EU regions. The report also suggested harmonizing and enhancing emergency measures in France, Greece, and Italy. This enhanced program should include surveillance, early detection of disease outbreaks, effective eradication strategies, and the planting of resistant Platanus clones in newly affected areas. Furthermore, surveillance efforts could be increased in EU Member States not yet known to be affected by the disease.

The EFSA Plant Health (PLH) Panel conducted a quantitative risk assessment for Ceratocystis platani, a damaging fungal pathogen of Platanus species, in the EU at the European Commission's request. This pathogen, originally from North America, entered France and Italy during World War II. Subsequently, in the early 2000s, it arrived in Greece, leading to significant harm to plane trees in both urban and rural settings.
The Panel understood the Terms of Reference as a directive to perform a comprehensive Pest Risk Assessment for the EU. This assessment aimed to evaluate the efficacy of various risk reduction options. These options were analyzed in relation to the spread mechanisms previously identified in the pest categorization, encompassing both natural and human-assisted methods like waterways, root anastomosis, contaminated tools, machinery, insects, and insect frass and sawdust.
To inform this risk assessment, a literature search updated findings since the 2014 pest categorisation, incorporating relevant new publications. Previously published information on C. platani from the 2014 categorisation was not duplicated.
Most quantitative estimates in this assessment relied on expert judgment due to incomplete or unavailable data. These expert-derived figures should be interpreted with caution, as different experts might offer varying estimates when evidence is scarce. However, an exception was the historical spread of the pathogen across Europe. For this, the Panel had access to data from France, Greece, and Italy at the NUTS3 regional level. This NUTS3 spatial unit was specifically selected for evaluating the pathogen's potential future spread within the risk assessment area.
The EFSA PLH Panel's quantitative risk assessment template was used. Appendix A details the assessment model through flow charts and formulas.
Three distinct scenarios formed the basis of the risk assessment. Scenario A0 represented the existing conditions, encompassing current EU legislation (Council Directive 2000/29/EC) regarding the pathogen and its host, alongside emergency measures implemented by affected EU Member States. Conversely, Scenario A1 depicted a worst-case situation, entirely devoid of risk reduction options (RROs). Finally, Scenario A2 incorporated the current situation but with the addition of further, highly effective RROs.
Current good agricultural practices are incorporated into all scenarios within the risk assessment area.
The Panel identified plants for planting, wood, and machinery as the primary pathways for Ceratocystis platani to enter the risk assessment area.
The introduction of Ceratocystis platani into the risk assessment (RA) area through primary entry pathways (plants for planting, wood, and machinery, including construction, terracing, logging, pruning, and cutting tools) is currently predicted to be relatively low. Under the A0 scenario, which reflects the current situation, fewer than one new established population is expected over a 10-year period (median value). However, if existing regulations were removed (A1 scenario), new introductions are projected to increase approximately six-fold compared to the A0 scenario. In a worst-case scenario (99th percentile) under A1, the number of new established populations could exceed 200 within 10 years. Conversely, implementing additional risk reduction options (A2 scenario) would significantly decrease new introductions, making them about 40 times lower than under the A0 scenario.
Scenario A2 incorporates additional risk reduction options (RROs) to mitigate the entry and establishment of *C. platani*. To reduce entry risk, a certification scheme is proposed for *Platanus* plants intended for planting. Existing wood regulations would be expanded to include all affected Third Countries and firewood. A new regulatory framework for machinery is also recommended, requiring it to be sourced from pathogen-free areas or to be cleaned, disinfected, and free of soil and plant debris when entering *Platanus*-growing regions, with a verifying certificate. For establishment risk, enhanced eradication programs and surveillance are included, aiming to reduce pathogen transfer to suitable hosts and thus decrease the number of potential founder populations.
Scenario A0 shows a relatively narrow range for the estimated number of established populations, indicating limited uncertainty compared to scenarios A1 and A2. While numerous factors affect the assessment of entry and establishment, their impact on overall uncertainty differs across scenarios. No single factor predominantly contributes to this uncertainty, making it challenging to identify specific areas for further data collection or expert knowledge elicitation. Nevertheless, future risk assessments could be enhanced by obtaining more data on the trade volumes of Platanus plants for planting and wood, as well as the movement of machinery from non-EU countries into the EU.
An estimated 84 NUTS3 regions are currently impacted by C. platani (France: 18, Greece: 12, Italy: 54). Over a decade, under the A0 scenario, this number is projected to rise to 99 regions, an increase of 15. Without risk reduction options (A1 scenario), the spread is expected to be more severe, reaching 111 affected regions (+27). In the worst-case A1 scenario (99th percentile), an additional 73 NUTS3 regions could become infested. However, implementing additional risk reduction options (A2 scenario) would significantly curb this spread, limiting the total to 88 affected regions, an increase of only 4. The primary mechanisms driving this long-distance spread, in order of importance, are machinery, wood, plants for planting, and natural dispersal via wind-borne sawdust.
The spread assessment excludes newly introduced C. platani populations. This is because the average probability of a new established population from new entries was determined to be 0.3 out of 1,240 EU NUTS3 regions containing Platanus plants, or 0.0002. This contribution to spread is considered minor.
Long-distance spread of Ceratocystis platani is primarily driven by machinery. Currently, this mechanism is unregulated. Implementing additional Risk Reduction Options (RROs) in the A2 scenario, specifically targeting machinery, would significantly lower the risk of C. platani spreading to unaffected EU NUTS3 regions. These RROs would mandate that machinery either originates from a pest-free area or, if moved from an affected region, is cleaned, disinfected, and free of soil and plant debris before entering areas with Platanus trees. Furthermore, such machinery would require a certificate verifying its cleaning and disinfection.
Surveillance, optimized eradication, and planting resistant Platanus clones are key risk reduction options for limiting spread. Eradication is highly effective when early detection is enabled by surveillance and optimized procedures are implemented.
Scenario A0 shows a relatively narrow range for newly affected NUTS3 regions, indicating less uncertainty in the spread assessment compared to scenarios A1 and A2. Consequently, the overall uncertainty for spread is lower in scenario A0.
The factors contributing to overall uncertainty in pathogen spread vary by mechanism and scenario. In the current situation (A0), the pathogen's annual growth rate is the primary source of uncertainty for all long-distance spread mechanisms. However, for scenarios without measures (A1) and with additional risk reduction options (A2), the effectiveness of these options in preventing spread becomes the most significant uncertainty factor, except for wood and natural spread in the A1 scenario.
The impact assessment considers multiple factors. It examines the initial percentage of C. platani-affected host plants in 2016 within currently impacted areas. The assessment also accounts for the pathogen's population growth rate, specifically the number of affected plants after a 10-year period in affected regions, primarily due to short-distance spread, across various scenarios. Furthermore, it includes the number of spatial regions that become occupied over the same 10-year period due to long-distance spread. A key assumption is that all affected host trees die relatively quickly.
Initially, in 2016, the estimated abundance of affected plants ranged from 0.01% to 20%, with a median of 1%. After ten years, the median proportion of dead host plants is projected to be 1.2% under scenario A2 and 2.1% under scenario A1. Scenario A0's median of 1.4% is similar to A2's, suggesting the 10-year timeframe is too brief for A2's additional risk reduction options to show full effectiveness.
At the EU level, the overall impact on dead host plants is projected to be significantly lower in scenario A2. It is estimated to be approximately four times less than in scenario A0 and about twelve times less than in scenario A1.
At the local level, the impact on ecosystem services, including provisioning (wood), regulating and supporting (e.g., pollution reduction, water regulation, shade, soil erosion prevention), and cultural services, varies significantly across scenarios. Compared to the current situation (A0), the A1 scenario (without risk reduction options) is projected to result in approximately 1.5 times greater reduction in these services. Conversely, implementing additional risk reduction options (A2) is expected to decrease this reduction by about two times compared to A0. This assessment does not quantify the overall impact on ecosystem services across the entire EU.
The impact assessment, specifically concerning dead plants, is highly uncertain. This is primarily because estimating the pathogen's abundance in affected habitats is difficult. Across all scenarios, the projected pathogen abundance in 2016 varied significantly, from less than 0.01% to approximately 20% of plants affected, representing a 2000-fold difference between the lowest and highest estimates.
The Panel concludes that current EU measures against *C. platani* entry, specifically Council Directive 2000/29/EC, need enhancement. This involves adding requirements for plants for planting, wood (including currently unregulated firewood), and machinery pathways. It's crucial to acknowledge that some affected Third Countries are not listed in the Directive, and more may become affected in the future. Removing existing regulations would significantly boost potential *C. platani* founder populations and, consequently, increase overall impacts. Regarding internal EU spread, while current measures under Council Directive 2000/29/EC can be maintained, they are insufficient to halt the disease's expansion. Implementing additional requirements, as outlined in scenario A2, could decrease the number of newly affected NUTS3 regions by approximately one-quarter over the next decade. These additional measures should include a certification scheme for plants for planting from affected areas, comprehensive regulations for all wood types from affected areas, and mandatory cleaning and disinfection of machinery moving from affected zones. Furthermore, the emergency measures currently employed by *C. platani*-affected EU Member States (France, Greece, and Italy) are inconsistent and could benefit from harmonization and improvement. An enhanced program should incorporate robust surveillance, early detection of disease foci, effective eradication strategies, and the use of resistant *Platanus* clones in new plantations within affected areas. Surveillance efforts should also expand to EU Member States not yet reporting the pathogen.

Ceratocystis platani, a pathogen, produces both asexual and sexual spores. Asexual spores include cylindrical endoconidia, doliform endoconidia, and aleurioconidia (chlamydospores). Sexual spores, called ascospores, are released in a sticky mass from perithecia. All these spore types are capable of causing infections in Platanus trees. Infection occurs when spores contact wounds or injuries on the host, which can be caused by abiotic or biotic factors. Upon contact, spores germinate, and the resulting mycelium colonizes exposed tissues. This mycelium then penetrates and spreads within the xylem tissues of the sapwood, growing both lengthwise and crosswise. Within 2 to 8 days of infection, all spore types form an ash-colored, powdery layer, primarily on pruning cuts. Aleurioconidia, in particular, can also be abundantly produced inside the host's xylem vessels within 10 to 20 days post-infection.
C. platani thrives optimally at 25°C, with no growth occurring below 10°C or above 35°C. While it can survive at 35°C, its viability is lost at temperatures exceeding 37.5°C (Pilotti et al., 2016) or after 48 hours between 35-40°C (Mutto Accordi, 1989). Within wood, the fungus endured 40°C for 24 hours but became inviable after 48 hours at this temperature (P. Tsopelas, unpublished). The pathogen exhibits remarkable cold tolerance, surviving for multiple years at -17°C (Pilotti et al., 2016). In soil, C. platani can persist for over three months during winter, though its survival diminishes rapidly in spring and summer. Additionally, it can survive for at least six months in wood dust on woodpiles and for 75 days in wood dust on soil (Mutto Accordi, 1989).

Waterways were another identified route.

In contrast, the soil and growing media pathway is deemed minor. Their significance is limited to specific situations, such as waterways connecting affected European Third Countries to neighboring EU Member States that host susceptible plants.
Ceratocystis platani exclusively infects Platanus species. Infected Platanus plants, grown in affected regions, can harbor the pathogen as mycelium or spores. Evidence suggests the pathogen's spread from Italy or France to Greece occurred via infected Platanus plants. Genotyping studies confirmed the Greek genotype matched those found in Italy, France, and Switzerland, differing from the US genotype. A single interception in Europhyt in February 2015 involved infected P. orientalis plants for planting, imported from Greece to Cyprus. Subsequent inspections of the Greek nursery revealed five infected plants out of 152. The pathogen can also be transported through wood, including wood packaging material.
Ceratocystis platani can persist in deadwood for extended durations, making all wood products from infected host plants potential carriers of the pathogen. This includes wood particles, sawdust, shavings, wood waste, and chips. The pathogen can enter and spread throughout the risk assessment area via various types of infected wood materials, such as saw logs, timber, lumber, wood chips, dunnage, pallets, packaging material, and firewood, originating from affected regions. Historically, C. platani is believed to have first arrived in Europe (Italy) from the USA during World War II, transported in wood used for packaging. Eurostat currently lacks specific data regarding the trade of Platanus wood. Additionally, machinery, including construction, excavation, terracing, and logging equipment, as well as pruning and cutting tools, also serve as pathways for the pathogen.
Machinery, including construction, excavation, terracing, or logging equipment, is a significant pathway for Ceratocystis platani to enter the risk assessment (RA) area. This occurs when contaminated soil, sawdust, or other woody debris from previously affected regions adhere to the machinery. Such equipment also facilitates the long-distance spread of the pathogen within the RA area. For instance, C. platani is believed to have moved from southern Greece's Peloponnese to northwestern Greece's Epiros via road construction machinery. Similarly, its spread from France to Spain has been attributed to machinery that had operated in French affected areas. The pathogen's presence in southeastern Albania led to speculation that cross-border construction and terracing machinery facilitated its movement between Greece and Albania.
Machinery used in construction, excavation, terracing, and logging significantly contributes to new infections of plane trees in both urban and rural settings. These machines wound the crowns, trunks, and roots of susceptible trees, facilitating pathogen transfer. Plane trees in public spaces like streets, parks, and parking lots are especially vulnerable. Wounds to their roots and trunks often occur during various activities, including pavement construction, utility line installation (power, gas, water), mechanical weed removal, and lawn cutting. Vehicles in open-air parking areas also inflict damage. This mechanical wounding, therefore, plays a major role in spreading the pathogen beyond its initial introduction.
Pruning and cutting tools, such as scissors, secateurs, axes, and saws, serve as a significant pathway for the entry and spread of C. platani within the risk assessment area. These tools can transfer the pathogen through spores or infected sawdust. Spores of C. platani can remain viable on pruning tools for approximately one month, while infected sawdust can harbor viable spores for at least five years. Research indicates that pruning saws effectively inoculated plane trees up to 12 days post-contamination, the longest period tested. Furthermore, studies over four years revealed that about 40% of wounds created during the growing season by saws previously used on diseased trees resulted in infection.
Soil and growing media, whether as a commodity, associated with living plants, or as a contaminant, is a minor pathway.
*C. platani* can persist in soil for over three months in winter, though its viability rapidly declines in spring and summer, according to Mutto Accordi (1989). While contaminated soil and growing media could theoretically introduce and spread the pathogen within the risk assessment area, no such instances have been reported. It is highly improbable for fungal propagules in soil to initiate infections by contacting wounded *Platanus* roots.

The Panel identified five major mechanisms for Ceratocystis platani spread within the EU: host plants for planting, wood, machinery (including pruning/cutting tools), root anastomosis, and natural spread via wind. Root anastomosis is one of these key pathways.
Root anastomosis serves as a mechanism for the short-distance spread of the pathogen within the risk assessment area. This occurs when *Platanus* trees, often found closely together in riparian zones or urban pavements, form interconnected root systems. Once the pathogen establishes in one tree, it can then transfer to adjacent trees via these root connections. This risk is amplified if trees are planted in close proximity or if infected, dead, or dying trees remain in place for extended periods. Experimental evidence by Mutto Accordi (1986) has demonstrated this spread, and numerous studies, including those by Panconesi (1999), Tsopelas et al. (2006), Tsopelas and Soulioti (2013), and Vigouroux (2013), have reported its occurrence. For instance, observations of *P. orientalis* root systems in Messinia Prefecture, Greece, indicated pathogen transmission between neighboring trees through root anastomosis (Ocasio-Morales et al., 2007). The danger of this spread method is particularly high for street *Platanus* trees planted in rows or in natural stands along waterways where dense growth is common (Panconesi et al., 2003). Similar findings were reported by Perry and McCain (1988) regarding disease dissemination in street *Platanus* plantings in Modesto, California, USA.
C. platani inoculum, including ascospores, conidia, and infected sawdust, can be dispersed by weather events like wind and wind-driven rain, though this hasn't been studied extensively. Airborne inoculum has been detected up to 200 meters from infected plane trees. Wind can also carry infected sawdust over long distances. Watercourses further contribute to spread by transporting spores, infected sawdust, wood pieces, and insect frass.
Waterways, including rivers, streams, and channels, facilitate both short and long-distance spread of the pathogen within the risk assessment area. This is a significant mechanism because *P. orientalis* naturally grows along riverbanks, and *P. × acerifolia* is commonly planted in rows along waterways in France, Switzerland, and Italy.
Water courses can transport C. platani spores, infected sawdust, insect frass, and pieces of wood or branches from diseased plane trees downstream, leading to new infection sites. Notably, chlamydospores can persist in river water and infect roots that have been wounded by rolling debris like pebbles or logs. Additionally, stream water can carry infected sawdust or insect frass, which can then contact and infect damaged roots. Biological agents such as insects, birds, and other animals are also capable of mechanically transferring the fungus.
The primary sources of *C. platani* inoculum are spores found in bark cracks, at the base of trees, and within debris generated by insects like nitidulids and wood-dwelling scolytids (Panconesi et al., 2003). This inoculum can be dispersed over significant distances by various biological agents, including insects, small rodents, and birds (Panconesi et al., 2003). These agents can play an active role by wounding trees and directly transporting the inoculum, or a passive role by merely causing wounds and allowing incidental infection. A fruity odor emitted by *C. platani*-infected plane wood attracts insects, a phenomenon also observed with other *Ceratocystis* species and ophiostomatoid fungi on woody hosts (Panconesi et al., 2003). This attraction is considered an adaptation for fungal dispersal by insects. The morphology of the pathogen's perithecia, featuring a globose base and a long neck, positions the spore mass above the substrate, further aiding insect-mediated dispersal (Panconesi, 1999). Insects are also capable of carrying other *C. platani* spore types, such as chlamydospores and endoconidia. It is a recognized fact that bark and ambrosia beetles are drawn to diseased plants and can transport fungal pathogen spores, leading to new infections in healthy plants (Ploetz et al., 2013).
Research indicates that various insects can transmit *C. platani*. In the USA, Crone (1962) identified several Nitidulidae species, including *Colydium lineola*, *Laemophloeus biguttatus*, *Colopterus semitectus*, *Colopterus unicolor*, *Colopterus niger*, and *Carpophilus lugubris*, as vectors. More recently, Soulioti et al. (2015) showed that the ambrosia beetle *Platypus cylindrus* transmits the pathogen to healthy trees. While *P. cylindrus* is prevalent on long-infected *P. orientalis* in Greece and Albania and on infected *P. acerifolia* in Italy, it typically avoids healthy plane trees. Instead, it is drawn to trees with fresh wounds from human activity, animals, or natural causes.
Insects feeding on infected plane trees can harbor C. platani, as fungal spores survive passage through their gut (Crone, 1962). Crone (1962) showed that frass from Carpophilus lugubris could infect healthy P. acerifolia trees. Similarly, Ocasio-Morales et al. (2007) isolated the fungus from P. cylindrus frass. This frass, containing live C. platani propagules, can be dispersed by wind, river water, or rain, potentially leading to new infections (Ocasio-Morales et al., 2007; Soulioti et al., 2015). While Corythucha ciliata, or the 'tiger of plane tree,' is common on plane tree leaves in southern and central Europe, its potential role in C. platani spore dispersal remains undocumented and unproven (Panconesi, 1999; Tsopelas et al., 2006).
While some researchers (Walter, 1946; Panconesi, 1999) proposed that birds, squirrels, and other rodents might transmit C. platani, this remains unproven. Rodents could wound Platanus roots near rivers, potentially carrying fungal propagules and causing infections. However, there is no evidence supporting this transmission route, and these animals are not considered significant in the pathogen's spread (Panconesi, 1981).
Rain and surface water can spread C. platani inoculum, including ascospores and conidia, though this mechanism is not thoroughly researched (CABI, 2014). New infections can also arise from infected sawdust, wood fragments, and insect frass transported by rain and surface water (Panconesi, 1999). Given that P. orientalis is a riparian species, the pathogen's ability to spread via watercourses facilitates rapid downstream dispersal (Woodhall, 2013).
Wound dressing was historically a highly effective method for spreading *C. platani*, as documented by Walter in 1946. Arborists' brushes, when applying wound dressing, would pick up contaminated sawdust and wood fragments from infected trees. These would then be transferred to healthy trees during pruning, infecting new wounds. Research has shown that the pathogen can persist in wound dressings for extended durations. However, this practice is now seldom, if ever, employed on plane trees.

To quantify uncertainty in estimating entry, establishment, spread, and impact, a probability distribution is employed. This distribution reflects experts' best estimates, incorporating both available data and their judgment. It is defined by a median value and four additional percentiles. The median signifies the point where the likelihood of overestimating or underestimating the true value is considered equal. Model calculations are performed through stochastic simulation, involving 20,000 repetitions. During each repetition, values are randomly selected from the specified distribution for each parameter, ultimately generating a probability distribution of future outcomes for the entry, establishment, spread, and impact processes over a defined period.
The model equation accounts for the uncertainty of each component, revealing its influence on the final result. This uncertainty is broken down to show the proportional contribution of each input to the overall uncertainty, with all contributions summing to one.
Section 3 presents the results of scenario calculations. These distributions describe the potential range of outcomes within the opinion's timeframe for each scenario. Uncertain components are characterized by their varying values and ranges.
The median, or 'best estimate' in this opinion, represents a central value where the likelihood of overestimating or underestimating the actual value is equal.
The interquartile range, also termed the 'uncertainty interval' in this document, indicates the precision of an estimate. This range is defined by the 25th and 75th percentiles of a distribution. It signifies an interval around the median where the actual value has an equal probability of falling inside or outside. A broader interquartile range implies higher uncertainty in the estimate.
In experimental designs, the precision of a measured parameter's estimate is typically conveyed using the mean (m) and standard error (± s), forming an interval of likely values as m ± s. This approach relies on replicated measurements. However, when dealing with uncertainty, assuming replicated judgments is inappropriate. Consequently, this assessment employs median and interquartile ranges, which, while different from the mean and m ± s interval, offer a comparable interpretation of judgment precision.
A credibility range is also provided, defined as the span between the 1st and 99th percentiles of the distribution. This range indicates that the true value is highly improbable to fall outside these boundaries, either above or below.
Additional intervals with varying coverage levels are derivable from the probability distribution, but this opinion does not include them as standard.
The precision of an estimation is not indicated by the number of significant figures used to present distribution characteristics. For instance, a variable with a median of 13 might have an interquartile range of 3–38, suggesting the true value is less than a few tens. This assessment aimed to convey all results through both numerical model outcomes and verbal interpretations.
Comparing variables across different scenarios is possible by examining their median values. For instance, if a variable has a median of 13 in scenario 1 and 6 in scenario 2, it indicates that the central value of the variable in scenario 2 is approximately half that of scenario 1. This comparative approach, using median values, also extends to other distributional characteristics like quartiles and percentiles.

Ceratocystis platani is particularly devastating to roadside plane trees that undergo frequent, severe pruning. In the United States, major cities like Boston, Chicago, Philadelphia, and Baltimore experienced significant losses, with 50-70% of their roadside plane trees dying over approximately two decades. In Europe, the impact has also been severe. Between 1960 and 1973, Marseille lost over 3,500 of its 11,000 century-old plane trees, with a similar number found infected. Italy saw 900 century-old plane trees along an avenue in Caserta cut down due to the disease. Forte dei Marmi reported that over 60% of its urban garden and roadside trees died between 1971 and 1981. In Greece, the pathogen has caused widespread mortality in natural stands of *P. orientalis*, destroying thousands of trees along major rivers in the Peloponnese and Epirus, threatening to eliminate the species from many areas.

Ceratocystis platani, a highly infectious pathogen, currently has a limited presence in only three EU member states (Italy, France, and Greece), despite its potential for significant impact.
The European Commission requested a comprehensive pest risk assessment to develop risk reduction options, focusing on spread mechanisms, after the EFSA PLH Panel's 2014 pest categorisation proposed listing the pathogen as a Union Quarantine pest.
The Panel conducted a Pest Risk Assessment (PRA) using three distinct scenarios. Scenario A0 represented the existing situation, incorporating current EU legislation (Council Directive 2000/29/EC) regarding the pathogen and its host, alongside emergency measures implemented by affected EU Member States. Scenario A1 depicted a worst-case outcome, assuming no Risk Reduction Options (RROs) were in place. Finally, Scenario A2 involved the current conditions supplemented by the most effective additional RROs. These scenarios formed the basis for the Panel's conclusions from the PRA.
The risk of C. platani entering the EU is relatively low under current conditions (A0 scenario), with fewer than one new established population anticipated over a decade. However, this risk is significantly amplified if existing regulations are removed (A1 scenario), leading to approximately six times more new introductions compared to the A0 scenario. In a worst-case A1 scenario (99th percentile), the number of new established populations could reach 230 within 10 years. Conversely, implementing additional risk reduction options (RROs) in the A2 scenario would dramatically decrease new introductions, making them about 40 times lower than in the A0 scenario. To mitigate entry risk in the A2 scenario, several RROs are proposed. These include establishing a certification scheme for Platanus plants intended for planting. Furthermore, existing wood regulations should be expanded to cover all affected Third Countries and include firewood. A new regulatory framework for machinery is also recommended. This framework would require machinery to originate from pest-free areas or, if from affected areas, to be thoroughly cleaned, disinfected, and free of soil and plant debris before being used where Platanus trees grow. Such machinery would also need to be accompanied by a certificate verifying its cleaning and disinfection. For establishment risk, additional RROs in the A2 scenario involve enhanced surveillance and eradication programs. These measures aim to reduce the transfer of the pathogen to suitable hosts, thereby decreasing the number of potential founder populations. Uncertainty in the assessment of established populations is relatively low for the A0 scenario but increases for scenarios A1 and A2. While numerous factors influence entry and establishment, no single factor predominantly contributes to the overall uncertainty, making it challenging to pinpoint specific areas for further data collection or expert elicitation. However, future risk assessments would benefit from more data on the trade volumes of Platanus plants for planting and wood, as well as the number of machinery units entering the EU from Third Countries. Regarding the spread of C. platani, primarily through machinery, wood, plants for planting, and natural wind-borne sawdust, a considerable increase in affected NUTS3 regions is projected at the long-distance level. Currently, 84 NUTS3 regions are affected (18 in France, 12 in Greece, 54 in Italy). Under the A0 scenario, this number is expected to rise to 99 (+15 median value) over 10 years. The A1 worst-case scenario predicts an increase to 111 (+27), with a worst-case 99th percentile showing an additional 73 NUTS3 regions becoming infested. Implementing additional RROs in the A2 scenario would significantly curb this spread, resulting in only 88 affected NUTS3 regions (+4). The assessment of spread does not account for newly introduced C. platani populations, as their average probability of establishing new populations (0.0002 out of 1,240 NUTS3 regions with Platanus plants) is considered negligible for overall spread. Machinery is identified as the most critical mechanism for long-distance spread. Consequently, focusing A2 scenario RROs on this currently unregulated pathway would be highly effective in preventing further spread to unaffected EU NUTS3 regions. Proposed RROs for machinery include requirements that it either originates from a pest-free area or, if moved from an affected region, is cleaned, disinfected, and free of soil and plant debris when entering areas with Platanus trees. A certificate confirming cleaning and disinfection would also be mandatory. Other effective RROs for reducing spread include surveillance, optimized eradication programs, and planting resistant Platanus clones in new plantations within affected areas. Eradication is particularly effective when early detection is achieved through surveillance and optimized procedures are applied. At the per-area unit level, the reduction in provisioning, regulating and supporting, and cultural ecosystem services is projected to be about 1.5 times higher in the A1 scenario compared to A0, and about two times lower in the A2 scenario compared to A0. In conclusion, current EU measures (Council Directive 2000/29/EC) for preventing C. platani entry could be enhanced by introducing additional requirements for plants for planting, wood (including currently unregulated firewood), and machinery (also currently unregulated). It is also crucial to account for affected Third Countries not currently listed in the Directive and the potential for new countries to become affected. Removing current regulations would substantially increase potential C. platani founder populations and, consequently, the magnitude of impacts. Similarly, existing measures to prevent C. platani spread within the EU should be maintained, but they are insufficient to halt disease progression into new areas. Implementing additional requirements, as outlined in the A2 scenario, could reduce the number of newly affected NUTS3 regions by a factor of four over the next decade. These requirements include a certification scheme for plants for planting from affected areas, measures for all types of wood from affected areas, and cleaning and disinfection of machinery moving from affected areas. Emergency measures currently applied by C. platani-affected EU Member States (France, Greece, Italy) should be harmonized and improved. An enhanced program should encompass surveillance, early detection of disease foci, effective eradication measures, and the planting of resistant Platanus clones in new plantations within affected areas. Surveillance should also be expanded to EU Member States not yet reporting the pathogen.

To quantify the risk of C. platani, the modeling approach integrates individual distributions of relevant quantities and state variables, such as population abundance, trade volume, and multiplication factors. These distributions provide the best estimates for state variables or the impact of processes altering them, like population growth within a pathway unit. This characterization occurs at each substep within the four primary assessment stages: entry, establishment, spread, and impact. The effectiveness of risk reduction options (RROs) is also represented by distributions. By combining these individual distributions, a final distribution is generated, enabling comparisons across different pathways and scenarios for all four core risk assessment components.
This model is a simplified, deterministic representation of the assessment. The full version, incorporating random variables from expert estimations or calculations, is detailed in the C. platani @Risk file (Annex A).
The entry model aims to quantify the total number of new potential founder populations (N1) of the pest entering the EU from Third Countries. This estimation considers all pathways collectively and compares various scenarios that incorporate different combinations of risk reduction options (RROs) across specified temporal and spatial scales.
For each scenario (i) and pathway (j), the number of potential founder populations is determined by tracking population abundance changes across a network of substeps. These changes are influenced by natural processes, such as population growth, and the application of Risk Reduction Options (RROs). Multiplication factors quantify these shifts in abundance.
Pest population abundance (P1ij) is measured at substep E1 (Entry) as the pest leaves the production site in the exporting country, specific to scenario (i) and pathway (j).
P2ij represents the pest's population abundance at the exporting country's border (substep E2 of Entry), specific to scenario 'i' and pathway 'j'.
For scenario 'i' and pathway 'j', P3ij represents the pest's population abundance upon reaching the EU point of entry, specifically at substep E3 of the Entry process.
For scenario 'i' and pathway 'j' in entry substep E4, P4ij represents the pest's population abundance after departing the EU point of entry.
For each scenario and pathway, N0ij represents the number of units that could transport the pest from its origin to the risk assessment area.
N1ij represents the total new potential founder populations of the pest entering the EU from Third Countries. This is calculated for a specific scenario (i), pathway (j), and across defined temporal and spatial scales.
For scenario *i* and pathway *j*, *m1ij* represents the multiplication factor altering pest abundance before leaving the production site. *m2ij* is the unit conversion coefficient for the same scenario and pathway.
The multiplication factor m3ij quantifies the change in pest abundance. This change occurs between substep E1 (after production) and substep E2 (before export country border crossing) for a given scenario (i) and pathway (j).
The multiplication factor m4ij quantifies the change in pest abundance. This change occurs between substep E2, after leaving the export country's border, and substep E3, before reaching the EU entry point, for a given scenario (i) and pathway (j).
The multiplication factor m5ij represents the change in pest abundance. This occurs between the pest's arrival at the EU point of entry (substep E3) and its departure from that point (substep E4) for a given scenario (i) and pathway (j).
The aggregation/disaggregation coefficient, denoted as m6ij, converts pathway units into transfer units for a given scenario and pathway.
The multiplication factor m7ij represents the change in pest abundance from leaving the point of entry (E4) to transferring to the host (E5) across scenarios and pathways. The total potential founder populations (N1i) for each scenario are calculated by summing the product of P4ij, N0ij, m6ij, and m7ij across all pathways. P2ij, P3ij, and P4ij are intermediate population abundance calculations.
The number of established populations (N2i) for a given scenario (i) is determined by multiplying the number of potential founder populations (N1i) from the entry step by the probability of establishment (m8i). This probability (m8i) can be further broken down into the various factors that influence establishment.
The probability of establishment in scenario 'i' is influenced by factor 'k', denoted as w Tki. This factor's contribution is modified by T k, a multiplication factor representing the effectiveness of combined Risk Reduction Options (RROs). Assuming independence of establishment factors, the modified probability of establishment, m 8 i ′, is determined by m8i∑kwTki×Tki. Subsequently, the number of established populations, N 2i, for scenario 'i' is calculated by multiplying the number of potential founder populations, N1i, by this modified probability.
When analyzing the historical spread of the pest, various interpolation models were evaluated. For the NUTS3 spatial resolution, a linear model was found to best describe the increase in occupied spatial units within the assessment area. This choice was further supported by the estimated spread rate and the 10-year assessment timeframe, allowing for a linear projection of future trends in occupied units. However, for longer time horizons, a non-linear model, such as a logistic model, would be more appropriate. This is because a non-linear approach can better capture the evolving pattern of pest dispersal, which intensifies as the occupied area grows and the number of available uninfested spatial units decreases.
The total number of occupied spatial units (N5i) at the end of the assessment period (T) for each scenario (i) is determined by adding the initial number of occupied units (N3i) to the product of the time horizon and the annual spread rate (m9i).
The initial number of spatial units affected by the pest (N3) reflects its distribution at the NUTS3 level in EU Member States in 2016. The assessment's timeframe (T) is set at 10 years.
The multiplication factor, m9i, determines the number of spatial units affected by the pest after 10 years, varying across different scenarios.
The annual rate of newly occupied spatial units, represented by the multiplication factor m9i, varies by scenario. This variation depends on the effectiveness of Risk Reduction Options (RROs) for each spread mechanism, ordered by importance: machinery, wood, host plants for planting, and natural means like wind-borne inoculum.
The maximum number of NUTS3 spatial units in the risk assessment area, representing potential pest establishment, was estimated. This figure, while not used in the linear model, helps define the pest's potential establishment area for relevant crops and habitats.

The impact assessment for *C. platani* focuses solely on environmental effects, as *Platanus* trees are seldom grown for wood in the risk assessment area. This environmental impact is determined by evaluating alterations in ecosystem service provision (including provisioning, regulating, and supporting services) within areas affected by the pest across various scenarios.
An epidemiological model is used to estimate the impact on ecosystem services. This model determines the disease's prevalence, defined as the proportion of affected trees within NUTS3 regions, at the end of the assessment period. The prevalence values range from 0 to 1. The initial condition for this model is the estimated current prevalence of the pest (P5) in relevant habitats within pathogen-occupied spatial units in 2016. The final prevalence at the end of the time horizon is then calculated using a straightforward exponential model: P5i** = P5 * exp(P5i*T).
P5i** represents the projected pest prevalence in affected areas at the end of the assessment period. This contrasts with P5, which denotes the pest's current prevalence in 2016.
The estimated annual growth rate of pest prevalence (P5i*) within affected habitats and spatial units, combined with the defined time horizon (T), determines the disease's progression under each scenario.
An exponential model was chosen for disease epidemics, rather than a logistic model with a maximum P5i** of 1. To determine the impact on ecosystem services, specific multiplication factors were estimated.
For scenario *i*, the multiplication factor m13i alters the provisioning ecosystem services based on pest abundance within occupied spatial units.

The fungus C. platani can survive over 24 hours at 40°C in wood, but 45°C for the same duration proved lethal to it.
ISPM 15 outlines specific heat treatment protocols for wood packaging material to prevent C. platani spread. Conventional heat treatment (HT) requires a minimum core temperature of 56°C maintained for 30 continuous minutes throughout the wood. For dielectric heating (DH), such as microwave treatment, wood packaging material under 20 cm² (smallest dimension) must reach a minimum of 60°C for one continuous minute throughout its entire profile. These measures are crucial for preventing the introduction and spread of the pathogen.
To prevent C. platani introduction and spread in the RA area, ISPM 15 mandates reaching a specific temperature within 30 minutes of treatment. This measure is considered highly effective.
The effectiveness of methyl bromide treatment (MB), part of ISPM 15, lacks experimental validation.
Machinery for construction, terracing, and logging, along with pruning and cutting tools, are key in pathogen dispersal. To minimize disease spread and reduce inoculum, all equipment, including plastic sheets, used during felling or pruning in affected areas must be disinfected before being moved or used on nearby trees.
Machinery near infected trees must be cleaned with a water jet to remove soil and plant debris, then disinfected before removal from the site. French practices involve disinfectants with quaternary ammonium compounds, ortho-phenyl-phenol, and 8-hydroxyquinoline sulfate. Minimizing tree wounding during pruning is crucial, and fresh wounds should be treated with fungicide to prevent fungal growth.
Machinery used in affected zones, particularly large equipment, needs a certificate confirming cleaning and disinfection. This recommendation extends to pruning and cutting tools, which should undergo similar treatment before being used near Platanus trees, regardless of prior use in an affected area.
Infected host trees and nearby trees should be promptly removed and destroyed in affected areas, as this is the most crucial sanitation measure. Pruning infected parts merely postpones tree death.
For effective eradication of the pathogen, early detection of new disease foci is crucial, preventing establishment and spread. Annual surveys in both affected and unaffected EU Member States are necessary to identify disease occurrence at an early stage. Eradication programs are most effective when implemented in the initial stages of the disease. However, once the disease reaches epidemic proportions, particularly in natural stands, eradication measures become ineffective.
Eradication efforts for infected trees necessitate specific precautions due to the high infectivity of contaminated sawdust, which can spread via wind or water, leading to new infections. To mitigate this risk, all sawdust and residues must be collected during felling. Operations in outbreak areas require suspending vehicle traffic and using large plastic sheets beneath infected trees to capture sawdust. Trees should be felled in a single piece or with minimal cuts. Afterward, the wood, debris, and sawdust must be destroyed by fire or buried in sanitary landfills. The felling site, including any remaining debris, should then be treated with a fungicide.
To minimize pathogen spread, particularly via root anastomosis, all infected trees and healthy neighboring trees should be eliminated using herbicide injections. This practice creates a buffer zone around infected areas, as the fungus cannot spread into roots of herbicide-killed trees. For instance, in France, glyphosate is used to kill infected trees and those within a 20–30 meter radius. Following eradication, replanting efforts should prioritize non-host plants or Platanus-resistant clones.
In affected areas, resistant *Platanus* clones are recommended. However, relying on a single clone carries risks due to limited genetic diversity, potentially allowing the pathogen to overcome resistance or new pathogens to emerge. Therefore, further research is necessary to develop additional resistant trees, despite the current clone exhibiting horizontal resistance controlled by multiple genes.
Planting resistant Platanus clones in natural environments is not recommended. This is due to the risk of hybridization with native species, which could lead to the resistant clone acquiring beneficial genes from P. orientalis, potentially making it invasive.
The impact of planting resistant Platanus clones in affected areas is unlikely to be measurable within a 10-year timeframe. This is because the rate of new plantations using these clones is too low relative to the vast number of existing plane trees across Europe.
