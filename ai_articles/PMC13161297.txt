This study investigated the impact of ozone therapy, diode laser irradiation, and the presence of teeth crowding or spacing on pain perception in orthodontic patients. A total of 76 participants, comprising 55 women and 21 men with an average age of 35.1 (±6.4) years, met the inclusion criteria and took part in the research. Following the immediate placement of fixed orthodontic appliances, patients received a single pain relief session. This treatment involved either a 635-nm diode laser (SmartM, Lasotronix, Warsaw, Poland) or ozone therapy (OzoneDTA, Apoza, New Taipei City, Taiwan). The handpieces for both treatments were positioned in the area of each tooth apex and interdental papillae, extending from the maxillary right first molar to the maxillary left first molar. The participants were allocated into three distinct groups. The control group (G1) consisted of 26 patients who received no pain treatment. The ozone group (G2) comprised 26 patients who underwent ozone therapy. This involved using a generator probe (type 3) for 5 seconds per point, applied to 23 points, resulting in a total application time of 1 minute and 55 seconds. The laser group (G3) included 25 patients who received diode laser treatment. This utilized a continuous mode diode laser with an output power of 400 mW, a handpiece diameter of 8 mm, and a spot area of 0.5024 cm². The power density per second was 1.59 W/cm², with a dose of 2 J per point. The treatment lasted 5 seconds per point across 23 points, totaling 46 J of energy per session and an application time of 1 minute and 55 seconds. The degree of teeth crowding was evaluated using the Lundstrom indicator. Patients completed a questionnaire utilizing the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS-11), with scores ranging from 0 to 10, at seven specific time points: 1 hour, 6 hours, and 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days after the placement of the orthodontic appliance. The average pain values recorded were 3.6 (±1.31) for the diode laser group (95% CI, 2.95–4.25), 5.25 (±3.37) for the ozone group (95% CI, 3.52–6.98), and 5.75 (±2.40) for the control group (95% CI, 4.69–6.81). Statistical analysis revealed significantly lower pain levels in the diode laser group compared to the control group (p = 0.0237). However, the application of ozone therapy in this study did not lead to a significant reduction in pain when compared to the control group (p = 0.8040) or the laser group (p = 0.1029). Furthermore, no discernible differences in pain perception were observed between patients with crowded teeth and those with spaced teeth within any of the groups (G1, p = 0.66; G2, p = 0.86; G3, p = 0.24). In conclusion, the use of a 635-nm diode laser was associated with decreased pain perception. Conversely, neither ozone therapy nor the presence of teeth crowding or spacing appeared to influence pain perception in orthodontic patients during the initial five days following the placement of their fixed orthodontic appliances.

Following the placement of fixed orthodontic appliances, pain, discomfort, and sensitivity are the most common adverse effects. A significant majority of patients, between 87% and 95%, report experiencing pain after orthodontic forces are applied, with the first 24 hours being particularly problematic. Additionally, 39% to 49% of patients endure discomfort throughout their treatment or during appliance removal. This pain is a significant factor that deters individuals from pursuing orthodontic treatment. Several factors contribute to pain perception, including a patient's age, pain threshold, motivation, prior negative dental experiences, and the intensity of the orthodontic force. Discomfort typically commences 2 to 4 hours post-force application, escalates over the subsequent 24 hours, and then gradually subsides within a week. The increased pressure associated with orthodontic treatment leads to ischemia, inflammation, and edema within the compressed periodontal ligament.
Pain during orthodontic treatment can be managed with pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are frequently prescribed and considered highly effective for pain relief. However, NSAIDs can cause adverse effects including gastrointestinal issues, blood disorders, skin reactions, high blood pressure, and headaches.
Biting wafers, laser, and ozone therapy are non-pharmacological pain relief options for orthodontic forces. Ozone, a gas, possesses bactericidal, virucidal, and fungicidal properties.
Ozone therapy fundamentally improves cellular oxygenation. Its application activates blood circulation, boosts hemoglobin levels, and enhances inflammatory responses like diapedesis and phagocytosis, while also stimulating antimicrobial reactions. Ozone also increases erythrocyte membrane elasticity through fatty acid peroxidation, facilitating easier passage through capillaries and thus improving tissue metabolism.
Laser technology finds diverse applications in orthodontics, encompassing procedures like soft and hard tissue surgery, the placement of orthodontic mini-implants, and bracket debonding. Low-level laser therapy (LLLT) is specifically utilized for pain relief during orthodontic treatment. Its mechanism involves stimulating sub-cellular photoreceptors, which enhances metabolic processes by influencing the electron transport chain, respiratory chain, and oxidation. This stimulation leads to blood vessel dilation and mast cell degranulation, releasing pro-inflammatory substances that promote tissue healing. Furthermore, laser therapy impacts neurons by stabilizing membrane potential, thereby slowing down pain signal activation.
This study investigated the impact of 635-nm diode laser and ozone therapy on orthodontic pain, considering the influence of teeth crowding.
This randomized, controlled trial obtained informed consent from participants and approval from the Local Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Wrocław Medical University (KB-546/2018).
Ninety patients (64 women, 26 men; mean age 32.6 ± 8.7 years) from NZOZ Ka-dent in Wschowa were initially considered for orthodontic treatment. Following inclusion criteria and consent, 76 participants (55 women, 21 men; mean age 35.1 ± 6.4 years) proceeded with the study, as detailed in a CONSORT 2010 flowchart.
Participants were first-time recipients of fixed orthodontic appliances. They had no systemic diseases, chronic pain, or neural pain, and had not used antibiotics recently. They were non-smokers, had fully erupted permanent teeth, and had undergone prior hygienist treatment.
Patients received explanations of the protocol and side effects, and provided informed consent before the experiments.
Following the placement of fixed orthodontic appliances, participants received a pain relief intervention. This involved either a 635-nm diode laser or an ozone generator, with intraoral probes applied to the tooth apex and interdental papillae areas of the maxilla. Pain perception was then assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS-11), a subjective measure for individuals over 10 years old, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (severe pain). Patients recorded their pain levels at seven intervals: 1 hour, 6 hours, and then daily for five consecutive days after appliance placement. The NRS-11 categorizes pain as mild (1-3), moderate (4-6), or severe (7-10).
Patients received Legend Mini (GC Orthodontics, USA) appliances with MBT, 0.018 slot prescriptions. Initial arches were NiTI 0.014 (Atlas, Prolinx Gmbh, Germany). The control group (n=26) received no pain treatment post-placement.
The ozone group (G2, n=25) received treatment with an OzoneDTA generator. Parameters included probe type 3, 5-second application per point in contact mode. Treatment covered tooth apex and interdental papillae areas from the maxillary left to right first molar (23 points), totaling 1 minute and 55 seconds for a single session.
The diode laser group (G3) consisted of 25 patients who received treatment with a SmartM diode laser (Lasotronix, Warsaw, Poland) emitting a 635-nm wavelength. This treatment was administered in a single session immediately after orthodontic appliance placement, using a bio-modulating handpiece in contact mode with soft tissue. The laser was applied to the area of each tooth apex and interdental papillae, spanning from the maxillary right first molar to the maxillary left first molar, covering 23 points. Each point received a dose of 2 J over 5 seconds, resulting in a power density of 1.59 W/cm². The laser operated in continuous mode with an output power of 400 mW, a handpiece diameter of 8 mm, and a spot area of 0.5024 cm², delivering a total energy of 46 J per session. The total application time for the laser treatment was 1 minute and 55 seconds.
Subjects were randomly assigned to groups using specialized software.
To assess the level of teeth crowding, patients were evaluated using the Lundstrom indicator. This involved dividing the upper dental arch into six segments, S1 through S6, each encompassing a pair of teeth and including the first molars. Measurements were taken for the length of each segment, defined as the distance between the tangent points of the tooth pairs, and the mesiodistal width of 12 teeth. This data was then recorded. The total width of each tooth pair was calculated to determine the required space within the dental arch. Any discrepancies between the required space and the actual arch size indicated either an excess or a deficit of space within specific segments. By summing these differences across all six segments (S1–S6), researchers could ascertain the overall deviation of the upper dental arch size from the actual tooth dimensions. Subsequently, patients within each study group were categorized as either having teeth crowding (Lundstrom indicator < 0) or not having teeth crowding (Lundstrom indicator ≥ 0).
Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica 12 software. Mean pain values across groups were compared using ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. The Mann-Whitney U test assessed pain differences related to teeth crowding. Significance was determined at a p-value below 0.05.
Pain perception was assessed using the NRS-11 scale. The diode laser group reported the lowest mean pain score of 3.6 (±1.31), with a 95% confidence interval of 2.95–4.25. The ozone group experienced a mean pain score of 5.25 (±3.37), and the control group reported the highest mean pain score of 5.75 (±2.40), with confidence intervals of 3.52–6.98 and 4.69–6.81, respectively. Statistical analysis indicated that the diode laser group experienced significantly less pain compared to the control group (p = 0.0237). However, ozone therapy did not demonstrate a significant pain-reducing effect when compared to the control group (p = 0.8040).
This study investigated pain levels related to tooth crowding using the NRS-11 scale and caliper measurements. The findings did not support the hypothesis that crowded teeth lead to increased pain compared to non-crowded teeth across all groups (G1, G2, G3). Statistical analysis showed no significant differences in pain perception due to crowding in any of the study groups.
Pain levels across the control, ozone, and laser groups peaked 24 hours after orthodontic treatment, a statistically significant finding compared to other assessment times. This suggests that laser irradiation is most beneficial within the initial 24-hour period following the placement of orthodontic appliances.
Orthodontic treatment frequently involves pain. This study aimed to determine the impact of photobiomodulation (PBM) and ozone therapy on pain perception during orthodontic treatment, considering the presence of teeth crowding. A key finding was that patients with crowded teeth and those without experienced comparable levels of discomfort. Laser application proved more effective in pain reduction compared to ozone therapy. Consistent with existing research [3, 8], this study observed that pain intensity was highest on the first day following orthodontic appliance placement. Discomfort commenced approximately 2 hours after appliance application, peaked at 24 hours, and then gradually subsided over the following 7 days. The average pain scores on the NRS-11 scale were 3.60 for the diode laser group, 5.25 for the ozone therapy group, and 5.75 for the control group.
For this study, a 635 nm diode laser was utilized. Light in the red to near-infrared spectrum (600–1500 nm) primarily undergoes scattering rather than absorption, allowing it to penetrate to depths of 8–10 mm. While red lasers have a shallower penetration than infrared ones, the 635 nm wavelength used here reaches a minimum depth of approximately 3 mm. This penetration is adequate to affect the soft tissues, tooth apex, and bone. Adhering to the Arndt-Schultz curve principles for energy dosage, a 2 J per point dose was administered to alleviate post-orthodontic appliance placement pain. Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT) offers analgesic, anti-inflammatory, anti-swelling, and regenerative benefits. Its anti-inflammatory action stems from increased serotonin secretion, leading to vasodilation. Enhanced microcirculation and reduced blood vessel permeability, facilitated by heparin and histamine, help prevent edema. This anti-inflammatory property is applied in managing pain across various dental fields, including surgery, periodontology, temporomandibular joint disorders, and orthodontics.
This study aimed to determine if a 635-nm diode laser could alleviate pain following orthodontic appliance placement. Our findings indicated a positive outcome for this laser wavelength when applied at a dose of 2 J per point (400 mW, 4 J/cm²). Previous research has reported comparable beneficial effects using an 810-nm diode laser at 100 mW and 2 J/cm² in studies by Farias et al. and Eslamian et al. However, conflicting results exist, with AlSayed et al. finding an 830-nm diode laser ineffective for orthodontic pain relief at doses of 4 and 16 J, particularly when elastomeric separators were involved. Additionally, a review by Li et al. concluded that insufficient evidence supports the efficacy of low-level laser therapy (LLLT) for orthodontic pain management due to methodological limitations and bias in existing randomized controlled trials.
Previous research by Qamruddin et al. [14] demonstrated positive outcomes with a 940-nm diode laser, reporting significantly lower mean NRS-11 scores for spontaneous pain after the application of initial 0.012-in and 0.014-in NiTi archwires. However, no significant difference was observed for thicker 0.016-in and 0.018-in wires when comparing low-level laser therapy (LLLT) to a placebo group. Our current study also yielded promising pain relief results for a 0.014-in NiTi archwire using a 635-nm wavelength laser, outperforming the control group. Additionally, Bayani et al. [33] investigated the efficacy of NSAIDs, bite wafers, and LLLT at 660 nm and 810 nm for orthodontic pain. Their findings indicated that the 810-nm laser was the most effective, suggesting that LLLT at various wavelengths could serve as an alternative to NSAIDs.
This study specifically investigated the impact of ozone therapy on orthodontic pain. The findings supported the hypothesis that ozone application did not significantly reduce pain. This limited efficacy may be attributed to ozone's superficial interaction with tissues compared to laser therapy. Additionally, diffusion barriers like plaque, saliva, and bacterial biofilm hinder ozone's penetration into tissues, further diminishing its effect. While ozone possesses biocidal properties and can improve tissue oxygenation and nutrition, which are beneficial during the aseptic inflammation common in orthodontic treatment, its antiseptic qualities did not translate into a notable decrease in reported pain scores in this research.
This study also investigated how tooth crowding influences pain perception during orthodontic treatment. Across all examined groups, no statistically significant differences in pain were observed between patients with crowded teeth and those without. Consequently, the hypothesis that crowded teeth lead to greater discomfort compared to non-crowded teeth was rejected. These findings align with previous research suggesting that tooth crowding or the forces applied by orthodontic archwires do not impact the initial pain experienced by patients [34, 35]. However, the literature on discomfort during orthodontic treatment is not entirely consistent. For instance, Luppanapornlarp et al. [36] found a correlation between stronger forces applied to teeth and more severe pain. Their research involved comparing pain intensity from different forces exerted by nickel-titanium coil springs on segmented archwires. The principles of Hooke's law dictate that increased force leads to proportional deformation in materials like orthodontic wires, which can then return to their original shape, a phenomenon known as elastic straining. In cases of severely crowded teeth, the behavior of orthodontic wires upon arch insertion may deviate from Hooke's law, potentially exerting consistent force irrespective of the activation level or deformation [37]. This suggests that the force applied to the teeth might remain uniform, regardless of the degree of tooth crowding. The outcomes of the present study support this notion.
Subject allocation to study groups was randomized using computer software. However, variations in patient age and gender distribution may have influenced pain thresholds, leading to a medium risk of bias. Differences in pain scores might also arise in studies involving elastic separators or initial archwires. Another limitation of this research was the reliance on the subjective Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for pain assessment. Consequently, future studies should explore more objective methods for measuring pain during orthodontic treatment.
Pain perception was similar between patients with crowded and non-crowded teeth. Peak pain occurred 24 hours after appliance placement, then gradually subsided over seven days. A 635-nm diode laser demonstrated the most effective pain relief, while ozone therapy showed no significant impact.
