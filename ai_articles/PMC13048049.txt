Tracheobronchial (TB) lumen narrowing in infants can necessitate prolonged positive-pressure ventilation, endotracheal intubation, or even surgical procedures. A less invasive approach involves therapeutic flexible bronchoscopy (TFB) for the placement of balloon-expandable metallic stents (BEMS), followed by management with forceps, laser, and balloon dilatation. This study investigated the placement, TFB-guided follow-up management, and long-term outcomes of BEMS in small infants. The research was a retrospective review of medical records and TFB videos from January 2005 to December 2017, focusing on infants with a body weight (BW) not exceeding 5.0 kg who underwent TB BEMS placement. A novel noninvasive ventilation technique, consisting of nasopharyngeal oxygen with intermittent nose closure and abdominal compression, supported all TFB procedures. A total of 41 BEMS were implanted in 24 infants, with a mean BW of 4.0 ± 0.7 kg and a mean age of 4.9 ± 2.4 months. Stents were placed in the trachea (20), carina (8), and main-stem bronchi (13). While seven infants (with 13 stents) passed away, no stent-related mortality was observed. Rigid endoscopy (RE) successfully retrieved seven stents from five infants. Initially, tracheal and bronchial stents measured 7.5 ± 1.1 mm and 5.4 ± 0.9 mm in diameter, respectively. These BEMS demonstrated significant gradual expansion (p < 0.01). At the study's conclusion, all 21 remaining stents in 12 infants remained functional, with tracheal stents measuring 9.6 ± 2.0 mm and bronchial stents measuring 7.2 ± 1.4 mm. The findings suggest that BEMS are a practical and effective option for selected small infants with benign TB narrowing, safely implanted and managed via TFB, with subsequent retrieval by RE.

Infants experiencing tracheobronchial (TB) narrowing, whether from congenital or acquired causes, can face severe, even life-threatening, breathing difficulties. When medical treatments prove insufficient, conventional approaches often involve highly invasive and costly surgical procedures like tracheostomy, tracheoplasty, bronchoplasty, thoracotomy, or extracorporeal life support, all carrying additional risks. In contrast, TB stent placement offers a less invasive method to quickly establish stable internal support, ensuring airway patency and enhancing quality of life. Metallic stent placement, available for children since 1990, presents a less invasive management option for selected cases. A particular advantage of balloon-expandable metallic stents (BEMSs) in pediatric patients is their capacity for further expansion to accommodate growing TB lumens. While therapeutic flexible bronchoscopy (TFB) has been utilized for years, its application in BEMS placement and management for small infants is not well-documented. Existing research on pediatric stent placement predominantly relies on rigid endoscopy (RE), endotracheal tubes (ETs), and mechanical ventilation, often requiring operating room transport with complex life support. Moreover, these studies typically involve limited patient numbers and lack detailed information on the placement, ongoing care, removal, and long-term results of metallic stents.
Our institution serves as a referral center for complex pediatric airway conditions. We frequently receive referrals from other tertiary centers, both domestically and internationally, for children who struggle to be weaned from prolonged endotracheal tube (ET) or positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) support. Our standard approach involves therapeutic flexible bronchoscopy (TFB) utilizing a novel noninvasive ventilation (NIV) technique. This NIV method, which we define as nasopharyngeal oxygen with intermittent nose closure and abdominal compression, allows for ventilation without the need for bags, masks, artificial airways, or ventilators. We have been developing and employing this TFB-NIV for various airway procedures, including the implantation and subsequent management of tracheobronchial balloon-expandable metallic stents (BEMS), since 1997. While this technique has been reported for BEMS placement and long-term outcomes in pediatric patients, its application and results in small infants remain underexplored.
This study reports our experience with TFB-NIV for managing tracheobronchial BEMS in small infants, covering placement, maintenance, long-term outcomes, and retrieval.
Infants weighing up to 5.0 kg who received BEMS between January 2005 and December 2017 were included. Medical records and associated BEMS videos were reviewed for this retrospective study.
Information gathered from medical charts and associated videos included patient demographics such as age, sex, and body weight at stent placement. We also collected data on symptoms, the reasons for stent implantation, the primary causes of tracheobronchial narrowing, and the specific locations where stents were placed. Details on stent diameter at initial placement and after final balloon dilatation, along with surveillance strategies, retrieval information, any complications encountered, causes of mortality, the final flexible bronchoscopy performed, and the long-term outcomes for both the stents and the patients were also recorded. The institutional review board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital provided approval for this study (TVGH IRB No.: 2018-04-008AC).
Procedures involving BEMS were conducted in the pediatric intensive care unit's endoscopy room, equipped with essential monitoring and resuscitation tools. Infants were positioned supine, with the bronchoscopist at their head. Sedation was administered intravenously, with midazolam (0.3-0.5 mg/kg), ketamine (1.0-2.0 mg/kg), and atropine sulfate (0.01-0.02 mg/kg, max 0.4 mg). Continuous monitoring of vital signs, including heart rate, respiration, and SpO2, was maintained. Blood pressure was intermittently measured noninvasively from the start of the procedure for up to four hours afterward. Post-procedure, infants were transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit for ongoing care.
For these patient groups, a flexible bronchoscope (FB) measuring 3.2 to 5.0 mm in outer diameter and 25-36 cm in working length was inserted through the infant's nostril. This bronchoscope, from Olympus (models HYF-V, ENF-VQ, ENF-V2, and ENF-VT2), could be equipped with an inner channel. The noninvasive ventilation (NIV) technique, previously detailed, involved supplying a continuous flow of heated and humidified pure oxygen at 1.0 L/kg/min (up to 5.0 L/min) through a nasopharyngeal catheter to maintain upper airway patency. Assisted breathing maneuvers were also employed. The infant's mouth was sealed by the bronchoscopist's index finger. Inspiration was aided by closing the infant's nose with the thumb and middle finger, along with pressure applied to the cricoid cartilage. Expiration was facilitated by releasing these maneuvers and simultaneously applying abdominal compression. This assisted ventilation could be performed 10 to 20 times per minute, guided by patient monitoring. The bronchoscopist managed the FB and the nose closure/release, while an assistant handled the abdominal compression and release. Crucially, no ventilation bags, masks, artificial airways, or mechanical ventilators were utilized during the entire procedure.
Stent placement was indicated for severe tracheobronchial (TB) malacia or narrowing, particularly when accompanied by frequent life-threatening episodes and prolonged dependence on endotracheal tube (ET) or positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) that could not be discontinued. This intervention was considered a final option before resorting to more invasive surgical procedures. Following stent implantation, therapeutic flexible bronchoscopy (TFB) interventions were scheduled at intervals of two to six months. These follow-up procedures were designed to monitor for and manage complications. Specific TFB interventions included forceps debridement and laser ablation to clear granulation tissue, and balloon dilatation plasty (BDP) to reduce granulomas and expand the stent to accommodate the growing TB lumen. The primary goal of these interventions was to preserve the patency and lumen of the implanted stents. During surveillance, BDP allowed for simultaneous expansion and measurement of stent lumens. The final diameter of each stent was determined by the balloon size used in the most recent BDP. Surveillance durations varied depending on individual stent outcomes, extending from the time of placement until six months post-retrieval, death, or the study's conclusion.
Data were presented as means ± SD, medians with ranges, or percentages. Stent diameters were compared using a paired samples t-test, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.
Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the 24 infants in this retrospective study. Over 13 years, 41 balloon-expandable metallic stents (BEMS) were implanted, with 100% successful placement. At implantation, infants had a median body weight of 4.0 kg (range 2.3-5.0 kg) and a median age of 4.8 months (range 0.3-10.2 months).
Cardiovascular disease was the leading cause of tracheobronchial narrowing necessitating stent placement, affecting 11 infants (45.8%). Iatrogenic malacia from prolonged endotracheal tube and positive-pressure ventilation use was the next most frequent cause in 6 infants (25.0%), followed by tracheoesophageal fistula with severe tracheomalacia in 4 infants (16.7%), and idiopathic causes in 3 infants (12.5%). Following BEMS placement, all infants experienced immediate and significant respiratory improvement, enabling rapid weaning from respiratory support. No infant required further surgical intervention for their tracheobronchial lumen issues.
Figure 1 illustrates the placement sites of the implanted stents. The most frequent location was the lower trachea, with 18 stents (43.9%), followed by the left main bronchus in 10 stents (24.4%). Eight carinal stents (19.5%) were also placed. These carinal stents, measuring 30-40 mm in length, were positioned to address malacia in the pericarinal area. They extended from one main bronchus, through the carina, and into the lower trachea. Subsequent balloon dilatation procedures (BDP) with varied balloon sizes were used to expand the tracheal and bronchial segments of these carinal stents to match their specific luminal dimensions.
At the study's conclusion, outcomes for both infants and stents were evaluated. A total of seven infants, representing 29.2% of the cohort, passed away, with 13 stents (31.7%) in situ. Causes of mortality included cardiopulmonary failure in four infants (seven stents), parental decisions in two infants with cerebral palsy (four stents), and pulmonary bleeding in one infant (two stents). Importantly, no deaths were attributed to the stents themselves. In 20.8% of cases (five infants), seven stents (17.1%) were successfully retrieved because the implanted lumens had sufficiently developed. The remaining 21 stents (51.2%) were still in place in 12 infants (50%) at the study's end.
The average surveillance duration for all stents was 39.9 months. Infants who died had an average surveillance of 11.7 months. Those whose stents were removed or retained until study completion had an average surveillance of 52.9 months.
The primary issues with stents involved obstructive granulation leading to narrowing, distortion, or loosening. These complications, along with infections or pneumonia, were effectively managed using the TFB-NIV technique.
Initially, 28 tracheal stents and 21 bronchial stents measured 7.5 ± 1.1 mm (range 4-10 mm) and 5.4 ± 0.9 mm (range 4-8 mm), respectively. Following implantation, these BEMSs demonstrated significant gradual expansion. By the end of the follow-up, the remaining 14 tracheal stents averaged 9.6 ± 2.0 mm (range 8-14 mm) and the 13 bronchial stents averaged 7.2 ± 1.4 mm (range 4-10 mm), indicating a statistically significant increase (p < 0.01).
This study represents the largest cohort of small infants to date investigating the management and long-term outcomes of BEMS implantation utilizing the novel TFB-NIV technique. Previous research on TB BEMS in children has often involved limited case numbers or older age groups. These earlier studies faced challenges due to small airway diameters, inadequate respiratory support, and increased risk of hypoxia during procedures like rigid endoscopy, endotracheal intubation, or patient transport. Furthermore, sedation and manipulation within the tracheobronchial tree could worsen existing cardiopulmonary issues. However, consistent with prior reports on the TFB-NIV technique, this study demonstrates that BEMS procedures can be performed safely, simply, and effectively.
Prior to stent placement, all infants in this study relied on endotracheal tube (ET) or positive-pressure ventilation (PPV). The novel noninvasive ventilation (NIV) technique, involving nasopharyngeal oxygen with intermittent nose closure and abdominal compression, effectively substituted this prior respiratory support. This method eliminated the need for face masks or other invasive airways like laryngeal mask airways, ETs, or ventilators. Nasopharyngeal oxygen insufflation, a well-established and accessible clinical practice, offers improved comfort, reduced oxygen flow, and less invasiveness. Furthermore, nose closure and abdominal compression aid in both PPV and expiration. Abdominal compression also provides circulatory support, demonstrating efficacy in cardiopulmonary support and resuscitation in various studies. This NIV technique has been proven safe, simple, and effective for respiratory support and rescue during pediatric flexible bronchoscopy (FB) procedures. Bronchoscopists using TFB-NIV can also adjust PPV pressure intensity dynamically, enhancing diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities.
Following BEMS placement, patients routinely receive daily nebulized saline and steroids, along with gastroesophageal reflux management. Clinical and flexible bronchoscopy assessments of the airway are scheduled every 2 to 6 months. The TFB-NIV technique is employed for monitoring and addressing issues like granulation, repair, expansion, and retrieval, preventing significant worsening.
Compared to silicon or membrane stents, the mesh design of BEMS helps preserve mucociliary function and permits airflow when positioned over bronchial branches. In this study, balloon dilatation plasty (BDP) served multiple purposes. It was employed to appropriately enlarge stent openings at bronchial orifices and to further expand the BEMS's luminal diameter, accommodating growing airways. BDP also allowed for the repair or remodeling of distorted stent structures, eliminating the need for replacement. Additionally, BDP effectively compressed or reduced stent-associated granulomas. The carina and bifurcation regions remained free of significant obstruction post-BDP. Ultimately, BDP proved useful in preparing implanted stents for retrieval by facilitating their fragmentation.
Stent retrieval was indicated for several reasons, including nonfunction, irreparable damage, serious complications, placement exceeding two years, or a visible portion of the stent protruding from the mucosa. In this study, seven stents were successfully removed using rigid endoscopy and forceps. Prior to retrieval, a guidewire was inserted between the stent and the mucosa. Balloon dilatation was then performed to detach the stent from the tissue, allowing for its removal as either fragments or a whole unit. Any remaining stent fragments were managed with appropriate balloon dilatation.
This study, conducted retrospectively at a single institution, has limitations. A control group was not included due to the long-standing effectiveness of the TFB-NIV method with BEMS at our center. Future research should involve larger, multicenter cohorts to further explore this approach.
Balloon-expandable metallic stents (BEMS) offer an effective alternative to invasive surgery for tracheobronchial malacia and narrowing. Therapeutic flexible bronchoscopy with noninvasive ventilation (TFB-NIV) provides a safe and convenient method for BEMS placement and management in small infants. This technique also facilitates successful stent retrieval by rigid endoscopy when needed.
