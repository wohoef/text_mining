This study aimed to examine how giving iron supplements to non-anemic pregnant women affects their pregnancy outcomes within an Iranian population. The research was conducted as a non-randomized clinical trial over two years at obstetrics clinics in Shiraz, southern Iran. Singleton pregnancies were included, with individuals having pre-existing medical conditions excluded. At the start of the study, serum ferritin levels were measured. Participants were then divided into three groups. Group 1 consisted of women with normal serum ferritin levels (≥30 µg/dL) who were given standard prophylactic iron supplementation throughout their pregnancy. Group 2 comprised women with minor thalassemia and elevated serum ferritin levels (≥30 µg/dL) who did not receive iron supplements, as well as women with normal ferritin levels (≥30 µg/dL) who declined iron supplementation due to gastrointestinal issues. Group 3 included women diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia, characterized by low serum ferritin levels (<30 µg/dL), who received standard iron supplementation during pregnancy. All participants were monitored until delivery, and their maternal and neonatal outcomes were documented and compared across the three groups. The study included 30 pregnant women in each group, with an average age of 28.66 ± 6.02 years. No significant differences were observed between the groups concerning gestational age at delivery (p = 0.250), birth weight (p = 0.893), Apgar scores at 1 (p = 0.532) and 5 (p = 0.590) minutes, or the method of delivery (p = 0.590). The overall incidence of maternal complications during pregnancy was also comparable across the three groups (p = 0.188). However, Group 1, which received prophylactic iron supplementation, exhibited a significantly higher rate of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) compared to the other two groups (p = 0.038). In conclusion, prophylactic iron supplementation for pregnant women with normal ferritin levels appears to be linked to an elevated risk of developing GDM. Other pregnancy and neonatal outcomes, however, did not seem to be influenced by this prophylactic iron supplementation.

Iron deficiency stands as a prevalent global nutritional deficit, contributing significantly to illness and death, particularly in Africa. Worldwide, anemia affects up to 38.2% of pregnant women, leading to adverse outcomes such as low birthweight, hypertensive disorders, and premature birth. Observational studies and meta-analyses indicate that anemic mothers face adverse pregnancy outcomes in roughly half of cases, imposing a substantial disease burden. A pregnant woman with normal weight has an estimated iron requirement of approximately 1000 mg per day. Consequently, the WHO advises daily supplementation of 30 to 60 mg of elemental iron and 400 mg of folic acid for all pregnant women, irrespective of their iron status, as a preventive measure. Factors contributing to anemia during pregnancy include inadequate iron intake, impaired absorption, short intervals between pregnancies, and preconception iron levels. For pregnant women already diagnosed with anemia, a regimen of 120 mg of elemental iron daily for three months, often combined with vitamin A, is recommended, showing enhanced benefits for those anemic in their second trimester.
Research has explored various aspects of iron supplementation during pregnancy. A Cochrane review compared intermittent and daily iron regimens, finding similar maternal and neonatal outcomes between them, though the intermittent approach resulted in fewer side effects. Another study indicated that initiating iron supplementation early in pregnancy could enhance perinatal outcomes. Despite numerous meta-analyses and systematic reviews examining the link between iron deficiency anemia and maternal/neonatal outcomes, the findings remain inconclusive. While observational studies, including cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control designs, have suggested associations between iron deficiency anemia during pregnancy and adverse outcomes like low birthweight, premature birth, and perinatal death, clinical data has not consistently supported these observations. The benefit of iron supplementation appears less clear for asymptomatic, nonanemic pregnant women. Recent meta-analyses suggest insufficient evidence regarding screening accuracy in this group, but fair evidence indicates moderate health benefits from treating asymptomatic iron deficiency anemia. Another meta-analysis found inconclusive evidence that routine prenatal supplementation for anemia improves clinical maternal or infant outcomes, although it may enhance maternal hematologic indices. It is also important to note that iron overload during pregnancy, similar to deficiency, can be associated with adverse outcomes for both mother and fetus.

This clinical trial investigated prophylactic iron supplementation's effects on pregnancy outcomes in nonanemic Iranian women.
From March 2016 to March 2018, a non-randomized clinical trial took place at the obstetrics clinics of Zeinabieh and Hafez hospitals in southern Iran. These hospitals are tertiary healthcare centers affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences and serve as referral centers for obstetrics. The study enrolled singleton pregnancies in their first trimester, with participants aged between 18 and 40 years, who were receiving prenatal care at these centers. Several exclusion criteria were applied. Women with pre-existing comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, liver or renal failure, cardiovascular disorders, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were not included. Additionally, individuals with a history of pregnancy complications, including pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, premature birth, abortion, stillbirth, low birthweight, or premature rupture of membranes, were excluded. Women who did not adhere to regular iron and folic acid supplementation or were lost to follow-up were also excluded. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Medical Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Registration code: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1396.21). It was also registered with the Iranian Registry for Clinical Trials (IRCT2017051533976N1). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study.
Participants were recruited from our clinics, with their contact information on file. An obstetrics and gynecology resident conducted initial examinations, collecting baseline characteristics and clinical data on standardized forms. To assess iron status, serum ferritin levels were measured at the outset. Based on these ferritin levels, participants were divided into three groups. Group 1 comprised women with normal serum ferritin (≥30 µg/dL) who received standard prophylactic iron supplementation. Group 2 included women with minor thalassemia and elevated ferritin (≥30 µg/dL) who did not receive supplementation, as well as those with normal ferritin (≥30 µg/dL) who declined supplementation due to gastrointestinal issues. Group 3 consisted of women with iron deficiency anemia and low ferritin (<30 µg/dL) who were given standard iron supplementation. All groups were carefully matched for baseline characteristics such as age, gestational age, parity, and gravidity.
In the first trimester, serum ferritin and serum iron levels were assessed for all participants. Complete blood counts (CBCs) were also performed across all three trimesters. Serum ferritin was quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) at Hafez laboratory, affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. Measurements utilized the Human Ferritin IRMA Kit from Isar Padtan Gostar, following manufacturer guidelines. Assay variability, both intra- and inter-assay, remained below 6%.
Participants received monthly check-ups until 28 weeks of gestation, then twice weekly until 36 weeks, and finally weekly until delivery. Throughout this period, maternal and neonatal indicators were documented. The study followed all participants until the end of their pregnancies, recording clinical outcomes. Maternal adverse events such as pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, premature birth, abortion, stillbirth, pregnancy duration, delivery method, and low birthweight were noted. Neonatal outcomes included Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes, aspiration pneumonia, and NICU admissions. These recorded results were then compared across the study groups.
To determine the necessary sample size, the prevalence of anemia in pregnant women was considered, aiming for 80% power to detect a 0.05 difference in primary study outcomes with an alpha of 0.01. This calculation indicated that 27 subjects per study group were needed. To account for potential non-evaluable participants, each group was expanded to include 30 women. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 16.0, with data entered into a computer database. Results are presented as mean ± SD where applicable. For normally distributed parametric variables, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test was employed. Non-normally distributed parametric variables were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Proportions were compared via the chi-square test. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
A total of 90 pregnant women with singleton pregnancies completed the study, with 30 participants in each of the three groups. The average age of these women was 28.66 ± 6.02 years, with an age range of 18 to 39 years. Baseline characteristics, including age, BMI, gravidity, and parity, were comparable across all three groups. Notably, Group 3 exhibited significantly lower ferritin levels compared to both Group 1 and Group 2 (p < 0.001 for both comparisons). The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends prophylactic iron supplementation for pregnant women to prevent adverse outcomes linked to iron deficiency anemia. However, its role in women with normal ferritin levels remains debated. To address this, our clinical trial evaluated the impact of prophylactic iron supplementation on maternal and neonatal outcomes in singleton pregnancies within an Iranian population. Our findings indicate that iron supplementation in pregnant women with normal ferritin levels is associated with an elevated risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Conversely, other pregnancy outcomes were not significantly affected. These results align with existing research from diverse populations. This study represents the first investigation into the role of iron supplementation in pregnant women with normal ferritin levels within Iran.
The role of iron in pregnancy is complex, presenting a double-edged sword. While iron deficiency is linked to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, excessively high iron levels, particularly in the third trimester, pose a risk and are associated with an increased incidence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). GDM affects approximately 18.1% of pregnancies, and iron intake during gestation can significantly elevate this risk. Excess iron beyond the body's needs can lead to unabsorbed iron in the gut, systemic oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and compromised function of the pancreatic beta cells. A meta-analysis by Iqbal and Ekmekcioglu, which summarized other meta-analyses, explored the impact of iron supplementation and iron status on maternal and neonatal outcomes. Their findings indicated that multi-micronutrient supplementation could offer benefits for certain neonatal outcomes. Importantly, they observed that elevated ferritin levels appear to raise the risk of GDM, while maternal anemia is associated with detrimental birth and neonatal outcomes. In parallel, a meta-analysis by Cantor et al. examining prophylactic iron supplementation in asymptomatic, nonanemic pregnant women found inconclusive evidence for improvements in maternal or infant clinical health outcomes from routine prenatal supplementation for anemia. However, such supplementation may lead to better maternal hematologic indices.
Our study revealed that providing iron supplementation to nonanemic pregnant women correlates with a higher incidence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). This finding aligns with prior research indicating that elevated hemoglobin and ferritin levels are linked to an increased risk of GDM. The prevailing hypothesis suggests that iron overload can promote oxidative stress. This occurs through enhanced production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, as well as hydroxyl radicals, via Fenton reactions. Such processes can inflict significant damage on cellular components, including membranes, proteins, and DNA. Consequently, while ferritin is a crucial indicator of iron status, elevated levels of free iron or ferritin may contribute to impaired insulin sensitivity due to heightened oxidative stress. Evidence suggests that high serum ferritin levels early in pregnancy are associated with GDM risk and insulin resistance syndrome, even in non-pregnant populations. The consumption of processed or red meat, which provides highly bioavailable heme iron, has also been linked to an increased risk of type 2 diabetes. Therefore, the observed association between serum ferritin and GDM may be attributed to iron-induced oxidative stress, lipid oxidation, and DNA damage. Additionally, a significant connection exists between ferritin and hepcidin, both of which play a role in the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes. Studies have also noted higher serum hepcidin and ferritin levels in pregnant women diagnosed with GDM.
The study acknowledges several limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size may introduce calculation bias. Despite this, the study was powered at 80% to detect a 5% difference in adverse pregnancy outcomes. Secondly, the research employed a cohort design, whereas a randomized clinical trial would have been the optimal methodology for addressing the research question. This design choice was necessitated by resource constraints, and a clinical trial is currently being planned to investigate the issue more definitively. To our knowledge, this research is the first to examine the impact of prophylactic iron supplementation on pregnant women with normal ferritin levels.
Prophylactic iron supplementation for pregnant women with normal ferritin levels increases GDM risk, while other maternal and neonatal outcomes remain unaffected. Further research is needed to clarify these findings.
