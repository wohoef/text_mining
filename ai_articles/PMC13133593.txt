For individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), physical inactivity presents a significant yet modifiable characteristic. While conventional exercise-focused pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) enhances symptoms and exercise capacity, its influence on physical activity (PA) levels is often restricted. Digital health (eHealth) interventions offer a potential solution to this limitation. This study sought to evaluate the practicality and user acceptance of integrating an eHealth PA coaching intervention within PR for COPD patients. The methodology involved recruiting patients from an outpatient PR program for a three-week PA coaching intervention. This intervention utilized a smart band linked to a mobile application for patients and a web application for healthcare professionals (HCPs). Key components of the intervention included daily PA monitoring (steps), weekly goal setting, and app notifications providing goal updates, achievement feedback, and motivational messages. Additionally, weekly telephone calls facilitated goal adjustments and the identification of barriers to PA. A patient focus group was conducted to explore the intervention's acceptability. Five COPD patients participated in the study (mean age 67, SD 9 years; 80% female; mean predicted forced expiratory volume at 1 second of 49%, SD 23%). The study achieved 100% retention and adherence, with participants synchronizing their devices daily. No adverse events or PA barriers were noted, apart from one instance of an app connection issue, which was resolved by restarting the application. Patients generally found the app user-friendly and beneficial for increasing their PA awareness and enabling remote monitoring by HCPs. They particularly valued the weekly goal adjustments and regular contact with an HCP. However, limitations identified included a lack of personalization in the app, goal setting being exclusively step-based, and occasional inaccuracies in step counting. In conclusion, the intervention demonstrated both feasibility and high acceptance among participants. Future research with a larger cohort is necessary to determine the intervention's effectiveness on PA outcomes.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common and debilitating lung condition, placing a substantial strain on healthcare systems. A key modifiable factor in COPD is physical inactivity, which significantly increases the risk of exacerbations, hospitalizations, and mortality. Consequently, enhancing physical activity levels in individuals with COPD is a global health priority.
Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) effectively improves symptoms and function in COPD patients. However, its impact on physical activity (PA) levels has been inconsistent. Therefore, integrating behavioral interventions like PA coaching into PR is crucial. This approach optimizes active lifestyle promotion and enables early detection and intervention for low PA adherence, thereby enhancing patient outcomes.
Optimizing physical activity (PA) levels during and after pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is crucial. E-health, including mobile applications, offers a promising strategy, leveraging its growing presence in daily life and healthcare. These technologies facilitate real-time PA monitoring, enabling continuous, automated self-monitoring and feedback. This approach allows for accurate, low-burden activity measurement and provides ongoing access to recorded data, both historically and in real time, thereby supporting improved PA outcomes.
Before effectiveness trials, eHealth interventions like PA coaching need feasibility and acceptability assessments for optimization. Identifying barriers is crucial for developing robust, personalized, and scalable interventions that promote long-term active lifestyles in COPD patients. Thus, this study evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of an eHealth-supported PA coaching intervention for COPD patients in a PR program.
The Local Health Unit of the Leiria Region's ethics committee approved this study (reference 10/2024, acta number 022024/01/16). All participant data adhered to relevant protection regulations. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all patients who agreed to participate in the study.
This study, a single-group feasibility and acceptability assessment, investigated a three-week physical activity (PA) coaching intervention integrated into a pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) program. It forms a component of the broader "Effectiveness of Physical Activity Coaching using eHealth in patients with COPD" study, which seeks to evaluate the efficacy of personalized eHealth coaching in elevating PA levels among individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The primary objective of this specific feasibility study was to determine the intervention's practicality and patient acceptance. This included evaluating the PA goal-setting algorithm and gauging participant receptiveness to the intervention, particularly the digital platform. The study adhered to established guidelines for reporting pilot and feasibility studies, adapted for a single-arm design, and all participant data were managed in compliance with relevant data protection regulations. A three-week duration was deemed necessary to allow participants sufficient interaction with the platform, enabling a thorough assessment of its acceptability and facilitating refinement of the underlying algorithm for future, larger-scale implementation.
Participants were recruited from Portugal's Local Health Unit of the Leiria Region. Eligibility criteria are detailed in Textbox 1.
Eligible patients were first identified by the PR program physician, who provided a brief study overview. Interested individuals were then contacted by a researcher via telephone. During this call, the researcher detailed study procedures, confirmed eligibility, and ascertained willingness to participate. Reasons for non-participation were documented. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection.
The study involved four patient interaction points, with initial (T1) and final (T4) assessments occurring three weeks apart. At baseline (T1), participants provided comprehensive sociodemographic and health data via a questionnaire. This included age, sex, education, occupation, smoking history, comorbidities (using the Charlson Comorbidity Index), exacerbation history, medication, weight, and height. Information on preferred physical activities (e.g., walking, swimming, dancing) and smartphone app usage was also collected. Lung function was evaluated using spirometry to categorize patients by Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) airflow obstruction severity (GOLD 1-4, based on forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1] percentages). Patients were further classified into GOLD A, B, and E categories according to their symptom burden and exacerbation history. Additionally, the study assessed the impact of COPD on daily life, dyspnea, fatigue, exercise tolerance, quality of life, and self-efficacy for physical activity. These health-related outcomes were detailed in a separate section.
Participants received a mobile app and smart band for the study duration. The initial week established a baseline step count for subsequent goal setting. At the end of the first week (T2), patients were educated on physical activity (PA) importance and recommendations, then collaborated to set their first PA goal. By the close of the second week (T3), a researcher reviewed the prior PA goal's achievement, investigated any PA barriers or adverse events, and, via telephone, established the next goal with the patient. Following the third week (T4), patients returned the app and smart band, undergoing reassessment. To gather feedback on the PA coaching intervention, a focus group was also conducted with patients. This session explored their experiences with the new technology, their views on the significance of PA promotion interventions, the app's functionality, and its overall acceptability, including any identified barriers or facilitators to its use. This comprehensive approach allowed for both quantitative tracking and qualitative insights into the intervention's implementation and reception.
The Fitrockr Hub App, an eHealth platform, facilitated personalized physical activity (PA) coaching. It monitored patient adherence to PA goals and delivered tailored notifications, enabling individualized support for each patient in the study.
The Fitrockr Hub App platform consists of two distinct applications: a web-based interface designed for healthcare professionals (HCPs) and a mobile application for patients. The patient's mobile app integrates with a Garmin Vivosmart 4 smart band, enabling comprehensive physical activity (PA) monitoring. This includes tracking steps, distance covered, and calories burned. Patients can also access their PA history, view daily PA goals established through the web application, and receive performance notifications related to their goal achievement. The web application provides HCPs with a centralized system to oversee patients' daily PA. From this interface, HCPs can define or modify individualized PA goals, which are then displayed on the patient's mobile app homepage. Furthermore, the web application facilitates patient management, allowing HCPs to add new patients, prescribe personalized step-based PA goals, monitor daily PA levels (e.g., daily step counts), verify goal attainment, and dispatch personalized notifications. For analytical purposes, all collected data can be exported to Excel files.
The principal researcher, acting as the sole healthcare professional (HCP) for physical activity (PA) coaching, delivered the intervention using an exclusive platform. This coaching supplemented the standard pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) program, which hospital HCPs provided to all participants.
The pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) program, lasting eight weeks, followed European Respiratory Society and American Thoracic Society recommendations. Participants, though at varying stages of their PR, all adhered to the same study protocol. This ensured consistent intervention delivery across the cohort.
Participants wore a smart band daily for a week to establish baseline step counts. This band, connected to the Fitrockr Hub App, required manual synchronization twice daily—once in the morning and once before bed—via an in-app button, as automatic syncing was unavailable. This process ensured consistent data collection for subsequent activity goal setting.
Following an initial week of activity monitoring, participants received in-person guidance on physical activity (PA) recommendations and its significance. At this point (T2), their first PA goal was established. This goal was individualized using an algorithm that factored in the average daily steps from the preceding week and the patient's self-reported confidence in modifying their activity levels. This confidence metric was then converted into a percentage increase over the prior week's activity, and the updated goal was displayed within the Fitrockr Hub App. To ensure patient safety and comfort, weekly PA goal increases were capped at 10%. Throughout the week, automated notifications were sent daily at 6 PM, informing patients of their progress toward their goals and encouraging further activity if necessary. Additionally, the researcher occasionally sent personalized notifications, such as suggestions for indoor activities during inclement weather or motivational messages. At the conclusion of the second week (T3), a telephone call was made to each patient to review their PA progression. If a patient had not met their previous week's goal, the researcher facilitated a discussion to identify barriers to PA improvement and collaboratively devise strategies to overcome them. Subsequently, the PA goal for the following week was set using the same algorithmic approach. Patients also had the option to contact the researcher by telephone for technical assistance throughout the intervention.
The study documented reasons for unmet goals, adverse events, and technical app issues. A diary detailed telephone calls and goal progression, included in Multimedia Appendix 2.
The intervention incorporated various behavior change techniques (BCTs) from the BCT Taxonomy v1, recognizing physical activity (PA) as a complex behavior. These included goal setting, planning, feedback, monitoring, and fostering self-belief. Further details on BCT implementation are available in Multimedia Appendix 3.
Intervention feasibility, defined as successful implementation in a given setting or larger study, was assessed by parameters evaluated during the intervention. This included examining recruitment, retention, adherence, and technical issues.
Patient acceptability of the intervention, defined as its perceived appropriateness for promoting physical activity (PA) during pulmonary rehabilitation (PR), was evaluated. This assessment utilized a focus group, guided by a semistructured protocol, to gather patient feedback. The discussions were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed for analysis, with no additional field notes taken during the session. The focus group explored two primary areas. Firstly, it sought comprehensive feedback on the intervention itself, delving into patients' overall experience. This included their views on the intervention's effectiveness in enhancing PA levels, identifying both obstacles and enablers to using mobile applications for PA promotion, and their perspectives on goal setting, progress tracking, notifications, and interactions with healthcare professionals (HCPs). Secondly, the focus group addressed the functionality of the application. Here, patients provided insights into the barriers and facilitators encountered while using the app, specifically regarding their experience with the smartphone application and smart band, and the perceived technological complexity of the system.
Although no changes in health-related outcomes were anticipated due to the intervention's brief, three-week duration, these measures were incorporated into the assessment phases. This was done to accurately replicate the data collection protocols planned for a subsequent randomized controlled trial. Several health-related factors were evaluated. Functional exercise capacity was quantified using the 6-Minute Walk Test, with predicted values derived from an equation specific to Portuguese adults. This test adhered to European Respiratory Society and American Thoracic Society guidelines, conducted within a 30-meter corridor. Vital signs were recorded both before and after the test. Dyspnea and fatigue symptoms were assessed via the modified Borg scale. The impact of COPD on daily life was gauged using the COPD Assessment Test. Dyspnea severity was further evaluated with the modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale. Quality of life was measured using the Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short Form Health Survey version 2. Self-efficacy for physical activity was determined through an adapted version of the Physical Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale. Finally, fatigue levels were assessed using the Checklist of Individual Strength, which categorized fatigue into three distinct levels: normal (score ≤26), mild (score between 27 and 35), and severe (score ≥36).
Sample characteristics were analyzed using descriptive statistics in SPSS. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) for normal distributions, or median (IQR) otherwise. Categorical variables were presented with absolute frequencies (n) and percentages (%).
The focus group data underwent thematic analysis, a qualitative method, to identify key discussion points. Participants were assigned alphanumeric codes for anonymity during this process. Two researchers independently analyzed the data, developing themes and categories within an Excel spreadsheet. They then convened to systematically compare their findings and reach a consensus. Themes were generated and analyzed sequentially, guided by the focus group questions. The information was then structured into a descriptive framework once no new categories emerged. This rigorous approach ensured a comprehensive and agreed-upon interpretation of the qualitative data.
Two female physiotherapists, experienced in respiratory care and physical activity (PA) promotion, conducted the interviews. They maintained neutrality during analysis, adhering to qualitative research guidelines. A senior team member, with expertise in COPD management, PA promotion, and qualitative research, also contributed to the research team.
From March to May 2024, five patients with COPD participated in this study. Six individuals from an outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation program were eligible, but one declined due to disinterest. This resulted in a final sample of five participants.
The study participants, five individuals with COPD, had an average age of 67 years (SD 8.9). The majority were female (80%, n=4) and all were retired. Most (80%, n=4) were classified as GOLD E, indicating a high risk of exacerbations and significant symptom burden. Their lung function showed moderate to severe airflow limitation, with a mean predicted FEV1 of 49% (SD 22.8%). Educationally, 60% (n=3) had completed 10 to 12 years of schooling. The mean COPD Assessment Test score was 13.0 (SD 9.0), and fatigue levels, measured by the Checklist of Individual Strength, averaged 78.4 (SD 28.9). Dyspnea severity, assessed using the modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale, had a median score of 3.0 (IQR 0.5-3.5). Each assessment session typically lasted about 40 minutes.
All patients in the study used smartphones for various tasks. Every participant browsed the internet and listened to music. Most also sent texts (80%), while 60% used their phones for photos, games, and social media. Fewer, 40%, utilized GPS.
Eighty-three percent recruitment, 100% retention, no dropouts.
The intervention experienced no adverse events or unplanned healthcare utilization. A single technical issue, an app connection problem, was resolved by a researcher restarting the application. Patient adherence was complete, with all five participants consistently synchronizing the mobile app daily. Goal progression calls averaged ten minutes. Records of goal progression and telephone interactions, detailing technical support, goal setting, and physical activity promotion, are available in Multimedia Appendix 2.
The focus group, lasting 66 minutes, identified several themes regarding the eHealth intervention.
Patients valued the eHealth PA coaching, recognizing its benefit for their own activity awareness and for healthcare professionals monitoring their physical activity levels. This intervention was seen as a valuable addition, enhancing both self-management and professional oversight of PA.
The Fitrockr Hub App's sole focus on step counts for goal setting, limited personalization beyond walking, occasional step miscounts, and cost were identified as barriers. Conversely, its user-friendly design, reasonable time commitment, and the ability to prescribe physical activity goals were seen as facilitators. Patients valued the app's ease of use and its role in promoting physical activity awareness and remote monitoring by healthcare professionals. However, they desired more personalized goal setting and improved step count accuracy.
Patients viewed the physical activity (PA) coaching intervention as a collaborative and personalized process, particularly valuing the negotiation of PA goals with healthcare professionals (HCPs). They found the weekly adjustment of PA goals, based on individual performance and willingness to increase activity, to be appropriate. Furthermore, patients emphasized that the weekly identification of PA barriers and the development of strategies to overcome them significantly enhanced their engagement. Key obstacles preventing patients from achieving their PA goals included adverse weather conditions, exacerbations or worsening of symptoms, and individual variability in response to PA. The consistent contact with HCPs throughout the intervention, whether through video calls or telephone, was also universally highlighted by patients as a crucial element. This ongoing interaction was seen as vital for support and guidance.
The eHealth physical activity (PA) coaching intervention demonstrated feasibility and strong patient acceptance. Participants viewed the app as user-friendly and beneficial for self-managing PA and enabling remote monitoring by healthcare professionals (HCPs). Key positive aspects included weekly goal adjustments, regular app notifications, and consistent contact with HCPs via calls. However, patients noted limitations: the app lacked personalization, exclusively focused on step counts for PA goals, and occasionally miscounted steps. These findings highlight areas for refinement in future eHealth interventions for individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
This study, despite its shorter intervention duration compared to other eHealth-based physical activity (PA) interventions for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), demonstrated strong feasibility through high recruitment and retention rates (over 80%). These rates align with or surpass those found in previous feasibility studies, indicating the practicality of incorporating PA coaching into pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) within this clinical setting. However, the generalizability of these findings is limited. The study involved a small, relatively homogeneous sample, primarily consisting of retired women familiar with smartphone use. To enhance the applicability of future research, it is crucial to recruit more diverse participant groups. This includes individuals with lower digital literacy, varying levels of motivation for PA, and a higher burden of multimorbidity, as these characteristics are known to impact engagement with eHealth interventions.
The intervention encountered no significant technical or physical activity barriers, contrasting with prior findings on eHealth adoption in COPD. This feasibility may stem from the brief intervention period and readily available technical support. However, sustained adherence could be impacted by factors like comorbidities, environmental issues, and psychological barriers, such as breathlessness-related anxiety, which tend to intensify over time.
Participants found the physical activity (PA) goal-setting algorithm acceptable, despite not always meeting weekly targets. They approved of the incremental increases and revision periods. This aligns with research suggesting weekly PA goal adjustments, with 5-15% step count increases, should be tailored to individual tolerance and adherence.
The intervention demonstrated strong acceptability, with all patients consistently adhering to daily smart band and app synchronization, irrespective of their goal attainment. Patients generally found the physical activity (PA) app user-friendly and easily integrated into their daily routines. This suggests that technological simplicity and usability may be more critical for engagement than extensive personalization, as technical problems frequently cause participant dropout. Participants also highly valued the weekly interactions with healthcare professionals (HCPs). This aligns with existing research emphasizing that patient-professional engagement is a crucial factor in promoting PA, especially within eHealth interventions. Furthermore, personalized goal setting, tailored to individual requirements, and timely feedback notifications were recognized as vital elements of these interventions. However, the study identified significant limitations. These included the app's exclusive reliance on step-based goals and its restricted personalization options. Such limitations could potentially diminish patient motivation, engagement, and the long-term sustainability of behavior change. Although the eHealth platform could not be modified in this study, future research should prioritize increased personalization to better address patient needs. Patients reported common barriers to PA, such as health-related issues (e.g., symptom flare-ups), individual differences, and environmental factors like weather. Overcoming these obstacles is essential for developing effective and customized PA interventions. This study incorporated several evidence-based behavioral strategies, including goal setting, feedback, monitoring, repetition, substitution, and fostering self-belief, which are known to enhance PA in individuals with COPD by facilitating barrier identification and problem-solving.
This feasibility study indicates the goal-setting algorithm suits COPD patients. Regular weekly contact between healthcare professionals and patients is crucial. This interaction should establish physical activity goals, identify and address potential barriers, and provide performance feedback. These elements are vital for larger future studies.
This study's findings suggest eHealth-supported physical activity (PA) coaching holds promise for COPD self-management. However, caution is warranted due to the small sample size and participants' existing digital literacy, which limits the generalizability of these results. Prior research indicates that individuals with greater technological experience and interest are more likely to benefit from eHealth interventions. Significant changes in PA behavior were not anticipated given the intervention's brief duration. Instead, the primary purpose of collecting health-related outcome measures at baseline and study completion was to evaluate the feasibility of data collection protocols and to guide refinements for subsequent, larger-scale trials.
This eHealth-supported physical activity (PA) coaching intervention proved feasible and well-received by patients. However, future research must address certain barriers to ensure sustained adherence. While remote monitoring was successful, the digital infrastructure needs strengthening to prevent technical errors and data inconsistencies, which could compromise clinical reliability. For long-term adherence and acceptability, future interventions should adopt a more personalized coaching model. This approach would customize PA goals and intervention strategies to each patient's unique clinical profile. Such refinement is crucial for optimizing the intervention's effectiveness and ensuring its broad applicability.
