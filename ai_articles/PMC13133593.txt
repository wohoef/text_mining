Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent and debilitating condition that significantly burdens healthcare systems and contributes to long-term disability. A critical, modifiable factor in COPD management is physical inactivity, which is strongly linked to increased exacerbations, hospitalizations, and mortality. Consequently, enhancing physical activity (PA) levels in these patients is a global health priority.

While traditional exercise-based pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) effectively alleviates symptoms and improves exercise tolerance, its impact on sustained PA levels has been inconsistent. This suggests a need to integrate behavioral interventions, such as PA coaching, into PR programs to foster an active lifestyle. Such an approach enables clinicians to identify and address low PA adherence early. Digital health (eHealth) interventions, including mobile applications, offer a promising avenue to support PA coaching, given their increasing ubiquity and integration into healthcare. These technologies can monitor PA in real-time, provide continuous self-monitoring and feedback, and offer accessible, accurate data for both patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs).

Before widespread implementation, eHealth interventions, particularly PA coaching, require thorough assessment for feasibility and acceptability. This ensures optimization and helps identify specific barriers, which is crucial for developing robust, personalized, and scalable interventions that promote long-term active lifestyles in COPD patients. This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of an eHealth-supported PA coaching intervention integrated into a PR program for individuals with COPD.

The study, approved by the ethics committee of the Local Health Unit of the Leiria Region (reference 10/2024, acta number 022024/01/16), was a single-group feasibility and acceptability investigation. It examined a 3-week PA coaching intervention delivered during a PR program, forming part of the larger "Effectiveness of Physical Activity Coaching using eHealth in patients with COPD (Physical Activity in COPD Using eHealth)" study. The primary objective was to assess the intervention's feasibility and acceptability, specifically testing the PA goal-setting algorithm and evaluating patient receptivity to the digital platform, to inform adjustments for a subsequent larger trial. The 3-week duration allowed sufficient patient interaction with the platform for testing and refinement. Participant data were handled in accordance with applicable data protection regulations.

Participants were recruited from the Local Health Unit of the Leiria Region, Portugal. Eligible individuals were identified by the PR program physician, who provided an initial overview of the study. Interested patients were then contacted by a researcher to detail study procedures (data collection, dates, intervention delivery), confirm eligibility, and ascertain willingness to participate. Reasons for non-participation were recorded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to any data collection.

The study involved four interaction points with patients. Baseline assessments (T1) included a questionnaire on sociodemographic and health-related information (age, sex, education, occupation, smoking history, Charlson Comorbidity Index, exacerbation history, medication, weight, height), preferred physical activities, and smartphone usage. Lung function was assessed via spirometry to classify patients according to Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) grades of airflow obstruction severity (GOLD 1-4) and categories (A, B, E) based on exacerbation history and symptoms. The impact of COPD on daily living, dyspnea, fatigue, exercise tolerance, quality of life, and self-efficacy for PA were also measured.

Following baseline assessment, patients received a mobile PA app paired with a smart band for use throughout the study. The first week established a baseline step count for subsequent goal setting. At the second time point (T2, end of week 1), patients received education on PA importance and recommendations, and their initial PA goal was set. At T3 (end of week 2), the researcher reviewed goal achievement, explored PA barriers and potential adverse events, and set the next goal via telephone. At T4 (end of week 3), patients returned the app and smart band and underwent reassessment identical to T1. A patient focus group was also conducted to gather feedback on the intervention, including experiences with new technology, the importance of PA promotion, app functionality, and acceptability, barriers, and facilitators of use.

The eHealth platform utilized was the Fitrockr Hub App (Fitrockr Health Solutions), designed to tailor PA coaching by monitoring adherence to predefined goals and enabling individualized notifications. This platform comprises a web application for HCPs and a mobile app for patients. The mobile app synchronizes with a Garmin Vivosmart 4 smart band, monitoring PA (steps, distance, calories), providing access to PA history, displaying daily PA goals, and delivering notifications on goal achievement. The web application allows HCPs to monitor daily PA, define and adjust individualized goals, manage patient profiles, and send personalized notifications. Data can be exported for analysis. In this study, the principal researcher acted as the HCP responsible for PA coaching, with exclusive access to the platform and intervention delivery. Hospital HCPs delivered the standard 8-week PR program, which all participants attended, with the PA coaching as an additional component.

Patients wore the smart band connected to the Fitrockr Hub App daily for one week to establish baseline steps (T1). Manual synchronization of the app and smart band was required twice daily (upon waking and before sleep). After the first week (T2), patients received PA education, and their initial personalized PA goal was set in person. Goal personalization was based on an algorithm considering the previous week's average daily steps and the patient's confidence in achieving an increased goal. This confidence level translated into a percentage increase (not exceeding 10% to prevent injury and ensure tolerability) from the previous goal, displayed in the app. Daily automatic notifications at 6 PM informed patients of their goal progress. Sporadic personalized notifications (e.g., indoor activity suggestions during bad weather, motivational messages) were also sent manually by the researcher. At the end of the second week (T3), a telephone call checked PA progression. If goals were not met, the researcher helped identify barriers and discussed strategies to overcome them. The next week's goal was then set using the algorithm. Patients could contact the researcher for technical support throughout the intervention. Reasons for not progressing goals, adverse events, and technical issues were recorded. A diary of telephone calls and goal progression was maintained.

The intervention incorporated several behavior change techniques (BCTs) from the BCT Taxonomy v1, including goals and planning, feedback and monitoring, natural consequences, repetition and substitution, comparison of outcomes, and self-belief, to address the complexity of PA behavior.

Feasibility was defined as the intervention's implementability within the study setting and was assessed through recruitment rate, retention rate, adherence to the intervention, adverse events, and technical issues. Acceptability was defined as patients' perception of the intervention's appropriateness for promoting PA during PR. This was assessed via a semi-structured, audio-recorded focus group, covering feedback on the intervention (importance, barriers/facilitators of mobile app use, goal setting, notifications, HCP contact) and app functionality (interaction with smartphone app and smart band, technological complexity).

Given the short 3-week duration, significant changes in health-related outcomes were not anticipated. However, these measures were included to replicate procedures for a subsequent randomized controlled trial. Functional exercise capacity was measured using the 6-Minute Walk Test. The impact of COPD on daily living was assessed with the COPD Assessment Test, dyspnea with the modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale, quality of life with the Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short Form Health Survey version 2, and self-efficacy for PA with an adapted Physical Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale. Fatigue was measured using the Checklist of Individual Strength.

Descriptive statistics (mean/SD, median/IQR, absolute frequencies/percentages) characterized the sample using SPSS Statistics (version 28.0; IBM Corp). Focus group data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Participants were anonymized (P1, P2, etc.). Themes and categories were developed in an Excel spreadsheet, and analysis was primarily conducted independently by two researchers, followed by systematic comparison and consensus. Themes were generated and analyzed question-by-question, following the focus group guide, until no new categories emerged, then organized into a descriptive framework. Two female physiotherapists with master's degrees in respiratory care and PA assessment/promotion conducted the interviews and analysis, ensuring neutrality. A senior member with expertise in COPD management, PA promotion, and qualitative research provided oversight.

Recruitment occurred in March 2024, with follow-up until May 2024. Of six eligible patients, one declined due to lack of interest, resulting in a final sample of five patients. The mean age was 67.0 (SD 8.9) years, with a mean predicted FEV1 of 49% (SD 22.8%), indicating moderate to severe airflow limitation. The sample was predominantly female (n=4, 80%), with most classified as GOLD E (n=4, 80%), suggesting high exacerbation risk and symptom burden. Most patients (60%, n=3) had 10-12 years of formal education, and all were retired (n=5, 100%). The mean COPD Assessment Test score was 13.0 (SD 9.0). Mean fatigue (Checklist of Individual Strength) was 78.4 (SD 28.9), and median dyspnea (modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale) was 3.0 (IQR 0.5-3.5). Each assessment session averaged 40 minutes. All patients used smartphones for various tasks, including internet browsing, music, messaging, photos, games, social media, and GPS.

The recruitment rate was 83% (5/6), with 100% retention and no dropouts. No adverse events or unplanned healthcare use were reported. One technical issue with app connection was resolved by restarting the app. Adherence was 100% (5/5), with daily mobile app synchronization. The average duration of the goal progression call (T2) was 10 (SD 3.13) minutes.

The focus group, lasting 1 hour and 6 minutes, identified several key themes. Patients universally valued the eHealth PA coaching intervention, recognizing its benefit for their own PA awareness and for HCPs in monitoring PA levels. Barriers to using the Fitrockr Hub App included the exclusive focus on step counts for goal setting, lack of personalization beyond walking activities, occasional step miscounts by the smart band, and the hypothetical cost of the app in a real-world context. Facilitators included a user-friendly layout, acceptable time demands, and the prescription of PA goals. Patients viewed goal setting as a collaborative and personalized process, emphasizing the importance of negotiated goals with HCPs. They found the weekly adjustment of PA goals (based on performance and willingness to increase) appropriate and valued the weekly identification of PA barriers and strategies to overcome them. Common barriers included bad weather, exacerbations or worsening symptoms, and individual variability in response to PA. All patients highlighted the importance of maintaining contact with HCPs (via video or telephone) during the intervention.

Overall, this eHealth PA coaching intervention demonstrated feasibility and high acceptability among patients. Positive feedback indicated the app was easy to use and valuable for PA self-management and remote HCP monitoring. Patients emphasized the importance of weekly goal adjustments, frequent app notifications, and regular HCP contact. However, limitations included a lack of personalization, the exclusive focus on step counts, and occasional step miscounts.

Despite a shorter intervention period than similar eHealth-based PA interventions in COPD, this study achieved high recruitment and retention rates (>80%), supporting its feasibility. These rates are comparable to or higher than those in previous feasibility studies, suggesting the practicability of integrating PA coaching into PR. However, the small, relatively homogeneous sample (predominantly female, retired, digitally literate) limits generalizability. Future studies should recruit more diverse samples, including individuals with lower digital literacy, varying PA motivation, and higher multimorbidity, as these factors influence eHealth engagement.

No major technical or PA barriers were identified, contrasting with reports highlighting digital literacy and technical challenges in eHealth adoption for COPD. This may be partly due to the short intervention duration and provided technical support. Over longer periods, comorbidities, environmental factors, and psychological barriers (e.g., fear of breathlessness) could impact adherence. The PA goal-setting algorithm was deemed appropriate, with patients accepting weekly revisions and incremental increases, even if goals weren't consistently met. This aligns with recommendations for weekly goal adjustments (5-15% step increases) tailored to individual tolerance and adherence.

The intervention's acceptability was evidenced by 100% adherence to daily smart band synchronization. Most patients found the app easy to use and well-integrated into daily life, supporting the idea that usability can be more critical than personalization for engagement, as technical issues often lead to dropout. Weekly HCP contact was highly valued, consistent with evidence highlighting patient-professional interaction as a key facilitator of PA promotion, especially in eHealth. Personalized goal setting and feedback notifications were identified as essential components. However, the exclusive use of step-based goals and limited app personalization were noted as limitations that could reduce motivation and sustained behavior change. While the eHealth platform could not be modified, future studies should prioritize greater personalization. Patients reported PA barriers consistent with literature, including health-related issues, individual variability, and environmental factors. Addressing these is crucial for effective, individualized PA interventions. The intervention successfully applied evidence-based behavioral strategies (goal setting, feedback, monitoring, repetition, substitution, self-belief) to support barrier identification and problem-solving.

This feasibility study suggests the goal-setting algorithm is appropriate for COPD patients. Weekly HCP-patient contact for goal setting, barrier identification, strategy development, and performance feedback emerged as essential components for future larger-scale studies.

While eHealth PA coaching shows promise for COPD self-management, the small sample size and predominance of digitally literate participants limit generalizability. Individuals with greater interest and experience in technology are often more likely to benefit from eHealth interventions. Given the short intervention duration, significant PA behavior changes were not expected; health-related outcome measures primarily assessed data collection feasibility and informed refinements for future trials.

In conclusion, this feasibility and acceptability study demonstrates that the eHealth PA coaching intervention is feasible and well-accepted by patients. However, future studies must address identified barriers to ensure long-term adherence. Regarding feasibility, robust digital infrastructure is crucial to eliminate technical errors and data discrepancies. For acceptability and sustained engagement, future interventions should adopt a more comprehensive, personalized coaching model tailored to each patient's unique clinical characteristics and PA goals.
