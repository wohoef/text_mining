This study aimed to compare volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) with pressure-limited ventilation (PLV) in preterm infants. Researchers investigated 100 preterm infants requiring mechanical ventilation across two distinct periods. During the first period, 50 infants received PLV, while in the second period, 50 infants were managed with VTV. A range of clinical outcomes were assessed, including mortality, duration of ventilation, air leak syndrome, and levels of carbon dioxide and oxygen in the blood (hypocarbia, hypercarbia, hypoxemia). Additionally, the study evaluated the combined outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), as well as rates of intraventricular hemorrhage and retinopathy of prematurity. The results indicated no significant differences between the VTV and PLV groups in the duration of mechanical ventilation, air leak syndrome, hypocarbia, hypoxemia, or BPD. However, the VTV group demonstrated significantly lower mortality rates, reduced hypercarbia, and a significantly decreased incidence of the combined outcome of death or BPD. The study concluded that VTV use in preterm infants was associated with improved outcomes, specifically lower mortality, less hypercarbia, and a significant reduction in the combined endpoint of death or BPD.

Traditionally, newborn infants have been managed with time-cycled pressure-limited ventilation (PLV). This method employs a set peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) within a defined time cycle, delivering a designated gas volume. However, PLV has been associated with both overexpansion (volutrauma) and under expansion/collapse (atelectrauma). Research indicates that tidal volume (VT), not inflation pressure, is the primary factor in ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI).
Volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) is designed to control and sustain a suitable tidal volume (VT). By prioritizing VT as the main control, VTV allows inflation pressure to decrease as lung compliance and patient effort improve, facilitating real-time pressure reduction. This dynamic pressure adjustment helps prevent excessively large VTs and can lead to shorter durations of mechanical ventilation. Prior research has highlighted several benefits of VTV over pressure-limited ventilation (PLV), such as reduced mortality or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), fewer pneumothoraces, less hypocarbia, and a lower incidence of severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) or periventricular leukomalacia (PVL).
This study aimed to compare the effects and safety of VTV versus PLV in preterm infants.
This study retrospectively analyzed data from preterm infants (<37 weeks gestational age) requiring mechanical ventilation at Chung Shan Medical University Hospital's Level III NICU. During the first study period (October 1, 2014, to February 28, 2016), infants received pressure-limited ventilation (PLV) using the Servo Ventilator 300. Subsequently, from March 1, 2016, to August 1, 2017, volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) was administered via the Babylog VN 500.
The study included 100 preterm infants requiring mechanical ventilation, with term infants and those with congenital malformations excluded. The infants were divided into two groups based on the ventilation period. During period 1, 50 infants received Pressure-Limited Ventilation (PLV). This group had a mean birth weight of 1.51 ± 0.56 kg and a mean gestational age of 30.14 ± 3.15 weeks. In period 2, 50 infants were ventilated using Volume-Targeted Ventilation (VTV). Their mean birth weight was 1.61 ± 0.50 kg, and their mean gestational age was 30.78 ± 2.52 weeks. Patient data, including heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation (SpO2), were continuously monitored. Table 1 details the patient characteristics for both the PLV and VTV groups. The primary conditions necessitating ventilation were similar across both groups. In the PLV group, the underlying diseases included respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) in 34 infants, neonatal pneumonia in 10, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) in four, and meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS) in two. Similarly, the VTV group comprised 33 infants with RDS, 11 with neonatal pneumonia, three with HIE, and three with MAS.
Pulmonary hypertension prompted the use of inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) in five patients in the PLV group and six in the VTV group. These infants underwent echocardiographic examinations with Doppler measurements to assess tricuspid regurgitation, patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) shunt direction, and atrial shunt direction. All infants receiving iNO therapy exhibited echocardiographic signs of pulmonary hypertension, such as elevated pulmonary artery (PA) pressure, right-to-left or bidirectional PDA, or patent foramen ovale shunting.
During the initial, acute phase of illness, infants managed with Pressure-Limited Ventilation (PLV) were placed in the assist/control (A/C) mode. Ventilator settings were established with a rate of 30 to 60 breaths per minute, an inspiratory time of 0.4 seconds, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) ranging from 5 to 8 cmH2O, and a peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) between 12 and 25 cmH2O. These PIP levels were adjusted based on the infant's chest movement and carbon dioxide elimination. As infants improved and recovered from their respiratory illness, indicated by a PIP below 18 cmH2O and an FiO2 less than 0.3, the ventilatory mode transitioned from A/C to synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation. Therapeutic targets for arterial blood gases included a pH of 7.25 to 7.45, PaO2 of 50 to 70 mmHg, PaCO2 of 35 to 55 mmHg, and SpO2 of 90% to 95%. Extubation was considered when the PIP was below 16 cmH2O, PEEP was below 5 cmH2O, the rate was 20 bpm or less, and the FiO2 was 0.3 or lower. Following extubation, infants received nasal bubble CPAP at 5 to 6 cmH2O via short binasal prongs.
Ventilatory settings for VTV followed established neonatal guidelines. Tidal volume was initially set between 4.0 and 6.0 mL/kg. Neither group received sedation or muscle relaxants during the study.
Arterial blood gas analysis was employed to measure PaCO2 and PaO2 levels. For infants under 72 hours of age, arterial blood gas samples were collected every 12 hours, with this frequency reduced to every 24 hours until extubation. Hypocarbia was identified when PaCO2 fell below 35 mmHg, while hypercarbia was defined as a PaCO2 exceeding 60 mmHg. Both hypocarbic and hypercarbic episodes were determined based on the arterial blood gas data. Hypoxemia was characterized by an SpO2 below 80% for more than 20 seconds, or a PaO2 less than 50 mmHg, as indicated by arterial blood gas measurements. Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) was diagnosed using a combination of radiological and clinical findings. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) was defined as requiring FiO2 greater than 0.21 for at least 28 days, coupled with an unsuccessful room air challenge test at 36 weeks postmenstrual age, with or without ongoing respiratory support. Sepsis was confirmed by a positive blood culture and subsequent antibiotic treatment for a minimum of 7 days. Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) was identified through cranial ultrasound, encompassing IVH with or without ventricular dilatation, or intracerebral (parenchymal) hemorrhage. Pulmonary hemorrhage was diagnosed by the presence of blood in the endotracheal tube and radiographic evidence of focal or diffuse ground-glass opacities. Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) staging followed the international classification, with stages 3 to 5 considered severe. Specifically, stage 3 ROP involved a ridge with fibrovascular extension, stage 4 indicated partial retinal detachment, and stage 5 represented total retinal detachment. The Ethical Committee at Chung Shan Medical University Hospital provided approval for this study.
Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 22. Student's t-tests compared means, while chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests analyzed categorical data. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
Infant characteristics, including birth weight, gestational age, and gender, showed no significant differences between the PLV and VTV groups. Similarly, the use of surfactant therapy, inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) therapy, and its duration and concentration were comparable between the two ventilation modes.
The study's outcomes, detailed in Table 2, revealed several key differences between the volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) and pressure-limited ventilation (PLV) groups. While no significant variations were observed in the duration of mechanical ventilation, air leak syndrome, hypoxemia, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), or the frequency of hypocarbia, the VTV group demonstrated superior results in other areas. Specifically, the mortality rate was considerably lower in the VTV group, with only one patient succumbing compared to eight in the PLV group (p = 0.031). Furthermore, the VTV group experienced significantly fewer episodes of hypercarbia (p = 0.036). A crucial finding was the significantly reduced combined outcome of death or BPD in the VTV group (p = 0.008). Other outcomes, including pulmonary hemorrhage, severe retinopathy of prematurity, sepsis, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) across different grades, and periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), showed no statistically significant differences between the two ventilation strategies.
For over three decades, time-cycled pressure-limited ventilation (PLV) has been the established method for neonatal respiratory support. This conventional PLV approach employs a consistent peak inflating pressure. However, the actual delivered tidal volume (VT) fluctuates with each breath due to changes in the infant's respiratory effort, lung compliance, and airway resistance. Such variability in VT can result in either lung overexpansion, known as volutrauma, or insufficient lung inflation, potentially leading to acidosis. Crucially, research indicates that VT, not just inflation pressure, is the primary factor contributing to ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI). Studies have shown that substantial lung injury can occur when large VTs are delivered, irrespective of the inflation pressure used to achieve them.
Volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) automatically adjusts peak pressure and cycle duration to achieve a target tidal volume. This mode aims to mitigate ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) that can arise from excessive or insufficient tidal volumes during conventional pressure-controlled ventilation.
VTV, compared to PLV, has been shown in a previous systematic review to reduce ventilation duration, death/BPD, pneumothorax, hypocarbia, and PVL/severe IVH.
Previous research indicates VTV's benefits. Lista et al. found VTV reduced inflammatory mediators IL-6 and IL-8 in tracheal aspirates of preterm infants with RDS. Peng et al. reported VTV use led to shorter mechanical ventilation duration, lower BPD incidence, less hypocarbia, fewer grade 3/4 IVH, pneumothorax, and PVL.
Volume guarantee (VG) ventilation is a prevalent form of VTV in neonates. This method dynamically adjusts peak inspiratory pressure for each breath to ensure a consistent tidal volume (VT), guided by exhaled VT from prior cycles. VG ventilation aims to prevent both excessive and insufficient VT delivery while consistently lowering peak pressure. Research by Keszler et al. indicated that VG ventilation effectively reduced both hypocarbia and excessively large tidal volumes. Similarly, Jain et al. found that VG ventilation, in standard clinical settings, led to a slight decrease in the duration of hypoxemic episodes (SpO2 < 85%) when compared to pressure control ventilation, although it did not impact more severe hypoxemia.
In line with previous research, this study found significantly lower mortality rates, hypercarbia, and a combined outcome of death or BPD in the VTV group compared to the PLV group. This aligns with findings from Wheeler et al., who also reported a reduction in the combined outcome of death or BPD with VTV ventilation.
In this study, inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) therapy was administered to five patients in the PLV group and six in the VTV group, all of whom exhibited echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension. Previous research indicates that iNO therapy can enhance oxygenation in preterm infants experiencing hypoxic respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension. The current study specifically utilized iNO for infants presenting with both hypoxemia and positive echocardiographic findings for pulmonary hypertension. Furthermore, numerous prior investigations have established that VTV mode ventilation is associated with a decreased incidence of hypocarbia when contrasted with PLV. For instance, Cheema et al. reported that volume guarantee (VG) ventilation significantly lowered the occurrence of both out-of-range PaCO2 and hypocarbia in infants exceeding 25 weeks of gestation.
While VTV notably reduced hypercarbia, no significant difference was found between groups. Both hypocarbia and hypercarbia are linked to brain damage in preterm infants.
The study had several limitations. It was conducted retrospectively at a single center. Furthermore, the use of different ventilators for each ventilation mode (Servo ventilator 300 for PLV and Babylog VN 500 for VTV) introduced inconsistency, which future research could address.
Volume-targeted ventilation (VTV) demonstrated lower hypercarbia, mortality, and combined death or BPD rates compared to pressure-limited ventilation (PLV) in preterm infants. The authors propose VTV as a lung protection strategy to reduce ventilator-induced lung injury.
