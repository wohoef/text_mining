Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine disorder affecting women of reproductive age, yet its underlying causes are not fully understood. Furthermore, a universally accepted set of diagnostic criteria for PCOS is still lacking. Previous efforts to identify a straightforward clinical biomarker for PCOS diagnosis have largely been unsuccessful. This study sought to explore a potential link between PCOS and anogenital distance (AGD), a known indicator of androgen exposure during fetal development. The research involved a prospective cohort study of 130 women, divided into two groups: 65 women diagnosed with PCOS and 65 healthy women serving as controls. All participants were between 18 and 40 years old. The study compared these groups across various demographic, clinical, and laboratory measures. For each woman, both anterior and posterior AGD measurements were taken, along with their calculated ratios. The findings revealed a statistically significant difference in the mean ratio of anterior to posterior AGD between the groups, with the PCOS group averaging 4.4 ± 1.0 and the control group 4.9 ± 1.0 (p = 0.003). Regression analysis further indicated that this AGD ratio showed a significant positive correlation with waist-to-hip ratio and a negative correlation with the free androgen index. Historically, AGD was utilized to differentiate sex in animals, with males exhibiting longer AGDs. This observation was later confirmed in humans, where boys were found to have longer AGDs than girls. More recent research, supporting the theory that excessive prenatal androgen exposure contributes to PCOS, has shown that adult PCOS patients have longer AGDs compared to control subjects. However, the need for a new biomarker beyond AGD to standardize these measurements remains. This study is notable as the first to specifically examine the ratio of anterior to posterior AGD in PCOS patients. While both anterior and posterior AGD measurements were longer in PCOS patients in this study, the ratio of anterior to posterior AGD emerged as the most robust predictor of PCOS.

PCOS, a prevalent endocrine and reproductive disorder affecting women of reproductive age, presents with various clinical and laboratory indicators. These include elevated insulin levels, excessive luteinizing hormone secretion, hyperandrogenism, and menstrual irregularities such as amenorrhea and oligomenorrhea, often leading to infertility. While the precise mechanisms underlying PCOS remain elusive, both genetic predispositions and environmental influences, particularly heightened intrauterine androgen exposure, are hypothesized to contribute to its development. Animal studies further support the notion that excess androgen can disrupt the hypothalamus-pituitary gonadal axis, leading to reproductive issues in adulthood. Consequently, identifying early biomarkers for excessive prenatal testosterone exposure would be highly beneficial if such exposure indeed increases PCOS risk, as suggested by animal models.
Anogenital distance (AGD) serves as an indicator of genital development and androgen exposure. Its utility was first established in defining sexual differentiation in animals, where males consistently exhibited AGD lengths 50% to 100% greater than females. This observation was later corroborated in human studies, demonstrating similarly longer AGDs in boys compared to girls. Researchers have also employed AGD to evaluate the harmful impacts of endocrine-disrupting chemicals on the reproductive systems of various experimental animals. For instance, Scott et al. observed that rodents exposed to phthalates, known to reduce fetal androgen levels, displayed not only shorter AGDs but also impaired testicular size and Sertoli cell function. Conversely, increasing prenatal testosterone in animal models, such as female rodents, rabbits, sheep, and nonhuman primates, leads to a lengthening of AGD.
Given the scarcity of human research on this topic, there's a growing interest in understanding the role of anogenital distance (AGD). Recent investigations have shown that adult women with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) exhibit longer AGDs compared to healthy controls. This observation lends credence to the theory that significant prenatal androgen exposure plays a part in the development of PCOS. Furthermore, one of these studies identified a direct relationship between AGD measurements and circulating testosterone levels. In healthy young women, extended AGDs have been associated with both the presence of multiple follicular ovaries and elevated serum testosterone. It's important to note, however, that adult studies don't rule out the influence of postnatal factors on AGD or ovarian function. Cross-sectional research involving adult women indicates that AGD correlates positively with age, negatively with hormonal contraceptive use, and may possess some degree of plasticity throughout adulthood. While AGD has been recognized as a relevant biomarker for PCOS in prior research, only one such study focused on adults. Our current study is unique in being the first to assess the ratio of anterior to posterior AGD in PCOS patients.
The study examined adult Turkish women to determine if PCOS is linked to AGD, a known indicator of fetal androgen exposure.
The study, conducted at Liv Hospital in Ankara, Turkey, received ethical committee approval. All participants provided written informed consent. The research involved 130 women aged 18 to 40 years, divided into two groups: 65 with PCOS and 65 healthy controls. PCOS patients were recruited from the hospital's gynecology outpatient clinic between January 2016 and June 2018, while controls were healthy women seeking routine gynecologic examinations at the same facility.
For inclusion in the study, PCOS was identified using the Rotterdam criteria. This required at least two of the following conditions: oligomenorrhea or anovulation, evidence of hyperandrogenism (either biochemical or clinical), or the presence of polycystic ovaries (defined as more than 12 cysts, each 2-9 mm in size, detected via ultrasound). Importantly, other potential causes like congenital adrenal hyperplasia, virilizing tumors, Cushing syndrome, prolactinoma, diabetes, hypertension, or other cardiovascular diseases had to be ruled out. Participants were excluded if they had used hormonal contraceptives or other metabolism-affecting medications, or followed a specific diet, within six months prior to the study. Smoking and alcohol consumption also led to exclusion. The control group comprised 65 women, carefully matched for age and BMI, who exhibited regular menstrual cycles and no signs of clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism or polycystic ovaries. Additionally, women with perineal masses, defects, or a history of vaginal delivery with episiotomy were excluded from the study.
Each participant underwent a comprehensive physical examination and provided a detailed clinical history. Body Mass Index (BMI) was determined by dividing an individual's weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters. To assess hirsutism, scores were assigned using the modified Ferriman-Gallwey score (FGS). The waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), an indicator of visceral fat, was computed by dividing the smallest waist circumference by the hip circumference measured at the greater trochanters. Standard laboratory tests were conducted to measure levels of various hormones, including estradiol (E2), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), prolactin (PRL), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-SO4), 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17OH-P), androstenedione, sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), and total testosterone (TT). The Free Androgen Index (FAI), a measure of androgenic status, was derived by multiplying the total testosterone level by 100 and then dividing by SHBG. Finally, anogenital distance (AGD) was measured from the anterior clitoral surface to the upper anal verge (AGDant) and from the posterior fourchette to the upper anal verge (AGDpost), with the ratio of these two measurements (AGD Ratioant/post) subsequently calculated.
A single physician conducted all ultrasonographic and pelvic examinations. Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) was identified via ultrasound when at least one ovary contained 12 or more follicles, each 2 to 9 mm in diameter, or had an ovarian volume exceeding 10 mm3. Menstrual cycles lasting over 45 days indicated oligomenorrhea, while amenorrhea signified a three-month absence of menstruation. Chronic anovulation was verified by mid-luteal phase serum progesterone levels below 5 ng/mL.
Hirsutism severity was assessed using the modified Ferriman-Gallwey score (FGS), with a score of 8 or higher indicating clinical hirsutism. A previously established method determined the presence and severity of acne and androgenetic alopecia. PCOS was categorized into three phenotypes: type A (classical, involving PCOM, oligomenorrhea, and hyperandrogenism), type B (PCOM and hyperandrogenism), and type C (PCOM and oligomenorrhea).
Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 22.0 for Windows. To assess data distribution, the single sample Shapiro Wilk test was employed for normality, while Levene’s test checked for homogeneity of variance. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± SD or as counts and percentages. For comparisons between groups, the t-test was utilized for independent variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. Pearson’s correlation analyses explored potential relationships between the Ratioant/post and other variables. Categorical group comparisons relied on either the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Linear logistic regression was conducted to determine the association between the Ratioant/post and factors related to PCOS. A two-tailed perception rate guided the evaluation of statistical hypotheses, with a p-value below 0.05 signifying statistical significance.
The study involved a total of 130 women, all within reproductive age (18 to 40 years), who participated in an ongoing case-control study. This cohort comprised 65 women diagnosed with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) and 65 healthy controls exhibiting normal ovulation and no signs of hyperandrogenism. While the average height was comparable between the two groups (p > 0.05), women with PCOS showed a significantly higher mean body weight (p = 0.010). Similarly, the Body Mass Index (BMI) was notably greater in the PCOS group (27.9 kg/m2) compared to the control group (25.8 kg/m2), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.008). The mean age for the PCOS group was 25.7 ± 3.6 years (ranging from 18-40 years), and for the control group, it was 26.6 ± 4.3 years (ranging from 20-40 years), with no significant age difference between them (p > 0.05). Further details on the participants' demographic, physical, and clinical attributes are presented in Table 1.
Hormone profiles, assessed during the early follicular phase, revealed distinct differences between groups. While FSH, E2, DHEA-SO4, 17OH-P, androstenedione, TT, FAI, PRL, and TSH levels showed no significant variation, the PCOS group exhibited notably higher LH and lower SHBG levels compared to controls (p < 0.05 for both).
Within the PCOS cohort, patients were categorized into three subgroups: A (36.9%), B (32.2%), and C (30.8%). A significant difference (p < 0.001) was observed in the prevalence of hyperandrogenism, affecting 45 PCOS patients (69.2%) compared to only three healthy controls (4.6%). Similarly, oligomenorrhea was considerably more common in the PCOS group, present in 44 patients (67.7%) versus seven controls (10.8%) (p < 0.001). Furthermore, acne was noted in 22 PCOS patients (33.8%) and five controls (7.7%) (p < 0.001), while alopecia affected 27 PCOS patients (41.5%) compared to five controls (7.7%) (p < 0.001).
While individual AGDant and AGDpost measurements did not show statistically significant differences, both were observed to be longer in the PCOS group (Table 1). A notable finding was the mean Ratioant/post, which was 4.4 ± 1.0 for the PCOS group and 4.9 ± 1.0 for the control group, a statistically significant difference (p = 0.003). The predictive capability of Ratioant/post for PCOS was assessed using ROC analysis, yielding an AUC of 0.644 (95% CI: 0.549-0.738, p = 0.005). A cut-off value of 4.5 for Ratioant/post was determined using the Youden index, demonstrating a sensitivity of 56.0% and specificity of 67.2% (Fig. 1). Further investigation revealed that Ratioant/post exhibited a significant positive correlation with WHR and a negative correlation with FAI (Table 4). Linear logistic regression analysis additionally identified these parameters as risk factors associated with Ratioant/post (Table 5).
Early detection of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a prevalent lifelong endocrine disorder, is crucial for improving patients' quality of life, a key motivation for this research. Diagnosing PCOS, especially in adolescents, presents significant challenges. Recent attention has focused on anogenital length measurement as a potential early indicator of PCOS in this demographic. Our study, therefore, examined anterior and posterior anogenital distance (AGD) in women with PCOS, contrasting them with healthy controls. Notably, we uniquely assessed the ratio of anterior to posterior AGD (Ratioant/post), identifying it as a novel and more effective biomarker than individual AGD measurements.
Our research found that AGD measurements were extended in individuals with PCOS, aligning with findings from other recent investigations. The established link between AGD length and intrauterine androgen exposure suggests that the prenatal environment, where PCOS is believed to originate, influences AGD. However, postnatal androgen exposure might also impact AGD during the antenatal phase. Prior research on AGD predominantly focused on the early neonatal stage. The challenge in standardizing and interpreting AGD measurements arises from the age-related and individual variability in anthropometric data among women.
PCOS diagnosis in young women presents challenges. Our study proposes using a standardized anogenital distance (AGD) ratio, specifically the AGDant to AGDpost ratio, as a diagnostic criterion. This ratio, a novel contribution to the literature, is expected to mitigate variations from individual anthropometric measurements, serving as a predictor for PCOS.
Genetic factors significantly contribute to PCOS, accounting for over 70% of its development. This is supported by observations that adolescent PCOS patients often have a mother with PCOS or a father with metabolic syndrome. The higher incidence of PCOS (20-40% greater) among mothers and sisters of affected women, compared to the general population, further suggests a genetic predisposition. Maternal PCOS is recognized as a risk factor, and twin studies also point to genetic involvement in the condition's origin. Consequently, when assessing perineal length measurements, it's crucial to consider the potential influence of genetic background and ethnic origin.
The anogenital distance (AGD) has been observed to be roughly twice as long in males compared to females, a characteristic historically used for sex determination. Studies have indicated a link between elevated testosterone (T) levels and longer AGD, potentially signaling a hyperandrogenic state in Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) patients, as noted by Wu et al. Furthermore, experimental evidence shows that prenatal T exposure can lead to virilization of female genitalia in rhesus monkeys and sheep. Similarly, administering T propionate during gestation has been demonstrated to lengthen AGD in female rats.
This study acknowledges several limitations. Potential biases related to measurement, selection, and other forms of prejudice should be considered. While the control group was drawn from the same hospital and population as the PCOS patients, ensuring some consistency, the study design itself presents challenges. Specifically, as a case-control study, it cannot definitively rule out the influence of other factors, such as elevated androgen levels during critical developmental stages like childhood or adolescence, which could also lead to longer anogenital distances. Furthermore, while AGD measurements were taken by a gynecologist blinded to the patients' PCOS status, any misclassification of PCOS could lead to an underestimation of the actual association.
PCOS is characterized by hyperandrogenism. While both anterior and posterior anogenital distances (AGDant, AGDpost) are extended in PCOS patients compared to controls, their ratio (AGDant/post) serves as the most robust predictor. This AGD ratio also shows strong correlations with the free androgen index (FAI) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), suggesting its utility as a diagnostic and standardization biomarker for PCOS.
