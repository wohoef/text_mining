This retrospective cohort study investigated trends in computed tomography (CT) scan utilization in Taiwan between 2000 and 2013. Data from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) was analyzed to determine the number of CT scans, their annual growth rate, and cumulative fold change ratio across various demographic and health categories. These categories included different age groups, sexes, and disease classifications (catastrophic illness vs. noncatastrophic illness). Overall, CT scan usage in Taiwan saw a substantial increase, rising 2.5-fold from 24,257 scans per million people in 2000 to 60,351 in 2013, with an average annual growth rate of 7.4% ± 5.9%. While males consistently showed a significantly higher annual number of CT scans across all age and disease groups, females exhibited slightly higher average annual growth rates and cumulative fold change ratios. The highest proportion of CT scans occurred in the middle-aged and young adult populations. Specifically, CT scan usage in young adults, children/adolescents, and middle-aged individuals increased by 3.7, 3.5, and 2.7 times, respectively, during the study period. Conversely, the old-age group experienced a decrease in CT scan use, showing a 0.8-fold change. Regarding disease categories, the noncatastrophic illness group accounted for the most CT scans, followed by the catastrophic illness cancer group and then the catastrophic illness others group. The study concluded that CT scan utilization in Taiwan steadily increased from 2000 to 2013, even among populations with elevated risks of radiation-induced cancer. Consequently, the authors recommend that healthcare professionals and policymakers carefully consider the diagnostic advantages against the specific risks associated with age and sex when making future decisions about CT scan use.

CT scans, a powerful X-ray imaging technique, enable cross-sectional examination of internal organs, bones, soft tissues, and blood vessels. Introduced in the 1970s, this technology has significantly enhanced healthcare quality by facilitating diagnosis, effective treatment, and patient management. Advancements in CT imaging have refined diagnostic accuracy for injuries and diseases. Its utility extends to crucial roles in cancer staging, therapy planning, and monitoring treatment efficacy. Increased CT scan usage has been linked to earlier disease detection, shorter hospital stays, and reduced healthcare expenditures. Consequently, developed nations have witnessed a substantial rise in CT scan utilization over the last two decades.
Computed tomography (CT) scans utilize x-ray beams, which are absorbed differently by various bodily structures, allowing for their distinction and diagnosis. This technology is a significant contributor to medical radiation exposure, delivering higher doses than other imaging methods. When biological tissues are exposed to these x-rays, hydroxyl radicals are typically formed. These radicals can then damage DNA, causing strand breaks or base alterations. While most radiation-induced damage is quickly repaired, DNA double-strand breaks are more challenging to fix and can occasionally result in point mutations, chromosomal translocations, and gene fusions—all factors implicated in cancer development. Despite these risks, the lifetime chance of developing a secondary cancer from a CT scan is generally considered low and acceptable, especially when weighed against the substantial diagnostic advantages. However, the widespread use of CT scans means that even a small risk of cancer across a large population could lead to a considerable number of cases. To mitigate concerns regarding radiation dose and unnecessary scans, continuous improvements have been made to CT equipment and technology. These advancements include the development of solid-state detectors, iterative reconstruction techniques, automatic exposure control, and refined collimator designs.
The National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) in Taiwan serves as a comprehensive repository for demographic and medical information for nearly the entire Taiwanese population, which numbers approximately 23 million. This extensive dataset is a direct result of Taiwan's government-operated, single-payer National Health Insurance (NHI) program, established in 1995, boasting over 99% coverage. Unlike many other healthcare databases that focus on specific populations or samples, the NHIRD's unique strength lies in its broad and representative scope. It meticulously records details such as birth dates, gender, income-based insurance amounts, residential areas, and a wealth of clinical data, including diagnoses, medical examinations, prescriptions, and surgical procedures. This makes the NHIRD an invaluable tool for researchers, allowing for the analysis of CT scan utilization trends across various age, gender, and disease categories by examining metrics like the number of scans, annual growth rates, and cumulative fold change ratios.
The National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) offers numerous benefits for researchers, extending beyond its accessible data. It compiles extensive computerized records for nearly all of Taiwan's population, spanning two decades. This comprehensive coverage, exceeding 99%, empowers researchers to test hypotheses across a vast, national cohort. The NHIRD's continuous data collection and annual updates allow investigators to trace beneficiary information from 1995 up to a year prior to study approval. These features significantly boost statistical power, particularly for studies involving rare diseases or uncommon outcomes. Furthermore, the database meticulously records every billing order, detailing service type, anonymized physician information, product brand, prescription dates, and costs. This granular data allows researchers to ascertain the location, timing, expenditure, frequency, and duration of patient interventions.
A representative subset of data from the NHIRD was used to create the Longitudinal Health Insurance Database, primarily for the ease of researchers. This database contains detailed information for one million individuals, randomly selected from all beneficiaries. Its representativeness is confirmed by its close resemblance to the full NHIRD population across age, sex, and healthcare cost distributions. Recognizing that diagnostic codes within the NHIRD are primarily for billing and might lack sufficient accuracy, diseases and outcomes were meticulously defined. To address this limitation, when necessary, the Registry for Catastrophic Illness Patient Database was consulted to establish dependable definitions. Access to this registry, which exempts enrollees from co-payments, is granted only upon meeting specific criteria. The current research was a retrospective analysis utilizing data from the Taiwan NHIRD.
Data from the NHIRD, spanning 2000 to 2013, was gathered to analyze CT scan trends. The study calculated three key metrics: the yearly CT scan usage per million people, its annual growth rate (representing year-over-year change), and the cumulative fold change ratio (comparing annual usage from 2001-2013 against 2000 levels).
The study categorized CT scan usage by analyzing data from the NHIRD across several demographic and health classifications. The entire population was initially segmented by sex into male and female cohorts. Further divisions were made based on age, creating four distinct groups: individuals under 20 years (child/adolescent), those aged 21 to 45 years (young adult), those between 46 and 70 years (middle age), and those over 71 years (old-age). Additionally, the population was grouped by catastrophic illness status: a noncatastrophic illness group (group 1) and two catastrophic illness groups (cancer-related, group 2; and other catastrophic illnesses, group 3). Each of these three illness categories was then further broken down by sex and age.
To receive a catastrophic illness certificate, a patient diagnosed by a physician with a condition meeting Department of Health guidelines for catastrophic or rare diseases must submit an application with supporting information. This designation signifies an illness demanding extensive hospitalization, costly treatments, or other care that would financially exhaust a family without specialized medical insurance. Our analysis then examined variations in the number, annual growth rate, and cumulative fold change ratio across the entire population and the three defined disease categories.
A paired-sample, two-tailed t-test (p < 0.05) determined significant differences in mean annual growth rates between male and female groups.
Data from the NHIRD, presented in Tables 1 and 2, illustrate the trends in CT scan utilization and its annual growth rate in Taiwan between 2000 and 2013. Over this period, the annual number of CT scans per million people saw a substantial increase, climbing from 24,257 in 2000 to 60,351 in 2013, representing an average yearly growth of 7.4% ± 5.9%. A consistent pattern emerged across all age categories, where males exhibited a significantly higher annual number of CT scans per million people compared to females (p < 0.001 for overall and each age group). Examining age-specific growth rates, the child/adolescent, young adult, and middle-age groups experienced average annual increases of 10.3% ± 7.5%, 10.9% ± 6.9%, and 8.2% ± 5.6%, respectively. In contrast, the old-age group showed a peak in CT scan use per million people in 2004, followed by a decline to 2013, resulting in an average annual decrease of −1.3% ± 6.6%. While females generally had slightly higher average annual growth rates across all four age groups, this difference was statistically significant only within the middle-age group (8.8% ± 5.5% for females versus 7.6% ± 5.9% for males, p = 0.01). Further analysis by disease category revealed that males consistently had a significantly higher annual number of CT scans per million people than females (p < 0.001). The average annual growth rates for CT scan use in the noncatastrophic illness group (Group 1), catastrophic illness cancer group (Group 2), and catastrophic illness others group (Group 3) were 6.4% ± 6.8%, 9.5% ± 5.2%, and 9.4% ± 8.1%, respectively. Although females in these three disease groups also displayed slightly higher average annual growth rates, these differences were not statistically significant.
Within the noncatastrophic illness group (disease group 1), the highest annual CT scan utilization per million people was observed in the middle-aged population. This was followed by young adults, then the elderly, with the child/adolescent group showing the lowest usage. Regarding growth rates, the child/adolescent group exhibited the highest average annual increase in CT scan uses (10.5% ± 7.9%), while the old-age group experienced a decline (−1.6% ± 6.9%). Young adults had a growth rate of 9.2% ± 7.9%, and the middle-aged group saw a 6.9% ± 6.6% increase. Although females generally had slightly higher average annual growth rates, this difference was only statistically significant for the middle-aged group (7.7% ± 7.5% for females vs. 6.1% ± 5.9% for males, p = 0.04).
Among patients with catastrophic illnesses, the middle-aged population exhibited the highest annual CT scan utilization per million people. Following them were young adults, then the elderly, and finally children/adolescents. Across all age categories within these two catastrophic illness groups, males consistently showed significantly higher annual CT scan usage per million people compared to females (p < 0.01), with one exception: in disease group 3, this gender disparity was not significant for individuals over 70 years old. Regarding the average annual growth rate of CT scan uses, the young adult group in disease group 2 experienced the most rapid increase at 16.2% ± 7.3%. This was succeeded by the child/adolescent group (10.8% ± 17.6%), the middle-aged group (9.9% ± 4.8%), and the old-age group, which saw a decline of -1.0% ± 7.7%. Similarly, in disease group 3, young adults led in growth rate at 12.9% ± 10.7%, followed by the middle-aged (11.2% ± 8.7%), child/adolescent (8.0% ± 11.2%), and old-age groups (1.4% ± 11.4%). Notably, no significant gender-based differences were observed in these growth rates within either catastrophic illness group.

Between 2000 and 2013, Taiwan experienced a significant increase in CT scan utilization, with the annual number of scans per million people climbing from 24,257 to 60,351. This represents an average annual growth rate of 7.4% ± 5.9%, a figure comparable to the 3.5% to 13.5% growth rates observed in over 20 American-European nations. The cumulative fold change in CT utilization in Taiwan over this period was 2.5. This rate of increase was similar to New Zealand (2.5) and the United Kingdom (2.3), and higher than Belgium (1.6), Spain (1.8), Hungary (1.9), and the United States (2.2). In 2013, Taiwan recorded 60.3 CT scans per 1,000 inhabitants annually. This was higher than Finland (31.0) and Slovenia (55.1), but considerably lower than many other American-European countries, which ranged from 70.3 to 240.3 examinations per 1,000 inhabitants. However, a crucial distinction lies in Taiwan's billing policy: a single reimbursed CT scan in Taiwan often encompasses multiple body parts or organs, whereas these would typically be counted as separate examinations in most other countries. Consequently, the actual frequency of CT scan usage in Taiwan is likely on par with that of other developed American-European nations, despite the seemingly lower reported numbers.
Between 2000 and 2013, CT scan utilization in Taiwan was predominantly observed in the middle-aged and young adult demographics. Specifically, the annual number of CT scans per million individuals in the middle-age group increased from 10,425 to 28,420, reflecting an average annual growth rate of 8.2% ± 5.6%. For young adults, this figure rose from 5,434 to 20,313, with an even higher average annual growth rate of 10.9% ± 6.9%. Despite having the lowest overall number of CT scans, the child/adolescent group experienced a significant increase, from 1,773 scans per million in 2000 to 6,139 in 2013, at an average annual rate of 10.3% ± 7.5%. In contrast, the old-age group saw a decline in annual CT scan uses per million people, dropping from 7,634 in 2004 to 5,479 in 2013, corresponding to an average annual decrease of 1.3% ± 6.6%. It is noteworthy that the rate of increase in CT scan frequency was higher among young adults and children/adolescents compared to the middle-aged and elderly populations. This trend is particularly concerning given that the biological effects of x-rays are stochastic, meaning the probability of harm increases with radiation dose. Younger individuals face a considerably elevated risk from radiation exposure due to their longer remaining lifespan, which provides more time for radiation-induced cancers to manifest. During this period, Taiwan's total population grew from 21.8 million to 23.3 million. Concurrently, the demographic composition shifted; young adults and children/adolescents, who constituted 42% and 31% of the total population in 2000, decreased to 39% and 22% by 2013, respectively. Conversely, the proportion of middle-aged and old-aged individuals increased from 21% and 5% to 31% and 8% over the same timeframe.
The observed decline in the average annual CT scan utilization rate solely within the old-age group was an intriguing finding. Future research could elucidate this trend by examining health status, healthcare practices, alternative imaging use, and national medical policies affecting this demographic.
Between 2000 and 2013, CT scan utilization consistently showed a higher number of scans per million people annually for males compared to females. However, females exhibited a significantly greater average annual growth rate (8.1% ± 6.6%) in CT scan use than males (6.9% ± 5.4%). This trend was also reflected in the cumulative fold change ratio, which was higher for females (2.7) than for males (2.3). Examining specific age groups, females generally had slightly higher cumulative fold change ratios in young adult, middle age, and old-age categories, while the ratio was comparable for both sexes in the child/adolescent group. Given the established elevated cancer risk from radiation exposure in women, the notable increase in CT scan usage among females warrants increased scrutiny.
Between 2000 and 2013, most CT scans were performed within the noncatastrophic illness group (disease group 1). The annual number of CT scans per million people in this group rose from 17,040 in 2000 to 37,415 in 2013, reflecting an average annual growth rate of 6.4% ± 6.8%, similar to the overall population's rate. Within disease group 1, the child/adolescent group exhibited the highest average annual growth rate (10.5% ± 7.9%) and cumulative fold change ratio (3.57) for CT scan utilization. Following them were young adults (9.2% ± 7.9% average annual rate, 3.05 cumulative fold change), then the middle-aged group (6.9% ± 6.6% average annual rate, 2.32 cumulative fold change). Conversely, the old-age group experienced a decline, with an average annual rate of -1.6% ± 6.9% and a cumulative fold change of 0.79. Across all age categories in disease group 1, males consistently showed a significantly higher annual number of CT scan uses per million people compared to females. While females generally had a higher average annual growth rate, this gender difference was statistically significant only in the middle-aged group (7.7% ± 7.5% for females vs. 6.1% ± 5.9% for males; p < 0.05). Given the elevated radiation exposure from CT scans compared to other imaging methods, the increasing use of CT in younger and female patients with noncatastrophic illnesses in Taiwan raises significant concerns regarding cancer risk.
Between 2000 and 2013, the proportion of Taiwan's population certified with a catastrophic illness rose from 2.6% to 4.7%. Despite constituting a smaller portion of the overall population, individuals with catastrophic illnesses exhibited a significantly higher frequency of CT scan utilization, approximately ten times greater than those without such illnesses. Overall, CT scans for catastrophic illness patients (groups 2 and 3 combined) represented about one-third of all CT scans conducted during this period, specifically ranging from 30% to 38% of the total. In 2000, the annual number of CT scans per million people for catastrophic illness cancer (group 2) was 5261, and for other catastrophic illnesses (group 3) it was 1956. By 2013, these figures had increased to 16,833 and 6103, respectively. While middle-aged patients with catastrophic illnesses showed the highest absolute number of CT scans, increasing from 2913 (group 2) and 815 (group 3) in 2000 to 9786 and 3118 in 2013, the average annual growth rates for these groups were 9.9% ± 4.8% and 11.2% ± 8.7%. However, young adults with catastrophic illnesses, despite having fewer total scans initially (795 for group 2 and 426 for group 3 in 2000), experienced a much more rapid increase in CT scan use, reaching 5484 and 1963 scans per million people by 2013. Their average annual growth rates were substantially higher at 16.2% ± 7.3% for group 2 and 12.9% ± 10.7% for group 3, surpassing those of the middle-aged group. This accelerated growth in young adults was also reflected in their cumulative fold change ratios from 2000 to 2013, which were 6.9 for group 2 and 4.6 for group 3, significantly higher than the middle-aged group's ratios of 3.4 and 3.8. The rise in CT scan utilization for catastrophic illness cancer patients (group 2) is likely linked to a 1.5-fold increase in newly diagnosed cancer cases in Taiwan between 2004 and 2013, alongside advancements in cancer treatment medications. Furthermore, the National Health Insurance (NHI) began covering targeted cancer therapies in 2004, which often necessitate frequent medical imaging for treatment efficacy monitoring. Given the heightened lifetime risk of radiation-induced secondary cancers, particular attention is warranted for the increasing rates of CT utilization among young adults, children, and adolescents, many of whom may have already undergone multiple CT scans and accumulated significant radiation exposure.
This study offers several key advantages. It utilized comprehensive, nationwide billing data from Taiwan's single-payer system, covering over 99% of the population. This provided actual CT scan usage figures, avoiding the biases of self-reported surveys or literature-derived data. Furthermore, the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) allowed for continuous and retrospective data collection from 2000 to 2013, enabling detailed analysis of CT scan use across different age groups, genders, and disease categories. However, the study also has limitations. The number of CT scan uses might be underestimated because a single patient visit for a CT scan in Taiwan can encompass multiple examinations, body parts, or organs. Similarly, precisely determining the anatomical area scanned or the exact radiation exposure level was not possible due to the common practice of including multiple body parts or organs in a single CT examination. Lastly, data from health examinations or screening studies were not included, as these are outside the scope of the NHIRD.
This retrospective cohort study, utilizing data from Taiwan's NHIRD, analyzed CT scan usage trends from 2000 to 2013, examining variations across sex, age, and disease categories. A continuous increase in CT scan utilization was observed in Taiwan during this period, with a cumulative fold change ratio of 2.5. A significant concern arises from the elevated radiation-related cancer risk, indicated by this ratio, being higher in females, young adults, and children/adolescents. The rising use of CT scans among healthy individuals is also problematic, as even a small lifetime risk of radiation-induced secondary cancer, when applied to a growing number of exposed individuals, particularly young and female patients, could lead to numerous cancer cases in Taiwan. Consequently, medical professionals, including clinicians and radiologists, alongside policymakers, must address the escalating CT utilization rates. They need to carefully balance the diagnostic advantages against the potential harms. Both healthcare providers and patients should be aware of the cumulative radiation-related cancer risk associated with repeated CT scans, especially for younger individuals. The increasing rates and cumulative fold changes in CT use among young adults, adolescents, and children are particularly worrying due to their potential for multiple exposures. To mitigate future adverse health outcomes and optimize healthcare resources, decisions regarding imaging modalities for each patient should meticulously consider sex-specific and age-specific radiation risks, alongside diagnostic benefits.
