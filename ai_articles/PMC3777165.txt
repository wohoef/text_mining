This study investigated the expression and cellular location of Yes Associated Protein (YAP) in 237 cases of human invasive breast cancer using immunohistochemistry. The findings were then correlated with various clinicopathological characteristics and E-cadherin expression. YAP, a transcriptional coactivator, plays a role in regulating organ size by influencing cell proliferation and survival. Its activity is mediated by interactions with TEAD transcription factors within the nucleus. However, phosphorylation can inhibit YAP's transcriptional functions by causing its relocation to the cytosol. Elevated YAP levels have been observed in several cancer types, including those affecting the lung, skin, prostate, ovary, and liver. More recently, a connection was established between YAP and E-cadherin-dependent contact inhibition in breast cancer cells. A key observation from this research was that invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) exhibited significantly higher levels of both nuclear and cytosolic YAP (p < 0.001). Furthermore, both high nuclear and cytosolic YAP expression were strongly linked to the E-cadherin deficient ILC subtype (p < 0.001). This association was also confirmed in human breast cancer cell lines and conditional mouse models of human lobular breast cancer. Interestingly, nuclear YAP expression did not show a correlation with other factors such as lymph node involvement, tumor grade, tumor size, mitotic activity, or the molecular subtypes of invasive breast cancer. The prevalence of nuclear YAP localization in breast cancers lacking functional adherens junctions suggests a potential role for YAP-mediated transcription in the development and progression of invasive lobular breast cancer.

The ability of tumor cells to invade and spread to new tissues significantly impacts breast cancer prognosis, and this process is associated with impaired cell-cell adhesion. E-cadherin, a tumor suppressor and vital part of the adherens junction (AJ), is commonly used in breast cancer to differentiate between invasive ductal (IDC) and invasive lobular breast cancer (ILC). While ILC development is causally linked to E-cadherin mutations, IDC typically retains E-cadherin expression. In IDC, AJ inactivation, often through epigenetic mechanisms, occurs later in disease progression, potentially leading to epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tumor advancement. However, the specific molecular events that are dysregulated downstream of E-cadherin loss and contribute to metastasis remain poorly understood.
The transcriptional coactivator Yorkie, initially discovered in Drosophila, plays a crucial role in controlling cell proliferation and apoptosis [8, 9]. This protein is a downstream effector of the Hippo pathway, which includes the kinase complexes Hippo-Salvador and Warts-Mats [9–11]. Yorkie's activity is suppressed by phosphorylation, specifically at Serine 168 in Drosophila, mediated by these Hippo pathway kinases [12]. Conversely, dephosphorylation of Yorkie leads to its nuclear translocation, where it then activates transcription to control organ growth [13, 14]. Humans possess two homologues of Yorkie: YAP (Yes Associated Protein) and TAZ (transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif, also known as WWTR1) [15]. YAP itself has two splice variants, YAP1 and YAP2, distinguished by having one or two dual WW domains, respectively [16]. While the core components of the Hippo pathway are conserved across mammals, the specific regulation of YAP signaling varies by tissue. For instance, in mouse liver, Mst1/2 (the Drosophila Hippo orthologue) inhibits YAP activity [12]. However, in mammalian skin, neither Mst1/2 nor Lats1/2 (Drosophila Hippo and Warts, respectively) affect YAP signaling [13]. Instead, the adherens junction (AJ) component αE-catenin regulates YAP activity in this context. αE-catenin binds to phosphorylated YAP (at Serine 127 in humans) through the 14-3-3 adaptor protein, thereby preventing YAP from interacting with and being dephosphorylated by the phosphatase PP2A [13]. When the AJ complex is disrupted, YAP can be activated by PP2A, leading to its translocation into the nucleus and subsequent transcriptional activation [13].
The precise regulatory mechanisms governing YAP in breast tissue remain largely unexplored. While YAP's regulation in breast tissue appears distinct from its orthologue Yorkie, the specifics of YAP phosphorylation and its impact on activity are still debated [17–22]. Studies in breast cancer cell lines have shown that E-cadherin influences contact-inhibited proliferation by controlling YAP activation [18, 23]. This process involves α-catenin and αE-catenin-mediated phosphorylation of YAP at serine 127 (S112 in mice), leading to its relocation into the cytosol, which inhibits cell proliferation in a density-dependent manner [18, 23]. Furthermore, the actin and microtubule cytoskeleton may also play a role in YAP regulation, though the exact mechanism is not fully understood [17, 20, 22].
YAP signaling appears to play a complex role in breast cancer, with evidence suggesting both oncogenic and tumor suppressive functions. On one hand, several findings point to an oncogenic role: YAP's gene locus was amplified in a mouse mammary tumor model, and cytosolic YAP was overexpressed in nearly a third of human luminal ductal breast cancers. Furthermore, elevated YAP expression in E-cadherin positive human breast cancer cell lines led to their transformation, and YAP/TAZ-driven transcription was associated with maintaining a cancer stem cell phenotype. Conversely, YAP has also been implicated as a tumor suppressor. Its reduced expression correlated with ERα and PR negativity in breast cancer. Additionally, loss of heterozygosity at the YAP gene locus (11q22.2) was common in sporadic breast cancer, and YAP loss was linked to increased invasiveness and resistance to anoikis.
This study investigated YAP's role in breast cancer by examining its expression and localization in 237 human invasive breast cancer samples, correlating these findings with clinicopathological features and E-cadherin levels. Existing research points to YAP's involvement in breast cancer and suggests regulation by adherens junctions (AJ) and their associated components. Our findings reveal a strong association between elevated nuclear and cytosolic YAP expression and the E-cadherin deficient invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) subtype. Further validation using human breast cancer cell lines and mouse models of ILC confirmed that nuclear YAP localization is directly linked to the absence of E-cadherin. Consequently, nuclear YAP appears to be a characteristic of invasive breast cancers with compromised AJ function, implying a significant role for YAP signaling in ILC development.
This study utilized 237 samples of invasive breast cancer, collected from patients who underwent surgery between 2003 and 2007. These samples were obtained from the pathology archives of the University Medical Center Utrecht and the Radboud University Nijmegen Medical Centre in the Netherlands. The clinicopathological details of these cases are summarized in Table 1. Histological grading followed the Nottingham scheme, while the mitotic activity index (MAI) was determined using previously established methods.
Tissue microarrays were created from paraffin blocks of primary tumors. A pathologist identified tumor regions on haematoxylin-eosin stained slides, and three 0.6 mm tissue cylinders were extracted from each tumor using a Beecher Instruments tissue arrayer. Normal breast tissue, confirmed to be tumor-free, was sourced from mammoplasty patients. In accordance with Dutch legislation and patient treatment contracts, the use of anonymized or coded residual material for research does not necessitate ethical approval.
For immunohistochemical analysis, 4 μm thick tissue sections underwent preparation, beginning with deparaffinization and rehydration. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then inhibited for 15 minutes using a buffer solution (46 mM citric acid, 100 mM sodium phosphate, pH 5.8) containing 0.3% hydrogen peroxide. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling in either 10 mM citrate (pH 6.0) for PR and YAP, or Tris/EDTA (pH 9.0) for E-cadherin, ERα, and HER2, each for 20 minutes. A 30-minute cooling period followed before the application of primary antibodies. Primary antibodies, including E-cadherin (1:200), ERα (1:80), PR (1:25), and HER2 (1:100), were diluted in PBS with 2% BSA and incubated for one hour at room temperature. The YAP primary antibody (1:50) was incubated overnight at 4°C. Signal amplification was achieved using Powervision poly-HRP anti-mouse, -rabbit, -rat. Finally, sections were counterstained with haematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.
Two independent observers, unaware of patient details or other staining outcomes, performed all scoring. Tumors were categorized into luminal, HER2-driven, or basal-like/triple-negative subtypes using immunohistochemistry for ER, PR, and HER2, aligning with the Sorlie/Perou classification. E-cadherin expression was evaluated for membranous staining using the DAKO/HER2 system, with scores of 1+, 2+, and 3+ deemed positive, except for HER2, where only 3+ was considered positive. Nuclear YAP staining was quantified by estimating the percentage of positive tumor nuclei, with samples exceeding 20% considered positive. Cytosolic YAP intensity was semi-quantitatively graded from 0 to 3, with scores of 2 and 3 indicating high expression.
To determine statistical significance, P-values below 0.05 were used. Pearson’s Chi-square test analyzed associations between categorical variables. SPSS Statistics version 18.0 facilitated these analyses.
The cell lines used in this study were maintained under specific culture conditions. All cell lines were grown in DMEM-F12 (Sigma) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 IU/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and kept at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified environment. The mouse cell lines, Trp53∆/∆-4, Trp53∆/∆-7, mILC-1, and mILC-3, were previously characterized [41]. The ILC cell line IPH-926 was provided by Dr. M. Christgen of Hannover Medical School and cultured as detailed elsewhere [42]. Human breast cancer cell lines MCF10A, T47D, and SKBR-3 were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), with T47D and SKBR-3 also being a gift from Dr. J. Martens at Erasmus Medical Center. All cell lines underwent validation through Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling.
Proteins were extracted from samples by lysing them in a buffer containing Tris-HCl, β-mercaptoethanol, SDS, bromophenolblue, and glycerol. Following a 10-minute heat treatment at 100°C, the proteins were separated using established PAGE and blotting methods.
For western blotting, cells were prepared by first growing them to confluence. They were then washed with PBS containing Mg2+ and Ca2+ (PBS+), scraped from the plate, and suspended in Buffer A, which consisted of 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl, and freshly added 1 mM DTT, 0.5 mM PMSF, 5 μg/ml leupeptin, and 5 μg/ml aprotinin. To isolate cellular fractions, the cells underwent a series of centrifugations and resuspensions. Initially, they were centrifuged at 400 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, then resuspended in 1 ml PBS+ and centrifuged again. This was followed by two more resuspensions in Buffer A and centrifugations. After incubation on ice for 10 minutes, the cells were centrifuged and resuspended in Buffer A before being mechanically lysed using a glass 2 mL-douncer with 50–75 strokes. The resulting cell lysates were centrifuged at 500 g for 10 minutes at 4 °C to separate the nuclear fraction (pellet) from the cytosolic fraction (supernatant). Both fractions were subjected to an additional wash and centrifugation step (10 minutes, 500 g at 4 °C) before being used for western blotting. For protein detection, rabbit anti-TAF5 was employed as a nuclear marker, while goat anti-AKT (1:1000; cat sc-1618, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) served as a cytosolic marker. The primary antibody used to detect YAP was mouse anti-YAP (1:200; cat sz101199, Santa Cruz), referred to as YAP-IF.
Lentiviral particles, generated using third-generation packaging constructs and X-tremeGene9 transfected Cos-7 cells, were used for YAP knockdown via pLKO1-shYAP1. After 48 hours, viral supernatant was filtered, concentrated, and then used to transduce cells overnight with polybrene.
Immunofluorescence microscopy was employed to visualize cellular components. For cell lines cultured on glass coverslips, the procedure involved initial fixation with 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS on ice for 10 minutes. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized using 0.3% Triton-X100 in PBS and then blocked with 4% BSA in PBS. For these cell lines, a mouse anti-YAP antibody (YAP-IF, Santa Cruz, cat sz101199) was applied overnight at 4°C, diluted 1:50 in 4% BSA. This was followed by a 1-hour incubation with a goat anti-mouse Alexa-555 secondary antibody (Invitrogen, cat A21422) at a 1:600 dilution in 4% BSA. Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections underwent a similar blocking step with 4% BSA in PBS after antigen retrieval, consistent with immunohistochemistry protocols. These FFPE slides were incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit anti-YAP antibody (YAP-IHC, Cell Signaling, cat 4912) at a 1:50 dilution. Additionally, a mouse anti-p63 antibody (Neomarkers, MS-115-P) was applied at 1:400 for 1 hour at room temperature. E-cadherin was detected using directly conjugated antibodies (BD Biosciences, 612130 and 560062) at a 1:150 dilution for 1 to 3 hours at room temperature. All primary antibodies were prepared in 4% BSA/PBS. Secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit Alexa-555 (Invitrogen, cat A21428) and highly cross-adsorbed goat anti-mouse and goat anti-rabbit Alexa-488 and Alexa-568 (Invitrogen, cats A11029, A11031, A11036, A11034), were incubated for 1 hour in 4% BSA/PBS. DNA was counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, cat D1306) at a 1:1000 dilution for 5 minutes at room temperature. Finally, coverslips were mounted with Vectashield (Vector Laboratories) and imaged using either a Zeiss LSM 510 Meta or a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal laser microscope, both equipped with a 63X 1.4 objective.
Our investigation into normal human breast tissue revealed that YAP is primarily found in the myoepithelium, which forms the outer layer of the ducts (Fig. 1a and Online Resource Fig. 1). In these myoepithelial cells, immunofluorescence showed significant nuclear YAP staining, despite low levels of YAP in the cytosol (Fig. 1a, arrowheads and Online Resource Fig. 1). Conversely, luminal cells, which express E-cadherin, displayed minimal YAP expression, predominantly situated in the apical snouts (Fig. 1a, arrow and Online Resource Fig. 1). We then examined YAP expression and localization in mouse mammary epithelium. Similar to human tissue, mouse myoepithelial cells exhibited a more pronounced nuclear YAP localization (arrowheads). In contrast, mouse luminal mammary epithelial cells mainly showed cytoplasmic YAP expression (Fig. 1b). The specificity of the YAP-IF antibody employed in these studies was verified through shRNA-mediated knockdown experiments (Online Resource Fig. 2, middle panel). Figure 1 illustrates YAP expression in both human and mouse normal breast tissue. Specifically, Figure 1a shows immunofluorescence for YAP (red) and E-cadherin (green) in human tissue, with nuclei stained by DAPI. The merged image highlights the low cytosolic YAP in luminal epithelial cells with clear adherens junctions, and the prominent nuclear YAP in myoepithelial cells (arrowheads), as well as YAP in apical snouts (arrow). Figure 1b similarly presents immunofluorescence for YAP (red) and E-cadherin (green) in mouse mammary glands, with nuclear YAP in myoepithelial cells indicated by arrowheads.
To understand how YAP is expressed in invasive breast cancer, we conducted an immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis on a tissue microarray (TMA) comprising 237 invasive breast cancer samples. The clinical and pathological details of these tumors are provided in Table 1. Given YAP's role as a transcriptional coactivator, our scoring method focused on estimating the percentage of nuclei exhibiting YAP positivity. Figure 2A illustrates representative images of nuclear YAP staining, showing examples of 0% and over 20% positive nuclei. Additionally, because YAP moves between the nucleus and cytoplasm, we also evaluated the intensity of cytoplasmic YAP, as depicted in Figure 2b, which shows examples of low and high cytoplasmic YAP expression. It's important to note that a distinct antibody (YAP-IHC) was employed for this IHC analysis, and its specificity was verified through a YAP knockdown experiment (Online Resource Fig. 2, top panel). Our analysis of YAP expression patterns revealed a significant correlation between both high cytoplasmic YAP levels and nuclear YAP localization with the histological subtype of invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC). Specifically, ILC showed higher cytoplasmic YAP (p = 0.002) and nuclear YAP localization (p < 0.001), as detailed in Tables 2 and 3. While we primarily used a 20% cutoff for positive nuclear YAP staining, cutoffs of 5% and 10% also yielded statistically significant results (p < 0.001 for both). In contrast, neither cytoplasmic nor nuclear YAP expression demonstrated a correlation with histological grade, mitotic activity, or lymph node status (Tables 2 and 3). We also investigated the relationship between YAP expression and tumor size. Recognizing that ILC tumors were generally larger than IDC tumors, we adjusted for this difference. After correction, nuclear YAP expression did not show a correlation with tumor size (odds ratio 1.451, 95% confidence interval 0.790 to 2.667). However, high nuclear YAP localization maintained a significant correlation with ILC tumors (odds ratio 8.829, 95% confidence interval 4.208 to 18.523), as presented in Table 3. Contrary to previous research [28], our study did not find any association between nuclear or cytoplasmic YAP localization and hormone receptor status, HER2 expression, or molecular breast cancer subtypes (Online Resource Table 1 and Table 2). In summary, our findings indicate that elevated cytoplasmic YAP expression and increased nuclear YAP localization are characteristic features associated with ILC.
Our investigation explored the relationship between E-cadherin expression and YAP activity in invasive breast cancer samples, building on previous research that linked E-cadherin to YAP regulation in human breast cell lines [17, 18, 23]. We found a significant inverse correlation: both high cytoplasmic and high nuclear YAP expression were associated with lower E-cadherin expression (p = 0.024 and p < 0.001, respectively, as detailed in Table 4, Table 5, and Fig. 3). To further confirm this association, particularly the link between E-cadherin loss and nuclear YAP localization in Invasive Lobular Carcinoma (ILC), we examined YAP expression in a panel of breast cancer cell lines, including those with intact E-cadherin and those with E-cadherin mutations. Consistent with our findings in clinical samples, E-cadherin-positive human cell lines, such as T47D and MCF10A, primarily displayed cytosolic YAP staining (Fig 4a). In contrast, E-cadherin mutant cell lines, specifically SKBR-3 and the lobular breast cancer cell line IPH-926 [42, 45], exhibited predominantly nuclear YAP localization (Fig 4c). We extended these observations to mouse models. We utilized tumor cell lines derived from mammary tumors in conditional mouse models where E-cadherin and/or p53 inactivation drives tumor development. These models are known to produce invasive and metastatic tumors that closely resemble human ILC due to E-cadherin inactivation [4, 5]. Through immunofluorescence, we compared YAP localization in Trp53∆/∆ cell lines (E-cadherin positive, p53 negative) with mouse ILC (mILC) cell lines (E-cadherin and p53 negative). Similar to the human cell lines, mILC cells showed an increased presence of nuclear YAP. While both mILC and Trp53∆/∆ cells contained cytosolic YAP, the YAP expression in Trp53∆/∆ cell lines was predominantly cytoplasmic (Fig 4b and d). To provide biochemical evidence for the nuclear enrichment of YAP in E-cadherin mutant breast cancer, we performed nuclear fractionation on both human and mouse cell lines, followed by western blot analysis. Using TAF5 as a nuclear marker, we confirmed that E-cadherin negative cell lines from both species showed a clear enrichment of YAP in their nuclear fractions when compared to their E-cadherin-expressing counterparts (Online Resource Fig. 3). In summary, our data consistently demonstrate an inverse relationship between YAP localization and E-cadherin expression. Specifically, nuclear YAP is a distinguishing characteristic of E-cadherin negative ILC in both human and mouse contexts. This suggests a critical role for E-cadherin status in regulating YAP's subcellular distribution.
This research establishes a novel link between the cellular location of YAP (both nuclear and cytosolic) and invasive lobular breast cancer, specifically in cases lacking E-cadherin. Given YAP's role as a transcriptional coactivator that moves between the cytoplasm and nucleus, our investigation focused on both the intensity of cytoplasmic YAP expression and the count of YAP-positive nuclei. Our findings indicate that in E-cadherin negative breast cancer, YAP not only moves into the nucleus, but its overall levels also appear to increase. While we didn't directly assess the correlation between YAP expression levels and its activity, it's worth noting that YAP signaling has been shown to be influenced by degradation processes. The precise role of E-cadherin in regulating YAP degradation remains an open question. One potential explanation is that the absence of E-cadherin might hinder YAP phosphorylation and subsequent degradation via ubiquitin, thereby leading to elevated YAP levels. However, our observations in human breast cell lines showed that while membranous E-cadherin expression correlated with cytoplasmic YAP localization, total YAP protein levels didn't consistently align with E-cadherin status. This divergence could stem from inherent differences between in vitro cell line models and actual clinical breast cancer samples.
Our study found no statistically significant link between YAP expression/localization and overall survival in breast cancer patients, unlike some prior research. This absence of correlation in our cohort is likely due to YAP's strong association with Invasive Lobular Carcinoma (ILC). Survival rate differences between ILC and Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC) patients typically become apparent only when analyzing extensive patient groups over periods exceeding a decade.
The relationship between YAP's location or activity and E-cadherin-negative IDC remains unclear. Unlike ILC, where E-cadherin is inactivated early by mutation, IDC typically loses E-cadherin later in progression through epigenetic changes. Our study couldn't thoroughly investigate YAP localization in E-cadherin-negative IDC because nearly all our IDC samples (97%) still expressed membranous E-cadherin.
Nuclear YAP appears to be a key regulator of breast cancer invasiveness, regardless of the specific upstream signaling pathway. Our findings align with previous research indicating that stress fiber formation leads to decreased YAP phosphorylation and its subsequent nuclear translocation. While these studies didn't directly examine cell-cell adhesion, it's been established that E-cadherin-mediated contact inhibition, which curtails proliferation, can be controlled by Lats-dependent YAP phosphorylation. However, cell-type variations exist, as F-actin bundling, influenced by cell morphology, can also regulate YAP independently of Lats. A consistent factor in YAP's nuclear localization seems to be the activation of Rho-Rock-driven actin polymerization. This suggests that both cellular shape and cadherin-mediated cell-cell interactions might influence YAP's transcriptional activity in a tension-sensitive manner. Furthermore, YAP overexpression in E-cadherin-positive breast cancer cells can induce transformation, marked by epithelial characteristic loss, mesenchymal marker expression, and increased invasiveness and anchorage-independence.
In metastatic invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), the loss of E-cadherin, which disrupts cell junctions, activates the Rho-Rock pathway through p120-catenin and MRIP. This active Rock signaling, in turn, influences anchorage-independent tumor growth and metastasis. We propose that the nuclear translocation of YAP, which appears to be largely exclusive of membranous E-cadherin expression in both mouse and human breast cancer, is driven by actin. This is supported by the fact that several components of the Hippo pathway interact with actin, and F-actin has been shown to regulate YAP based on cell morphology. Our prior research also showed that p120 regulates anchorage independence via MRIP-dependent F-actin bundling, suggesting another regulatory mechanism. Further investigation is needed to determine if p120 connects actin polymerization to nuclear YAP expression in ILC and how these processes contribute to ILC's development and spread.
Our study reveals a significant correlation between the invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) breast cancer subtype and both nuclear YAP localization and overall YAP expression. ILC is known to be caused by the mutational inactivation of E-cadherin. This observation was further supported by experiments using human and mouse E-cadherin mutant cell lines, which exhibited increased nuclear YAP localization compared to cell lines expressing E-cadherin. We propose that the loss of E-cadherin and the subsequent disruption of the adherens junction complex lead to YAP's translocation into the nucleus. Once in the nucleus, YAP may initiate a transcriptional program that promotes the development and progression of ILC tumors. Therefore, our findings indicate that nuclear translocation of YAP is a direct result of early E-cadherin mutational inactivation and the subsequent activation of Rock-dependent actin polymerization, mediated by p120. Consequently, YAP and its associated target genes represent promising avenues for developing new therapeutic strategies for metastatic ILC.
