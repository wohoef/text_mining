Thumb basal joint arthritis, a prevalent and debilitating hand condition, is often treated surgically with ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition (LRTI). This retrospective study aimed to assess the long-term radiological and clinical effectiveness of a modified LRTI technique for this condition. The study included 91 hands from 84 patients, with complete medical records. Participants had an average age of 65.4 years and were followed for a mean of 11.7 years, with a range of 1 to 21.6 years. Functional outcomes were evaluated by assessing pain levels, joint stability, power-grip and key pinch strength, and the thumb's radial abduction angle. Radiographically, the height of the trapezial space was measured. The results indicated that 66 hands achieved excellent outcomes and 23 had good results. The reduction in trapezial space height was 4.2 mm for stage III arthritis and 5.2 mm for stage IV. Patients with stage III arthritis demonstrated superior power-grip and key pinch strength, as well as a greater thumb radial abduction angle, compared to those with stage IV. Importantly, out of 68 hands followed for over 5 years, 66 exhibited excellent or good outcomes, highlighting the procedure's durability. The study concluded that the modified LRTI procedure offers good and lasting surgical results, making it a viable treatment option for thumb basal joint arthritis.

Thumb basal joint arthritis, affecting the first carpometacarpal joint, is a prevalent and debilitating condition with a 10% lifetime prevalence. It predominantly impacts women over 45, including about one-third of postmenopausal women. While conservative treatments are effective for mild cases, advanced stages or failed conservative management necessitate surgical intervention. Despite numerous described surgical techniques, none has definitively proven superior. The ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition (LRTI) procedure, first detailed by Burton and Pellegrini in 1986, is the most frequently employed. This technique integrates volar ligament reconstruction principles from Eaton and Littler with space interposition arthroplasty as outlined by Froimson. Our study focused on a modified LRTI approach, incorporating a flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon reloop for enhanced suspension, to evaluate its long-term outcomes in treating thumb basal joint arthritis.
This retrospective study analyzed chart data, with informed consent waived due to its nature. Radiographic assessment of basal joint degeneration utilized the Eaton and Littler staging system. Between 1982 and 2004, 102 cases across 94 patients with stage III to IV thumb basal joint arthritis, treated with modified LRTI procedures, were evaluated. A single, highly experienced surgeon (Tang's level 4) performed all surgeries. For patients with bilateral involvement, procedures were staged at least one year apart.
Our surgical approach involved a modification of Burton's ligament reconstruction and interposition arthroplasty, following Wagner's technique. A skin incision of 2 to 3 cm was made, with careful protection of the dorsal cutaneous branch of the radial nerve and the radial artery. A longitudinal capsulotomy was then performed volar to the abductor pollicis longus (APL) tendon's attachment on the trapezium. Following subperiosteal dissection of soft tissue attachments, the trapezium was removed using a rongeur. It was crucial to avoid damaging the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon volarly. Additionally, any osteophytes impinging on the bases of the first and second metacarpals, or the trapezoid, were identified and removed as needed.
The ulnar half of the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon was severed at the musculotendinous junction in the mid-forearm. This tendon slip was then carefully dissected and retracted through several transverse incisions along the volar forearm. The resulting tendinous strip was directed into the trapezial space, extending distally towards the FCR's original insertion, serving as a distally-based graft. With the thumb pronated, a bony tunnel was created. This tunnel began approximately 5 mm distal and ulno-dorsal to the abductor pollicis longus (APL) tendon insertion, extending to the articular surface of the thumb metacarpal base. The tunnel's openings were then enlarged using progressively larger gouges. The harvested FCR tendon strip was threaded through this tunnel and interwoven with the APL tendon. While maintaining the thumb in a neutral position, gentle traction was applied, and the trapezial space was re-evaluated using fluoroscopy. The tendon graft was secured with 4-0 polypropylene (Prolene) sutures. Subsequently, the FCR tendon graft was routed back to the remaining native FCR tendon near its insertion and fixed under tension with 4-0 Prolene. This portion of the FCR tendon graft was then interwoven and interposed within the trapezial space. To stabilize the thumb metacarpal, it was transfixed to the scaphoid using a 1.6-mm K-wire. Following this, the incised trapezial space capsule was repaired, and the skin incision was closed. Finally, a forearm-based thumb spica splint was applied.
Sutures were removed 10-14 days post-operation. The K-wire and thumb spica splint were taken out at 4 weeks, at which point active thumb range of motion exercises began. The splint was then worn nightly for another 4 weeks. Muscle strengthening and motion exercises commenced at 8 weeks.
Following surgery, patients were seen every two weeks for the first month, then monthly for two additional months. Subsequent follow-ups occurred every six months, with additional appointments as needed. Radiographs were taken at each visit. A project investigator, independent of the patient's treatment, conducted all evaluations.
Surgical outcomes were assessed through subjective pain reports, clinical measurements, and radiographic findings. Pain frequency was categorized as none, minimal, occasional, or frequent. Clinical assessments encompassed power-grip strength, key pinch strength, and the thumb's radial abduction angle. Power-grip strength was measured using a Jamar dynamometer at the second setting, while key pinch strength was assessed with a Jamar pinch gauge, pressing it between the thumb and index finger pads. For both grip and pinch strength, each hand was tested three times, with the highest reading recorded. Radiographic evaluation involved measuring the change in trapezial space height, defined as the distance between the scaphoid's distal articular surface and the first metacarpal's proximal articular surface, using standard anteroposterior views. Overall outcomes were classified into four categories: excellent, good, fair, and poor. This classification was determined by the worst performance among three parameters: pain, joint stability, and key pinch strength.
The study included 91 hands from 84 patients (52 female, 32 male) with complete records and an average follow-up of 11.7 years (1-21.6 years). Patients' average age at surgery was 65.4 years (48-84 years). Of these, 42 hands had Eaton stage III arthritis, and 49 had stage IV.
The study found that 66 hands achieved excellent functional outcomes and 23 had good results, according to the classification in Table 1. The majority of patients reported satisfaction with pain relief. Functional measurements showed average power-grip strength of 32.4 Kg and key pinch strength of 6.9 Kg, representing 97.5% and 93.3% of the contralateral hands, respectively. The average thumb radial abduction angle was 69.8°, or 88.9% of the uninjured side. Radiographically, the trapezial space height diminished by an average of 4.7 mm. This diminution was 4.2 mm in stage III arthritis and 5.2 mm in stage IV.
A significant majority, 75% of cases (68 hands), were followed for over 5 years, averaging 15.8 years. Among these, excellent results were reported in 66.2% (45 hands), and good results in 30.9% (21 hands).
Two patients experienced superficial wound infections, which resolved with oral antibiotics. No other complications were noted.
Basal joint arthritis is common, especially in postmenopausal women. However, our study enrolled more men than women, likely because many patients at our hospital were male veterans.
The LRTI procedure, combined with trapeziectomy, stabilizes the first metacarpal using tendon grafting for ligament suspension. It also forms a new joint within the trapezial space via tendon graft interposition. This reconstructive method offers better functional outcomes compared to joint fusion, as supported by our study's findings of comparable pain relief and functional improvements to existing research. Crucially, our results offer further evidence of the procedure's long-term efficacy.
Various modified LRTI techniques exist, differing in tendon choice, bone tunnel use, fixation methods (sutures or anchors), and inclusion of hematoma distraction arthroplasty, all demonstrating good short-term outcomes. Our approach offers a simple modification to traditional LRTI, incorporating a looped tendon for enhanced ligament reconstruction and metacarpal suspension. This looped tendon also contributes to the interposition. Promising long-term results have been observed with this modified procedure.
Other surgical options like prosthesis arthroplasty or partial trapeziectomy with tendon interposition can preserve the scaphotrapezial joint for basal joint arthritis. However, if this joint also shows degeneration, functional outcomes may suffer. Therefore, when scaphotrapezial issues are a concern, LRTI presents a viable alternative.
Our findings indicated no correlation between proximal metacarpal migration and pinch strength, a conclusion supported by previous research. The observed decrease in trapezial space height in our study was comparable to, or even less than, that reported in other investigations. Furthermore, we did not find a link between reduced trapezial height and pinch or grip strength. This height reduction also showed no correlation with functional staging, patient age, or the severity of arthritis. The retrospective nature of our study, coupled with variations in uncontrolled factors such as work characteristics and follow-up duration, likely contributed to these nonsignificant results. Nevertheless, the LRTI procedure appears effective on its own, suggesting that concerns about significant proximal metacarpal migration may be minimal. While direct comparison of our height reduction data with other studies is challenging due to differing designs, we believe our modified looping technique offers at least neutral, and potentially improved, outcomes compared to standard direct interposition with a long FCR tendon graft.
Patients diagnosed with stage III thumb basal joint arthritis demonstrated superior power-grip strength, key pinch, and thumb abduction angles compared to those with stage IV arthritis. Radiographically, stage III patients also experienced less reduction in trapezial space height than stage IV patients. While formal correlation analysis was deemed inappropriate due to the study's retrospective design and extensive follow-up period, a discernible trend indicated that stage III patients tended to exhibit less trapezial space height diminishment and achieved better surgical outcomes. The authors suggest that more severe arthritis (stage IV) likely contributes to prolonged functional weakness and increased attenuation of surrounding ligaments and tendons. This could result in diminished functional recovery and greater trapezial space height loss in stage IV patients. Nevertheless, the study concluded that functional outcomes remained both good and satisfactory for patients with both stage III and stage IV arthritis following the modified LRTI procedure.
Two patients experienced fair surgical outcomes, both exceeding 80 years of age. Despite minimal proximal metacarpal migration (1 mm), their results were poor due to pain and inadequate key pinch strength. This may stem from post-immobilization stiffness and weakness, compounded by older patients' potentially limited rehabilitation capacity and motivation. Consequently, for such individuals, a less invasive approach with earlier rehabilitation might be a more appropriate treatment strategy.
Patients aged 40 to 80 with advanced basal joint arthritis consistently achieved excellent or good functional outcomes following the modified LRTI procedure. While trapeziometacarpal arthrodesis might be considered for younger individuals anticipating better strength recovery, it often results in reduced thumb motion, increased complications, and less functional improvement. Research by Spekreijse et al. indicates that trapeziectomy with LRTI offers superior pain reduction and functional outcomes compared to trapeziometacarpal arthrodesis in women over 40 with stages II to III basal joint osteoarthritis. Therefore, the LRTI procedure presents a reliable and long-lasting treatment option for individuals of various ages suffering from advanced basal joint arthritis.
This retrospective study has limitations, including a potentially insufficient sample size for robust statistical conclusions.
The modified LRTI procedure effectively treats thumb basal joint arthritis, offering good stability and functional outcomes even in younger patients. This approach provides durable results with a low complication rate.
