The impact of discontinuing synthetic human PTH 1-34 (hPTH 1-34) therapy on skeletal and metabolic parameters in hypoparathyroidism has been previously undocumented. This study investigated nine hypoparathyroid patients who received hPTH 1-34 via subcutaneous injections, two to three times daily, for durations ranging from 19.8 to 61.3 months. Following this treatment period, patients transitioned back to conventional calcium and calcitriol therapy. Assessments of biochemistry and bone mineral density (BMD) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) were conducted at three key stages: baseline (pre-treatment), during hPTH 1-34 treatment, and at a follow-up visit 3 to 12 months post-discontinuation. Initial attempts to abruptly cease hPTH 1-34 in two subjects led to hypocalcemia. Consequently, the discontinuation protocol was modified to a gradual weaning process over several weeks to prevent this complication. Upon complete cessation of hPTH 1-34, patients required two to three times their pre-treatment doses of calcitriol and calcium to maintain stable blood calcium levels. These elevated medication requirements were then progressively reduced over many weeks until calcium levels stabilized at doses comparable to baseline. Bone turnover markers showed significant changes: bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BSAP), N-telopeptide (NTX), and osteocalcin (OC) all increased during hPTH 1-34 therapy. At follow-up, BSAP and NTX levels reverted to baseline, while OC remained slightly elevated. BMD measurements also varied across different skeletal sites. During hPTH 1-34 treatment, BMD remained stable at the hip and lateral spine, but decreased at the anterior-posterior (AP) spine, radius, and total body. Interestingly, during the weaning phase, BMD increased. Specifically, hip and lateral spine BMD surpassed pre-hPTH 1-34 levels, and whole-body BMD returned to baseline. AP spine BMD also showed a non-significant increase compared to baseline at follow-up. These findings highlight the necessity of a gradual hPTH 1-34 weaning process in hypoparathyroid patients, accompanied by high doses of oral medications to prevent hypocalcemia. The transient increase in medication requirements and subsequent BMD gains after long-term hPTH 1-34 therapy suggest a reversal of the expanded remodeling space, favoring bone formation as the skeleton transitions back to a low-turnover state, a phenomenon akin to hungry bone syndrome. Further research and close patient monitoring are crucial to ensure a safe transition to conventional therapy and to fully understand the underlying physiological mechanisms.

Hypoparathyroidism, characterized by insufficient parathyroid hormone (PTH) production, can stem from genetic issues, autoimmune disorders, infiltrative diseases, or surgical complications in the neck. Individuals with this condition exhibit hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and either normal or elevated urinary calcium excretion. In adults, hypoparathyroidism leads to a low bone turnover state, evidenced by reduced bone turnover markers, decreased tetracycline uptake, and increased bone volume in iliac crest biopsies. Bone mineral density (BMD) assessments using DXA and QCT show inconsistent changes.
While hPTH 1-34 replacement therapy effectively manages hypocalcemia in hypoparathyroidism and induces a high bone turnover state, its long-term effects on bone are complex. Intermittent hPTH 1-34 treatment increases bone turnover markers and enhances both trabecular bone and cortical porosity, as observed in iliac crest bone biopsies. However, DXA measurements reveal inconsistent changes in bone mineral density (BMD), sometimes showing bone loss in the distal 1/3 radius. This suggests that hPTH 1-34 therapy impacts trabecular and cortical bone differently. Prior to this study, the consequences of discontinuing hPTH 1-34 on mineral metabolism, bone turnover, and BMD were unknown.
A study investigating the skeletal effects of 5-year hPTH 1-34 replacement therapy at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) enrolled nine hypoparathyroidism patients, who had experienced the condition for over a year. The cohort consisted of three men and six women, with an average age of 37 ± 13.3 years (Table 1). Exclusion criteria for participation included significant liver or kidney disease (eGFR < 25 mL/min/1.73 m2), chronic conditions impacting mineral metabolism, prior use of chronic steroids or bisphosphonates, active thyroid cancer, or pregnancy. All female participants were premenopausal when hPTH 1-34 therapy commenced. Previous publications detailed the mineral metabolism, bone density, and BMD outcomes for the initial 18 months of hPTH 1-34 therapy in four of these subjects. However, one subject from that prior report (subject #4) was excluded from the current analysis due to pregnancy during hPTH 1-34 discontinuation. Furthermore, data from only eight subjects are presented here, as one individual did not undergo hPTH 1-34 weaning. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR) Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol, and all adult participants provided written informed consent, while adolescents provided written assent, and their parents provided written informed consent.
After an initial period of optimized conventional treatment, calcitriol was stopped for all participants. Subsequently, subjects began receiving subcutaneous injections of synthetic human hPTH 1-34. Six subjects received these injections twice daily (BID), while three initially received them thrice daily (TID), starting the morning after their last calcitriol dose. The study's original intent was to compare BID and TID dosing, with participants randomly assigned to groups. However, a change in the principal investigator led to a shift in focus towards skeletal outcomes as the main endpoint, rather than comparing dosing regimens. Consequently, the study design was modified: subjects initially on TID PTH were transitioned to BID, and all new participants were started on BID injections. This meant only one subject was still on TID injections when hPTH 1-34 was discontinued, but this individual (subject #9) was excluded from the analysis because their hPTH 1-34 dose was not gradually reduced.
The NIH Pharmaceutical Development Service prepared and packaged the hPTH 1-34 lyophilized powder, which was acquired from Bachem, Inc. (Torrance, CA, USA), as previously detailed. Initial daily dosages averaged 0.45 ± 0.09 μg/kg for twice-daily (BID) subjects and 0.37 ± 0.17 μg/kg for thrice-daily (TID) subjects. These doses were quickly modified to keep serum calcium within the target range of 7.6 to 9 mg/dL (1.9 to 2.25 mmol/L), or slightly below the lower normal limit. Patients on hPTH 1-34 aimed for a daily calcium intake of 1000 to 2000 mg from diet or supplements, with additional supplementation for those with insufficient dietary intake. Vitamin D-deficient subjects received ergocalciferol or cholecalciferol to ensure 25-hydroxy vitamin D levels exceeded 62.5 nmol/L (25 ng/mL).
Participants underwent evaluations every six months at the NIH Clinical Center in Bethesda, MD. These assessments encompassed a range of measurements: routine blood and urine biochemistries, 25-hydroxy vitamin D levels, and specific bone turnover markers. Bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BSAP) was measured using an immunoenzymatic assay from Mayo Medical Labs, while osteocalcin (OC) was determined via electrochemiluminescence at the NIH Clinical Center. Additionally, 24-hour urine collagen cross-linked N-telopeptide (NTX) was analyzed using a Vitros ECi competitive assay from Mayo Medical Labs. Bone mineral density (BMD) was quantified using DXA (Hologic, Bedford, MA, USA) at six-month intervals during hPTH 1-34 treatment and again at a follow-up visit occurring 6 to 12 months after the start of hPTH 1-34 weaning. Precision testing for DXA showed low coefficients of variation: lumbar spine (1.1%), total body (1.1%), 1/3 distal radius (1.6%), femoral neck (1.8%), and total hip (0.9%). Daily spine phantom BMD values, assessed over six-month periods, consistently exhibited a coefficient of variation below 0.4%. To account for age and gender differences, Z-scores were computed using established normative databases for adolescents and the Hologic adult normative database. However, Z-scores were not calculated for the lateral spine due to the absence of normative data for men or children. Between scheduled NIH visits, subjects' local laboratories monitored blood and urine mineral metabolism according to protocol, allowing for therapy adjustments to keep serum calcium within the target range of 7.6 to 9 mg/dL (1.9 to 2.25 mmol/L).
The initial two subjects undergoing hPTH 1-34 discontinuation experienced an abrupt reduction of the drug over 2 to 3 days, following a 2 to 3-day period where calcitriol and calcium were reintroduced at their baseline doses while hPTH 1-34 remained unchanged. This rapid cessation led to severe symptomatic hypocalcemia. Subject #1 required reinitiation and gradual tapering of hPTH 1-34 along with high doses of calcium and calcitriol to manage this. Subject #9, however, was not restarted on hPTH 1-34 and was subsequently excluded from the analysis. This experience prompted a revision of the protocol for subsequent participants. To prevent hypocalcemia, calcitriol and calcium supplements were restarted at doses two to three times higher than baseline. Serum calcium levels were then closely monitored one to two times weekly. During this period, hPTH 1-34 was gradually reduced, with corresponding adjustments to calcium and calcitriol, to keep serum calcium within the desired range. After the final hPTH 1-34 dose, monitoring continued every 1 to 2 weeks, and calcium and calcitriol doses were adjusted until stable calcium levels were achieved. Once stable, calcium levels were checked every 1 to 3 months until the subjects' follow-up visit at NIH.
Data analysis involved mixed models repeated measures (PROC MIXED, SAS version 9.3) to assess outcomes across three distinct time points: baseline (pre-hPTH 1-34), initiation of hPTH 1-34 weaning, and follow-up. To ensure data normalization and consistent variance within treatments, bone turnover markers, calcium, and phosphorus levels underwent log-transformation, and their geometric means are reported with 95% confidence limits. For bone mineral density (BMD), both absolute BMD values and BMD Z-scores were analyzed, except for lateral spine Z-scores, which lacked normative data for adolescents or men. Treatment effects between these time points were compared using pairwise post hoc contrasts, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. If the overall repeated measures effect of the time point was not significant, Bonferroni corrections were applied to the pairwise contrasts, adjusting for multiple comparisons, and a stricter significance level of p < 0.0167 was used. All results are presented as mean ± SD unless specified otherwise.
Before starting hPTH 1-34 treatment, patients received standard therapy, which included an average daily dose of 0.69 ± 0.22 μg of oral calcitriol and 1425 ± 656 mg of elemental calcium. The hPTH 1-34 therapy lasted for an average of 46 ± 18 months before weaning began. While five participants completed the entire 5-year treatment as planned, four stopped early. One subject discontinued due to bone pain, and three others stopped because their radial BMD T-score or Z-score dropped below -2. In the two months leading up to weaning, the average calcium supplementation was 514 ± 565 mg/day, and no calcitriol was being administered. At the start of weaning, the average hPTH 1-34 dose was 0.54 ± 0.24 μg/kg/day.
The initial attempts to discontinue hPTH 1-34 therapy involved subjects #1 and #9. For these individuals, hPTH 1-34 was quickly reduced and stopped over a period of 2 to 3 days, while calcitriol and calcium were reintroduced at doses 1 to 2 times their pre-treatment levels. This abrupt discontinuation led to hypocalcemia in both subjects. Subject #9 developed a calcium level of 6.3 mg/dL three days post-discontinuation, which was promptly rectified by increasing calcium and calcitriol, without restarting hPTH 1-34. Subject #1 also experienced symptomatic hypocalcemia, with a serum calcium of 6.6 mg/dL, necessitating the reintroduction of hPTH 1-34 and a more gradual weaning process. Consequently, the study protocol was modified to ensure all subsequent patients underwent a slow, monitored weaning from hPTH 1-34, guided by frequent assessments and symptom presentation.
The weaning period for hPTH 1-34 therapy, excluding subject #9, averaged 60 ± 34 days. During this time, maintaining target serum calcium levels necessitated significantly higher doses of calcitriol and elemental calcium upon hPTH 1-34 discontinuation, specifically two to three times the pre-treatment amounts (calcitriol: 1.28 ± 0.78 μg/day; elemental calcium: 3737 ± 1391 mg/day). These elevated dosages were required for several weeks in some participants before being gradually lowered as calcium levels stabilized. Interestingly, subject #1 experienced acute hypercalcemia (14.7 mg/dL, 3.67 mmol/L) three months after stopping hPTH, despite regular monitoring and medication adjustments, indicating a rapid return to a low-turnover state. By the follow-up visit, the average calcitriol (0.67 ± 0.22 μg/day) and elemental calcium (2363 ± 1307 mg/day) doses for the group had returned to levels comparable to those before hPTH 1-34 treatment. No significant differences were observed in serum calcium and phosphorus measurements across baseline, the start of weaning, or follow-up.
Bone turnover markers, initially normal or low in hypoparathyroidism, significantly rose with hPTH 1-34 treatment, staying elevated throughout. Post-treatment, BSAP and NTX levels reverted to baseline. However, osteocalcin remained somewhat elevated, showing a statistically significant difference from its initial levels.
The impact of hPTH 1-34 treatment on bone mineral density (BMD), as measured by DXA, exhibited site-specific variations (Fig. 3). At the initiation of weaning, BMD and its corresponding Z-scores were markedly reduced from baseline in the anterior-posterior (AP) spine and radius. Conversely, no significant alterations were observed in the lateral spine, femoral neck, or total hip during this period. While total body absolute BMD showed a significant reduction, the change in its Z-score was not statistically meaningful. By the follow-up assessment, BMD and Z-scores had improved across all sites compared to the start of weaning, with the exception of the distal radius, where only the Z-score demonstrated an increase. These improvements were substantial, with the radius, AP spine, and whole body returning to baseline levels. Notably, the femoral neck, total hip, and lateral spine even surpassed their initial baseline values.
For hypoparathyroid patients receiving intermittent subcutaneous hPTH 1-34 therapy for 18 to 60 months, without calcitriol, abrupt discontinuation of hPTH 1-34 was not feasible. To prevent hypocalcemia and keep serum calcium within the desired range, a gradual reduction of hPTH 1-34 was necessary, accompanied by high doses of calcium and calcitriol. Even after hPTH 1-34 was fully stopped, patients needed significantly elevated maintenance doses of calcium and calcitriol for several weeks until their bone metabolism reverted to the baseline, low-turnover state observed before hPTH 1-34 treatment. During hPTH 1-34 therapy, bone turnover markers were substantially elevated. While BSAP and NTX levels normalized within 6 to 12 months post-weaning, osteocalcin remained slightly above pre-treatment levels. Follow-up DXA scans showed significant BMD increases, indicating that the combination of a slower decrease in bone formation relative to bone resorption and the high calcium and calcitriol doses during weaning led to increased calcium deposition in the skeleton.
The skeletal effects of parathyroid hormone (PTH) are complex and seemingly contradictory. In primary hyperparathyroidism, sustained high PTH levels promote osteoclastic bone resorption, resulting in an expanded remodeling space, elevated calcium levels, and osteoporosis. Analysis via backscatter electron microscopy reveals that hyperparathyroid bone exhibits a reduced mean age and mineralization density compared to healthy controls, indicative of heightened bone turnover, with these metrics normalizing upon hyperparathyroidism correction. Conversely, intermittent PTH administration for osteoporosis also boosts bone turnover, leading to a greater proportion of newer, less mineralized bone. However, unlike hyperparathyroidism, this intermittent use tends to be anabolic, promoting osteoblast progenitors, hindering osteoblast apoptosis, and enhancing bone density in the lumbar spine and hip, while decreasing it in the distal radius. Following discontinuation of PTH therapy for osteoporosis, bone mineral density gains in the spine and hip diminish, and radial BMD improves. In hypoparathyroidism, where PTH is absent, patients exhibit reduced bone formation rates and remodeling activation frequency. Over time, this leads to increased cancellous and cortical bone mass, despite hypocalcemia. While less extensively researched, the mineralization density of hypoparathyroid bone has been observed to be comparable to controls in some cases, suggesting that the increased bone mass in hypoparathyroidism stems from an overall increase in bone volume rather than enhanced mineralization.
Treatment of hypoparathyroidism with hPTH 1-34 and rhPTH 1-84 consistently leads to significant and sustained elevations in bone remodeling markers, often surpassing normal limits. However, the specific skeletal outcomes, as measured by iliac crest biopsies, DXA, and QCT, have varied based on the drug's dosage and administration schedule. Our small pilot study, involving five subjects on a year of full replacement therapy, revealed substantial increases in trabecular bone volume and intratrabecular tunneling, alongside increased cortical porosity. While lumbar spine BMD Z-scores remained stable, a decrease was observed at the distal radius. These results indicate that hPTH 1-34 replacement therapy exerts distinct effects on cortical and trabecular bone. Similar findings of increased trabecular bone, intratrabecular tunneling, and cortical porosity have been reported in uncontrolled studies using rhPTH 1-84. Conversely, a 6-month placebo-controlled rhPTH 1-84 study, which involved high doses and frequent hypercalcemia, showed a reduction in trabecular volume and a tendency towards increased cortical porosity. This study also noted a decrease in spinal areal BMD (aBMD) but an increase in spinal volumetric BMD (vBMD), further supporting the idea that PTH therapy has differential impacts on cortical and trabecular bone. In a 2013 placebo-controlled study spanning six months, Mannstadt and colleagues demonstrated that hypocalcemia could be effectively managed with adjustable rhPTH 1-84 doses, supplemented by calcitriol when necessary. Although this study did not focus on skeletal endpoints, it did highlight an increased incidence of hypocalcemic events following drug discontinuation. Specifically, within the first four weeks post-discontinuation, 31% of rhPTH 1-84-treated patients experienced hypocalcemic events (32 events), compared to only 9% of placebo-treated patients (four events).
The substantial need for calcium and calcitriol during the hPTH 1-34 tapering process in this study brings to mind the "hungry bone syndrome." This syndrome, characterized by prolonged hypocalcemia, occasionally manifests after parathyroid surgery in individuals with hyperparathyroidism. While its precise mechanism remains unclear, hungry bone syndrome post-parathyroidectomy is believed to stem from the sudden cessation of PTH-driven bone resorption, even as bone formation continues at an elevated rate. This leads to calcium being redirected into the skeleton, resulting in postoperative hypocalcemia, heightened requirements for calcium and vitamin D, and subsequent increases in bone mass. Factors associated with an increased risk of postoperative hungry bone syndrome include older age, the presence of large parathyroid adenomas, and pre-existing radiological signs of hyperparathyroid bone disease. Some, though not all, studies have also linked a higher risk to elevated preoperative levels of PTH, calcium, and alkaline phosphatase. The duration of hungry bone syndrome can vary, with some cases lasting up to nine months. In our study, despite hypoparathyroid subjects not experiencing hypercalcemia during hPTH 1-34 therapy, their bone turnover markers rose, and lumbar spine and forearm BMD declined. This pattern is consistent with a mixed hypoparathyroid/hyperparathyroid state. When hPTH 1-34 was abruptly stopped in two patients, severe hypocalcemia occurred, likely because bone resorption ceased while bone formation persisted. Consequently, a slow withdrawal of hPTH 1-34, accompanied by high doses of calcium and calcitriol, was necessary and led to increased BMD. It's worth noting that the return to a baseline low-turnover state can be sudden, as exemplified by subject #1, who experienced acute hypercalcemia despite close monitoring and medication adjustments.
Despite limitations like a small, diverse patient group, varied therapy durations, and no control group, the significant rise in calcium and calcitriol needed during weaning, alongside marked BMD increases post-treatment, strongly indicates a genuine effect of discontinuing PTH therapy in hypoparathyroidism.
This investigation marks the initial detailed examination of PTH therapy cessation in individuals with hypoparathyroidism. The substantial calcium and calcitriol dosages needed during hPTH 1-34 withdrawal, alongside the subsequent rise in bone mineral density (BMD), indicate a specific skeletal response. As the skeleton transitions from a high-turnover to a low-turnover state, bone formation outpaces bone resorption for several weeks to months following hPTH 1-34 discontinuation, even as the overall bone turnover decreases and remodeling space contracts. While rhPTH 1-84, recently FDA-approved for long-term hypoparathyroidism treatment, has not been specifically studied for this phenomenon, Mannstadt and colleagues did observe a higher rate of hypocalcemic events after its cessation. Considering the comparable impacts of PTH 1-34 and 1-84 on bone turnover markers, densitometry, and biopsies, it is plausible that rhPTH 1-84 discontinuation would also lead to this bone formation dominance and increased oral supplement requirements. Further research and careful patient monitoring are crucial to ensure a safe transition from PTH to standard therapy and to fully understand the underlying physiological mechanisms.
