This study investigates human exposure to airborne pollutants from desktop 3D printers in a typical small office setting. Drawing on recent measurements of ultrafine particle (UFP) and volatile organic compound (VOC) emission rates from various 3D printers and filaments, we modeled predicted pollutant levels across different office locations. Our findings indicate that certain 3D printer and filament combinations, particularly those with high emissions, can lead to hazardous concentrations of UFPs and VOCs. Specifically, in areas close (within 3 meters) or moderately close (3 to 18 meters) to these printers, UFP and VOC levels may surpass recommended exposure limits (RELs) for some VOCs and typical indoor concentrations for both pollutant types. For instance, caprolactam concentrations near printers using nylon-based filaments are projected to exceed both acute and chronic RELs established by the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment. UFP concentrations in close proximity to the highest-emitting printer and filament setups could reach up to 80,000 particles per cubic centimeter. Conversely, printer and filament combinations with low emission rates are not expected to produce concerning pollutant levels. To mitigate these risks, we also evaluated several control strategies. The most effective methods for reducing both UFP and VOC concentrations were found to be the installation of a high-flow spot ventilation system and operating the printer within a sealed enclosure equipped with high-efficiency gas and particle filtration.

Desktop 3D printers, particularly the more affordable models, commonly employ fused filament fabrication (FFF). This technique, where a thermoplastic filament is melted and extruded through a heated nozzle onto a moving baseplate, builds 3D objects layer by layer. The increasing use of 3D printers for prototyping and manufacturing spans industrial, educational, commercial, and residential environments, highlighting their growing popularity.
Several recent investigations have identified hazardous gas and particle emissions from numerous Fused Filament Fabrication (FFF) 3D printers and their associated filaments. These emissions include ultrafine particles (UFPs), defined as particles under 100 nanometers, released by many printer and filament pairings. Additionally, certain combinations emit dangerous volatile organic compounds (VOCs), such as caprolactam and styrene. The significance of these emissions is underscored by the known toxic effects of thermal decomposition products from materials like acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) and other thermoplastics on both animals and humans. Furthermore, exposure to UFPs from various origins has been connected to diverse negative health consequences in people.
This study builds upon previous research, specifically a screening analysis by Azimi et al. (2016), which indicated that certain 3D printer and filament combinations with high emissions could produce concerning levels of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and ultrafine particles (UFPs) in small office settings. While prior work highlighted potential health risks, a comprehensive investigation into human exposure to these gas and particle emissions from desktop FFF 3D printers in realistic indoor environments has been lacking. To address this gap, our current research utilizes recently published data on UFP and speciated VOC emission rates from diverse desktop FFF 3D printers and filaments. The primary goal is to forecast the extent of human exposure to these pollutants across various locations within a typical small office. Furthermore, we employ worst-case UFP and VOC emission scenarios to evaluate the effectiveness of different control strategies in mitigating these exposures.
To forecast pollutant concentrations from various desktop FFF 3D printer and filament combinations, we employed CONTAM 3.2, a multizone airflow and contaminant transport modeling software. Our simulations assumed printer operation within a single-story office building spanning 511 square meters. The foundational office building layout and dimensions were sourced from the Department of Energy Commercial Reference Building data set (Ng et al. 2012). However, we adjusted certain building parameters within the CONTAM model to better reflect real-world conditions. The building's floor plan, segmented into distinct zones with their respective maximum occupancy levels, is illustrated in Figure 1. This figure also provides a key for abbreviations used in the model, such as 3D for three-dimensional, MO for maximum occupancy, A for floor area, and m2 for square meters.
The study modeled a small office building, divided into several zones, each assumed to have consistent temperature, humidity, and pollutant levels. A single desktop 3D printer was positioned in a 9-m2 shaded area in the top-left of the floor plan (Figure 1). This area, termed the "near-distance" zone, lacked physical partitions but represented a space where an individual would be within approximately 3 meters of the operating printer for prolonged periods. Occupants in other areas would encounter printer emissions only after they had traveled from this source zone. These included an "adjacent zone," located 3 to 18 meters away, and a "far zone," situated 18 to 33 meters from the printer at the building's opposite end.
Each zone, excluding the bathroom and the "near-distance" zone, was equipped with its own air-handling unit (AHU). These AHUs were designed to both recirculate indoor air and introduce outdoor air for ventilation purposes. The total airflow rates for both supply and return were consistent across all AHUs. All five AHUs were modeled to adhere to the minimum ventilation standards outlined in ASHRAE Standard 62.1-2010, which considers both floor area and occupancy. Additionally, the bathroom was assumed to have an exhaust fan continuously operating at an airflow rate of 1.8 cubic meters per hour, venting directly to the exterior. The necessary outdoor air ventilation rate for each zone, denoted as Qvent,i (m3/hr), was determined using a specific equation: Qvent,i = A × Noccupants,i + B × Azone,i. In this formula, 'A' represents the outdoor air ventilation required per person in a small office (9 m3/hr per person), and 'B' signifies the outdoor air ventilation per floor area (1.08 m3/hr per m2). Noccupants,i refers to the maximum number of occupants in zone 'i', and Azone,i is the floor area of zone 'i' in square meters.
Our baseline simulations incorporated HVAC filtration standards from ASHRAE 62.1-2010. Specifically, the air-handling units (AHUs) were assumed to use MERV 8 particle filters, offering no gas-phase filtration. To determine the effectiveness of these MERV 8 filters against ultrafine particles (UFPs) from 3D printers, we estimated a 43.5% removal efficiency. This estimation involved correlating the size-resolved removal efficiency data for filters from Fazli and Stephens (2012) with the size distribution of 3D printer-emitted particles (under 100 nm) in an office setting, as detailed by Stephens and colleagues (2013) and the method in Azimi and colleagues (2014).
The simulation incorporated specific operational parameters for the building and its equipment. A single desktop 3D printer was presumed to run continuously for an 8-hour period each weekday, from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., reflecting a common, extended operational duration. The building's occupancy and HVAC system schedules were based on the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) model, with occupants present from 7:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. on weekdays, and the HVAC system active from 6:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m. on those days. To account for air infiltration, the model considered the building to be situated in Chicago, Illinois, utilizing Typical Meteorological Year (TMY) 3 data for that location (NSRDB 2012). Air leakage through exterior walls was simulated by including three distinct types of openings in each wall—cracks, gaps at corners, and areas around windows and doors. For these small openings, an effective leakage area of 5.27 cm²/m² was set at a reference pressure difference of 4 pascals (Emmerich and Persily 2012). Furthermore, all zones within the building were interconnected by large openings, each with a 1 m² cross-sectional area, to represent doorways. The boundaries of the "near-distance" 3D printing zone were specifically modeled as walls containing large openings equivalent to the zone's 9 m² cross-sectional area.

This includes a recent study by Azimi et al. (2016), which measured ultrafine particle (UFP) and speciated VOC emissions from five common FFF 3D printers and nine distinct filaments. These filaments comprised ABS, polylactic acid (PLA), high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), semitransparent nylon, laybrick (an imitation brick material of unknown composition), laywood (an imitation wood material of unknown composition), transparent polycarbonate, PCTPE (a semitransparent nylon-based plasticized copolyamide thermoplastic elastomer), and T-Glase (a transparent polyester resin filament). The main VOCs identified were caprolactam from nylon-based filaments, styrene from ABS and HIPS, and lactide from PLA. For pollutants consistently analyzed across various studies, such as UFPs from PLA and ABS, the emission rates reported by Azimi et al. (2016) were generally consistent with other findings, typically falling within an order of magnitude, making them representative of broader research.
Our analysis utilizes emission rates for ultrafine particles (UFPs) and key volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from the comprehensive Azimi et al. (2016) study, covering nine different filaments. We predict how pollutant concentrations change over time in the near, adjacent, and far zones of an office environment. For this modeling, we considered these emitted pollutants as additions to existing indoor air, meaning we set outdoor and background indoor concentrations to zero. Within the CONTAM model, specific diffusion coefficients were assigned: caprolactam at 0.065 cm2/s and styrene at 0.071 cm2/s (NJDEP 2012). Default CONTAM values of 0.2 cm2/s were used for UFP and lactide diffusion coefficients. We also adopted CONTAM's default settings for UFP effective density (1 g/cm3) and specific heat (1 kJ/kg-K). Furthermore, a UFP decay rate of 25 × 10−5 s−1 was applied, based on median values from a study on residential UFP decay (Wallace et al. 2013).
To assess methods for reducing pollutant levels, we investigated several control strategies. These included enhancing the central HVAC filtration system across the entire office, deploying portable air cleaners near the 3D printer, implementing spot ventilation systems close to the printer, and enclosing the 3D printer in a sealed unit with gas and particle filtration. Our analysis focused on worst-case scenarios, using the highest recorded emission rates for key pollutants: 6.1 × 10^10 particles per minute for UFPs, 183 micrograms per minute for caprolactam, 50 micrograms per minute for styrene, and 4 micrograms per minute for lactide. This approach helped us understand the potential effectiveness of each control measure.
For the initial exposure control strategy, we considered upgrading the central HVAC system's filters. These filters, located in the air-handling units (AHUs), were assumed to be replaced with MERV 16 media, which included activated carbon for both gas and particle filtration. We determined the ultrafine particle (UFP) removal efficiency of these MERV 16 filters to be 97%, employing the same data-matching method used for MERV 8 filters, as detailed by Fazli and Stephens (2012). Due to insufficient published data on the gas-phase removal effectiveness of activated carbon filters for specific VOCs like caprolactam, styrene, or lactide, we referenced Sidheswaran and colleagues (2012). Their study indicated that activated carbon fiber filters achieved VOC removal efficiencies between 60% and 80% for various VOCs. Consequently, we adopted a 70% removal efficiency for all emitted VOCs by these high-efficiency particulate arrestance (HEPA) filters with activated carbon fibers.
To assess the impact of portable air cleaners, we simulated two scenarios with different Clean Air Delivery Rates (CADRs) for ultrafine particles (UFPs): 100 and 300 m3/hr. This choice was informed by existing research, which shows UFP CADRs for commercial air cleaners typically falling between 100 and 300 m3/hr, with single-pass efficiencies generally ranging from 45% to 60% (Mølgaard et al. 2014; Sultan et al. 2011). For instance, Waring and colleagues (2008) observed a single-pass removal efficiency of approximately 60% for two HEPA air cleaners, with average CADRs of 188 and 324 m3/hr. Based on an assumed 60% single-pass efficiency for the HEPA filters, the corresponding airflow rates for our two modeled air cleaners were determined to be 167 and 500 m3/hr. Additionally, we incorporated the assumption that these filters included impregnated carbon, achieving a 70% removal efficiency for the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) under consideration (Sidheswaran et al. 2012). This translated to VOC CADRs of 117 and 350 m3/hr for the lower and higher efficiency air cleaner scenarios, respectively. Within the CONTAM model, both air cleaners were represented as air handling systems operating within the near-distance zone, maintaining equal supply and return airflows and no air exchange with the outside environment.
Our next step involved evaluating the effectiveness of spot ventilation within the immediate vicinity of the 3D printer in the near-distance zone. Since specific exhaust system data for 3D printers is unavailable, we drew parallels with exhaust hoods commonly found in small commercial or residential kitchens. These hoods were assumed to be positioned roughly 1.5 meters above the 3D printer, venting emissions directly outside. Research on kitchen exhaust fans has shown a broad spectrum of nominal and actual airflow rates, alongside varying capture efficiencies for both gas and particle emissions from cooking. Nominal airflow rates in these studies ranged from 325 to 1,300 cubic meters per hour (m3/hr), with measured rates between 75 and 650 m3/hr. Pollutant capture efficiencies (CEs) varied from under 50% to over 75%, generally increasing with airflow. For context, if the 67.3 m2 printing area were classified as a "chemical storage room," ASHRAE Standard 62.1 would mandate a minimum exhaust airflow of 27 m3/hr per m2, totaling 1,817 m3/hr, to satisfy ventilation standards.
Considering the plausible range of airflow rates and capture efficiencies (CEs), we developed three distinct spot ventilation scenarios for our model. These scenarios included: a low-effectiveness option with an airflow of 90 m3/hr (25 L/s) and a 25% CE; a medium-effectiveness option at 360 m3/hr (100 L/s) with a 63% CE; and a high-effectiveness option providing 1,800 m3/hr (500 L/s) and a 100% CE. To implement these in the CONTAM model, we created a hypothetical 1-m2 area within the near-distance zone, equipped with an exhaust fan venting outdoors. We then allocated the pollutant emission sources between the zones according to the CE of each spot ventilation scenario. For instance, if the CE was 25%, 75% of the total emissions from the four primary pollutants were directed into the near-distance zone, allowing for transport and exposure in other areas. The remaining 25% of emissions were confined to the 1-m2 "spot ventilation zone." Additionally, the HVAC system's supply airflow rates were distributed between the near-distance and spot ventilation zones to ensure uniform pollutant concentrations when the 3D printer was active and the spot ventilation airflow was off.
To assess the effectiveness of a sealed enclosure with recirculating gas and particle filtration, we conducted a pilot experimental chamber study. This involved placing two high-emission 3D printer and filament combinations inside a prototype enclosure. The enclosure demonstrated an average removal efficiency of approximately 90% for total VOCs and 80% for UFPs. Lacking further data, we applied these measured efficiencies uniformly in our CONTAM model. Specifically, we assumed the enclosure reduced all gas-phase VOC emission rates by 90% and all UFP emission rates by 80% compared to the baseline scenario.

Pollutant concentrations from 3D printers show distinct patterns across different zones. The near-distance zone experiences peak exposures to ultrafine particles (UFPs) and styrene that are typically over 9 and 4 times higher, respectively, than those in the adjacent zone. Conversely, a significantly smaller portion of these pollutants reaches the far-distance zone. The model indicates that both gas and particle concentrations in the near and adjacent zones rise almost immediately upon printer operation. In contrast, concentrations in the far-distance zone reach their peak much later. Concentrations in the far zone also exhibit greater variability, influenced by changes in air infiltration. For instance, the relative standard deviation of predicted maximum 1-hour concentrations over a year is only 2% for UFPs and 15% for styrene in the near zone. This variability increases to 41% for UFPs and 58% for VOCs in the adjacent zone, and is highest in the far zone at 114% for UFPs and 106% for VOCs. These concentration profiles remain largely consistent on weekdays throughout the year.

Each series includes 260 data points, corresponding to the weekdays within a year. The calculation of daily maximum 8-hour concentrations involves averaging hourly predictions over 8-hour intervals and then identifying the highest average for each day. For the daily 24-hour concentrations, a straightforward daily average of the predicted concentrations for each weekday throughout the year is used. In the immediate vicinity of the printer (near zone), daily maximum 1-hour ultrafine particle (UFP) concentrations varied significantly. Filaments like Laybrick, Laywood, and PLA resulted in concentrations just below 100 particles per cubic centimeter (#/cm3), while ABS and polycarbonate filaments produced nearly 80,000 #/cm3. Volatile organic compound (VOC) concentrations also showed a wide range. Caprolactam-emitting filaments (nylon and PCTPE) led to the highest values, from 0.7 to 80 μg/m3, whereas styrene-emitting filaments (ABS and HIPS) ranged from 8 to 22 μg/m3. Lactide-emitting filaments consistently stayed below 2 μg/m3. Due to the 8-hour daily printer operation, 1-hour and 8-hour maximum daily concentrations were comparable, but 24-hour average concentrations were considerably lower, by about 3% and 67% respectively, compared to the 1-hour maximum.
In the adjacent zone, daily maximum 1-hour UFP concentrations varied significantly, from under 20 #/cm3 for low-emission filaments like Laybrick, Laywood, and PLA, up to almost 10,000 #/cm3 for ABS and polycarbonate filaments. Similarly, median 1-hour VOC concentrations in this zone ranged from 0.2 to 20 μg/m3 for caprolactam-emitting filaments, 2 to 6 μg/m3 for styrene-emitting filaments, and remained below 1 μg/m3 for lactide-emitting filaments. Moving to the far zone, both UFP and VOC concentrations exhibited similar trends but at considerably lower absolute levels. For instance, when using polycarbonate and ABS filaments, the median 1-, 8-, and 24-hour UFP concentrations in the far zone were merely 60, 20, and 7 #/cm3, respectively. The highest median 1-hour maximum concentrations of caprolactam, styrene, and lactide in the far zone were a mere 0.5, 0.1, and 0.01 μg/m3 when using nylon, ABS, and PLA filaments. Consequently, emissions from the lowest-emitting filaments had virtually no effect on concentrations in the far-distance zone.
Human health is significantly impacted by the predicted pollutant concentrations in the near and adjacent zones. Specifically, caprolactam levels from 3D printing with most nylon-based filaments (excluding T-Glase and polycarbonate) are projected to surpass the 8-hour and chronic Reference Exposure Levels (RELs) established by the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA). The OEHHA sets these RELs at 50 μg/m3 for acute (1-hour average), 7 μg/m3 for 8-hour average, and 2.2 μg/m3 for chronic (continuous lifetime) exposure. Furthermore, printing with nylon and PCTPE filaments is expected to result in caprolactam concentrations in the near zone that also exceed the acute REL. High caprolactam exposure is known to irritate the eyes and respiratory tract and can affect the central nervous system. In contrast, caprolactam concentrations in the far-distance zone consistently stay below all OEHHA RELs for all nylon-based filaments modeled.
While current indoor styrene concentration guidelines primarily address industrial and workplace settings, they include an 8-hour time-weighted average of 85 mg/m3 from the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists and 426 mg/m3 from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration Standard #29 CFR 1910.1000. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health sets a significantly higher limit of nearly 3,000 mg/m3. Additionally, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) has a Reference Concentration of 1 mg/m3 for styrene, based on its effects on the central nervous system in workers. Despite these established limits, our findings indicate that even lower styrene concentrations can pose health risks. For instance, epidemiological studies have linked styrene levels as low as a few μg/m3 to an increased risk of pulmonary infections in infants. Our simulations show that median styrene concentrations in the near-distance zone can exceed this value by more than tenfold. These levels can also be three times higher than the maximum styrene concentration of 7.5 μg/m3 typically found in commercial buildings, as reported in the US EPA BASE study. Furthermore, styrene is recognized as a "possible human carcinogen" by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC classification group 2B) and is "reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen" by the National Toxicology Program. Therefore, even though the predicted styrene concentrations in all zones and with all printer/filament combinations in this study are below the official exposure limits, the potential health risks to occupants remain a concern.
Despite the absence of specific indoor regulatory limits for ultrafine particle (UFP) concentrations, the significant increases observed from 3D printing are noteworthy. Polycarbonate and ABS filaments lead to UFP concentrations of approximately 80,000 #/cm3 in the near-distance zone and 10,000 #/cm3 in the adjacent zone. Other filaments also cause substantial increases, reaching around 40,000 #/cm3 and 5,000 #/cm3 in these respective zones. These levels are concerning when considering the known health impacts of outdoor UFPs. For instance, concentrations of 80,000 #/cm3 are comparable to those found near busy roads, where elevated UFPs are linked to adverse health outcomes. Furthermore, studies indicate that outdoor UFP increases of 10,000 #/cm3 correlate with a 3% rise in daily mortality, and even a 1,000 #/cm3 increase can affect children's blood pressure.
Modeled UFP concentrations from 3D printers can significantly surpass those found in other indoor settings. For instance, a concentration of 80,000 #/cm3 is approximately 50% greater than the highest time-averaged UFP levels previously recorded in schools (Diapouli et al. 2007). Additionally, using filaments like PCTPE, T-Glase, and HIPS can elevate near-zone UFP concentrations to 2 to 5 times the typical indoor UFP levels observed in other buildings, affecting both adjacent and near-distance areas. While the health impacts of indoor-generated UFPs are not fully understood, research on nanoparticles from photocopiers and laser printers highlights potential risks. Studies have shown that UFPs from a university copy center caused lung injury and inflammation in mice (Pirela et al. 2013), and upper-airway inflammation and oxidative stress in human volunteers (Khatri et al. 2013). This evidence indicates that managing emissions and exposures from high-emitting 3D printer and filament combinations is crucial in environments like the one studied.
To investigate how different control strategies affect pollutant concentrations, we focused on the printer and filament combinations that produced the highest emissions for each specific pollutant. For instance, ABS filaments were chosen for their high UFP and styrene emissions, nylon for caprolactam, and PLA for lactide. The predicted effects of these control strategies on the maximum 1-hour ultrafine particle (UFP) concentrations in the near, adjacent, and far zones are illustrated in Figure 5. This figure, which also defines terms like CADR, #/cm3, and MERV, presents the modeled impacts, assuming the 3D printer operated on the same schedule as in previous simulations.
For reducing ultrafine particle (UFP) concentrations in the immediate vicinity of 3D printers, direct source control methods are most effective. A high-flow spot ventilation system, which also boasts high capture efficiency, offers the greatest potential for UFP reduction in the near zone. Following closely in effectiveness is operating the printer within a sealed enclosure equipped with both particle and gas filtration. These two strategies are projected to significantly lower median daily maximum 1-hour UFP concentrations from approximately 80,000 #/cm3 to below 4,000 #/cm3. In contrast, upgrading central HVAC filters to MERV 16 with activated carbon and implementing a low-flow, low-capture-efficiency spot ventilation system provide the least reduction in UFP levels. Portable air cleaners, while having some impact, offer only moderate reductions. This indicates that targeting emissions at their origin is preferable to broader air cleaning or filtration for mitigating exposure in close proximity to operating 3D printers.
In the adjacent zone, UFP concentration reductions show some variation, but generally follow similar patterns to the near zone. The most significant decreases in median daily maximum 1-hour UFP concentrations (all falling below 500 #/cm3) are achieved by moderate- and high-flow spot ventilation systems, along with the sealed enclosure. While portable air cleaners with low CADR and upgraded central HVAC filtration offer the smallest reductions in this zone, their relative impact is greater here than directly next to the printer. Despite their lower effectiveness, air cleaners and low-flow spot ventilation can still keep UFP concentrations under 5,000 #/cm3 in the adjacent zone. The far zone exhibits comparable concentration profiles, but its inherently low baseline UFP levels (around 60 #/cm3) suggest that additional control measures might not be necessary.

This includes caprolactam from nylon, styrene from ABS, and lactide from PLA filaments. The figure details the modeled impacts across near, adjacent, and far zones, using metrics like clean air delivery rate (CADR) and minimum efficiency reporting value (MERV).
The impact of control strategies on reducing daily maximum 1-hour VOC concentrations mirrors their effect on UFP levels, as the underlying assumptions for pollutant behavior are largely consistent. However, the exact concentration reductions depend on the initial emission strength of the source. For VOCs in the near zone, central HVAC filtration upgrades and low-flow spot ventilation offer the least significant improvements. Consequently, daily maximum 1-hour caprolactam concentrations from nylon-based filaments often remain above the OEHHA acute REL of 50 μg/m³. In contrast, all other control strategies effectively lower the median daily maximum 1-hour caprolactam concentration below this threshold. Specifically, both high-flow spot ventilation and sealed enclosures with gas and particle filtration are projected to decrease this caprolactam concentration to under 2 μg/m³. Similarly, these same highly effective strategies—moderate- and high-flow spot ventilation systems and sealed enclosures—are expected to bring the median daily maximum 1-hour styrene concentration down to less than 7.5 μg/m³, which is below the highest level recorded in U.S. commercial buildings during the BASE study.
In the adjacent zone, significant VOC reductions are achieved by moderate- and high-flow spot ventilation systems, as well as sealed enclosures. These methods can lower daily maximum 1-hour caprolactam and styrene concentrations to below 0.5 μg/m3. While portable air cleaners offer more modest reductions in VOC levels across both adjacent and far zones, they can still keep caprolactam peaks below the OEHHA acute REL on most days. All control strategies, excluding the low-CADR portable air cleaner, are effective in maintaining the median daily maximum 1-hour styrene concentration in the adjacent zone under 2 μg/m3. Furthermore, lactide emissions from PLA filaments are unlikely to pose a human exposure risk, as their median daily maximum 1-hour concentrations consistently stay below 2 μg/m3 in all three zones and across all scenarios.
The effectiveness of various control strategies in reducing UFP and VOC concentrations across different zones was determined using the same dataset, with the results presented in Figure 7. This effectiveness, denoted as E_control,i, was calculated using Equation 2, which compares the median pollutant concentration with a control strategy (C_i) to the median baseline concentration without any controls (C_baseline,i). The calculated removal effectiveness varied significantly depending on the control method and the specific zone. For instance, a low-flow spot ventilation system showed minimal impact on UFPs in the near zone, with an effectiveness as low as 2%. In stark contrast, a high-flow spot ventilation system or a sealed enclosure with filtration demonstrated a nearly complete reduction, achieving 95% to 100% effectiveness for both UFPs and VOCs across all zones. Portable air cleaners exhibited consistent effectiveness across all zones, with the low-CADR option achieving 38% for UFPs and 46% for VOCs, and the high-CADR option reaching 64% for UFPs and 72% for VOCs. The impact of upgrading central HVAC filtration varied by zone, showing 6% (UFP) and 21% (VOC) effectiveness in the near zone, increasing to 43% (UFP) and 73% (VOC) in the adjacent zone, and reaching 77% (UFP) and 95% (VOC) in the far zone.
The effectiveness of different control strategies for reducing ultrafine particles (UFPs) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) varied significantly. High-flow spot ventilation systems and sealed, filtered enclosures proved most effective, achieving 95% to 100% removal for both pollutants across all zones. Conversely, a low-flow spot ventilation system had minimal impact on UFPs in the near zone, with only 2% effectiveness. Portable air cleaners showed consistent effectiveness across all zones: the low-CADR model removed 38% of UFPs and 46% of VOCs, while the high-CADR model removed 64% of UFPs and 72% of VOCs. Upgrading central HVAC filtration also demonstrated variable effectiveness depending on the zone, ranging from 6% for UFPs and 21% for VOCs in the near zone to 77% for UFPs and 95% for VOCs in the far zone.
The study's findings offer valuable insights into how different control strategies affect indoor pollutant levels from 3D printers. However, it's important to consider the practicality and cost-effectiveness of these solutions. High-flow spot ventilation, while effective, might be too expensive or difficult to implement in many settings, making sealed and filtered enclosures a more viable alternative. Similarly, high-CADR air cleaners, despite their significant impact on UFP and VOC concentrations across all zones, come with a notable energy cost. Additionally, the emission rates presented in Table 1 represent a range of typical values, but other printer and filament combinations could exhibit different emission magnitudes and types of VOCs. Therefore, future research should aim to validate these findings through experimental data, focusing on real-world exposures and the effectiveness of control strategies in actual environments.
The simulations conducted offer valuable insights into potential human exposure levels to gas and particle emissions from desktop 3D printers, alongside practical advice for managing these exposures. The study's findings indicate that in areas close to or moderately distant from certain active desktop 3D printers, concentrations of ultrafine particles (UFPs) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) can surpass recommended exposure limits, raising concerns for both short-term and long-term health impacts. Regarding control strategies, the most impactful methods for reducing UFP and VOC concentrations across all zones, especially for high-emitting setups, were identified in a specific order. A high-flow spot ventilation system proved to be the most effective. Following this, operating the printer within a sealed enclosure equipped with high-efficiency gas and particle filtration showed significant benefits. A moderate-flow spot ventilation system was next in effectiveness, followed by the use of a high-CADR (clean air delivery rate) stand-alone air cleaner with both gas and particle filtration positioned near the operating 3D printer. Conversely, less effective approaches included upgrading central HVAC filtration, implementing low-flow spot ventilation, and utilizing a low-CADR portable air cleaner. The study also highlights the importance of choosing lower-emission 3D printer and filament combinations, such as PLA filaments which produce minimal UFPs and VOCs, to mitigate human exposure.
