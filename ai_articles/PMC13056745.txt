In 2007, Austria's economy relied significantly on international trade, with imports constituting about 34% of its total material input and exports contributing nearly 60% to its GDP. However, conventional material flow accounting (MFA) methods do not account for the upstream material inputs required to produce these traded goods. To address this, we explored various methods for quantifying these upstream material inputs, termed raw material equivalents (RME), within the broader context of consumption-based environmental accounting. Our study calculated Austria's annual RME for trade and consumption from 1995 to 2007 using a hybrid approach. This involved employing an environmentally extended input-output model, informed by supply and use tables and MFA data, for exports and competitive imports. For noncompetitive imports, we utilized life cycle inventory coefficients to determine upstream material inputs. Our findings revealed that the RME for Austria's imports and exports were roughly three times the volume of the trade flows themselves. In 2007, Austria's raw material consumption exceeded its domestic material consumption by 30 million tonnes, or 15%. This paper further examines the material composition and temporal trends of these flows, underscoring the necessity of a consumption-based perspective in MFA for accurate dematerialization and resource efficiency assessments in open economies.

The globalized economy has seen a surge in trade volumes, leading to significant shifts within economies and a more dispersed global landscape for production and consumption. Despite these changes, conventional national inventories for energy, materials, and emissions largely maintain a domestic or production-oriented viewpoint. This means that the resources consumed and emissions generated during the manufacturing of imported goods are assigned to the exporting nation, rather than the country where the goods are ultimately consumed. For economies heavily involved in international trade, these production-based accounting methods are becoming insufficient for accurately measuring the full environmental impact of domestic consumption throughout a product's lifecycle. Acknowledging the growing significance of global trade in environmental accounting and policy, alternative methodologies have emerged. These range from early "energy costs" approaches to more contemporary "consumption" or "footprint" perspectives, including those for energy and greenhouse gas emissions. Similar concerns are relevant for comprehensive material flow accounts and their associated national indicators. The production of material goods inherently involves the consumption and transformation of substantial raw materials, much of which becomes waste or emissions and is not physically part of the final product.
Standard Material Flow Accounting (MFA) indicators like Direct Material Input (DMI) and Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) adopt a production-based perspective. This means that the materials consumed and emissions generated during the manufacturing of imported goods are attributed to the exporting nation, rather than the country that ultimately consumes them. Consequently, when evaluating resource efficiency and dematerialization in open economies, these indicators have limitations. A decrease in their values could stem from genuine improvements or simply from the relocation of material-intensive production processes to other countries. To address this, efforts to create consumption-based MFA approaches began in the 1990s. The Eurostat framework introduced the concept of raw material equivalents (RME) to incorporate the upstream material inputs associated with both imports and exports, thereby shifting towards a consumption-based view.
Despite the potential for RME accounting methods to benefit from advancements in energy and carbon footprinting, practical applications and detailed RME accounts remain uncommon. This scarcity is primarily due to the intricate and often poorly documented nature of socioeconomic material consumption. Nevertheless, recent years have seen a rise in empirical RME studies employing various calculation techniques. This paper introduces the inaugural annual RME calculations for Austria's trade and material consumption spanning 1995 to 2007. Our methodology involved a hybrid approach, integrating an environmentally extended input-output (IO) model with life cycle inventory (LCI) coefficients.
Austria's economy exemplifies the increasing role of international trade in industrial resource consumption. Between 1960 and 2008, imports rose from 16% to 34% of the total material input, meaning only one-third of materials originated domestically. Furthermore, 23% of all processed materials in Austria were exported. While biomass and nonmetallic minerals are largely extracted domestically, Austria lacks domestic sources for fossil fuels and many crucial metals, leading to declining domestic extraction and a growing reliance on imports due to their strategic importance and sustained demand.
This paper is structured in two parts. The first section reviews existing RME calculation methodologies and details our hybrid approach. The second section presents the findings regarding Austria's RME for trade and consumption.
RME calculation methods fall into three categories: Input-Output (IO) approaches, coefficient/LCI-based approaches, and hybrid IO approaches.
Input-Output (IO) approaches leverage standardized monetary IO tables, rarely physical ones, derived from national accounts. These tables offer comprehensive economic coverage, align well with economy-wide Material Flow Accounting (MFA) and the UN's System of Environmental-Economic Accounting, and integrate seamlessly into national accounting systems. However, a significant drawback is their reliance on monetary data for interindustry flows and final demand, which can lead to inaccurate physical flow allocations due to the high level of aggregation in economic activities. For instance, Austrian IO tables group livestock and crop production under a single agricultural activity, despite their vastly different physical input requirements. Despite this limitation, the IO approach has been employed in two primary methods for calculating Raw Material Equivalents (RME).
The single-region input-output (SRIO) model calculates raw material equivalents (RME) for imports and exports by utilizing a single country's input-output (IO) table. This method assumes that the domestic input quantities and mix are representative of the production processes in the exporting countries. Consequently, the RME for imports calculated using this approach reflect the hypothetical resource demand if the imported goods were produced domestically, serving as a measure of virtual resource savings or losses. This methodology was employed in early RME studies and more recently in research on Latin American nations. However, the SRIO model's effectiveness is limited, particularly for small, open economies. In such cases, where specific goods are not produced domestically or raw materials are unavailable, and where national IO data inadequately capture relevant production processes, the calculated RME can significantly diverge from the actual upstream material inputs involved in producing traded goods.
Multiregional Input-Output (MRIO) models address the limitations of Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) models by incorporating input-output data from various countries or regions into a single global table. This integration allows MRIO models to more accurately reflect the specific interindustry monetary relationships within the economies that produce imported goods, making them the most sophisticated approach for this purpose. However, harmonizing the differing numbers of reported economic activities across these regions presents a challenge. The extensive data requirements for MRIO calculations necessitate significant computational resources and sustained effort. To manage this complexity, comprehensive MRIO models often include input-output data for numerous countries. Simplifications can be achieved by merging country-specific data for key trading partners with an average "rest of the world" table or by employing generalized regional input-output tables. Studies by Bruckner and colleagues (2012) and Wiebe and colleagues (2012), focusing on emerging economies, have utilized MRIO models, such as the Global Resource Accounting Model, for RME calculations. Additional examples of MRIO frameworks include those based on the Global Trade Analysis Project (Narayanan et al. 2012), EXIOBASE (Tukker et al. 2009), Eora (Lenzen et al. 2012), and the World Input-Output Database (Timmer 2012). Unlike SRIO-derived RME, MRIO results aim to represent the genuine material inputs involved in the production of traded goods.
Coefficient-based methods for estimating upstream material inputs rely on specific coefficients for individual products or resources. Unlike input-output (IO) approaches that aim for economy-wide coverage, these coefficient methods adopt a bottom-up strategy, concentrating on particular products or product categories. The coefficients typically originate from life cycle inventories (LCIs), which provide detailed physical flow information for numerous products and processes, contrasting with the monetary data of IO tables. A key difficulty with this method is ensuring comprehensive coverage without duplicating counts. The Wuppertal Institute pioneered and applied this coefficient approach, building on material intensity analyses by Ritthoff and colleagues. Their resulting aggregate indicator is the total material requirement (TMR). A notable distinction of TMR from other raw material equivalent (RME) calculation methods is its inclusion of unused extraction. Furthermore, TMR allocates upstream requirements to material groups based on their end-use. For instance, all materials used in biomass production, regardless of their origin (e.g., fossil fuels or nonmetallic minerals for infrastructure), are categorized as "biomass." This classification makes direct comparison of TMR results with other approaches challenging.
A hybrid approach, combining Input-Output (IO) and Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) data, is employed to address system boundary challenges, as established by the LCI community. Within Raw Material Equivalent (RME) calculations, this method enhances a Single-Region Input-Output (SRIO) model by incorporating an LCI module specifically for noncompetitive imports. These are products or activities inadequately represented in domestic IO structures. The RME calculations for Austria detailed in this paper utilize this hybrid approach. This methodology is similar to those used in studies for Germany, the Czech Republic, and more recently, the EU27.
For Austrian trade from 1995-2007, RME for exports and competitive imports were calculated using an extended input-output model. Non-competitive imports were incorporated using life cycle inventory coefficients.
Our calculation of trade's raw material equivalents (RME) employed a generalized open static input-output (IO) model, building upon established frameworks (Leontief 1941, 1986; Lenzen 2001; Miller and Blair 2009). This model operates under the industry technology assumption, where each industry consistently utilizes a specific production technology for all its outputs. The model's structure involves several key matrices. The matrix B, representing product by industry, captures the proportion of intermediate inputs relative to an industry's total output. Concurrently, matrix D, from industry to product, details each industry's market share in the domestic production of every product. Multiplying these matrices, B and D, yields the n x n interindustry requirements matrix A, which reflects product-to-product relationships and is often referred to as the technology matrix. In this model, 'x' denotes the vector of total economic output, 'A' is the interindustry requirements matrix, and 'y' represents the vector of final demand. Equation 1, in standard matrix notation, illustrates that total economic output is the aggregate of all intermediate and final consumption: x = Ax + y. By rearranging this equation, we derive the total output 'x' as presented in Equation 2: x = (I - A)⁻¹y. Here, 'I' is the identity matrix, and (I - A)⁻¹ is the Leontief inverse, also known as the total requirements matrix. Each component within the Leontief inverse, denoted as {lij}, quantifies the total direct and indirect intermediate inputs of activity 'i' required to produce a single unit of final demand for activity 'j'.
The described model can be expanded to incorporate any factor inputs necessary for producing a given final demand, as detailed by Lenzen (2001). This study focuses specifically on material factor inputs. We define F as a k × n matrix representing material-use intensities, where 'k' denotes the number of materials under consideration. Each entry {fαi} within this matrix quantifies the direct input of material α into activity 'i' per unit of output from that activity. The total material requirements, encompassing both direct and indirect inputs (represented by the k × n matrix E with elements {eαi}), embedded within final demand are calculated using equation 3: E = F(I − A)⁻¹y. The expression F(I − A)⁻¹ is recognized as the multiplier, with further analysis available in Lenzen (2001). Given our interest in trade flows, we segment final demand 'y' into two parts: foreign final demand (yex) and domestic final demand (yd). This allows us to compute the direct and indirect material factor inputs for 'k' materials required to generate a specific vector of exports (yex), as illustrated in equation 4: Eˣ = F(I − A)⁻¹yᵉˣ.
The same multiplier is used to determine the direct and indirect material inputs for competitive imports. The raw material equivalents (RME) for both exports and competitive imports are then calculated by adding these upstream material requirements to the actual mass of the traded goods.
For the Austrian economy, key non-competitive imports were identified through production statistics. These included products from the initial extraction and processing of metals like iron, copper, and aluminum, as well as raw materials for fertilizer production and the extraction of petroleum and gas. Standard input-output (IO) statistics for these imports do not offer the necessary technological detail or commodity breakdown required for Raw Material Equivalent (RME) calculations. To address this, a Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) module was employed. This module utilizes coefficients, expressed in tonnes per tonne extracted, sourced from GEMIS and based on the origin of Austrian imports. These coefficients detail the upstream material inputs associated with non-competitive imports. The LCI offers a more granular view of commodity groups compared to IO tables; for instance, while IO tables might group iron, copper, and aluminum together, LCI provides distinct coefficients for each metal. To reconcile these differing levels of detail, import flows within the IO module were disaggregated using United Nations (UN) Comtrade data. This data, categorized by the three-digit Standard International Trade Classification (SITC; Rev.3), identifies 261 distinct products. For example, in 2000, Austria's iron imports consisted of 61% iron ore and concentrates and 39% iron and steel products. The LCI coefficients for iron were then weighted to reflect this import composition, combining the coefficient for iron ore (0.61) with that for iron and steel products (0.39). Finally, the resulting upstream material inputs were consolidated into the four primary Material Flow Accounting (MFA) categories.
The calculated weighted and aggregated coefficients for several noncompetitive imported goods are presented in Figure 3. Adhering to the Eurostat MFA methodology, metal extraction is quantified in gross ore, encompassing waste rock. For instance, as illustrated with copper in Figure 3, an average ore grade of roughly 1% results in significantly higher upstream material input coefficients, with waste rock constituting a substantial portion. Data on average ore grades from the U.S. Geological Survey were utilized: 1% for copper, 50% for iron, and 25% for aluminum/bauxite. Figure 3 visually contrasts the material flow accounting categories with and without waste rock on the left, and the input coefficients for metal production in gross ore (including waste rock) on the right, all expressed in tonnes per tonne.
The LCI module's calculated flows for noncompetitive imports were reintegrated into the IO module, reflecting their indirect use in Austrian final demand production.
Statistics Austria annually publishes Supply and Use Tables (SUTs) for the Austrian economy. Our analysis utilized version A, which incorporates imports into both the use and final demand tables. These SUTs are presented in monetary units at current prices and are symmetric, detailing 57 activities and commodities. To determine the direct input coefficient matrix A, which operates in a commodity-by-commodity dimension, we employed equation 5. This equation involves two matrices: B, a 57x57 matrix representing the inputs of commodity i per unit of output from industry j (in commodities by industries dimensions), and D, another 57x57 matrix. Matrix D outlines the market share of each industry i for every commodity j (in industries by commodities dimensions). The product of B and D yields matrix A. For matrix F, which quantifies the material-use intensities of each economic activity, we utilized a disaggregated form of Direct Material Input (DMI). This disaggregation incorporates results from the Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) module, measured in tonnes, alongside total output (x) in Euros. Consequently, all elements within matrix F are expressed in units of tonnes per Euro [t/€].
Direct Material Input (DMI), a key indicator from economy-wide material flow accounts, quantifies total domestic extraction and imports annually in tonnes. While domestic extraction (DE) data is available from Statistik Austria in 65 material groups, national statistical offices do not provide a breakdown of material flows by economic activities, as found in the National Accounting Matrix with Environmental Accounts. Consequently, we undertook an allocation of DMI. For DE, we assigned the raw material's use as a factor input to the primary producing activity. This allocation is generally straightforward for agriculture, forestry, and mining. However, construction minerals present an exception. In Austria, construction activities directly extract roughly half of the nonmetallic minerals used, rather than this being solely attributed to mining. Therefore, we divided the DE of construction minerals equally, allocating 50% to mining and 50% to construction.
To allocate the import component of Direct Material Input (DMI) to specific economic activities, annual matrices were generated using detailed UN Comtrade data. This process was essential due to the incompatibility between the product and activity classifications in the Austrian Input-Output (IO) table (CPA/NACE) and the Material Flow Accounting (MFA) material groups, which lack a clear correspondence. By extracting UN Comtrade data (SITC Rev.3) in physical units for Austrian imports from 1995 to 2007 at the AG5 level, we achieved unambiguous allocation to NACE sectors. This enabled us to determine the proportion of each import flow, categorized by MFA, that should be assigned to each economic activity.
The hybrid method was employed to determine the raw material equivalents (RME) for both imports (RIM) and exports (REX). These RME figures encompass not only the physical trade flows but also the upstream material inputs required for their production. Leveraging these RIM and REX values, alongside data on domestic extraction (DE) from Austria's Material Flow Accounting (MFA), we computed RME-adjusted versions of conventional MFA indicators. These include the raw material trade balance (RTB), raw material consumption (RMC), and raw material input (RMI). This calculation process mirrors the standard methodology for MFA indicators. Furthermore, by utilizing the equation E = F (I − A)−1y as the basis for our RME calculations, we could also derive RMI by substituting total final demand for 'y', and RMC by substituting domestic final demand for 'y'.
Upstream material inputs for traded goods retain their original material category and are not reclassified based on the final product's category.
For 1995-2007, the hybrid method calculated Austria's raw material equivalents (RME) for traded goods across biomass, metal ores, nonmetallic minerals, fossil energy carriers, and other products.
In 2007, Austria's material imports reached approximately 91 million tonnes. However, the upstream material inputs required for their production in originating economies totaled 239 million tonnes. This means Austria's Raw Material Equivalents of Imports (RIM) were 2.6 times greater than the physical import volume. While this ratio was similar in 1995 (2.5 times), the total import tonnage in 1995 was only 58% of the 2007 figure. In 2007, fossil energy carriers constituted the largest portion of Austria's imports (31%), followed by biomass (25%) and metal ores (23%).
In 2007, metal ores constituted the largest portion of Austria's Raw Material Equivalents of Imports (RIM), accounting for 49%. Fossil energy carriers and nonmetallic minerals followed, each representing 20%. The RIM for metals were notably higher, reaching 5.5 times the volume of actual metal imports. This disparity stems from the processing stages of metal ores, which typically involve trade as concentrates or finished products, leading to a significant reduction in material mass from extraction to final product. Furthermore, the extensive use of metals, particularly steel, in industrial production and infrastructure amplifies the importance of metal ores within RIM. Fossil energy carriers, essential inputs across various production processes, also exhibited substantially higher RIM (1.7 times imports). Similarly, Austria's nonmetallic mineral RIM were 4.1 times greater than imports, reflecting their crucial role in production and transportation infrastructure. Over the period from 1995 to 2007, the growth in Austria's RIM and its overall imports showed a similar trajectory, increasing by a factor of 1.8 and 1.7 respectively. This alignment is attributed to shifts in import composition and the upstream material inputs identified by the input-output model.
In 2007, Austria's exports totaled about 58 million tonnes. Unusually for an industrialized nation, biomass constituted the largest portion (38%), with highly processed metal goods being the second-largest export category (24%).
Over the period from 1995 to 2007, Austria's exports and their associated raw material equivalents (REX) experienced nearly identical growth rates, increasing by factors of 2.1 and 2.4, respectively. REX rose from about 73 million tonnes in 1995 to 177 million tonnes by 2007. The production of exports heavily relies on inputs like metals, nonmetallic minerals, and fossil energy carriers, leading to REX being 5.2 to 5.7 times greater than the actual exported goods for these categories. Consequently, the proportion of material used in production relative to the mass of exported goods saw a slight increase, rising from 2.6 times in 1995 to 3.0 times in 2007.
Between 1995 and 2007, Austria's trade significantly increased in importance, impacting both the supply of domestic demand through imports and the production of exports. Over these 12 years, exports doubled. Notably, fossil energy carriers, despite being the smallest material category within REX, experienced the most substantial growth, with exports increasing by a factor of 4.9 and REX by a factor of 3.0. This surge is noteworthy given Austria's limited domestic fossil fuel reserves, necessitating reliance on imports. However, the petroleum refining sector plays a crucial role in the Austrian economy, contributing significantly to GDP. OMV, a major Austrian company, operates a refinery that processes predominantly imported petroleum, with a portion of its output being exported.
Austria's physical trade balance (PTB) in 2007 indicated a net import of materials, with a positive balance of just over 32 million tonnes. This signifies Austria's role as a net importer and global consumer of materials. Fossil energy carriers were the primary contributor to this balance, accounting for 23 million tonnes, or 73% of the total. Metal ores represented the second largest component, making up 22% of the PTB. Both fossil energy carriers and metals are resources with limited domestic availability in Austria. In contrast, the PTB for other material categories was considerably smaller. For instance, biomass imports and exports were substantial at 23 million tonnes and 22 million tonnes respectively, resulting in a nearly balanced trade and a PTB of only 0.9 million tonnes.
In 2007, Austria's raw material trade balance (RTB), which accounts for the upstream material inputs of traded goods, was approximately double its physical trade balance (PTB), a factor of 1.9. This significant difference is primarily attributed to the substantial quantity of metal ores needed for the production of imported goods, with the raw material equivalents of metals exceeding their physical trade by 50 million tonnes. Fossil energy carriers represented the second largest component of the RTB, contributing 22 million tonnes. Over the period from 1995 to 2007, Austria experienced a shift from being a net importer to a net exporter of nonmetallic minerals' raw material equivalents. While the physical trade balance for nonmetallic minerals was a surplus of 2 million tonnes, the RTB showed a deficit of 6.6 million tonnes in 2007. For biomass, both the PTB and RTB indicated very small net imports, amounting to 0.8 and 1.1 million tonnes respectively. Between 1995 and 2007, the PTB increased by 30%, reaching 32 million tonnes, while the RTB saw a more modest increase of 10% to 62 million tonnes. This divergence is due to the more significant growth in Austria's exports and their associated raw material equivalents compared to its imports and their upstream requirements.
From 1995 to 2007, Austria's Domestic Material Consumption (DMC), calculated as domestic extraction plus imports minus exports, increased from 177 to 206 million tonnes, a 1.2-fold rise. This translates to a per capita increase from 22 to 25 tonnes annually. Nonmetallic minerals constituted the largest portion of DMC, accounting for 16 tonnes per capita (63%), followed by biomass (5 tonnes/capita, 20%) and fossil energy carriers (3 tonnes/capita, 13%). By incorporating Raw Material Equivalents (RME) into the DMC calculation, we arrive at Raw Material Consumption (RMC), which accounts for all raw materials utilized to meet Austria's final demand. In 2007, RMC totaled approximately 236 million tonnes, or 28 tonnes per capita. This figure was 1.1 times higher than DMC, representing an additional 4 tonnes per capita. Nonmetallic minerals remained the dominant component of RMC (52%), with metal ores (20%), biomass (18%), and fossil energy carriers (10%) following.
Austria's consumption patterns lead to the indirect use of resources in other countries. This practice, while shielding the domestic environment, results in increased global environmental burdens. For instance, in 2007, the raw material equivalents (RME) of Austria's fossil energy carrier imports were approximately 48 million tonnes, exceeding the physical import volume by 19 million tonnes. This translates to an additional 174 million tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions, bringing the total to 433 million tonnes, generated in exporting nations due to Austrian demand.
Our hybrid approach for calculating Austrian trade's raw material equivalents (RME) builds upon a single-region input-output (SRIO) model, applying Austria's domestic economic structure to its imported goods.
In 2000, Austria imported approximately 65 million tonnes of material. A comparison of calculation methods, as depicted in Figure 8, reveals significant differences in estimated upstream material inputs. If Austria were to substitute all its imports with domestic production using its existing input-output (IO) structures, a single-region IO (SRIO) approach suggests a lower material input requirement (78 million tonnes, a 1.2-fold increase over imports) compared to the actual imports. However, this scenario is hypothetical, assuming Austria possesses the capacity for all domestic production. In contrast, the hybrid approach, which more accurately accounts for international production, estimated the raw material equivalents of imports (RIM) at approximately 159 million tonnes. This figure is 2.4 times the import volume and twice the RIM calculated by the SRIO method. The SRIO method also underestimated the raw material equivalents of exports (REX). For the 38 million tonnes of material exported in 2000, SRIO estimated REX at 60 million tonnes, while the hybrid approach calculated it at 100 million tonnes. The hybrid method's advantage lies in its superior ability to capture the material inputs associated with non-competitive imports, particularly when the weight of the imported product differs substantially from the weight of the raw resource extracted, such as in the case of metal concentrates versus gross ore.
Similar hybrid approaches for calculating raw material equivalents (RME) have been employed in studies for the Czech Republic and Germany. The German study determined the material-use intensity matrix (F) by examining the relationship between direct material input (DMI) and total supply, while the Czech study focused on the link between domestic extraction (DE) and domestic supply. When comparing data from 2003, a consistent pattern emerged across these three Central European economies: raw material consumption (RMC) consistently exceeded domestic material consumption (DMC) by a factor ranging from 1.1 to 1.4. Germany and the Czech Republic showed comparable per capita material usage, with RMC figures of 23 and 22 tonnes per capita per year, respectively. Austria, however, displayed higher material consumption, with an RMC of 29 tonnes per capita per year. This disparity is largely attributed to Austria's significantly greater consumption of nonmetallic minerals. This difference in data quality and estimation methods also plays a role. Austria's statistical office recently adopted a new method for estimating construction mineral consumption, which has substantially reduced previous underestimations of extraction common in many countries. This enhanced data coverage led to an increase in Austria's total material use (DMC) by 4 tonnes, or a factor of 1.2, reaching 23 tonnes per capita in 2003.
Austria's economic structure is characterized by its small size and its significant reliance on international trade. In 2007, exports represented a substantial 59% of the nation's GDP, and between 1980 and 2010, export growth outpaced GDP growth by a factor of 1.6. This trade-intensive nature is further underscored by the fact that in 2008, imported materials constituted 34% of all materials utilized within Austria. Consequently, trade plays a crucial role in fulfilling the country's resource needs and sustaining its economic momentum. A key motivation for examining the raw material equivalents (RME) of trade was to investigate whether apparent productivity gains in industrialized nations might be an artifact of shifting material consumption to other countries. Our findings indicate that Austria's raw material consumption (RMC), which accounts for upstream inputs, is higher than its domestic material consumption (DMC). Specifically, in 2007, RMC exceeded DMC by 30 million tonnes, or 1.1 times. This disparity highlights that a portion of the material consumption linked to Austria's final demand actually occurs in other economies. Interestingly, the increasing reliance on resource imports from abroad has not led to a correspondingly dynamic increase in RMC. Instead, RMC has remained relatively stagnant throughout the study period, mirroring the trend observed in DMC. This suggests that resource productivity trends, when measured by RMC, would closely resemble those derived from DMC. Austria is a net importer of several key material categories, including biomass, metals, fossil energy carriers, and nonmetallic minerals. The production of these imported goods necessitates material inputs in their countries of origin. Simultaneously, imported raw materials are utilized in the manufacturing of exported goods. Therefore, while the outsourcing of production stages does occur, not all of this outsourcing is directly attributable to Austria's final demand. These results underscore a critical point: resource use indicators that disregard upstream requirements are susceptible to significant distortions. Consequently, strategies aimed at sustainable resource management, such as those being developed within the European Union that focus on material productivity improvements or reductions in material use, must incorporate the implications of production for trade. To achieve this, the development of robust indicators and reliable data is essential. This will enable accurate monitoring to determine if improvements in resource productivity or reductions in material consumption are genuine or merely a result of "leakage effects," where environmental burdens are shifted elsewhere. The complex global network of outsourcing, which intensifies with increasing global economic integration, means that RME data and indicators are vital for addressing issues of environmentally unequal exchange and the relocation of environmental burdens within the framework of sustainable trade policies.
To enhance our understanding of trade relationships through RME indicators, a globally applicable calculation method is needed, one that national statistical offices can integrate into their regular reporting. This paper introduces a hybrid approach that offers more reliable RME calculations than single-region input-output (SRIO) methods. However, this hybrid approach is not without its limitations, particularly its assumption of domestic production technology for competitive imports, which likely introduces significant inaccuracies. Our findings offer an initial glimpse into Austria's global material footprint and underscore the urgent need for improved RME calculation methodologies. Evaluating an economy's environmental impact and resource efficiency fundamentally requires considering the RME of its trade.
