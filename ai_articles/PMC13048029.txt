This study explored the efficacy of adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) in treating burn wounds, an area still undergoing development. A significant challenge in animal models for burn wound healing research is the confounding effect of wound contraction in loose skin. To address this, a novel rat model featuring a skin island burn wound was developed, circumventing interference from primary wound contraction. Deep partial thickness burn wounds were created by applying hot copper plates for 30 seconds. These skin islands were then assigned to two groups: a control group (n=9) received intradermal injections of PBS, while the ASCs-treated group (n=9) received 5 x 10^5 ASCs intradermally. Wound healing progression was monitored weekly for four weeks (1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th week). This involved measuring the mean wound area and conducting histological and immunohistochemical analyses after exposing the skin island. The findings demonstrated that local intradermal ASCs injection significantly improved burn wound healing across all time points, with a particularly notable effect during the initial two weeks (p < 0.05). Between weeks 3 and 4, the ASCs-treated groups showed a gradual increase in the percentage of live follicles compared to controls (p < 0.05). Furthermore, both vascular density and the proliferating cell nuclear antigen index were significantly elevated in the ASCs-treated groups. In conclusion, this research introduced an innovative rat model for studying burn wounds, minimizing the impact of wound contraction. The study revealed that local administration of ASCs not only enhanced burn wound recovery but also promoted angiogenesis and the regeneration of skin appendages.

While severe burns still cause significant morbidity and mortality, recent advancements in resuscitation, inhalation injury treatment, infection control, and critical care have enhanced outcomes for severely burned patients. This devastating injury impacts almost every organ.
Stem cell therapy, particularly involving mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), has recently gained prominence as a novel approach for treating conditions like burn injuries, hypertrophic scars, and sepsis. MSCs are recognized for their dual role in promoting local wound healing and modulating systemic inflammatory responses. Severe burn injuries trigger a sustained inflammatory state, which heightens vulnerability to infections and sepsis, potentially culminating in multiorgan failure and death. This inflammatory response is characterized by the release of both pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-10, granulocyte-colony stimulating factor). Elevated cytokine levels, frequently observed in burn patients, correlate with increased sepsis and mortality risks. MSCs are known to intervene at multiple stages of inflammation, particularly in early sepsis, by regulating a broad spectrum of inflammatory cytokines and preventing leukocyte infiltration into various organs. Beyond inflammation, MSCs also contribute to wound healing and skin appendage regeneration, partly through the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. Growing evidence from both fundamental and clinical research highlights the angiogenic capabilities of MSCs, demonstrated in treatments for ischemic limbs, myocardial infarction, and retinopathy. More recently, adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) have emerged as a promising cell therapy. ASCs are readily accessible in large quantities from fat tissue obtained via liposuction. These cells possess the ability to differentiate into various cell lineages and stimulate the secretion of numerous growth factors. Their therapeutic potential is currently under investigation for diverse conditions, including degenerative knee issues, renal failure, hepatic failure, and myocardial infarction. Furthermore, ASCs have been successfully utilized in tissue engineering applications for bone, auricular tissue, periodontal tissue, and tendons.
Despite the potential of ASCs in burn treatment, their efficacy, particularly for acute injuries, remains largely unconfirmed by extensive research. A systematic review by Conde´-Green et al. acknowledged the positive impact of fat grafts and ASCs on acute burn healing but highlighted the absence of conclusive evidence. While Bliley et al. demonstrated in a mouse model that human ASCs could enhance full-thickness burn wound healing by reducing wound area and promoting vascularity, collagen deposition, and adipogenesis, their study did not investigate the role of ASCs in regenerating skin appendages after burn injury. Currently, there is a clear need for cost-effective and efficient therapies that can accelerate burn wound healing, minimize excision areas, and prevent the progression of deep partial-thickness burns to full-thickness injuries.
This study aimed to examine how locally delivered Adipose-Derived Stem Cells (ASCs) impact the healing of deep partial-thickness burn wounds in rats. To address the challenge of primary wound contraction in rodent models, which can hinder accurate assessment of wound repair, we developed a novel skin island burn wound model. This innovative model allowed us to evaluate wound healing and skin appendage regeneration following local ASC injection.
Adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) for this study were obtained from the inguinal fat pads of Sprague Dawley rats (250–300 g, BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd), then isolated and cultured. Prior to local injection, these ASCs, specifically from passages three to six, underwent characterization. Flow cytometry (FACS Canto II; Becton Dickinson) was used to analyze cell surface markers, including CD11B, CD29, CD31, CD45, and CD90. Their ability to differentiate into bone and adipose tissue was confirmed using von Kossa/Oil red O staining (Sigma-Aldrich, Taiwan) with an Olympus IX51 light microscope.
An animal care committee (Taipei Veterans General Hospital, IACUC2014-163) approved this study. To create a nonlethal contact burn model, Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were first anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil (0.1 mL/100 mg). Burns were induced on the rats' backs using a 1 cm x 1 cm copper plate, heated to 90°C in boiling water, and applied for 30 seconds to a depilated skin area. Each skin island received three such burns, each matching the copper plate's dimensions. Following injury and treatment, these skin islands were surgically enclosed in subcutaneous pockets by suturing the surrounding skin, as depicted in Fig. 1D. This approach served two key purposes: it minimized the number of animals required and prevented confounding factors like wound contraction and epithelial migration from adjacent healthy skin due to the circumferential incision isolating the injured area.
To assess the impact of localized ASC injections on burn wound healing, a comparative analysis was conducted between control and ASC-treated wounds within the same animal. Each animal featured two skin islands, with each island containing three contact burn wounds. These wounds were then allocated to either a control group (n=3) or an ASCs-treated group (n=3), as depicted in Figure 1A. Following the burn induction protocol, control wounds received an intradermal injection of 0.2 mL of PBS after 30 minutes. Concurrently, ASC-treated wounds were intradermally injected with 5 x 10^5 ASCs suspended in 0.2 mL of PBS. Wound healing progression was monitored at weekly intervals (1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th weeks), with three rats evaluated at each time point, totaling 12 rats. At these intervals, the sutured skin island was opened, and its macroscopic appearance was documented via standardized digital photography. The mean wound area was subsequently quantified using ImageJ. For histological and immunocytochemical analyses, nine full-thickness skin samples from contact burn wounds were collected per group at each time point. These samples underwent H&E staining, as well as CD31 and PCNA staining. Follicular density, specifically the total and live follicle count, was determined under ×200 magnification. Additionally, vascular density and the expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) were assessed.
Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed paired Student's t-tests and one-way ANOVA in SPSS 12.0 (p < 0.05). Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± SD.
Prior to injection, rat ASCs, harvested and passaged three times, underwent flow cytometric analysis and differentiation assays. These purified cells exhibited high expression of CD29 and CD90, with low expression of CD11B, CD31, and CD45. Furthermore, the rat ASCs demonstrated differentiation into both adipogenic and osteogenic lineages.
Twelve animals were utilized in this study, each receiving six deep partial thickness burn wounds. These wounds were evenly split, with three designated as controls and three treated with ASCs. Healing progression was monitored weekly over a four-week period. Throughout the experiment, no animals experienced wound infections or systemic adverse reactions. Histopathological analysis confirmed the burns were indeed partial-thickness, as necrotic tissue reached the reticular dermis (Fig. 3).
The progression of burn wound healing was monitored by examining the gross appearance of the embedded skin islands after opening the sutured skin at weekly intervals (weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4). To facilitate comparison, the average area of the deep partial thickness contact burn wounds was quantified at these four distinct time points. Across all measured intervals, the ASCs-treated groups exhibited enhanced wound healing, evidenced by superior hair growth compared to control groups. This improvement was particularly notable during the initial two weeks (p < 0.05). Further assessing ASCs' impact on healing, follicular density was determined at ×200 magnification. A gradual increase in the percentage of live follicles was observed in the ASCs-treated groups between weeks 3 and 4, significantly surpassing control groups (p < 0.05).
To understand how ASCs promote wound healing, skin biopsy samples were analyzed using CD31 and PCNA staining. Angiogenesis, evidenced by a significantly higher count of CD31 positive cells and increased vascular density, was noted in the ASCs-treated group during weeks 3 and 4 post-injury (p < 0.001). This increase in vascular density in ASCs-treated groups was apparent as early as the first week of recovery. Additionally, the PCNA index, derived from counting PCNA-positive stromal cells (including endothelial cells and fibroblasts), showed a significant rise in the ASCs-treated group by the third week compared to the control group (p < 0.05).
Severe burn injuries lead to significant skin loss and systemic inflammation in patients. This inflammatory cycle, if unchecked, delays wound healing, raising the risk of sepsis or infection and increasing mortality. While advancements in burn care, including synthetic dressings, debridement, skin grafting, tissue-engineered substitutes, and growth factors, have improved treatment, effective methods for regenerating skin appendages and preventing hypertrophic scars are still needed.
The novel skin island burn wound model developed in this study addresses a significant challenge in rodent burn research: interference from wound contraction. Unlike previous models that relied on complex and often limited mechanical fixation devices or splints to maintain wound size, our skin island method offers a more convenient and easily implemented solution. While mechanical devices helped quantify wound response by stabilizing volume, they were complicated to apply, lacked durability, and couldn't prevent local cell influx. Our innovative approach, involving a circumferential incision, not only isolates the injured skin to prevent cell and appendage migration from healthy surrounding tissue but also provides protection against infection. This new methodology for evaluating wound healing is distinct and has not been previously described in the literature.
Our study showed that injecting ASCs locally into burn wounds in rats is a straightforward procedure, leading to enhanced wound healing and appendage regeneration. This suggests that local ASCs application could be a less invasive therapeutic option in the future. We propose that the injected ASCs either differentiate into endothelial cells or stimulate angiogenesis via paracrine effects, thereby promoting wound healing. This hypothesis stems from observing increased neoangiogenesis at the injury site after ASCs injection. Previous research also indicates ASCs' ability to differentiate into endothelial cells and improve angiogenesis in critical limb ischemia. Further investigation is needed to fully understand ASCs' role in angiogenesis.
A notable increase in hair follicle count was observed three weeks following ASCs injection. While stem cells have been reported to differentiate into appendage progenitor cells during skin regeneration, the regenerative effects of ASCs in this study are more likely attributable to their paracrine signaling. This is indirectly supported by PCNA staining, suggesting ASCs accelerate the proliferation and differentiation of appendage progenitor cells. ASCs are known to release angiogenic growth factors like HGF and VEGF, which promote tissue repair through angiogenesis and antiapoptotic actions. Furthermore, in vitro preincubation with bFGF causes ASCs to secrete numerous other growth factors. Therefore, we hypothesize that the observed skin appendage regeneration is driven by growth factors secreted by the transplanted ASCs.
The precise mechanisms by which ASCs promote tissue repair are not fully understood. While ASCs are known to positively influence outcomes by modulating inflammatory responses, such as suppressing TNF-α, IL-6R, and IL-12b, TNF-α itself also stimulates ASC proliferation. This stimulation leads to increased expression of proangiogenic factors (FGF-2, VEGF, IL-8, MCP-1) and inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6), thereby activating ASCs' angiogenic and regenerative capabilities. Furthermore, under acute hypoxic stress, ASCs can upregulate HIF-1, stimulating VEGF and enhancing resistance to oxygen deprivation.
The chosen delivery method for ASCs, whether local injection or systemic administration, often depends on the specific tissue or organ affected and the extent of the damage. Growing evidence suggests that the therapeutic benefits of ASCs primarily stem from their paracrine effects, rather than direct differentiation after local implantation. Earlier research from our lab demonstrated that ASCs can protect tissue from acute ischemic injury through their antioxidative and antiapoptotic properties. Furthermore, ASCs are known to release soluble factors like CINC-1, which promote cell survival, tissue repair, and appendage recovery by activating ERK1/2 and Akt phosphorylation. Consequently, local injection of ASCs holds promise for enhancing skin appendage and wound healing.
The effectiveness of ASCs therapy hinges on delivering enough cells to the injury site, regardless of the administration method. Systemic delivery, such as venous injections, has shown promise for conditions like bladder dysfunction, erectile dysfunction, and chronic kidney disease. Intraarterial injection is another successful systemic approach, with examples including suppressing kidney transplant rejection via renal arteries and treating cerebral artery occlusion through carotid artery injection. These delivery methods rely on ASCs naturally migrating to the affected area.
The number of Adipose-Derived Stem Cells (ASCs) used and the resulting outcomes from local injections have shown variability across studies. In the current research, 5 × 10^5 ASCs injected into the subcutaneous space persisted at the injection sites throughout the observation period, gradually migrating into deeper subcutaneous fat layers. Other studies have reported differing findings regarding ASC dosage and efficacy. For instance, Bliley et al. observed that a single application of 6.8 × 10^6 ASCs via subeschar injection effectively enhanced vascularity, collagen deposition, and dermal adipogenesis. Conversely, Loder et al. demonstrated improved burn wound healing, specifically in wound area, depth, and reduced apoptotic activity, with a local injection of 1 × 10^6 ASCs in a mouse model. Chang et al. also highlighted the therapeutic benefits of autologous ASCs for acute burn wounds in rats, using 5 × 10^6 ASCs through local injection. Kuo et al. found a significant reduction in wound healing time in diabetic rats with full-thickness wounds after subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10^7 ASCs. Additionally, Hanson et al. indicated that intradermal injection of 1 × 10^6 ASCs could improve partial-thickness cutaneous wound healing in a porcine model. However, not all studies have yielded positive results. Karimi et al. reported no significant improvement in acute burn wound healing with 1 × 10^6 ASCs. Such inconsistent outcomes might stem from factors like disruption of the delicate microenvironment, inflammation, and the multifocal nature of many disorders. Consequently, optimizing cell delivery methods is crucial for successful cell-based therapies. Approaches like hydrogel and collagen scaffold delivery of stem cells have already shown promise in significantly enhancing wound healing. Further research is needed to explore various cell-delivery routes, including cell-seeded skin substitutes, to improve treatment efficacy.
This study has several limitations, beyond just the method of cell delivery and the rate at which cells engraft. The size and depth of the burn wounds also present a constraint. Because the immune response can vary significantly between minor and major burn injuries, future research should focus on larger and deeper wounds to more accurately reflect clinical situations and better assess the therapeutic potential of ASCs. While our findings suggest that ASCs enhance wound healing through angiogenesis and skin appendage regeneration, the precise mechanisms remain to be fully understood and were outside the scope of this particular study. Specifically, it is not yet clear whether ASCs directly differentiate into endothelial cells to form new blood vessels, or if they contribute to neovascularization and healing through other supportive roles at the wound site. Our laboratory is currently pursuing further investigations to clarify this point.
Our study, utilizing a novel skin island burn wound rat model, indicates that local ASCs injection offers therapeutic benefits for burn wounds. This treatment improved wound recovery and enhanced skin appendage regeneration. The underlying healing mechanism likely involves ASCs stimulating neoangiogenesis and inhibiting apoptosis through paracrine effects.
