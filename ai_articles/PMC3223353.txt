Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are known to influence cancer development and progression. However, there is conflicting evidence regarding whether CAFs, beyond epigenetic modifications, also experience somatic genetic alterations and if these alterations contribute to carcinogenesis and tumor advancement. To investigate this, we employed multiparameter DNA flow cytometry, flow-sorting, and 6K SNP-arrays. Our study focused on cervical cancer-associated stromal cell fractions (n=57) derived from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples, examining DNA aneuploidy, S-phase percentage, loss of heterozygosity (LOH), and copy number alterations (CNAs). The presence of CAFs was confirmed in tissue sections. Microsatellite analysis was also utilized to validate LOH findings. Immunohistochemistry for smooth muscle actin and vimentin confirmed the presence of CAFs in all tested samples. Crucially, our analysis revealed no evidence of DNA aneuploidy or somatic genetic alterations within the vimentin-positive stromal cell fractions. In contrast, the keratin-positive epithelial cell fractions exhibited high frequencies of DNA content abnormalities (43/57) along with significant CNAs and LOH. Microsatellite analysis further corroborated the LOH hot-spots identified on chromosomes 3p, 4p, and 6p. Based on these findings, we conclude that stromal cell fractions from cervical carcinomas are diploid, genetically stable, and possess a genotype indistinguishable from patient-matched normal tissue. Our results suggest that stromal genetic changes do not appear to play a role in cervical carcinogenesis and progression. Furthermore, the stromal cell fraction of cervical carcinomas can serve as a valuable reference for large retrospective studies using archival FFPE tissues when normal reference tissue is unavailable.

Carcinoma-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are a significant cellular component of epithelial tumor stroma. Research indicates that CAFs interact with tumor cells, contributing to tumor progression and metastasis, and possess distinct biological characteristics compared to normal fibroblasts. CAFs, which resemble myofibroblasts and express vimentin and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), are thought to originate from bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells. Their secretion of stromal cell-derived factor 1α (SDF-1α) also strongly promotes tumor growth and angiogenesis.
Epigenetic changes in the stroma can impact clinical outcomes. For instance, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, myofibroblastic SMA expression correlated with aggressive tumors. Researchers also found gene expression profiles in fibroblasts from various oral tissue stages that mirrored tumor progression. These profiles highlighted pathways like TNF-α and TGF-β, with high ACTA2 (encoding α-SMA) expression. Similarly, breast cancer expression signatures showed a strong link to clinical results.
Studies using microarrays have examined cervical cancer stromal cell fractions at the expression level. As the epithelial component progresses from CIN I to CIN III, the underlying stromal cells change their gene expression patterns. A pro-invasive gene signature was identified during the transition from CIN III to squamous cell carcinoma.
Previous research highlights the significant involvement of stroma and CAFs in tumor development and progression. Beyond epigenetic modifications, studies have reported genetic alterations like copy number alterations (CNAs) and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in CAFs from various cancers, including bladder, breast, and colon cancer, as well as colitis ulcerosa. These findings suggest a crucial role for these modified fibroblasts in carcinogenesis and tumor evolution.
The question of whether cervical cancer stroma fibroblasts accumulate somatic genetic alterations remains unexplored. It is unclear if such changes, if they occur, influence tumor development and maintenance. This leaves open the possibility that cervical CAFs possess a normal genotype and are solely subject to epigenetic modifications, or conversely, that they acquire distinct genetic alterations compared to the epithelial tumor cells. Understanding the genetic status of cervical CAFs could impact our comprehension of tumor behavior, therapeutic strategies, and the overall understanding of this prevalent cancer.
This study examined somatic genetic alterations and DNA content in stromal cell fractions from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) cervical carcinomas. Prior to 6K GoldenGate SNP-array analysis for chromosomal aberrations, copy number alterations (CNAs), and loss of heterozygosity (LOH), we employed FACS to isolate pure stromal and epithelial tumor subpopulations. Patient-matched normal endometrium served as a reference. This novel methodology allowed for the parallel determination of DNA content, genotype, and genomic status in both stromal and epithelial fractions from FFPE cervical carcinomas.
Cervical carcinomas were sourced from the FFPE-tissue bank at Leiden University Medical Center. All samples were managed in accordance with ethical guidelines set by the Dutch Federation of Medical Sciences for the secondary use of human tissue.
To ensure reliable DNA aneuploidy detection by flow cytometry, which is influenced by G0G1 population CV and aneuploid cell percentage, we selected 57 FFPE cervical carcinoma samples. These samples exhibited DNA histograms with a keratin-positive G0G1 population CV of 6.0% or less. The ploidy status of the stromal cell fractions was unknown at the time of selection.
A pathologist reviewed H&E-stained paraffin sections from all samples, marking the tumor nodule and its intra-tumoral stroma. Before flow cytometry, surgical knives were used to trim normal epithelium and excess stromal or smooth muscle tissue, guided by the marked sections. This enrichment process focused the carcinoma tissue on both tumor and associated stroma, preventing the inclusion of stromal cells from outside the tumor nodule.
To prepare cell suspensions, six to ten 60 μm sections were obtained from each paraffin block of 57 cervical carcinomas, plus an additional case with poorly differentiated tumor cells, along with matching lymph nodes and/or endometrium. A 4 μm section was then taken and stained with H&E to confirm the presence of tumor tissue. The remaining thick tissue sections were processed for flow cytometry. This involved dewaxing the sections, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 80°C for 60 minutes. Subsequently, the sections were dissociated using a collagenase/dispase mixture at 37°C. The resulting cells were then harvested, washed, counted, and kept on ice until they were used.
To prepare cells for analysis, immunocytochemistry was performed with slight modifications to established protocols. Five million cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with a mixture of monoclonal antibodies in PBS/1% BSA/0.05% Tween 20 (PBATw). This mixture included anti-keratin antibodies MNF116 (10 μg/ml) and AE1/AE3 (25 μg/ml), along with anti-vimentin V9-2b (1:5 dilution). The following day, cells underwent two washes with ice-cold PBATw and centrifugation at 750 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with premixed FITC- or RPE-labelled secondary reagents, specifically Goat F(ab2)′ anti-mouse IgG1-FITC and goat F(ab2)′ anti-mouse IgG2b-RPE, diluted 1:100 in PBATw. After a 30-minute incubation on ice, cells were washed twice more with ice-cold PBATw. Finally, cells were incubated with 1,000 μl of NST DNA staining solution containing 50 μM DAPI. The cells were kept at room temperature for 30 minutes before overnight storage at 4°C. For compensation controls, the concentration of primary antibodies was reduced to one-fifth of that used for sample labeling, and the DAPI concentration was lowered to 10 μM to minimize cell loss.
To confirm the presence of carcinoma-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immunohistochemistry was conducted on 4 μm paraffin sections. These sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated before antigen retrieval using citrate. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then quenched with hydrogen peroxide. Following washing in PBS, the slides were incubated overnight at room temperature with primary antibodies. A SMA-specific antibody (clone 1A4, 1:80 dilution) was used to detect smooth muscle actin. Vimentin and keratin 13 expression were assessed using clone V9 (1:80 dilution) and clone 1C7 (1:40 dilution) respectively. After further PBS washes, the standard three-step ABC method was applied. Immune complexes were visualized using diaminobenzidine, with Mayer's hematoxylin serving as a counterstain. Cytoplasmic brown staining indicated a positive result. Negative control slides were prepared without the primary antibody.
For data acquisition, an LSRII flow cytometer was utilized, equipped with 488 nm and 355 nm lasers. A gate was established to gather 20,000 keratin-positive single cell events. To isolate single cells for analysis, a pulse-processor was employed. FITC and RPE fluorescence signals were collected using specific detectors (BLUE 488 BP 530/30-A and BLUE 488 BP 575/26-A, respectively). The resulting data file encompassed all acquired events.
WinList 6.0 and ModFit 3.2.1 software were employed for DNA index (DI) and coefficient of variation (CV) calculations. Post-acquisition spectral cross-talk correction was performed using N-color compensation, adhering to manufacturer guidelines and avoiding hyperlog transformation or log bias.
To minimize the impact of fragmented or damaged nuclei on genomic analysis, cell sorting was specifically focused on the G0G1 phase populations. This included vimentin-positive, keratin-negative stromal cells and vimentin-negative, keratin-positive tumor cells. Utilizing a FACSAria I flow-sorter, cells were excited by 488 nm and 407 nm laser lines at 40 psi with a 100 μm nozzle, operating at approximately 52.4 kHz in purity mode. This process typically yielded 800,000 cells per 5 ml tube. Detector and filter settings were configured for FITC, RPE, and DAPI fluorescence. Doublets and aggregates were excluded from sorting by employing a specific dot plot gate. Following sorting, cells were centrifuged at 4,000 x g for 10 minutes prior to DNA extraction.
Following overnight proteinase K digestion at 56°C, DNA was purified using Promega Protein Precipitation solution. The DNA concentration was subsequently determined with the Picogreen method.
Genotyping was conducted using SNP arrays at the Leiden Genome Technology Center, following established protocols with slight adjustments. For each sample, 0.4–1.0 μg of DNA was utilized for a multi-use activation step before being resuspended in 60 μl. Genotypes and GCS scores were obtained using Beadstudio 2.3 software. To ensure data quality, only genotypes with a GCS score exceeding 0.5 were included. Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) was assessed by comparing tumor samples to a reference. Specifically, informative SNPs, which were heterozygous in the reference, were examined for homozygosity in the tumor. The presence of two adjacent homozygous informative SNPs in the tumor sample indicated LOH.
Copy number profiles, allele-specific intensities, and DNA index were combined using the beadarraySNP package.
To assess genomic alterations, the lesser allele intensity ratio (LAIR) was calculated for informative single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). A LAIR value near 1 indicates that both alleles of an SNP contribute equally to the total intensity in the tumor, mirroring the reference sample. Conversely, a LAIR close to 0 signifies a loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in the tumor, where one allele's signal is absent. Intermediate LAIR values suggest allelic imbalances. Regions with a LAIR close to 1 are expected to have an even copy number. Subsequently, a segmentation process identifies genomic regions with consistent copy number and LAIR. The copy numbers assigned to these segments are then adjusted based on the DNA index measured by flow cytometry. This adjustment allows for the determination of the copy number for both alleles within each genomic region, defining the allelic state.
To confirm Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH) findings from SNP-array analysis in the keratin-positive tumor fraction, genomic DNA was isolated from 12 flow-sorted carcinoma samples exhibiting LOH on chromosomes 3p14.2, 4p15.2, and/or 6p21.3. Patient-matched, vimentin-positive normal endometrium fractions served as the reference. Ten informative microsatellite primer pairs were selected, including specific markers for the targeted chromosomal regions. For each primer set, the forward primer was fluorescently labeled with either FAM for the keratin-positive fraction or JOE for the vimentin-positive fraction, while the reverse primers remained unlabeled. Standard polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were conducted using 10 nanograms of DNA per reaction. The resulting fluorescent PCR products were analyzed on an ABI 310 automated sequencer with a size standard, and results were interpreted using GeneScan 3.1 software. All selected markers yielded interpretable data.
Flow cytometry reliably assesses clonal expansion in carcinomas. DNA content analysis reveals cell cycle phase contributions and allows calculation of tumor cell DNA index (DI).
The stromal cell fractions exhibited an average coefficient of variation (CV) of 3.7% ± 1.2% in their DNA histograms, indicating good quality. Despite this, no cell populations with abnormal DNA content were identified in any of the 57 stromal samples. Patient-matched normal endometrium and lymph node samples, used as unimodal controls, showed comparable CVs (3.3% ± 1.0% and 3.8% ± 1.3%, respectively) and also lacked detectable DNA aneuploidy via flow cytometry. However, the study's methodology was capable of detecting DNA aneuploidy in stromal fractions, as evidenced by a rare case (sample-58) that contained poorly differentiated tumor cells.
Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significantly lower percentage of cells in the S-phase within the tumor stromal cells, normal endometrium, and lymph nodes compared to the epithelial cells. Specifically, stromal cell fractions exhibited an average S-phase percentage of 1.4% (± 1.8%), while epithelial cells showed a considerably higher average of 14.6% (± 8.1%). This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0001). Normal endometrium and lymph node samples also displayed very low S-phase percentages, at 0.1% (± 0.3%) and 0.6% (± 1.0%), respectively. These findings are summarized in Table 1, which details various flow cytometric parameters for different cell populations.
Figure 1 illustrates the process of identifying DNA content abnormalities within the vimentin-positive, keratin-negative stromal cell fractions of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) cervical carcinomas using multi-parameter DNA flow cytometry. Cells, isolated from 60 μm sections after antigen retrieval and enzymatic dissociation, were simultaneously labeled for keratin (FITC), vimentin (RPE), and DNA (DAPI) before analysis on an LSRII flow cytometer. Patient-matched normal endometrium served as a DNA diploid reference. In cervical carcinoma samples, the epithelial fraction (red) and the stromal fraction (green) could be clearly distinguished using anti-keratin and anti-vimentin antibodies, as shown in panels (a) and (e) respectively. When the analysis was gated on the vimentin-positive, keratin-negative stromal cell fractions (indicated as 's' in green), unimodal DNA histograms were consistently observed (panels b and f). These histograms were of good quality, yet no DNA aneuploidy was detectable in these stromal fractions. The DNA histograms from the stromal cell fractions were highly comparable to those obtained from the patient-matched normal endometrium samples (panels c and g). In contrast, DNA aneuploidy was readily identified within the vimentin-negative, keratin-positive epithelial cell fractions. For instance, sample-22 (panel d) displayed a keratin-positive DNA aneuploid population with a DNA index (DI) of 0.94, although some keratin-positive cells also expressed vimentin. Sample-16 (panel h) exhibited a bimodal DNA histogram in its epithelial fraction, showing both a DNA aneuploid population (DI = 1.91) and a DNA pseudo-diploid population (DI = 0.99). Examples of DNA histograms with low background noise from both the stromal compartments and patient-matched normal endometrium are provided for sample-22 and sample-16, respectively.
Conversely, 14% of keratin-positive epithelial fractions were DNA pseudo-diploid, while 75% exhibited DNA aneuploidy. Ten of these aneuploid samples were bimodal, presenting a secondary, minor epithelial population that was either pseudo-diploid or aneuploid.
To confirm the presence of carcinoma-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) within the tissue samples, immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on trimmed tissue sections using antibodies against smooth muscle actin (SMA) and vimentin. Figure 2 illustrates two examples from FFPE cervical carcinomas, labeled A and B. In these examples, the stromal cells situated between epithelial tumor cells (marked as 'S') exhibited strong expression of both SMA and vimentin, while the tumor cells themselves (marked as 'T') were negative for these markers. Additionally, some cells infiltrating within the tumor also stained positive for vimentin. Based on SMA staining, twenty of the fifty-seven samples were selected for further analysis. These selected samples were characterized by a low proportion of infiltrating cells (30% or less) and a high proportion of CAFs (greater than 70%). These selected samples were then subjected to flow cytometry and cell sorting. The IHC results demonstrated that these stromal fibroblasts strongly expressed SMA and vimentin, consistent with myofibroblast characteristics.
Flow-sorted G0G1 fractions of stromal and epithelial cells were processed for DNA extraction and subsequent analysis using the GoldenGate 6K SNP-array. Patient-matched normal endometrium, vimentin-positive and keratin-negative, served as the reference. Genome-wide genomic profiles from the sorted stromal cells were then compared against these patient-matched controls and the keratin-positive tumor cell fractions.
Genotype concordance between cervical cancer stromal cells and paired endometrial samples exceeded 99.9%. Integrating the DNA index allowed for true copy number estimation using the LAIR-analysis method.
Analysis of the stromal cell fractions from cervical cancer using a 6K SNP-array revealed no detectable copy number alterations (CNAs) or loss of heterozygosity (LOH). Representative LAIR (lesser allele intensity ratio) plots illustrating this finding for the stromal compartments of two DNA aneuploid cervical carcinomas, sample-7 (DI = 0.92) and sample-10 (DI = 1.97), are presented in Figure 3b and 3d, focusing on chromosomes 10-15 and 21. In stark contrast, the keratin-positive epithelial cell fractions from these same cases exhibited substantial numbers of CNAs and LOH, including copy-neutral LOH. Further examples of comprehensive LAIR analyses for the epithelial fractions are available in Supplementary Figure S2. The LAIR analysis, performed on DNA extracted from flow-sorted cervical stromal and carcinoma cells, utilized DNA from flow-sorted vimentin-positive endometrium cells as a patient-matched control for genotyping. Figure 3 displays representative LAIR plots for chromosomes 10-15 and 21, comparing the epithelial and stromal fractions of two DNA aneuploid cervical carcinomas: sample-7 (DI = 0.92) and sample-10 (DI = 1.97). As expected, numerous alterations were identified within the epithelial cell fractions, consistent with findings in Supplementary Figure S2. The notation A, AB, or AAB, indicated in blue, represents the number of copies and the ratio between alleles, respectively. LOH, marked by red dotted lines, was observed on chromosomes 11, 13, and 14 in both samples (Figure 3a and 3c). Notably, copy-neutral LOH was detected on chromosomes 13 and 15 in sample-7 (Figure 3a), against a DNA aneuploid background with a DI of 0.92. Similarly, in sample-10 (Figure 3c), copy-neutral LOH was found on chromosomes 11 and 14, relative to a highly DNA aneuploid background with a DI of 1.97. Allelic imbalances were also present on chromosomes 11, 14, and 15 (indicated as AAB, AAAABB, and AAB, respectively) in sample-10 (Figure 3c). In sample-7 (Figure 3a), a segment of chromosomes 10 and 21 displayed both two and one copies, with clear evidence of imbalance (2HetL and 1HetL), potentially attributable to mixtures of AB and AA, and AB and A genotypes. Conversely, the vimentin-positive stromal cell fractions (Figure 3b and 3d) from both samples maintained a normal heterozygous (AB) genotype across all chromosomes, with no detectable CNAs or LOH. These findings are further supported by analyses presented in Supplementary Figure S3.
The remaining 37 tumors were analyzed as no somatic genetic alterations were found in the initial stromal fractions. These additional samples also showed no detectable CNAs or LOH. Supplementary Figure S3 provides LAIR analysis for all stromal and matched endometrium cell fractions.
Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) was frequently observed in the epithelial cell fractions, particularly affecting chromosomes 3p, 4p, and 6p, with losses occurring in 47.4%, 40.5%, and 45.4% of cases, respectively. To validate these findings, microsatellite analysis was performed on flow-sorted epithelial and stromal cell fractions, along with matched normal endometrium from 12 samples, using informative markers. The LOH identified in the epithelial cells by LAIR-analysis was consistently confirmed by microsatellite analysis across all 12 tested samples. Figure 4 illustrates these results, showcasing data from samples 10, 56, and 7 using markers D3S1313, D4S1546, and D6S265. In stark contrast to the epithelial fractions, which displayed only a single allele, the tumor stromal cell fractions, mirroring the vimentin-positive endometrium cells, demonstrated retention of heterozygosity at all tested loci. This was evident in the presence of both alleles in their profiles, as depicted in Figure 4 when comparing stromal/endometrium fractions with epithelial fractions. The microsatellite analysis further revealed a complete loss of one allele in the epithelial samples, indicating clonal expansion of these alterations and pure tumor populations due to FACS. Conversely, no LOH was detected in the vimentin-positive, keratin-negative stromal cell fractions of the cervical carcinomas or the patient-matched normal endometrium.
Our study, employing DNA content flow-sorting and SNP-arrays, found no evidence of somatic genetic alterations in cervical carcinoma stromal cell fractions. These stromal fractions were demonstrated to be DNA diploid and genetically indistinguishable from patient-matched normal endometrium. Consequently, we conclude that somatic genetic alterations within cervical cancer stroma do not contribute to the development or progression of the disease.
Contrary to previous research indicating frequent genomic aberrations like LOH and CNAs in various tumor stroma types [12, 24–27], our study found no evidence of DNA aneuploidy in the stromal fractions of 57 cervical cancer samples. Given that LOH and CNAs are linked to DNA aneuploidy [28, 29], our multi-parameter flow cytometry should have detected even small aneuploid populations. To demonstrate our technique's sensitivity in identifying such subpopulations, we included an additional sample with a minor fraction of poorly differentiated cells that had lost keratin expression (Supplementary Figure S1).
Prior research on somatic genetic alterations within tumor stroma has yielded conflicting results. While a limited number of studies, primarily utilizing microdissected fresh or frozen breast and ovarian cancer samples, have reported a lack of such alterations, earlier investigations employing microdissected formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) archival samples have indicated their presence. This discrepancy raises concerns that genetic changes observed in CAFs from FFPE tissues might be artifacts stemming from low DNA yields and degradation inherent to microdissection of such samples. Low DNA input and degraded DNA have been implicated as potential sources of spurious genomic findings. In contrast, our study demonstrates that our preparation techniques, combined with fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), enable the isolation of sufficient DNA from FFPE tissues for SNP-array analysis. This process effectively avoids the introduction of artificial loss of heterozygosity (LOH) or copy number changes, which can arise from low DNA yields or whole-genome amplification. The high cell yields obtained through our collagenase/dispase dissociation method for FFPE tissues, coupled with FACS's ability to separate tumor subpopulations based on DNA index, facilitate the investigation of intratumoral heterogeneity. Moreover, by restricting sorting to the G0G1 population, we exclude damaged or fragmented nuclei, thereby enhancing the quality of SNP-array genomic data. This research also establishes the feasibility of conducting such studies on FFPE archival samples using multi-parameter DNA flow-sorting and SNP-arrays. This approach is particularly valuable for retrospective studies where FFPE tissue is often the only available material, and obtaining matched normal reference tissue can be challenging. FACS effectively addresses this limitation by generating patient-matched tumor and control DNA with identical handling and storage conditions.
Our findings align with the established understanding of cervical carcinogenesis, which posits that the epithelial component of cervical carcinomas harbors the HPV-induced cellular and molecular alterations, while the stromal fraction remains free of this oncogenic virus. Previous research indicates that epigenetic modifications within the stroma play a role in the development and progression of cervical cancer. Within the tumor microenvironment, alongside infiltrating lymphocytes expressing tumor-associated cytokines and chemokines, fibroblasts can be observed with altered morphology and protein expression. Notably, smooth muscle actin (SMA) is abundantly present in the vicinity of tumor nodules, signifying the presence of myofibroblasts. Similar observations of elevated SMA expression have been reported in oral squamous cell carcinomas, where it correlated significantly with disease mortality. Our previous work demonstrated elevated levels of activated transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) in cervical cancer stroma. Activated TGF-β can account for the high SMA expression, as it is known to induce SMA production. Moreover, TGF-β is a potent inhibitor of cell cycle progression in normal cells, which may explain the low percentage of stromal cells in the S-phase, comparable to that found in normal endometrium and lymph nodes. These observations are consistent with findings in genetically unstable oral squamous cell carcinomas, where cultured CAFs exhibited reduced proliferative activity compared to other fibroblast subtypes, and cell cycle-related genes were downregulated. In contrast, cancer cells can circumvent TGF-β-mediated cell cycle arrest, as evidenced by the significantly higher average S-phase (approximately 15%) observed in epithelial cell fractions. It is likely that CAFs do not proliferate locally but rather represent a population of fibroblasts that have proliferated and migrated from outside the tumor nodule, subsequently differentiating into myofibroblasts. This is supported by studies on the origin of murine CAFs, which suggest that at least 25% originate in the bone marrow and migrate to the tumor stroma. Collectively, these findings challenge the notion of local proliferation within the tumor microenvironment and clonal expansion of stromal cells harboring somatic genetic changes. The complete loss of an allele, as detected by microsatellite analysis, is also difficult to reconcile with the heterogeneous composition of tumor stroma and the use of microdissection for cell population purification. Such findings can only be explained by the clonal expansion of a cell population entirely devoid of unaffected cells. For DNA isolation in previous studies, tumor stroma nodules up to 5 mm from the primary tumor were selected. Tumor stroma, in addition to fibroblasts and CAFs, is primarily composed of varying numbers of leukocytes. This is a common characteristic not only in cervical cancer but also in breast, colon, and ovarian carcinomas, among others. The presence of these genetically normal cells can hinder the detection of absolute loss of heterozygosity (LOH), leading to measurements of allelic imbalances.
While the GoldenGate SNP-array employed in this study has a resolution of approximately 500 kb, this is adequate for detecting large-scale chromosomal alterations, such as whole chromosome arm losses or changes affecting multiple microsatellite markers in tumor stroma, as reported in prior research. Our SNP-array analyses unequivocally demonstrated that all epithelial cell fractions, including those classified as DNA pseudo-diploid, exhibited genetic alterations. Further confirmation through microsatellite analysis corroborated the loss of heterozygosity (LOH) identified by SNP-arrays at known cervical cancer hotspots on chromosomes 3p, 4p, and 6p within the epithelial cell fractions. Crucially, this microsatellite analysis also failed to detect any genetic alterations in the stromal cell populations. Moreover, the epithelial fractions displayed DNA aneuploidy in 75% of cases, a finding consistent with multiparameter flow cytometry studies on fresh/frozen samples, thereby validating our methodology for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues.
Our study definitively demonstrates, using DNA content flow cytometry, FACS, and SNP-arrays, that cervical cancer stromal cells are diploid and genetically stable. Their genotype is indistinguishable from matched normal endometrium. This finding is significant for understanding cervical carcinogenesis, suggesting epigenetic rather than genetic stromal alterations are involved. Moreover, our methods enable reliable genotyping of sorted tumor subpopulations from FFPE archival samples. Crucially, the stromal compartment of cervical carcinomas can serve as a DNA-matched control, facilitating extensive retrospective studies of archival FFPE tissues lacking normal reference material.
