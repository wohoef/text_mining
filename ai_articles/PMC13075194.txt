Tractor overturns represent the primary cause of fatalities on US farms, a risk that can be mitigated by rollover protection structures (ROPS). To combat these preventable deaths, a ROPS rebate program was initiated in New York in 2006. The program's effectiveness led to its expansion into six other states, culminating in its establishment as the National ROPS Rebate Program (NRRP) in 2017. This study aimed to assess the NRRP's implementation success by examining both immediate and delayed outcomes related to ROPS adoption. Furthermore, it sought to identify which elements of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) were associated with these outcomes. To achieve these objectives, stakeholders engaged in the NRRP implementation were surveyed at four distinct intervals, starting from the program's launch and continuing every six months. These surveys assessed 14 pertinent CFIR constructs. By analyzing the correlations between specific CFIR survey items, which represented these constructs, and three key outcome measures—program intakes, funding progress, and tractor retrofits—the study identified CFIR survey items that could predict these outcomes. The findings revealed eight CFIR survey items with a strong correlation (rho ≥0.50) to at least one of the three outcome measures. These items pertained to four constructs: access to knowledge and information, leadership engagement, engaging in fundraising and funding requests, and reflecting and evaluating. Ultimately, this research offers valuable insights for the ongoing implementation of the NRRP and can inform similar implementation initiatives and the application of the CFIR across diverse contexts.

Agricultural workers experience exceptionally high rates of fatal occupational injuries, with 24.0 fatalities per 100,000 workers, significantly exceeding the all-worker rate of 3.5. The agriculture, forestry, and fishing sector also reports substantial non-fatal injury rates, with the Bureau of Labor Statistics indicating 5.7 injuries per 100 workers. This figure is likely an underestimate, as injuries are considerably underreported within these professions. Consequently, agricultural health and safety are crucial elements of public health initiatives.
Despite efforts to find solutions for agricultural health and safety problems, translating these solutions into practice has received limited research attention. This study noted a lack of published work on scaling up agricultural safety interventions. A significant obstacle to this type of work is the incomplete understanding of how to effectively translate research into practice and the reasons behind successful or unsuccessful implementation of occupational health and safety innovations. This gap in knowledge hinders the widespread adoption of proven safety measures.
Implementation science has seen significant growth in clinical medicine and public health, despite lagging progress in agricultural safety and health. Researchers in these fields have focused on creating frameworks and models to address critical questions about implementation. One such model is the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), designed to determine what works, where, and why. This study employs the CFIR to quantitatively assess the national expansion of an agricultural safety initiative. The goal is to generate valuable insights into the implementation of evidence-based agricultural safety interventions.
The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) was created for health services, encompassing clinical medicine and public health. Its development involved synthesizing 19 existing theories, frameworks, and models for implementation efforts into a unified structure. Researchers chose the CFIR as their evaluation tool because of its thoroughness and its adaptability to various contexts.
The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) comprises five core domains: individual characteristics, inner setting, outer setting, intervention characteristics, and process. Supplemental domains, namely implementation outcomes and client outcomes, further elaborate on implementation efforts. Within each CFIR domain, various constructs are defined to detail specific aspects of the implementation process.
The CFIR framework includes several domains. The "individual characteristics" domain, with five constructs, focuses on the qualities of people involved in implementation. The "inner setting" domain, comprising five constructs and nine sub-constructs, examines interactions and the immediate environment. Lastly, the "outer setting" domain, with four constructs, addresses external influences from stakeholders and networks beyond the core implementation team.
The intervention characteristics domain, as its name implies, encompasses eight constructs detailing the intervention's qualities during implementation. The process domain, comprising four constructs and sub-constructs, logically outlines the implementation's progression. Lastly, implementation and client outcomes, with five and two constructs respectively, clarify the success of the implementation.
The CFIR's construct definitions and guiding questions have undergone individual validation over time, stemming from its development from prior implementation frameworks. Furthermore, a systematic review of its application has confirmed the CFIR's validity as a framework. However, prior to this study, which developed the survey instrument used here, the CFIR had not been applied within the agricultural safety domain. Therefore, its usefulness in this specific setting remains to be fully established.
Tractor overturns are the primary cause of fatalities in agriculture. Rollover protection structures (ROPS) can prevent these deaths by creating a safety zone for operators during an overturn. Despite ROPS becoming standard on new tractors in 1985, a significant portion of US farm tractors, estimated at 40–50%, still lack these devices. This is largely attributed to the long operational life of tractors, coupled with the substantial cost (around $1200 per kit) and effort required to retrofit older models. Furthermore, farmers' perceptions of personal risk, or lack thereof, can make it challenging to justify the expense of retrofitting.
Launched in New York in 2006, the ROPS rebate program followed extensive formative research into farmer barriers and motivators for installing these safety devices. An early pilot study demonstrated that a combination of targeted messaging, technical assistance, and financial incentives was crucial for increasing the adoption of ROPS-equipped tractors. Over the subsequent decade, research has consistently affirmed the program's efficacy, effectiveness, and cost-efficiency.
The ROPS rebate program's success led to its expansion into six other states between 2010 and 2016, including Vermont, Pennsylvania, New Hampshire, Wisconsin, Massachusetts, and Minnesota. To date, over 2800 tractors have been retrofitted. Annual surveys of program participants revealed that farmers self-reported 17 instances where ROPS likely saved tractor operators from fatal overturns. Additionally, over 220 close calls, near overturns, and falling object incidents were reported.
A national effort to expand ROPS rebate programs began in 2014 with the formation of the National Tractor Safety Coalition (NTSC). This coalition, comprised of researchers, industry partners, farmers, corporations, media, and various organizations, was led by a 15-representative steering committee. After several years of collaboration, the group successfully launched the national ROPS rebate program (NRRP) in June 2017.
The National ROPS Rebate Program (NRRP) is designed with three core elements: a toll-free hotline and website, a social marketing campaign to boost farmer engagement, and 70% rebates for ROPS purchase and installation. While national funding for rebates is limited, states are urged to secure their own allocations. This limited national funding currently restricts the social marketing campaign to states that have already established their own rebate funds, meaning only the hotline and website are accessible in other states. The ultimate goal is for all 50 states to have access to all NRRP components, including sufficient rebate funding.
A two-part strategy aims to foster adoption and sustainment of the NRRP intervention's components for full implementation. This strategy involves National Tractor Safety Coalition (NTSC) support, encompassing information, guidance, promotion, and fundraising, alongside a media advocacy campaign to boost non-farming stakeholder backing. These components are accessible nationwide, even in states without NTSC members.
This study evaluated the National ROPS Rebate Program's success using ROPS outcome measures and identified which Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research components correlated with these outcomes.
Prior to the official June 2017 launch, a March 2017 soft launch rebranded existing ROPS rebate programs as the NRRP, updating the website and database. This pre-launch phase allowed for the correction of technical issues. The study's 24-month timeframe encompassed this implementation period, starting six months before the soft launch and concluding 18 months after.
To facilitate the study, the 50 states were organized into ten implementation regions. These regions were established considering geographic proximity, the presence of at least one NTSC member in each region at every data collection point, and the varying availability of NRRP intervention components across states.
Every state in the US has access to the NRRP's toll-free hotline and website. However, the availability of additional program components varied by region. States in regions one, two, and three had already secured state funding for rebates before March 2017, allowing them to implement the social marketing campaign. Region one, encompassing New York, was distinct due to its longer program history. Regions two and three were geographically defined, with region two covering northeastern states (Vermont, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts) and region three covering midwestern states (Wisconsin, Minnesota). The remaining regions, four through ten, had access only to the hotline and website, with limited national funding and no state-allocated funds or social marketing campaigns.
Previously, NTSC members (N=65) rated CFIR constructs for NRRP implementation importance. Based on these scores, program evaluators created a 36-question survey instrument, covering 14 constructs. This instrument, termed "CFIR survey items," was developed by experts in clinical medicine, public health, anthropology, and implementation science.
Data collection occurred across four distinct time points: March 2017, September 2017, March 2018, and September 2018. Each survey prompted participants to recall the preceding six months. Consequently, the surveys covered four six-month intervals: September 2016 to February 2017 (period one), March to August 2017 (period two), September 2017 to February 2018 (period three), and March to August 2018 (period four). The initial survey in March 2017 established a baseline, coinciding with the NRRP's soft launch. The subsequent three surveys were designed to track shifts in CFIR constructs over time.
Surveys were administered to all active National Tractor Safety Coalition (NTSC) members at each data collection point, with the active membership ranging from 56 to 68 individuals. While the total number of members varied due to retirements and new additions, the proportion of responses from each region remained consistent throughout the study. To maximize participation, a multi-pronged survey approach was employed at each interval. Initially, an email invitation containing study details, ethical information, and a link to the online survey was sent on day one. Non-responders received mailed packets on day eight, which included the original invitation materials along with a paper survey and a pre-addressed, stamped return envelope. A general reminder email was sent to all NTSC members on day fifteen. For those still not responding, telephone contact was initiated on day twenty-two, offering the option to complete the survey verbally, with up to three call attempts made. The survey period concluded on day twenty-nine.
Survey respondents received a raffle entry for a $1,000 Amazon gift card. The Mary Imogene Bassett Hospital Institutional Review Board approved the study (project #2015).
The project team, comprised of researchers, the hotline coordinator, and marketing coordinator, assigned each state a progress score from 0 to 6 for every six-month survey period. This score, ranging from no program and fundraising activity (0) to a fully funded program (6), was determined using program records like emails and meeting notes. This seven-level metric was designated as "progress."
The NRRP hotline facilitated the tracking of program sign-ups, referred to as "intakes," and the number of tractors retrofitted with ROPS, termed "retrofits." This data was collected for all ten intervention regions over a two-year period, from September 1, 2016, to August 31, 2018. In cases where an individual both registered for the program and completed a retrofit within the study timeframe, only the retrofit was recorded. Crucially, the date attributed to this retrofit was the date of the initial intake. For instance, an intake in January 2017 followed by a retrofit in March 2017 would be categorized as a retrofit in January 2017, within the first study period.
To assess changes in the 36 CFIR survey items across ten regions and four time periods, a four by ten mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. This model also analyzed changes in the three outcome measures: retrofits, intakes, and progress scores. Specifically, the F-test for region by time interaction determined if item changes over time varied significantly between regions.
Since no significant time or region-by-time interaction effects were found in the ANOVA models, data for both CFIR survey items and the three outcomes were aggregated by region, using the mean value across the four time periods.
Spearman's rho was used to analyze the correlations between time-averaged CFIR survey items and the three outcomes. The small sample size (N=10 regions) limited the statistical power of these analyses, potentially masking practically relevant correlations. Consequently, any CFIR survey item with a Spearman's correlation of ≥0.50 with an outcome was considered predictive, irrespective of statistical significance.

Across all regions during the study, 1445 intakes and 404 retrofits were recorded. Table 5 details the average progress, intakes, and retrofits per region and period. Notably, regions 1, 2, and 3, which had secured rebate funding beforehand, demonstrated the highest progress scores, along with the majority of intakes and nearly all retrofits.
Eight CFIR survey items showed strong correlations (≥0.50) with at least one outcome measure. These items represented four constructs: access to knowledge and information, leadership engagement, engaging, and reflecting and evaluating.
A total of eight CFIR survey items demonstrated a correlation with at least one of the three outcome measures, as detailed in Table 6. Specifically, CFIR survey items two and three were associated with both progress and retrofits, while items one, four, six, and seven showed correlations with all three outcome variables. These eight items spanned four constructs across two domains: access to knowledge and information (inner setting), leadership engagement (inner setting), engaging (process), and reflecting and evaluating (process). Notably, the CFIR survey items linked to access to knowledge and information and engaging were correlated with all three outcomes.
To advance the NRRP implementation, CFIR survey items strongly associated with all three outcome measures are particularly significant. These include NTSC members' conviction that fundraising is part of their responsibilities (CFIR survey item six) and their active participation in submitting funding requests (CFIR survey item seven). This connection is logical, as short-term program success and overall outcomes are directly tied to securing rebate funding. Consequently, states that already possessed established funding (regions 1, 2, and 3) demonstrated the highest success rates, as anticipated. Nevertheless, even in other regions, minor achievements were observed, such as improvements in progress scores and a consistent, albeit small, number of farmer intakes across all regions throughout the study period. Progress scores exceeding zero suggest that initial implementation efforts have yielded some success. It is important to acknowledge, however, that securing substantial funding, represented by progress scores of 4 or 6, may require more time than the duration of this study. Therefore, a reassessment a year later might reveal higher progress scores.
Securing state-allocated rebate funding enables the implementation of the social marketing campaign, which in turn prompts more farmers to enroll and retrofit their tractors. While all study regions reported intakes, regions 4 through 10 showed significantly lower numbers. These limited successes, however, suggest underlying farmer interest in the NRRP, as farmers in unfunded regions would have had to independently seek out the program for tractor retrofitting.
Beyond fundraising-related CFIR survey items, the finding that "program materials are engaging" (item one) showed a strong correlation with all measured outcomes is noteworthy. This suggests that well-designed materials, aimed at both those who can secure state funding and potential farmer participants, can serve as a powerful motivator. Such materials can encourage stakeholders to actively fundraise, register for the NRRP, or further distribute program information. The ease of engagement with these materials is key, aligning with social marketing principles that indicate more accessible content is more likely to drive desired behaviors. This effectiveness was previously observed when developing social marketing messages to boost farmer participation in ROPS rebate programs.
Stakeholders' understanding of their role in providing feedback on implementation activities and fundraising, as measured by a specific CFIR survey item, showed a strong correlation with all three outcome measures. This suggests that when individuals offer input on program implementation and fundraising efforts, they develop a heightened sense of ownership. This ownership, a well-documented motivator across various populations including employees and stakeholders, can lead to increased involvement. In public health, particularly within community coalitions, ownership is recognized as a significant factor in boosting engagement and improving health outcomes.
Two specific survey items related to leadership engagement demonstrated a significant negative correlation with both progress and retrofit outcomes, suggesting practical importance. These items, reflecting whether the NTSC steering committee encourages member involvement and expresses support for the program, showed an unexpected inverse relationship with program success. This finding was surprising, as increased leadership engagement is typically anticipated to correlate positively with improved outcomes. To understand this counterintuitive result, the study team conducted a qualitative component, interviewing steering committee members, general NTSC members, and non-NTSC stakeholders. Two primary explanations emerged. Firstly, the steering committee's composition of highly regarded figures in agricultural research may inadvertently position the program as a "top-down" initiative. This perception could diminish motivation among others to actively participate in fundraising or state-level promotion. While some stakeholder engagement literature advocates for collaborative approaches, certain psychological research indicates that individuals may be more inclined to participate in activities promoted by authority figures, such as steering committee members. Secondly, it was proposed that states with established and well-funded programs might have achieved their success independently of the steering committee's direct involvement. Consequently, entities that have already secured state-allocated funding may not perceive significant value in the steering committee's contributions.
NTSC updates and participation in implementation events showed a strong correlation with program intakes, but not with funding progress or retrofits. This suggests these activities effectively encourage stakeholders to promote the program to the target audience. However, they may not sufficiently motivate stakeholders to engage in fundraising efforts.
These findings offer crucial guidance for the ongoing implementation of the NRRP. Addressing barriers to stakeholder engagement, particularly for NTSC members and those in regions with fewer intakes and retrofits, is essential. The significant negative correlations observed between steering committee engagement and outcome variables highlight the need to foster greater communication and enthusiasm for the NRRP across the entire NTSC. Future research, including an ongoing social networking analysis of NTSC member interactions, will further illuminate communication and collaboration patterns within the coalition.
The applicability of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) within occupational safety settings, like the one examined here, warrants further discussion. Previous research by our team indicated that while the CFIR could be applied to agricultural safety contexts, its use in large-scale, multi-site initiatives presented certain challenges compared to single-site studies. This observation aligns with a 2016 review of CFIR applications. Therefore, the findings from this study suggest that the initial assessments of the CFIR's utility in agricultural safety were accurate. To confirm this, additional evaluations of the CFIR's usefulness across other types of occupational safety interventions are recommended.
This study's limitations include a focus solely on CFIR constructs correlated with ROPS outcomes, with only a subset of possible constructs examined. This means other relevant NRRP implementation factors might have been overlooked. The decision to limit constructs was a trade-off to maintain survey length and response rates, though qualitative interviews are being used to supplement this information.
A significant limitation is the study's small sample size, stemming from the National Tractor Safety Coalition's (NTSC) membership size and geographical distribution. Since NTSC members are not present in every state, survey responses were not obtainable from all states, necessitating the use of regions as the unit of analysis. Comparing results at an individual or state level might yield different outcomes. Therefore, these conclusions should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.
The average response rate for the CFIR surveys was 57%. While this is a reasonably good rate for such surveys, it's possible the findings would differ if all National Tractor Safety Coalition (NTSC) members had responded. Non-respondents might hold significantly different views on the implementation process. Consequently, the observed correlations might have varied had every NTSC member participated in all survey requests.
As an observational study, this research cannot definitively establish causality. Therefore, it's uncertain whether changes in CFIR items directly cause alterations in outcomes like intakes and retrofits.
This study revealed factors that both aided and impeded the NRRP's implementation. To advance the program, consulting stakeholder and employee engagement literature for strategies is recommended. For instance, refining communication, adopting shared leadership, and addressing emotions, beliefs, and behaviors concurrently could enhance stakeholder involvement. Evaluating shifts in the CFIR survey items examined here would further assess the effectiveness of these strategies.
Beyond the NRRP, these findings offer value for similar implementation efforts. The study's evaluation structure can be adapted for other occupational and public health initiatives. Additionally, the key results provide guidance for planning implementation studies that necessitate broad stakeholder involvement.
