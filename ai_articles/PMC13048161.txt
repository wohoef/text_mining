Damage to the perforator's vascular territories can compromise the pedicle and flap survival. To address this, our study introduced a transillumination technique for intraoperative mapping of the radial forearm flap's subfascial perforator plexus. We then compared the perioperative outcomes and complications of this method against the standard folded, bipaddled forearm flap used in head and neck reconstruction. Our investigation involved 12 patients who underwent head and neck reconstruction with forearm flaps utilizing the transillumination method between January 2011 and December 2017, following oral cancer resection (case group). For comparative analysis, we selected 12 age- and gender-matched patients who received reconstruction with standard folded, bipaddled radial forearm flaps (control group). We analyzed demographic data, diagnoses, flap dimensions, perioperative metrics, and postoperative complications for both groups. The results indicated no statistically significant differences between the groups regarding harvesting time, operative time, or blood loss. Furthermore, no donor-site complications were observed in any patient. Similarly, recipient-site complications did not differ significantly between the case and control groups. In conclusion, the transillumination method effectively enables plastic surgeons to readily identify the radial forearm flap's perforator vascular plexus. This facilitates intraoperative flap design for head and neck reconstruction without extending harvesting time or increasing the risk of postoperative complications.

Plastic surgeons face significant challenges in head and neck reconstruction. The radial forearm flap, a standard option among seven, is valued for its thin, pliable soft tissue, ideal for fitting defects. This flap can be modified into a bipaddled configuration, where the skin between paddles is de-epithelialized or incised to the fascia to aid folding. Bhathena et al. noted that this bipaddled approach allows for simultaneous provision of skin coverage and mucosal lining using multiple separate paddles. Despite documented forearm skin vascular territories, perforator characteristics exhibit anatomical variability. Failure to accurately identify these perforator vascular territories risks pedicle damage and compromises flap survival.
To address the challenge of accurately identifying perforator vascular territories in radial forearm flaps, we developed an intraoperative mapping technique utilizing transillumination. This method, which involves passing light through a sample to visualize its internal structures, has a long-standing history of application across various medical fields. For instance, pediatricians employ it to distinguish between cystic and solid masses by observing light transmission. Its utility extends to vascular access, a concept dating back to the 1970s, and even to visualizing the mesenteric vascular network, where it enhances surgical clarity. Inspired by these applications, we adapted transillumination for intraoperative mapping of the subfascial plexus of perforators in radial forearm flaps. This study details our technique and presents a comparative analysis of its perioperative outcomes and complications against the standard folded radial forearm flap used in head and neck reconstruction.
From January 2011 to December 2017, the transillumination method was used in 12 patients undergoing head and neck reconstruction with forearm flaps following oral cancer resection (case group). A control group of 12 age- and gender-matched patients received reconstruction with standard folded, bipaddled radial forearm flaps. The study retrospectively compared demographic factors, diagnoses, flap sizes, perioperative data including harvesting and operative times and blood loss, and postoperative complications between the two groups. All patients were followed up for at least three months postoperatively. This case-control study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at National Yang-Ming University Hospital.
Following the surgical resection of oral cavity cancer, head and neck reconstruction utilized a forearm flap. Initially, the flap was planned, and perforator location was identified using a handheld acoustic Doppler. The flap was then harvested while preserving the radial artery pedicle. For the transillumination technique, the surgeon elevated the forearm flap, and an assistant positioned a light source behind it. This illumination revealed the subfascial plexus, the distribution of perforator branches, and the watershed area. This identified watershed area served as an optimal site for partial flap division. Consequently, the flap could be further designed and shaped, relying on a single perforator. The reconstructed flap was then inset into the defect.
Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range), while categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages). The Mann-Whitney U test and Fisher Exact test were employed for comparing continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Statistical significance was determined by a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 19.0.
A comparison of the case and control groups is presented in the table. A significant difference was observed in the distribution of diagnoses between the two groups. The case group primarily consisted of patients with tongue and mouth floor cancers, while the control group predominantly had buccal, gingival, and palate cancers. No significant differences were found in harvesting time, operative time, or blood loss. Furthermore, no donor-site complications were reported for any patient. Recipient-site complications also showed no significant difference between the groups. Both groups underwent a median follow-up period exceeding six months.
This study introduced an intraoperative transillumination technique for mapping the subfascial plexus of forearm flaps. It compared this method's perioperative outcomes and complications against the standard folded radial forearm flap in head and neck reconstruction. No significant differences were found in harvesting time, operative time, blood loss, or overall complications between the two approaches.
For head and neck reconstruction, the radial forearm flap is a valuable option due to its reliability and ease of harvesting. Its thin, pliable soft tissue is well-suited for the complex demands of this area. To address aesthetic and functional needs, the folded, bipaddled forearm flap has become a common technique, allowing for simultaneous provision of skin cover and mucosal lining as described by Bhathena. However, this method often requires de-epithelialization or fascial excision of the intervening skin between paddles, potentially compromising the vascular territories of the radial artery pedicle. While Lamberty et al.'s cadaveric study supports the anatomical basis for bipaddling, variations in perforator characteristics are still observed. Consequently, an intraoperative method for identifying the vascular plexus is crucial for enabling a more adaptable flap design.
Previous research has explored various intraoperative techniques for vascular assessment. For instance, Sacks and colleagues documented a case series of 15 patients where laser-assisted near-infrared indocyanine green angiography was employed to map perforator perfusion zones, aiding in anterolateral thigh flap design. Similarly, Buehrer et al. utilized intraoperative indocyanine green monitoring in a case report to identify adequate perforasomes before harvesting and evaluate vascular flow post-anastomosis for a combined anterolateral thigh and tensor fascia latae perforator flap. Sacks et al. highlighted that this angiography offers real-time, quantitative data for optimizing flap design, noting its superiority over preoperative handheld Doppler use for perforator identification. However, these indocyanine green angiography methods are considerably more complex than the transillumination approach described in this study.
The transillumination technique, previously utilized in procedures like venipuncture, hydrocele examinations, and mapping mesenteric vasculature, shares key characteristics with its intraoperative application in forearm flap surgery. Its simplicity is a notable advantage; a standard penlight suffices as the light source, and the procedure requires no specialized equipment beyond naked-eye observation. This ease of use allows plastic surgeons greater confidence when dividing and shaping forearm flaps for reconstruction, enabling a more adaptable "free-style" approach. While this study found no significant differences in perioperative outcomes or complications between the transillumination method and standard techniques, these findings should not diminish the method's potential value. The ability to design more precise flaps tailored to individual defects may positively influence patient satisfaction, even if not immediately apparent in clinical outcomes. Further research is recommended to investigate the impact of transillumination on patient satisfaction, functional recovery, and overall quality of life.
The observed differences in diagnoses between the case and control groups were primarily due to varying reconstruction requirements across anatomical sites. Cancers of the buccal region, gingiva, or palate typically create large defects that primarily necessitate flap coverage. In contrast, the removal of tongue or mouth floor cancers often leads to more complex defects. Reconstructing the tongue is considerably more challenging, aiming to restore swallowing, protect the airway, aid articulation, and provide adequate bulk to fill the oral cavity. Consequently, tongue reconstruction demands a more meticulously designed flap. The surgical demands associated with tongue or mouth floor cancers are notably more intricate than those for buccal, gingival, or palate cancers. This disparity in complexity may lead to an underestimation of the transillumination method's impact when comparing these groups, potentially explaining the study's null findings. Further research is recommended to explore the potential advantages of the transillumination technique.
This study's limitations include its retrospective design, which may introduce selection and report bias. Furthermore, a small sample size and the fact that all procedures were performed by a single surgeon limit the generalizability of the findings.
The transillumination method enables easy identification of the radial forearm flap's perforator vascular plexus. This facilitates intraoperative flap design for head and neck reconstruction, without increasing harvesting time or postoperative complications.
