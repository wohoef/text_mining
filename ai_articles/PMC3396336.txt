Tumor cells exhibit aerobic glycolysis, a process that fuels proliferation by providing biomass and results in extracellular acidification due to lactate efflux mediated by monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs). The irregular and spastic nature of tumor vasculature leads to fluctuating oxygen levels, promoting tumor cell survival and metastasis. Brain metastases are a common occurrence in advanced breast cancer, underscoring the urgent need for effective treatment strategies against this specific complication. To investigate variations in lactate exchange capabilities, this study examined human T-47D breast cancer cells and human glioblastoma T98G cells. These cell lines were cultured under either 4% or 20% oxygen conditions. Their plasma membrane expression of MCT1, MCT2, and MCT4 was then quantified using post-embedding immunogold electron microscopy. Unlike prior research that focused on mRNA and total protein levels in cell extracts, this study specifically assessed protein concentrations within the microvillous plasma membrane protrusions, which are specialized for transmembrane transport. In normoxic conditions, both tumor cell types demonstrated high expression of MCT4, a low-affinity transporter primarily involved in monocarboxylate efflux. For high-affinity transport, breast tumor cells predominantly expressed MCT1, while brain tumor cells, similar to neurons, favored MCT2 over MCT1. Hypoxia led to an upregulation of MCT1 and MCT4 in both breast and brain tumor cells. While MCT2 expression also increased in hypoxic breast cancer cells, it decreased in hypoxic brain tumor cells. These observed changes in protein levels were corroborated by quantitative immunoblotting. The distinct expression patterns and regulatory responses of MCTs on the plasma membranes of hypoxic and normoxic tumor cells of different origins offer a basis for developing novel tumor therapies by selectively targeting these transporters. The targeted inhibition of specific MCTs could prove to be an effective method for disrupting a crucial energy source in both primary breast tumors and metastatic brain cancer tissue.

Effective strategies are urgently needed to combat brain metastases, a common complication in advanced breast cancer. Cancer cells, even when oxygen is plentiful, exhibit a high rate of glycolysis and a reduced number of mitochondria. This phenomenon, known as the Warburg effect, redirects glucose towards building blocks essential for tumor proliferation. The resulting surge in glycolysis leads to increased intracellular lactate and pyruvate. Elevated lactate levels are associated with a greater propensity for metastasis and a poorer patient prognosis. To maintain cellular homeostasis, tumor cells must regulate their cytoplasmic lactate and pyruvate concentrations. This regulation is heavily influenced by the expression and activity of monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) on their cell membranes. As tumors grow, their vascular supply becomes less efficient, leading to variations in local oxygen and glucose availability. Research suggests that tumors comprise both oxygenated and hypoxic regions that engage in a metabolic crosstalk involving lactate exchange. Targeting MCT1 has been proposed as a potential anti-tumor therapy, and MCT4 and MCT2 also play roles in lactate transport. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that MCT expression might differ between tumor cells experiencing hypoxic versus normoxic conditions. Altered oxygen levels within tumors can trigger distinct metabolic pathways, which could manifest as changes in MCT expression, potentially offering new therapeutic targets. Crucially, no prior research had examined the plasma membrane expression of various MCTs in hypoxic tumor cells compared to their normoxic counterparts. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether cyclical hypoxia, specifically at 4% oxygen, significantly alters the protein levels of MCT1, MCT2, and MCT4 on the plasma membranes of tumor cells. We employed quantitative post-embedding immunogold electron microscopy to measure protein densities in two human cell lines: T98G, a glioblastoma cell line representing a prevalent brain tumor, and T-47D, a breast cancer cell line.
Human T-47D breast cancer and T98G glioblastoma cells were procured from ATCC and LGC Standards. These cells were cultivated as monolayer cultures in RPMI 1640 medium, which was enriched with 10% fetal calf serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 200 units/L insulin, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cultures were maintained at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. For experimental purposes, the cells were subjected to two distinct oxygen conditions. One group was cultured in a standard incubator providing approximately 20% oxygen (ambient air with 5% CO2). The other group was placed in an IN VIVO2 400 glove box hypoxia workstation, set to deliver 4% O2 and 5% CO2. These cells underwent bi-weekly reseeding. Pericellular oxygen concentration was precisely monitored using a Unisense Profix 3.0 system, which incorporated an auto kinetic micro-sensor, motor controller, and motorized micromanipulator. This setup allowed for detailed measurements with an OX10 Unisense micro-sensor connected to a pico-ammeter, as previously detailed in established protocols.
Cells were seeded at densities of 100,000 T98G and 500,000 T-47D per flask, then incubated under either standard conditions or a 4% oxygen hypoxia glove box. Over five days, daily measurements of supernatant pH, lactate, and glucose were taken using specific monitoring systems and strips.
Western blotting for antibody validation was performed on T98G and T-47D cells maintained in 20% oxygen. For post-embedding immunogold electron microscopy, cells of both lines were cultured under either 4% or 20% oxygen conditions. Following harvesting by centrifugation (15000 rpm for 10 min), cells were preserved in a solution of 4% formaldehyde and 0.1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4°C for subsequent processing.
Antibodies for MCT1 were sourced from Millipore. GenWay Biotech provided the MCT2 antibody (G), while Abcam supplied the MCT4 antibody (A). Additional MCT2 (H) and MCT4 (H) antibodies were obtained from Professor A. P. Halestrap at the University of Bristol.
To prepare cell samples for Western blotting, cells were harvested using trypsinization and washed twice with PBS. They were then scraped into RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (aprotinin, PMSF, leupeptin, and pepstatin). Following centrifugation to remove insoluble material and protein concentration measurement, equal protein amounts from each sample were boiled in SDS-loading buffer for 5 minutes at 100 °C. These samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in PBST for 2 hours at room temperature. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in PBST/5% milk. After this, secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies were applied. Immuno-complexes were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence. Protein band quantification was performed using Nikon image analysis software. All protein levels were normalized to GAPDH. These Western blotting experiments were replicated at least three times.
Adapted from Bergersen et al. [12], the post-embedding immunogold electron microscopy protocol involved incubating ultrathin sections overnight with primary antibodies for MCT1, MCT2, or MCT4. These antibodies were diluted in TBST with 2% HAS, at a concentration of 5 μg/ml for all MCTs. Following this, bound antibodies were visualized by a 2-hour incubation with secondary immunoglobulins conjugated to 10 nm colloidal gold. The sections were then examined using a Tecnai 12 electron microscope, with images randomly captured from the inter-cellular border regions at a primary magnification of ×43,000.
To quantify the expression of monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) on the plasma membranes, electron microscopic immunogold micrographs were analyzed. A random selection of micrographs displaying clear plasma membranes was made from samples prepared from T98G and T-47D cells. These cells were cultured under either normoxic (20% oxygen) or hypoxic (4% oxygen) conditions. For each specific antibody (targeting MCT1, MCT2, or MCT4) and each cell type/oxygen condition combination, a predetermined number of micrographs were examined: 120 micrographs for MCT1 M, MCT2 G, and MCT4 A antibodies, and 50 micrographs for MCT2 H and MCT4 H antibodies. Using ImageJ software with its Point density plug-in, cell membranes within each micrograph were precisely marked. The software then calculated the area between adjacent cells, specifically focusing on the border region that includes microvillous protrusions and other surface membrane areas. This defined region, extending 25 nm on either side of the membranes, was identified as containing the majority of the plasma membrane. Gold particles, indicating the presence of MCTs, within this demarcated area were subsequently marked. The approximate density of transporters associated with the plasma membrane was then determined by dividing the total number of gold particles by the calculated area. This method allowed for a quantitative assessment of MCT protein concentrations specifically within the specialized membrane structures involved in solute exchange.
To compare plasma membrane transporter density, 20 micrographs of each cell type and antibody, showing clear cytoplasmic areas without mitochondria, were randomly selected. The gold particle density in these cytoplasmic regions was then calculated.
Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests.
To simulate hypoxic conditions, cells were cultured in a hypoxia box with 4% oxygen, mirroring normal tissue oxygen levels (3-5%). A minimum of two weeks was required for cellular adaptation. For MCT analysis, T98G cells were maintained under 4% oxygen for four weeks, while T-47D cells underwent two weeks of this condition, both with weekly reseeding. This cultivation method resulted in cycling hypoxia, with pericellular oxygen levels fluctuating from 4% at reseeding to below 0.1% as flasks became confluent.

To ensure sufficient glucose for proliferation, the culture medium was replenished on day 2, impacting subsequent glucose and lactate measurements, as well as pH levels. Therefore, these measurements are presented from day 2 onwards (Fig. 2d-f). The study included data from flasks with T98G cells seeded at the same density as T-47D cells (approximately 5 × 10⁵), and also at the lower density used for MCT analysis (approximately 1 × 10⁵). By day 4, T-47D cells, regardless of oxygen condition, had depleted their glucose supply. Densely seeded T98G cells exhausted glucose reserves around day 3, while sparsely seeded T98G cells retained some glucose. Lactate production between days 2 and 3 was greater in flasks with higher cell counts. Although lactate levels tended to equalize by day 4 across all flasks, hypoxic cells generally showed increased lactate production compared to normoxic cells on day 3. This trend was an exception in densely seeded T98G flasks, where high lactate levels were observed on day 3, mirroring those seen on day 4 in all flasks.
Hypoxic conditions (4% oxygen) led to a significantly low pH in both cell lines by day 4. Densely seeded T98G and hypoxic T-47D cells reached this low pH one day earlier than sparsely seeded hypoxic T98G cells. Generally, cells in 20% oxygen tended to have a higher pH than those in 4% oxygen, with the exception of densely seeded T98G cells on day 3. This difference was statistically significant for T-47D cells on day 4 (p = 0.004).
Western blot analysis confirmed the specificity of the antibodies used for MCT1, MCT2, and MCT4. When applied to T98G and T-47D tumor cell extracts, each antibody produced a single band at a molecular weight consistent with the known sizes of these monocarboxylate transporters. These findings, illustrated in Figure 3a, indicate that the antibodies accurately detected their respective targets. Quantification of total MCT protein levels was performed by normalizing the intensity of MCT bands to that of GAPDH, a loading control. The results revealed significant changes in MCT expression under hypoxic conditions. Specifically, both T-47D breast cancer cells and T98G glioblastoma cells showed a significant increase in MCT1 and MCT4 levels when cultured in hypoxia compared to normoxia (Figure 3b, c). In contrast, MCT2 expression exhibited differential regulation. T-47D cells demonstrated an increase in MCT2 levels following hypoxic cultivation, while T98G cells exhibited a significant decrease in MCT2 expression under the same hypoxic conditions (Figure 3b, c). These findings highlight distinct responses of MCT subtypes to oxygen availability in different tumor cell types.
Immunogold analysis revealed the presence and distribution of cellular MCTs in electron micrographs. Across all MCT subtypes examined, their densities were significantly greater in surface membrane regions compared to cytoplasmic areas. This concentration in the plasma membrane occurred even though the measured membrane area included extracellular space and intravillous cytoplasm. Figure 4 illustrates these findings. The density of MCT1 and MCT4 on the membranes of both T-47D and T98G cells was notably higher under hypoxic conditions than in normoxia. Conversely, MCT2 density showed a different pattern: it was elevated in hypoxic T-47D cells but reduced in hypoxic T98G cells when compared to their normoxic counterparts. These differences in membrane density between hypoxic and normoxic cells were statistically significant for all MCTs analyzed.
Hypoxia significantly increased the membrane density of MCT1 in both T-47D and T98G cells when compared to normoxic conditions, with p-values less than 0.0001. Similarly, MCT4 membrane density was notably higher in hypoxic T-47D and T98G cells compared to their normoxic counterparts, also demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.0001). These findings were consistent across different antibody preparations. The study utilized electron microscopy with immunogold labeling to visualize and quantify MCTs on the plasma membranes of tumor cells. Gold particles, indicating MCT immunoreactivity, were detected in the region between cells, specifically within microvillous processes of the surface membrane. This area was meticulously analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the density of transporters by dividing the number of gold particles by the measured area. The quantification process involved marking gold particles within a 25 nm radius of the membranes, a distance comparable to the lateral resolution of the immunogold method.
A notable difference emerged in MCT2 density between T-47D and T98G cells. T-47D cells showed a significant increase in plasma membrane MCT2 density under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions. Conversely, T98G cells exhibited a significant decrease in plasma membrane MCT2 density when grown in 4% oxygen versus normoxia. These findings, consistent across different antibody sources, suggest distinct hypoxic responses for MCT2 in these cell types. The antibodies from GeneWay and Halestrap produced comparable results, with the latter showing a less pronounced relative change between hypoxic and normoxic states.
Our research revealed that hypoxia alters the concentrations of MCT proteins within the surface membrane protrusions of malignant tumor cells. Specifically, the breast cancer cell line T-47D exhibited an upregulation of MCT1, MCT2, and MCT4 under hypoxic conditions. Conversely, the glioblastoma cell line T98G showed increased expression of MCT1 and MCT4, but a downregulation of MCT2 when exposed to hypoxia.
The observed changes in monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) expression suggest that tumor cells adapt their lactate handling in response to varying oxygen levels. In hypoxic conditions, tumor cells may enhance lactate export from highly glycolytic cells and simultaneously increase lactate uptake by more oxidative cells. This altered lactate flux has several potential consequences. Increased lactate export to the extracellular environment can lower the pH, potentially influencing matrix remodeling. Conversely, increased lactate uptake can alter mitochondrial respiration. Elevated mitochondrial activity can lead to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, causing oxidative damage to proteins and DNA, which in turn promotes genomic instability. Notably, elevated ROS and mitochondrial dysfunction have been associated with malignancy in breast cancer. A decrease in mitochondrial respiration, on the other hand, would reduce ROS levels and lead to a different form of genetic instability. This scenario is reminiscent of the Warburg effect, where ROS-mediated inhibition of pyruvate kinase reduces pyruvate oxidation in mitochondria, diverting glycolytic products towards biosynthesis and increased ROS breakdown, both of which contribute to tumor growth.
This research is the first to reveal significant alterations in monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) protein concentrations within the plasma membrane of tumor cells. Our findings indicate these changes are concentrated in the microvillous projections of the cell surface, which are specialized for transmembrane transport. While prior research has documented protein levels in tumor tissues, this study specifically quantifies MCT concentrations in both plasma membrane regions and cytoplasmic areas. We observed that MCT levels are substantially lower in cytoplasmic regions compared to plasma membranes, with less pronounced differences between normoxic and hypoxic conditions in the cytoplasm. The large volume of cytoplasmic areas suggests a considerable intracellular reservoir of MCTs. This intracellular pool, relative to the amounts inserted into or removed from the membrane under hypoxic stress, could explain the minimal changes detected in cytoplasmic MCT densities. Consequently, this may also account for previous reports of no observed hypoxia-induced changes in MCT protein expression when analyzed in whole cell extracts.
While previous studies on MCT expression in hypoxic tumors have yielded varied results, our Western blot analysis of cell extracts reveals that hypoxia-induced changes extend to the total cellular levels of these transporters, not just their redistribution between cellular compartments. This indicates that overall MCT production is affected by oxygen levels. We also examined the functional roles of different MCT subtypes. MCT4, a low-affinity transporter with a Km of approximately 35 mM, is primarily involved in exporting monocarboxylates from cells. Conversely, MCT1 and MCT2 are high-affinity transporters believed to mainly facilitate the uptake of these molecules from the extracellular environment. The observed increase in MCT4 expression in both T-47D and T98G cells under hypoxic conditions aligns with the increased lactate export seen in our experiments. Furthermore, the upregulation of MCT1 in both cell lines, and MCT2 in T-47D cells, under hypoxia, could contribute to lactate efflux. Alternatively, these changes might support a symbiotic metabolic relationship within tumors. This proposed interaction involves normoxic cells near blood vessels importing and metabolizing lactate released by hypoxic cells located further away. This process conserves glucose for the hypoxic cells. Tumors also experience fluctuating oxygen levels, necessitating cellular adaptability for both lactate release and uptake to optimize energy production when oxygen is available.
Tumor oxygenation levels are comparable to those in adipose tissue, especially in obese individuals. Notably, adipocytes exhibit similar hypoxia-induced changes to our glioblastoma T98G cells: increased MCT1 and MCT4 expression, and decreased MCT2. Adipocytes share characteristics with neuroendocrine cells, including neurotransmitter production machinery. Given that T98G cells originate from the nervous system and contain neural stem cells, their metabolic behavior is likely more akin to adipocytes than to breast cancer cells like T-47D.
The downregulation of MCT2 in T98G cells under hypoxic conditions warrants explanation. One potential reason is the cell's need to prevent the loss of essential substances or the uptake of harmful ones. Notably, MCT2 exhibits a higher affinity for pyruvate compared to other MCTs, both intrinsically and relative to lactate. Consequently, reduced MCT2 expression would restrict pyruvate leakage into the surrounding medium. This conserved pyruvate could then be utilized for anaerobic energy production via conversion to lactate, regenerating NAD+ from NADH, or for generating metabolites crucial for cell proliferation and growth. Another plausible explanation involves pH regulation, a critical factor in tumor biology, particularly for metastasis. MCT2's high substrate affinity might offer distinct advantages over MCT1 and MCT4 in managing cellular pH.
The experimental design, specifically the adjustment of cell numbers to achieve similar cellular mass versus using identical cell numbers, warrants discussion. One observation supporting the chosen method is the differential glucose exhaustion: densely seeded T98G cells depleted glucose within 3 days, unlike T-47D cells, and this pattern was not seen in sparsely seeded T98G cultures. This difference partially validates the approach. Conversely, the pH progression in densely seeded T98G cultures mirrored that of T-47D cultures more closely than sparsely seeded T98G cultures. Regardless of the seeding strategy, cells were harvested after multiple hypoxia/reoxygenation cycles, a treatment that demonstrably altered MCT protein levels in both cell lines compared to normoxic conditions. Notably, even when lactate levels were consistent across all flasks, hypoxic cell cultures exhibited lower pH levels, a finding statistically significant for T-47D cells. Further analysis of lactate versus pH (data not shown) suggests that pH continues to decrease even after lactate levels stabilize, irrespective of oxygen availability. This indicates that as cells reach confluence, additional acidification mechanisms likely come into play.
In normoxia, both T-47D breast cancer and T98G glioblastoma cells demonstrated high expression of MCT4, a low-affinity transporter primarily involved in monocarboxylate export. Hypoxia led to an upregulation of MCT4 in both cell types. MCT1, a high-affinity transporter, was highly expressed in T-47D cells but less so in T98G cells under normoxic conditions; however, its expression also increased in both cell lines upon exposure to hypoxia. Glioblastoma cells, unlike breast cancer cells, expressed MCT2, a high-affinity transporter, similar to neurons, rather than MCT1. Notably, T98G cells downregulated MCT2 under hypoxic conditions. This downregulation of MCT2, which has a high affinity for pyruvate, may serve to conserve pyruvate for anaerobic energy production via lactate conversion. These distinct patterns of MCT expression and regulation in response to hypoxia in different tumor cell types offer potential avenues for novel therapeutic strategies targeting monocarboxylate metabolism.
