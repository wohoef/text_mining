To address the growing need for clinical data in research, the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences (UAMS) repurposed its enterprise data warehouse (EDW). This initiative led to the EDW's rebranding as the Arkansas Clinical Data Repository (AR-CDR) and included significant content upgrades and the introduction of a self-service cohort estimation tool in late 2016. To foster AR-CDR adoption, a dedicated team comprising physician informaticists and IT professionals organized informational sessions across campus, aiming to raise awareness of the repository and its informatics features. This strategic restructuring of the data warehouse saw a fourfold increase in AR-CDR data service utilization during 2017. To measure the AR-CDR's acceptance and the impact of its services, Everett Rogers' diffusion of innovation (DOI) framework was employed, alongside a distributed survey. Key factors identified as driving increased adoption included the crucial role of physician informaticists in bridging communication between researchers and analysts, the high quality of the data, effective researcher engagement and communication, and the AR-CDR team's responsive and customer-centric approach.

Clinical enterprise data warehouses (EDWs) were developed to meet organizational data requirements by integrating and enhancing data quality. The vast quantities of data generated by electronic medical records (EMRs) spurred the adoption of EDWs for discovering new knowledge to improve patient care. These systems can also be used to actively enhance the care of hospitalized patients. Numerous healthcare organizations are already realizing the advantages of data warehouses and acknowledging their importance for research.
UAMS established an in-house data warehouse in 2011 due to the need to integrate diverse, siloed clinical and administrative data and to support growing clinical and translational research demands. However, initial adoption was poor. In 2016, UAMS leadership relocated the enterprise data warehouse (EDW) to the department of biomedical informatics to better address researchers' data needs, leading to increased utilization. This strategic move also involved rebranding the EDW as the Arkansas Clinical Data Repository (AR-CDR), signaling UAMS' dedication to using its extensive data for innovative clinical research and maintaining a competitive edge. The AR-CDR now allows researchers to bypass the cumbersome process of gathering data from separate systems to answer their research questions.
Increased AR-CDR adoption stemmed from three key areas. Technically, expanding subject areas enhanced data quality and query efficiency. Behaviorally, researchers were empowered by self-service cohort estimation tools. Organizationally, a customer-service-oriented team, including a physician informaticist, fostered improved researcher relationships and trust.
In 2011, UAMS implemented its first EDW to simplify reporting by integrating disparate clinical, ancillary, and administrative data. This deployment occurred in three phases: first, integrating clinical and financial systems; second, incorporating the Epic EMR; and finally, repurposing the EDW for clinical and translational research.
To support clinical and translational science initiatives, data warehouses became essential for medical research, enabling efficient participant recruitment and the secondary use of clinical data for knowledge discovery. UAMS' initial data warehouse, designed to address operational questions, contained patient demographics, diagnoses, charges, and laboratory and medication data. Its ETL process drew information from four clinical systems—Centricity, SunRise, AllScripts, and GE EMR—along with two financial systems, MeKesson and Siemens.
Following the data warehouse deployment, UAMS introduced I2B2, a self-service tool for research cohort estimation. This de-identified EMR data subset, including diagnoses, medications, and lab results, aimed to expedite research study initiation.
In 2014, Epic EMR replaced older clinical systems, becoming the primary charting tool for both outpatient and inpatient care. The data warehouse's ETL process was then reconfigured to pull data from the EPIC Clarity repository, but without adding new clinical data subjects to the EDW. This led to data incompleteness and, by 2016, a decline in EDW utilization due to persistent data quality issues.
In 2016, the enterprise data warehouse (EDW) at UAMS was rebranded as the Arkansas Clinical Data Repository (AR-CDR). This renaming signaled a shift towards establishing a comprehensive research data platform that integrated clinical information with disease registries. The AR-CDR found a new home within the department of biomedical informatics, benefiting from the faculty's combined clinical and informatics expertise. A specialized support team, comprising analysts and developers and overseen by a physician informaticist, was formed. This new team engaged researchers through direct interviews to gauge their data requirements and gather feedback on their prior experiences with the EDW. Researchers voiced significant concerns regarding data quality, the technical staff's lack of clinical terminology knowledge, the I2B2 tool's limitations and user-friendliness, and inadequate communication channels with the technical team.
To address researcher concerns, the AR-CDR team initiated direct engagement through individual meetings and presentations at clinical department gatherings. These sessions highlighted the system's improvements and new informatics tools. Key enhancements included expanding data content to encompass surgery, anesthesia, health literacy, social history, oncology, and obstetrics and gynecology. Furthermore, tumor and trauma registries, along with tissue bio-specimens, were integrated. A significant technical advancement was the deployment of TriNetX2, a self-service web-based tool for cohort estimation. To streamline operations, a simplified research data request process was established, and RedMine3 was implemented for project management, tracking requests, communications, and system enhancements.
To gauge researcher adoption of the AR-CDR, Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovation (DOI) framework was employed. This model categorizes technology adopters into innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%), and laggards (16%), allowing for an evaluation of the stages researchers progressed through to utilize the system.
To gauge researcher perceptions of the AR-CDR versus the former EDW, a 14-question survey was administered. It assessed service quality, workflow efficiency, data utility, publication/grant success, and overall user satisfaction.
A campus-wide campaign effectively showcased the AR-CDR's capabilities and the cohort estimation tool's functionality, driving its positive transformation. Throughout 2017, 156 individuals, including 110 researchers and participants in 13 group and departmental presentations, were exposed to the AR-CDR and its tools. This outreach resulted in AR-CDR adoption rates exceeding Roger's diffusion of innovation framework across most categories, with innovators at 7%, early adopters at 22%, and the early majority at 38%. Only the late majority (30%) and laggards (3%) showed rates below the framework's predictions.
In 2017, the AR-CDR experienced a significant increase in research activity, with 99 IRB-approved research queries submitted, a fourfold rise compared to the average of 25 queries in 2015 and 2016. Additionally, 37 researchers utilized the cohort estimation tool, executing 455 queries. A survey was distributed to 60 researchers who received data from the AR-CDR in 2017. After excluding two individuals no longer at UAMS, 32 out of the remaining 58 researchers completed the survey, yielding a 55% response rate. Respondents were divided into two groups: Group 1 (n=27) comprised those who first learned about the AR-CDR between January and December 2017, while Group 2 (n=5) consisted of researchers familiar with the EDW prior to its transformation.
Both surveyed groups expressed high satisfaction with the AR-CDR's quality of service. Notably, Group 2, despite prior dissatisfaction with the legacy EDW, reported even higher satisfaction than Group 1. Participants in both groups confirmed the data's high quality and its strong correlation with clinical systems, along with timely query completion. Group 1 submitted more individual research queries, with 57% submitting one and 43% submitting multiple. In contrast, Group 2 researchers each submitted at least four queries. The outcomes were equally impressive: 100% of Group 2's queries led to grant or publication submissions, compared to 60% for Group 1. Furthermore, 40% of Group 1 utilized the data for hypothesis generation.
The AR-CDR saw significant engagement, with 105 out of 156 aware users gaining access to the cohort estimation tool. Of these, 37 ran at least one query, and 60 submitted IRB-approved queries, resulting in a total of 99 research queries. This surge in AR-CDR utilization in 2017 is attributed to a combination of factors. Organizationally, its placement within the department of biomedical informatics, coupled with the expertise of its clinical and informatics staff, fostered trust and collaboration with researchers. Behaviorally, frequent meetings, open communication, and managing researcher expectations built strong professional relationships. Technically, the introduction of new self-service tools and enriched AR-CDR content empowered researchers for their clinical and analytical endeavors. Sustained demand for the AR-CDR hinges on the continued provision of high-quality data and effective informatics tools.
