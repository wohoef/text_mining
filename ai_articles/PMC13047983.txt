This study investigated the relationship between acute pain after colorectal cancer surgery and long-term patient outcomes, a topic with limited clinical research despite animal studies suggesting pain's role in suppressing immunity and promoting tumor metastasis. The methods involved collecting data from 2401 patients who underwent colorectal cancer resection at a single medical center from November 2010 to December 2014. Postoperative pain intensity was measured using a numeric rating scale at multiple time points: 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours. To analyze these longitudinal pain scores, group-based modeling was employed to categorize patients into distinct pain trajectories. The primary outcomes, recurrence-free survival and overall survival, were then analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models. The analysis of 13,931 pain score observations revealed three distinct inpatient postsurgical pain trajectories. The largest group (70.3%) experienced mild pain that decreased to low levels (group 1). A smaller group (20.0%) initially had moderate to severe pain that subsequently dropped to mild levels (group 2). The smallest group (9.7%) showed moderate pain that rebounded to severe levels (group 3). Initial univariate models indicated a significant association between these pain trajectories and both recurrence-free survival (e.g., group 3 vs. group 1: HR, 1.63) and overall survival (e.g., group 3 vs. group 1: HR, 1.81). However, these associations were no longer significant after adjusting for other important risk factors. The study concluded that abnormal pain resolution, as identified by pain trajectory analysis, likely reflects complex interactions between disease progression, surgical factors, and analgesia, and may serve as an indicator of a poorer prognosis following colorectal cancer resection.

Animal research indicates that pain, by stimulating the stress response, can weaken the body's immune system and potentially encourage the spread of tumors. This suggests that effective pain management during cancer surgery is crucial, as it could help maintain the body's defenses against tumor dissemination. While human studies have also connected pain levels and frequency to cancer outcomes, particularly showing that severe chronic cancer pain predicts shorter survival in advanced non-small cell lung and prostate cancers, most of this research has concentrated on chronic pain. There is a notable lack of studies examining how acute pain after cancer surgery impacts cancer recurrence.
Previous research on the link between pain and clinical outcomes has largely relied on simplified pain measurements, often taken at single time points or averaged. However, pain is dynamic, changing in both intensity and nature over time due to the interplay of treatment and symptoms. Postoperative pain scores, recorded at various intervals, demonstrate significant patient-to-patient variability during hospitalization. To address this, pain trajectory analysis offers a more precise and informative approach than traditional assessments, as it not only quantifies pain intensity but also tracks its evolution. While some studies have explored how different postsurgical pain trajectories relate to outcomes like 30-day readmission risk or chronic pain after knee surgery, the connection between pain trajectories and oncologic outcomes in cancer surgery patients remains unexamined. Our hypothesis was that atypical pain resolution, as revealed by trajectory analysis, could predict poorer long-term outcomes after cancer surgery. Therefore, this retrospective study sought to define and investigate the intricate relationships between postoperative pain trajectories and their impact on cancer recurrence, overall survival, and other relevant risk factors in patients who underwent colorectal cancer resection.
Medical records were examined for patients who underwent primary tumor resection for stage I-IV colorectal adenocarcinoma at our hospital between November 2010 and December 2014. This retrospective review received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (IRB-TPEVGH No. 2017-12-025BC), which also waived the requirement for written informed consent. Patients were excluded from the analysis if they experienced postoperative complications requiring intensive care unit admission or reoperation prior to discharge, or if they had fewer than two postoperative pain assessments during their hospitalization. The electronic medical database utilized for this study has been previously employed in the authors' published research.
Standard colorectal cancer resection at the center involved general anesthesia with neuromuscular blockers and inhalation agents. Postoperative pain management was individualized, with patients receiving either intravenous opioid analgesia (patient-controlled or as-needed) or epidural analgesia, based on patient and anesthesiologist discretion.
Postoperative pain management involved several approaches. For patients receiving intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA), morphine was delivered using a Gemstar Yellow ambulatory infusion pump. This involved a continuous infusion of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/h, supplemented by 1 mg boluses with a 6-minute lockout period. IV-PCA was generally maintained for 48 to 72 hours post-surgery, after which patients transitioned to oral acetaminophen or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Alternatively, epidural analgesia was an option. An epidural catheter was inserted and checked for functionality a day before surgery. Epidural analgesia commenced pre-incision, utilizing local anesthetics like bupivacaine (0.25% or 0.5%), potentially combined with fentanyl (1 to 2 µg/mL), at a rate of 5 to 10 mL/h, adjusted according to the patient's hemodynamic status. Like IV-PCA, epidural analgesia typically lasted 48 to 72 hours. For those on as-needed analgesia, intravenous or oral narcotics (such as morphine and tramadol) or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were administered.
Trained specialist nurses documented acute surgical pain using a self-reported numerical rating scale, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). These scores were initially taken every 8 hours for the first 24 hours post-surgery, then every 12 hours for four days, and daily afterward. For this study, the highest pain score was specifically recorded at 12, 24, 36, 48, 96, and 120 hours after surgery.
The study's main objective was to assess recurrence-free survival, measured from the surgery date until the first confirmed cancer recurrence. Recurrence was identified through imaging techniques such as plain films, CT, MRI, or PET scans, indicating either local or distant tumor deposits. A secondary objective was to determine overall survival, calculated from the surgery date to the patient's death. Death dates were sourced from medical records or death certificates. Patient status was tracked through follow-up appointments or hospital admissions until August 2016. For individuals who did not experience recurrence or death by the study's end, their survival data was treated as censored observations.
Specialist anesthesiologists, independent of the statistical analysis, utilized an electronic medical database to gather patient characteristics and identify risk factors linked to cancer recurrence and mortality. The authors confirmed the accuracy of this extracted data through random sampling. The study incorporated various clinical covariates, including demographic information, pretreatment carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, and the use of perioperative analgesics such as acetaminophen, ketorolac, tenoxicam, and hydrocortisone, administered within seven days of surgery. Additionally, perioperative blood transfusions, defined as packed red blood cell transfusions within seven days of surgery, were considered. Cancer adjuvant therapy, encompassing chemotherapy or radiotherapy given within 90 days of surgery, particularly for patients with stage III and IV disease, was also included.
The analysis considered several pathological characteristics: tumor differentiation, mucinous or signet-ring histology, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion. Tumor location was categorized as right-sided (cecum to splenic flexure) or left-sided (splenic flexure to rectum). Cancer staging, from I to IV, followed the American Joint Committee on Cancer's seventh edition criteria.
Pain trajectories were analyzed using group-based modeling of longitudinal pain scores, specifically employing the PROC TRAJ procedure in SAS. To establish the number of distinct trajectories, we required at least one significant slope parameter (linear, quadratic, or cubic). This determination was made by evaluating the Bayesian information criterion across various models and visually examining the resulting trajectory patterns. Ultimately, three pain trajectories were identified, with their average scores and standard errors at seven time points presented in Figure 1.
To compare patient characteristics across the three pain trajectory groups, categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test, while continuous variables were assessed with either analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test, depending on suitability. Survival outcomes were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves for both recurrence-free and overall survival, and differences in survival distributions between the pain trajectories were statistically tested with the log-rank test. For initial assessment of factors influencing recurrence-free and overall survival, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on all collected variables. Variables showing significance in this univariate analysis were then considered for multivariate analysis through a stepwise model selection process. The criteria for including or excluding factors in these multivariate models were set at a significance level of 0.05 for entry and 0.1 for removal. Following this, the impact of pain trajectories on both recurrence-free and overall survival was further examined within these multivariate models. Beyond survival, the influence of various collected variables on the assignment to specific pain trajectories was also investigated. This was done using the PROC TRAJ algorithm, with results presented as odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. A backward model selection approach was additionally employed in multivariate analysis to pinpoint significant factors linked to pain trajectories. All statistical hypotheses were tested with a two-sided significance level of 0.05. All analyses were executed using SAS software, version 9.4.
A total of 2401 patients contributed 13,931 pain score observations, with a median follow-up period of 28.6 months (interquartile range, 17.5-45.5 months). This data was used for grouping pain trajectories and subsequent survival analysis. Pain intensity was categorized using a 0-10 scale: 0-2 for low, 2-4 for mild, 4-6 for moderate, and above 6 for severe. Three distinct inpatient pain trajectory categories emerged from this classification. Group 1, comprising 1688 patients (70.3%), experienced mild pain that decreased to low levels. Group 2 included 479 patients (20.0%) who initially had moderate/severe pain that then reduced to mild. Finally, Group 3 consisted of 234 patients (9.7%) whose moderate pain worsened, rebounding to severe levels.
Group 3 patients exhibited several distinct characteristics: elevated pretreatment CEA levels, a greater prevalence of right-sided tumors, extended anesthesia durations, and more advanced cancer stages. They also received more acetaminophen, ketorolac, hydrocortisone, and both neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy/radiotherapy. Pathologically, group 3 showed increased poor cell differentiation and lymphovascular invasion. In contrast, group 2 patients were generally younger and had a higher incidence of perioperative blood transfusions and perineural invasion.
The three distinct pain trajectory groups showed a significant difference in recurrence-free survival distribution (log-rank test, p < 0.001, see Fig. 2). Furthermore, a univariate analysis confirmed a significant association between these pain trajectories and the risk of recurrence (p < 0.001). Specifically, compared to group 1, group 2 had a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.23 (95% CI, 1.02-1.47; p = 0.028), while group 3 had an HR of 1.63 (95% CI, 1.30-2.04; p < 0.001). Other significant predictors of cancer recurrence identified in the univariate analysis included ASA class ≥ 3, chronic kidney disease, pretreatment CEA levels, laparoscopic surgery, blood transfusions, anesthesia duration, and preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy (Table 2).
Following the model selection, nine factors were identified as independent predictors of cancer recurrence. These included an ASA class of 3 or higher, chronic kidney disease, and elevated preoperative CEA levels (expressed on a base-10 logarithmic scale). Other significant predictors were blood transfusion, advanced cancer stage (with increasing hazard ratios from stage II to IV compared to stage I), and the presence of pathologic lymphovascular and perineural invasion. Additionally, both preoperative chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, and postoperative radiotherapy were found to be independent prognostic factors. Importantly, once these significant risk factors for cancer recurrence were accounted for, the previously observed association between pain trajectories and recurrence risk was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.25). Specifically, the hazard ratios for groups 2 and 3 compared to group 1 became 0.92 (95% CI, 0.77-1.12) and 1.16 (95% CI, 0.92-1.46), respectively.
The study observed a notable disparity in overall survival across the three distinct pain trajectory groups (p = 0.001, log-rank test, Fig. 2). A univariate analysis confirmed a significant link between pain trajectories and overall survival. Specifically, compared to group 1, group 2 demonstrated a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.36 (95% CI, 1.05-1.77; p = 0.020), while group 3 had an HR of 1.81 (95% CI, 1.31-2.51; p < 0.001). This analysis also identified several other factors predicting overall survival, such as age, ASA class ≥ 3, various comorbidities, pretreatment CEA levels, tumor characteristics, and perioperative treatments (Table 2).
Several factors were identified as independent predictors of mortality through multivariate regression analysis. These included increasing age (HR = 1.01), an ASA class of 3 or higher (HR = 1.7), and the presence of chronic kidney disease (HR = 1.39). Higher pretreatment CEA levels (HR = 1.61) and blood transfusions (HR = 1.5) also contributed to increased mortality risk. Advanced cancer stages showed a marked increase in hazard ratios compared to stage I (stage II: 2.84; stage III: 6.79; stage IV: 28.18). Tumor differentiation, specifically moderate (HR = 1.47) and poor (HR = 3.22) compared to good, and perineural invasion (HR = 1.53) were also significant. Postoperative chemotherapy was associated with a reduced risk (HR = 0.49), while postoperative radiotherapy increased it (HR = 1.6). Notably, after accounting for these significant predictors, the initial association between pain trajectories and overall survival was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.21).
Patients in group 2 exhibited distinct characteristics compared to group 1, showing a lower incidence of older age and diabetes, but a higher likelihood of receiving blood transfusions, experiencing lymphovascular invasion, and undergoing preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Meanwhile, group 3 patients were more frequently associated with elevated CEA levels, right-sided tumors, and extended anesthesia durations.
This research investigated the link between acute surgical pain and cancer outcomes after colorectal cancer resection, utilizing a pain trajectory approach. It offers fresh insights into the intricate connections between postoperative pain, cancer recurrence, and mortality. The study boasts several strengths. A large cohort was analyzed, enhancing the statistical power of the findings. Furthermore, repeated pain score measurements were examined through trajectory grouping models, allowing for quantification of pain intensity and differentiation of pain resolution over time. Finally, the models incorporated a wide array of crucial clinicopathologic factors related to cancer outcomes, thereby reducing potential confounding influences.
Postoperative pain, while anticipated, is frequently not managed effectively in clinical settings. The level of pain a patient experiences can be indicative of surgical complications and the severity of their underlying disease. When pain is not controlled, it negatively affects quality of life and can contribute to readmissions and chronic postsurgical pain. Our trajectory analysis revealed that almost 10% of cancer surgery patients (group 3) endured ongoing moderate to severe pain for 120 hours after their operation. This group faced the highest risk of cancer recurrence and mortality following colorectal cancer resection, significantly more than those in the mild (group 1) and moderate (group 2) pain trajectory groups. Additional investigation showed that group 3 patients exhibited higher pretreatment CEA levels and longer anesthesia times compared to group 1, suggesting more aggressive disease and more extensive surgical procedures. Clinically, patients whose pain does not resolve normally warrant special consideration in terms of pain management, cancer therapy, and ongoing monitoring.
While initial analysis showed a link between acute pain trajectories and cancer outcomes after surgery, this connection vanished once other significant clinical and pathological factors were considered. This suggests that acute pain's influence might stem from the surgery's complexity and the cancer's aggressiveness, acting as a marker of the perioperative experience and disease severity, rather than pain intensity itself. Pain is a multifaceted experience, shaped by health, patient perception, and treatment. Therefore, more rigorous research is needed to fully understand the relationship between postoperative pain and cancer outcomes.
Patients in the mild pain trajectory group were characterized by older age and a lower incidence of lymphovascular invasion compared to the moderate pain trajectory group. Several factors could explain the reduced pain experience in older patients, including a diminished pain perception or a more effective response to analgesics. It's also possible that their higher ASA class contributed to a desensitization to pain. Furthermore, less aggressive disease in older patients might have led to more conservative surgical resections, resulting in less intense postoperative pain.
Diabetic patients in our study exhibited a reduced likelihood of experiencing a moderate pain trajectory. This observation aligns with existing research indicating that individuals with early diabetic neuropathy possess a higher pain threshold. Such neuropathy, an early pathological manifestation of diabetes, primarily affects small-diameter nerve fibers. Further research is needed to determine if this diminished pain sensitivity in diabetic patients correlates with a decreased need for perioperative analgesics.
This study has several limitations. Its observational nature is one, and data on individual narcotic use were unavailable. Furthermore, the analysis did not account for the confounding influence of other surgical factors on pain levels and cancer outcomes.
Our study concludes that an inferior prognosis after colorectal cancer resection may be indicated by abnormal pain resolution, as revealed through pain trajectory analysis. This abnormality arises from intricate interactions involving disease progression, surgical intervention, and pain management. Utilizing pain trajectory analysis offers a more thorough understanding of the complex interplay between pathological findings, surgical trauma, and pain control in colorectal cancer patients. Future prospective research is needed to confirm the link between abnormal pain resolution and cancer outcomes, and to determine if more aggressive pain management can decrease recurrence and mortality post-surgery.
