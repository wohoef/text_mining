Postpartum health significantly impacts both mothers and infants. While herbal medicine is a common postpartum care practice in South Korea, particularly within Korean medicine, comprehensive safety data are currently limited. To address this gap, this study establishes a multicenter registry within Korean medicine clinics. The primary goal is to investigate adverse events (AEs) and treatment patterns linked to postpartum herbal medicine use. This practice-based research network, operating in primary care settings, will provide crucial information for clinical decision-making and pharmacovigilance in postpartum care. Ultimately, it aims to enhance the quality and safety of care and guide the monitoring of co-medications across various community clinics. The study's objective is to create this multicenter registry to systematically examine AEs and treatment patterns associated with herbal medicine use in postpartum Korean medicine clinics. This prospective observational registry plans to enroll 1000 postpartum patients receiving herbal medicine treatment from 15 Korean medicine clinics. Enrollment will occur between July 2024 and June 2027, including patients within six months postpartum who are receiving herbal medicine. Data collection will span up to eight weeks for each participant, encompassing demographic details, medical history, specific herbal medicine prescriptions, reported AEs, and any concomitant medication use. Patients will self-report AEs, and their causality will be assessed using established methodologies: the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Centre system and the Naranjo algorithm. Standard operating procedures will govern all data collection and entry processes. The registry was officially registered with the Clinical Research Information Service in June 2024 (KCT0009552). Recruitment commenced in July 2024 across the 15 participating Korean medicine clinics, with the target of 1000 postpartum patients to be enrolled by June 2027. This initiative marks the first large-scale, multicenter registry specifically designed to examine herbal medicine use during the postpartum period within Korean medicine clinics. By systematically gathering real-world data on AEs related to postpartum herbal medicine, the study aims to build a comprehensive safety database for both individual herbal medicines and their constituent herbs. The anticipated findings are expected to bolster pharmacovigilance efforts for herbal medicine and contribute to improved postpartum care outcomes for both mothers and their infants. The trial is registered with WHO ICTRP under KCT0009552.

Childbirth, a natural yet universally stressful event, frequently leaves mothers in a weakened state with diminished quality of life. Research indicates that between 49% and 94% of women encounter at least one health problem within eight weeks postpartum. Common issues, such as fatigue, headaches, musculoskeletal pain, urinary incontinence, and depression, often extend beyond the first year. These persistent and widespread health challenges significantly impact the well-being of both mothers and their children. Consequently, there is a clear demand for safe and effective postpartum care approaches, prompting many to explore herbal medicine as a potential treatment.
Herbal medicine is widely used for postpartum care, particularly in East Asian countries where it is integrated into national healthcare systems. This prevalence stems from perceived benefits, including its potential to alleviate postpartum pain and, when combined with conventional treatments, mitigate postpartum depression. Consumers in these regions often report high satisfaction with herbal medicine for postpartum recovery. However, a significant concern persists regarding the limited safety data available for both mothers and infants. This lack of comprehensive safety information may deter some individuals from utilizing herbal medicine. For example, a Taiwanese study revealed elevated lead levels in the breast milk of breastfeeding mothers consuming traditional Chinese herbs, highlighting the critical need for robust safety monitoring. This global context underscores the urgent demand for rigorous safety research on postpartum herbal medicine use. In Korean medicine, individualized herbal prescriptions, such as Boheo-tang, Ssanghwa-tang, Gunggwijohyeol-eum, and Dangguijakyak-san, are frequently employed to address common postpartum symptoms like fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, and circulatory issues. While recent studies suggest that these herbal interventions can improve pain, quality of life, and depressive symptoms in postpartum women, often with acceptable safety profiles, the overall scarcity of detailed safety data remains a challenge. This gap necessitates further investigation to ensure the safety and efficacy of herbal medicine in postpartum care.
Korean medicine, particularly herbal remedies and acupuncture, is a standard postpartum treatment in South Korea. Its perceived value is high, with 78.68% of mothers willing to reuse Korean medicine for future births, and 72.13% expressing satisfaction with herbal medicine specifically. This widespread acceptance is further bolstered by local government subsidies for postpartum herbal medicine expenses. This support underscores the significant role of traditional Korean medicine in maternal care, highlighting its integration into the healthcare system and its positive reception among new mothers.
South Korea's drug review service currently lacks adverse event (AE) data for herbal medicine. Existing national statistics on herbal medicine AEs rely on patient reports or surveys, which do not include expert evaluations or specific treatment details. This absence of comprehensive safety information means that some reported AEs may not be directly linked to herbal medicine use, highlighting the need for clear causality assessments. Consequently, a significant gap exists in our understanding of the risks associated with postpartum herbal medicine. This deficiency underscores the critical need for robust safety data to inform clinical practice and ensure patient well-being.
This study establishes a registry to address the scarcity of official safety data concerning herbal medicine use in postpartum care. Focusing on Korean medicine clinics, which constitute approximately 96% of such institutions in South Korea, ensures the collection of representative clinical data. The registry systematically gathers information on herbal medicine components, adverse events (AEs), and their causality. This comprehensive data collection aims to provide robust evidence for monitoring the safety of herbal medicine during the postpartum period, thereby filling a critical knowledge gap in this prevalent treatment modality.
This study investigates the incidence rate of adverse events (AEs) linked to postpartum herbal medicine use. It also identifies specific herbal medicines and their constituent herbs involved in reported AEs. Furthermore, the research aims to classify the causality of these observed AEs.

The SAFEHERE-KM registry aims to systematically gather and analyze clinical data concerning adverse events (AEs) linked to herbal medicine use in South Korean postpartum patients. This web-based platform will document patient demographics, herbal medicine prescriptions, and AE details. A crucial aspect involves assessing the causality of any AEs that emerge during treatment, utilizing both the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Centre assessment and the Naranjo algorithm. Ultimately, by creating comprehensive AE monitoring databases, this registry seeks to advance the development of an active pharmacovigilance system for herbal medicine within South Korea. This initiative will enhance the safety monitoring of postpartum herbal medicine.
This multicenter, prospective, observational study, named SAFEHERE-KM, aims to gather data on routine clinical care and adverse events (AEs) in postpartum women. The registry will enroll at least 1000 eligible patients receiving herbal medicine treatment at Korean medicine clinics. Recruitment is scheduled from July 2024 to June 2027, with an anticipated annual enrollment of around 400 participants, or approximately 27 patients per clinic. This sample size is based on prior research indicating its effectiveness in reducing variation in AE incidence reporting.
This study enrolls postpartum patients who consent to data collection, receiving individualized herbal medicine as routine care from Korean medicine doctors. The registry primarily investigates adverse events (AEs) in these women, as infants are not study participants and their AEs are not systematically evaluated. Doctors prescribe herbal medicine without restrictions, reflecting real-world practice.
Data collection for this study encompasses demographics, medical history, details of Korean medicine treatments, prescribed herbal medicines, and any co-administered medications. This information is gathered over a treatment period that can last up to eight weeks, depending on the herbal medicine regimen. Adverse events (AEs), or their absence, are systematically recorded through scheduled assessments, occurring roughly every two weeks during treatment and continuing for up to eight weeks of follow-up. Patients self-report AEs via an online survey, with these reports then entered into online databases. The electronic case report form is designed with specific fields to capture AE details, including whether an event qualifies as serious. To maintain data consistency and accuracy, Korean medicine doctors adhere to standardized operating procedures developed for this registry. Furthermore, all reported AEs, including those deemed serious, undergo review and validation by licensed Korean medicine doctors.
Across South Korea, 15 Jayoon Korean medicine clinics, specializing in gynecological disorders, will recruit patients. This network offers broad geographical reach, with clinics in Seoul, Gyeonggi-do, Jeju Province, Busan, Incheon, Daejeon, and Daegu, ensuring diverse participant representation for the study.
Recruitment began July 7, 2024, for the registry (KCT0009552), registered with Clinical Research Information Service.
The registry includes women visiting primary Korean medicine clinics within six months postpartum for herbal medicine prescriptions. Participants must have diagnoses primarily related to puerperium (O85-O92, ICD-10) or puerperal wind disorder (U32.7, KCD-8). Puerperal wind disorder encompasses pain and autonomic disturbances resulting from insufficient postpartum care. Specific inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Textbox 1.
Recruitment for this study, which began in July 2024, is ongoing across 15 primary Korean medicine clinics providing postpartum care. To encourage participation, clinics display recruitment posters. Healthcare professionals explain the study's purpose, data usage, confidentiality, and sharing protocols to potential participants. Written informed consent is secured from eligible individuals before data collection commences. Participants retain the right to withdraw at any point without affecting their clinical care.
Eligible participants, after providing informed consent, receive a study registration number. Data collection then proceeds for up to 8 weeks, coinciding with their herbal medicine consumption. Details regarding this data collection and the study schedule are outlined in Table 1.
Data for this study will be collected using the myTrial Electronic Data Capture system (NIKOM), an electronic case report form system validated by the National Agency for Korean Medicine Innovative Technologies Development. This system allows Korean medicine doctors at each clinic to securely enter data remotely via a web server, using unique login credentials. To ensure data integrity, each case entered into the registry will undergo regular review, and any discrepancies will trigger data queries, as outlined in the data monitoring manual. Furthermore, monitors will conduct routine visits to participating Korean medicine clinics. During these visits, they will verify original registry records and research files, and collaborate with coinvestigators and principal investigators to address and resolve any identified issues. All collected data will be stored on a dedicated server, protected by stringent access-control permissions, ensuring confidentiality and security throughout the study.
Data analysis will employ R (version 4.5.0). For participants withdrawing consent, data collected up to that point will be included. Continuous variables will be summarized with means and standard deviations, while categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages. Herbal medicine exposure will be classified by prescription frequency, major groups, and functional categories. The incidence of adverse events (AEs) will be reported as the proportion of participants experiencing at least one AE, with all AEs summarized descriptively. When AEs occur, the specific herbal medicines and their constituent herbs will be detailed using frequencies and proportions to identify observed patterns. This approach allows for a comprehensive understanding of AE occurrence in relation to herbal medicine use.
An independent Adverse Reaction Assessment Committee, linked with the Regional Drug Safety Center at the Korea Institute of Drug Safety and Risk Management, will evaluate reported adverse events (AEs). This evaluation will determine the severity of AEs using established criteria, which are standard in clinical safety studies and apply to general symptoms irrespective of treatment. Additionally, the committee will assess the causality of AEs, employing both the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality assessment and the Naranjo algorithm score. This dual approach aligns with methodologies from prior research. This systematic assessment aims to provide robust data on the nature and origins of any AEs, contributing to a comprehensive understanding of treatment safety.
This registry, collecting real-world data on routine clinical practice, excludes patient involvement in its design, conduct, and dissemination.
Adverse event data are collected for research, but Korean medicine doctors manage any adverse events as part of routine clinical care. This ensures participants receive appropriate care throughout the study.

A revised protocol (version 1.1) received approval on June 24, 2024, after research plan amendments. All protocol changes will require institutional review board approval and investigator notification. Study findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and academic conferences. Prior to enrollment, all participants will provide written informed consent. Participant data, recorded in case report forms, will be stored in secure, access-restricted databases to maintain privacy and confidentiality. Participants will not be compensated financially for their involvement in this registry.

This groundbreaking SAFEHERE-KM study establishes the first large-scale, multicenter registry to evaluate herbal medicine safety in postpartum care within primary Korean medicine clinics. This approach facilitates the collection of real-world data on herbal medicine use in its most common clinical setting. Consequently, the study aims to yield more representative and clinically relevant information than prior research conducted in hospital environments.
The postpartum period often extends beyond the initial 6 to 8 weeks, even after reproductive organs have largely recovered. During this time, many mothers face ongoing physical and mental challenges due to childcare, household duties, and stress. Inadequate care during this prolonged phase can result in various health complications. In South Korea, a survey indicated that mothers typically seek herbal medicine treatment around 13 to 14 weeks postpartum. Recognizing the importance of extended health management, numerous local governments in Korea subsidize Korean medicine treatments for up to six months after childbirth. Consequently, this study's registry includes women up to six months postpartum. The decision to monitor adverse events for eight weeks following the start of herbal medicine treatment was based on the clinical experience of participating Korean medicine doctors. They observed that most patients complete their herbal medicine courses within this timeframe. This strategy allows for maximizing participant enrollment within budgetary limits while still capturing the most pertinent period of herbal medicine use in postpartum care.
This study's findings will have significant clinical utility. By creating a comprehensive safety database for individual herbal medicines and their components, we will systematically gather data on adverse events (AEs) linked to postpartum herbal medicine use. This database will detail AE signs, symptoms, frequency, and severity, offering crucial insights for practitioners and patients. Assessing the causality of reported AEs will bolster evidence for herbal medicine safety, fostering guidelines for monitoring and decision-making in postpartum care. Furthermore, data on causal herbs, dosages, and co-administered drugs will facilitate advanced analysis of herb-herb and herb-drug interactions.
The SAFEHERE-KM study employs a robust methodology, ensuring high data quality and clinical relevance. Standardized data collection, utilizing detailed operating procedures and an electronic case report form, guarantees consistency across multiple sites. The study will recruit at least 1000 patients from 15 geographically diverse Korean medicine clinics throughout South Korea, mitigating selection bias and providing a comprehensive understanding of herbal medicine-related adverse events (AEs). This extensive recruitment from varied primary care settings enhances the external validity of the findings, making them highly applicable to real-world clinical practice. Furthermore, the study actively monitors and collects patient-reported AEs, preventing underreporting and incorporating patient perspectives, which is crucial for a complete safety profile.
This study has several limitations. Participant recruitment from a single network of Korean medicine clinics, all specializing in gynecological disorders, could restrict the variety of herbal medicine prescriptions observed. Although personalized herbal medicine is prescribed within this network, the study may not fully represent the diverse herbal medicine practices throughout South Korea. Furthermore, relying on patient-reported adverse events (AEs) might lead to an underestimation of negative clinical outcomes. However, this is partially addressed by standardized reporting procedures and routine clinic visits. Lastly, since the registry focuses on AEs linked to individual prescriptions, comprehensive cumulative dose-response analyses based on total herbal medicine exposure are not feasible.
Building on the SAFEHERE-KM study, future research should broaden its scope. Expanding to diverse primary care settings would yield more comprehensive evidence on herbal medicine use in postpartum care. Additionally, long-term studies are needed to understand the extended safety profile of postpartum herbal medicine, a dimension beyond this study's current focus.
The SAFEHERE-KM registry aims to generate crucial real-world safety data on postpartum herbal medicine use within Korean medicine clinics. This research will enhance pharmacovigilance and deepen our understanding of the safety profile of these treatments. Ultimately, the study's findings will benefit both Korean medicine practices and global integrated approaches to postpartum care.
