Material flow analysis (MFA) is a growing tool for resource management and environmental protection, though it's typically used statically for single years, overlooking temporal effects on material budgets. This research developed a comprehensive multiyear model of Austria's phosphorus (P) budget from 1990 to 2011. The primary goal was to analyze its evolution over time and determine if a multiyear perspective could enhance static budget assessments. A new methodology was also introduced to evaluate data quality, characterize its features, and quantify associated uncertainties. The analysis revealed substantial changes in approximately half of the material flows since 1990, with some shifts occurring abruptly. However, due to inherent uncertainties, it was challenging to definitively differentiate between flows that remained constant and those that underwent moderate changes. A key finding was that the amount of P in waste streams grew faster than its recovery. In 1990, recovery accounted for 55% to 60% of total waste P, but this figure dropped to 40% by 2011. Losses of P in landfills and cement kilns consistently ranged between 40% and 50%. Methodologically, the multiyear approach expanded the budget's conceptual model, making it more robust for material accounting and monitoring. Furthermore, examining the data reconciliation process over an extended period proved effective in pinpointing systematic errors within the model.

Phosphorus (P) is an indispensable element in contemporary society, serving as a critical component in fertilizers and often limiting growth. Beyond agriculture, it has diverse industrial uses, including car engine lubricants, pesticides, rechargeable batteries, and flame retardants (Schipper 2014). The sole source of P is mineral deposits, and its long-term availability is uncertain. Estimates for phosphate rock reserves, resources, peak production, and lifespan are highly contentious, with projections varying widely from decades to centuries (Cordell et al. 2009; Van Kauwenbergh 2010; Van Vuuren et al. 2010; Cooper et al. 2011; Cordell and White 2011; Sverdrup and Ragnarsdottir 2011; Vaccari and Strigul 2011; Ott and Rechberger 2012; Edixhoven et al. 2013; Koppelaar and Weikard 2013; Mohr 2013; Scholz and Wellmer 2013). Even if scarcity concerns are debated, other compelling reasons exist for improving P management. Key challenges include water body eutrophication, heavy metal accumulation in agricultural soils from mineral fertilizer use, and geopolitical risks stemming from the concentrated nature of mineral deposits (Condron et al. 2013; Ulrich et al. 2013a, 2013b). Recognizing these supply risks, the European Commission recently designated phosphate rock as a critical raw material (European Commission 2013).
Since 2008, the challenges surrounding phosphorus (P) have spurred the formation of multistakeholder platforms and increased research efforts, as noted by Ulrich and Schnug (2013). Material flow analysis (MFA) has been crucial in this context, helping to evaluate P flows and stocks and pinpoint areas with inefficient management or high recycling potential. This is evident from two recent global reviews of P MFAs conducted at various scales (Cordell et al. 2012; Chowdhury et al. 2014). Historically, these MFA studies have predominantly relied on static, single-year budgets—balanced models of material flows and stocks. However, a significant knowledge gap identified by Chowdhury and colleagues (2014) is the scarcity of multiyear analyses, despite the substantial insights gained from the limited studies that have adopted this approach.
Several studies have demonstrated the value of multiyear Material Flow Analysis (MFA) in understanding phosphorus (P) dynamics. For instance, Li et al. (2012) investigated urban P metabolism in China between 1986 and 2006, linking urban P inflow to population growth, dietary shifts, and urban expansion speed. Their work also revealed how increased urban per capita income influenced urban P stock and P outflow to non-urban areas. Similarly, Ma et al. (2012) performed a comprehensive P MFA for all of China from 1984 to 2008. This study not only documented a substantial rise in P consumption and waste, fluctuating but increasing P ore extraction, and decreasing P recycling rates, but also connected these trends to specific socioeconomic drivers. Beyond China, multiyear analyses have provided crucial insights in Europe. Senthilkumar et al. (2012) observed that France significantly reduced its agricultural mineral fertilizer use by half from 1990 to 2006, yet maintained consistent agricultural output and environmental P losses. In Sweden, Neset et al. (2008) examined P flow in food consumption and production for a city from 1870 to 2000. They found that P reaching consumers increased due to both population growth and a dietary shift towards more meat and dairy. Their research also highlighted a sharp decrease in the reuse-to-loss ratio for P in waste and wastewater. Furthermore, Lamprecht et al. (2011) quantified the profound effect of the Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy crisis on Switzerland's P cycle, specifically the ban on reusing P-rich meat and bone meal in animal feed.
Multiyear Material Flow Analyses (MFAs) offer valuable insights for environmental, resource, and waste management. While multiyear approaches are common in broader economic accounting, their application to phosphorus studies is hindered by data scarcity, as noted by Chowdhury and colleagues (2014). However, Austria presents a unique opportunity for such research. The country benefits from a comprehensive phosphorus budget (Egle et al. 2014) covering 2004-2008 and extensive historical data, making it an ideal case study to overcome typical data limitations.
This research undertakes a multiyear examination of Austria's phosphorus (P) budget, spanning from 1990 to 2011. A primary objective is to pinpoint and evaluate the magnitude of temporal shifts within the system over these two decades. This study distinguishes itself from prior multiyear P material flow analyses (MFAs) through its exceptionally detailed model, facilitating a systematic assessment unlike any before. A key innovation involves characterizing data quality and uncertainty, alongside quantifying and discussing their impact on evaluating temporal changes. A secondary aim is to ascertain if a multiyear MFA can enhance data quality comprehension and improve the model itself, thereby benefiting single-year static budgets. Consequently, beyond simply analyzing evolving P flow and stock characteristics, this work explores new methodological strategies potentially applicable to other materials.
Despite growing acknowledgment of data uncertainty's crucial role in Material Flow Analysis (MFA) studies, it frequently receives inadequate attention, often being overlooked or addressed only qualitatively (Laner et al. 2014; Rechberger et al. 2014). Laner and colleagues (2014) categorize existing approaches into three main types: qualitative/semi-quantitative methods, which convey author confidence without formal data quality assessment; data classification methods, which evaluate quality without rigorous uncertainty propagation; and statistical methods, which actively propagate uncertainties within the model. This research employs a new methodology developed by Laner and colleagues (2015). This method integrates data classification with exponential-type uncertainty characterization functions, drawing inspiration from Hedbrandt and Sörme (2001), and incorporates the life cycle inventory data quality assessment framework by Weidema and Wesnæs (1996). The selection of this novel approach was driven by several advantages: it characterizes data quality using various indicators to highlight data characteristics and issues; its uncertainty quantification process ensures internal model consistency; it utilizes coefficients of variation, making its results compatible with the STAN MFA software used for model balancing; and it offers a transparent and well-documented uncertainty characterization.
This study employs the Material Flow Analysis (MFA) methodology outlined by Brunner and Rechberger (2004) to examine phosphorus (P) flows at a national scale. Categorized as "Accounting" by Van der Voet (2002), this approach is ideal for monitoring material flows and stocks, aiding policymakers in identifying trends, evaluating policy effectiveness, and pinpointing data gaps. The model developed is a multiyear static design, balancing 22 annual snapshots concurrently. Each of these yearly nodes operates independently, meaning flow values are input individually for every year. However, stocks are an exception to this independence. Only the initial stock value for 1990 is directly input; subsequent stock amounts are derived from annual changes calculated using closed mass balances. This approach was chosen due to limited data availability for total stock values, making it more practical to base calculations on the better-documented flow data after an initial stock estimation. It's important to distinguish this method from dynamic MFA modeling, which treats time as a variable to explore the temporal behavior of stocks and flows, often used for analyzing persistent toxic accumulations or predicting future waste and emission scenarios (Van der Voet 2002; Müller et al. 2014). In contrast, this multiyear static model does not aim to predict future scenarios or capture the dynamic interplay between stocks and future flows. Instead, its purpose is to provide a comprehensive, data-driven description of the system's evolution over time.
The foundational conceptual model for Austria's national phosphorus (P) budget, developed by Egle and colleagues (2014), served as the starting point for this study. While maintaining a consistent model structure across the entire time series, certain adjustments were necessary. For instance, a flow representing meat and bone meal used as animal feed was incorporated, reflecting its significant role prior to a 2001 ban. The growing bioenergy sector also warranted its own distinct process within the model. Conversely, the chemical industry's impact in Austria, as identified by Egle and colleagues (2014), was deemed insufficient to merit a separate process and was thus integrated into the broader "Industry" process. This "Industry" process additionally covers the trade and production of food, feed, raw phosphates, and fertilizers. The revised model, detailed in Figure 1, comprises nine processes, all of which, except for "Crop farming" and "Water bodies," are further elaborated through subsystems provided as Supporting Information (S1). Overall, the system encompasses 56 processes, eight stocks, 122 flows, and two transfer coefficients.
The methodology for calculating input values, including specific equations and data sources, is comprehensively detailed in the Supporting Information (S2). The majority of material flows were determined by multiplying the quantity of "goods" (defined as economically valuable substances or mixtures, per Brunner and Rechberger [2004]) by their respective phosphorus (P) concentrations. However, for a select group of flows, direct data on P mass per unit time were already available due to extensive prior research and statistical tracking of P. Examples of these directly available P flows include wastewater, sewage sludge and effluents, mineral fertilizers, and environmental P flows such as atmospheric deposition and agricultural/forestry soil emissions to water bodies. While all system compartments benefit from substantial data availability for both goods and direct P flows, there are notable variations in the frequency and granularity of this data. Highly detailed, yearly data are readily accessible from supply balance sheets for the production, import, export, and consumption of food and animal feed products. Similarly, national statistics and official sector reports offer detailed annual data for processes like Animal Husbandry, Crop Farming, Forestry, food consumption, and chemical import/export. The Bioenergy process, however, requires a more nuanced approach due to differences among its subprocesses. Biofuels production is well-regulated and documented in official annual reports, though these reports primarily focus on the biofuels themselves rather than the input materials or by-products, which are crucial for the P budget. In contrast, data for biogas and biomass plants provide more information on input materials and by-products, but this information is not consistently available on an annual basis. For urban wastewater treatment, official reports, now published biannually in accordance with European Directive 91/271/EEC, provide data on P loads entering and exiting municipal treatment plants, along with average P removal rates and sewage system connection rates. However, direct data on the specific P contributions from households and industries are lacking, necessitating estimations based on average per capita P loads. The primary data source for Waste Management is national waste management plans, which document waste volumes, origins, management, and disposal. These plans, however, are published only every five years and often aggregate information, particularly concerning waste flow management and disposal, making them less than ideal for this study. Supplementary official reports offer more detailed and sometimes multi-year information on specific processes or waste flows, partially compensating for these limitations. Despite these efforts, the Waste Management process, critical to the P budget, does not possess the same level of informational detail as the production and import-export sectors. Finally, estimating environmental P flows, despite considerable data availability, largely depended on modeling due to the inherent complexity of these processes.
Multiyear Material Flow Analysis (MFA) studies encounter challenges beyond just incomplete data, specifically the temporal consistency of data sources. This can diminish data availability compared to single-year budgets. For instance, in this study, FAOSTAT was unusable for mineral fertilizer trade data because its accounting methods changed in 2002, creating inconsistent datasets.
Information on phosphorus (P) concentration is widely accessible, mainly from peer-reviewed literature and scientific reports, though often originating from studies in various countries and years. While less comprehensive than data for goods, P concentration data diminishes in detail from production and consumption to waste management. However, this information generally exceeds the requirements of current material flow categories across the system.
The study employs a method for characterizing material flow data uncertainty, building upon the work of Laner et al. (2015), which itself draws from Hedbrant and Sörme (2001) and Weidema and Wesnæs (1996). This multi-step process begins by evaluating how well available data represent the actual values of interest. Similar to the pedigree matrix concept by Weidema and Wesnæs (1996), this evaluation uses a set of quality indicators and established criteria for assigning scores. Six key indicators are used to assess data quality. "Reliability" gauges the documentation and transparency of data collection, sampling, and verification. "Completeness" measures how fully the available data cover the actual values of interest. "Temporal correlation" and "Geographical correlation" assess the divergence of available information from the target data in terms of time and location, respectively. "Further correlation" accounts for other types of discrepancies, such as those related to specific technologies or processes. A novel addition to the original Laner et al. (2015) framework is the "Composition" indicator, which evaluates the level of detail regarding the composition of goods. This is particularly crucial for accurately determining phosphorus (P) concentration, especially for heterogeneous or aggregated flows. Each indicator is assigned one of four evaluation scores, ranging from 1 (best quality) to 4 (worst quality). These indicators and their scoring criteria are detailed in Table 1. While "Reliability" and "Completeness" are universally important, the influence of other indicators on overall uncertainty varies by case. Therefore, their contribution to the final uncertainty also considers the data's "sensitivity"—the degree to which a deviation (e.g., temporal or geographical) from the existing data point impacts the value of interest. Three sensitivity levels are defined: "Highly sensitive," "Sensitive," and "Not sensitive," indicating high, relevant, or irrelevant impact, respectively. The assigned evaluation scores for data quality indicators are then converted into coefficients of variation (CVs) using continuous exponential-type functions, which also allow for intermediate scores. As highlighted by Laner et al. (2015), the choice of these functions is critical, as it significantly influences the resulting uncertainty ranges. Exponential-type curves were selected due to their historical prevalence in similar studies, including that of Hedbrant and Sörme (2001), which forms a basis for this approach. Modelers must also carefully select the parameters defining these functions to ensure their plausibility for each specific case study. In this research, the mathematical functions detailed in the Supporting Information (S3) were utilized, yielding the CVs presented in Table 2. These CV values align with uncertainty levels reported in other P Material Flow Analysis (MFA) studies, which typically range from 0% to 100% (e.g., 1.5%–50% in Klinglmair et al. (Forthcoming), 7%–51% in Van Dijk et al. (2015), 10%–100% in Egle et al. (2014), 5%–100% in Ott and Rechberger (2012), 5%–50% in Neset et al. (2008), and 0%–50% in Antikainen et al. (2005)). Assuming normality and recognizing each indicator as an independent aspect of overall data uncertainty, the total uncertainty of the input data, represented by the coefficient of variation CVa, is calculated using Equation 1. This equation combines the coefficients of variation for Reliability (CVr), Completeness (CVc), Composition (CVco), Temporal correlation (CVt), Geographical correlation (CVg), and Further correlation (CVf). If no data are available and input values are based on expert judgment, uncertainty is directly assessed through the "Expert judgment" indicator.
When data is absent, and input values rely on expert judgment, the "Expert judgment" indicator directly quantifies the associated uncertainty.
The methodology employed in this study, while robust, inherently carries a degree of subjectivity in two key areas. Firstly, assigning evaluation scores to the quality indicators involves subjective judgment. While the best (1) and worst (4) scores are clear-cut for most indicators, intermediate scores are less definitive, allowing modelers to leverage their expertise to capture nuanced differences. The indicator "Temporal correlation" is an exception, as its scores are tied to a precise number of years, offering less ambiguity. Secondly, the selection of mathematical functions to convert these scores into Coefficients of Variation (CVs) is not unique. Validating these functions is often challenging or impossible in such studies due to a scarcity of empirical data. Consequently, caution is advised when comparing the uncertainties derived here with those from other research. Despite these subjective elements, the approach offers significant benefits. It provides a consistent framework for estimating uncertainties within the model, thanks to its use of continuous functions. This internal consistency is crucial in any Material Flow Analysis (MFA), particularly given its impact on data reconciliation and identifying critical data points. In this study, where flows, their temporal evolution, and associated uncertainties are systematically assessed and compared, this consistency is especially valuable. Furthermore, the structured characterization of uncertainty enhances transparency, providing readers with a clear documentation of the rationale and calculations behind the MFA results, thereby ensuring reproducibility.
Laner and colleagues (2015) offer a comprehensive explanation of this method and its relation to other approaches.
The study aimed to quantify the temporal changes within the system over a 22-year period by analyzing 122 material flows and eight stock change rates. To establish a baseline, the percentage change for each flow was calculated annually relative to 1990, the initial year of the time series. Addressing potential distortions from null values, an artificial 100% change was assigned when a new flow emerged, balancing the -100% change observed when a flow ceased. Subsequently, flows and stock rates were classified into three categories based on their degree of temporal change: "constant" for no alteration, "moderately changing," and "extremely changing." To ensure consistent treatment of both increases and decreases, the dividing line between the "moderately" and "extremely" changing categories was set at a factor of 2. This factor corresponds to a 100% positive change or a -50% negative change, as visually represented in Figure 2a. This categorization allowed for a structured assessment of how significantly each element evolved over the study's duration.
To determine how uncertainty influenced the detection of temporal changes, the analysis introduced various tolerance levels (Figure 2b). These included fixed ranges of ±5%, ±10%, ±15%, and ±20%, within which any observed flow changes were disregarded, effectively treating them as constant. Additionally, the study incorporated two tolerance levels based on the inherent uncertainty of each flow: ±1 standard deviation and ±2 standard deviations. Under these conditions, a flow's variation was only classified as a genuine change if the corresponding 68.3% and 95.5% data intervals of its normal distribution did not overlap.
To determine if changes in flows were gradual or abrupt, the analysis examined year-over-year variations.
Stocks were omitted from the analysis due to their significantly larger total value compared to annual or multi-year flows, and their considerable initial uncertainty.
The STAN software (Cencic and Rechberger 2008) was employed for balancing the model. This software's algorithm operates on the premise that parameters are independent, normally distributed random variables, with uncertain values represented by their mean and standard deviation. STAN then utilizes Gaussian error propagation and data reconciliation to adjust mean values and determine overall uncertainty. During reconciliation, the standard deviation acts as a weighting factor, leading to a greater adjustment for values with higher uncertainty. Further mathematical specifics of these calculations are detailed in Fellner and colleagues (2011).
To determine if systematic errors existed, the impact of the reconciliation process on the time series was examined. This involved checking whether flow alterations were consistently in one direction (increased or decreased) rather than random. Changes under 5% between input and reconciled values were deemed random and insignificant. Additionally, the overall system's reconciliation was evaluated across the study period to identify year-to-year differences. This was achieved by summing the percentage variation of input values for all flows annually, then normalizing these sums against the 1990 reconciliation for comparison. While typically requiring consideration of the model's overdetermination (equations versus unknown variables), this was unnecessary here as the model maintained a consistent degree of overdetermination each year.
This study characterizes data quality using a method that assigns evaluation scores (1 to 4) to a predefined set of indicators. To gauge the system's overall quality, the arithmetic mean of these scores was computed for every indicator and year across all flows, with the results visualized in Figure 3. Out of 106 quantifiable flows, over 80% were determined by multiplying goods by their phosphorus (P) concentration. The remaining flows had P flow data directly available. Consequently, the quality assessment was performed independently for these two distinct data types. Figure 3 illustrates the average scores for six quality indicators: reliability, completeness, composition, temporal correlation, geographical correlation, and further correlation, for both goods (mass per time) and directly available P flows (P mass per time).
Data documentation, or reliability, has historically been strong for phosphorus concentration and direct phosphorus flows, supported by extensive P content measurements in various goods. While reliability for goods was initially weaker, it has improved in production and trade statistics due to international guidelines. However, the Consumption and Waste management processes still exhibit the lowest levels of documentation and transparency.

Figure 4a illustrates the findings regarding the extent of changes in the budget compared to the 1990 baseline. The sensitivity of these results to the chosen tolerance levels is notable. When tolerance levels between 0% and ±5% are applied, the analysis indicates that one-third of the flows and stock change rates underwent moderate alteration, while two-thirds experienced extreme variations. However, as the tolerance range increases from ±10% to ±20%, the proportion of moderately changing flows and stock rates progressively declines to 15%. The application of a specific standard deviation yields results closely resembling those of the ±20% range. Furthermore, using a tolerance level of twice the standard deviation reduces both the extreme and moderate fractions to 50% and 5%, respectively. Ultimately, the analysis concludes that approximately half of the flows and stock change rates exhibited significant changes, including the emergence or disappearance of certain flows, and others at least doubling or halving their original values. For the remaining half, however, the high uncertainty of the results prevents a definitive determination of whether they remained constant or experienced moderate changes. Figure 4 visually categorizes the temporal change of 122 flows and eight stock change rates: (a) based on changes relative to 1990, and (b) based on annual changes. The y-axis represents the count of flows and stock change rates within each category, with results presented across various tolerance levels (uncertainty thresholds for detecting temporal changes).
Figure 4b presents the second part of this analysis, examining annual changes in flows and stock change rates to determine if shifts were gradual or abrupt. While most flows exhibited gradual and moderate changes, a significant portion—between 24% and 33%, depending on the tolerance level—experienced at least one extreme and sudden variation. The study also revealed that incorporating uncertainty ranges makes it challenging to identify smaller year-on-year changes, which has implications for effective phosphorus management monitoring.

The study's examination of the data reconciliation process across the time series revealed that under 10% of the material flows consistently underwent reconciliation, meaning their values were adjusted in a uniform direction. This consistent alteration, even if minor, points to systematic errors within the model. A prime illustration of this is the Composting subprocess, part of the Waste Management sector. Throughout the study period, as depicted in Figure 7, the reconciliation process invariably decreased input flows while increasing output flows for composting. This systematic discrepancy could stem from two possible issues: the aggregation of composted sewage sludge data with other mechanically-biologically treated sludges, and the varied phosphorus content in compost products. While the precise cause remains elusive due to insufficient information, the identification of this systematic inconsistency allows for future correction with more accurate data. This reconciliation analysis was not merely a final step but was integrated into the iterative Material Flow Analysis (MFA) methodology, as outlined by Laner and colleagues (2014). This integration proved instrumental in pinpointing errors, which were then resolved by either acquiring more detailed data or refining estimations, ultimately enhancing the budget's underlying database.
The study's findings indicate a general improvement in data quality over time, leading to a reduced impact of reconciliation on input values across the entire system. However, despite this overall enhancement, the reconciliation's influence exhibits fluctuations within the time series. This suggests that the quality of a static Material Flow Analysis (MFA) is dependent on the specific year chosen for its execution.
Over the past two decades, Austria significantly decreased its use of mineral fertilizers, mirroring a trend observed in other Western European nations. Concurrently, the country's livestock population declined, leading to a further reduction in phosphorus accumulation in agricultural soils. Despite these reductions, the substantial phosphorus surplus built up in previous decades meant there was no discernible effect on crop yields or phosphorus emissions into water bodies, a finding consistent with research from France.
The study observed a consistent average daily phosphorus (P) intake of 1.6 gP/cap*d among the Austrian population. This figure aligns with research from Germany (Welch et al., 2009) and Central/Eastern Europe (Elmadfa, 2009), though it surpasses the recommendations by DGE et al. (2003). While food availability might suggest a certain intake, the actual P consumption can vary significantly due to losses of edible food and nutrients during consumption. A notable finding was the increase in wasted available P, rising from approximately 30% in 1990 to 40% by 2011. Conversely, the recovery rate of P from waste within the Consumption process showed significant improvement, climbing from about 20% in 1990 to roughly 60% in 2011. This positive change is largely attributed to better separate collection of organic waste. Home composting also contributed to this improvement, albeit to a lesser extent; however, its effectiveness as a management strategy is debatable due to the potential for P accumulation in gardens, as highlighted by Egle et al. (2014). Another key trend identified was the substantial rise in imported food P, escalating from 1,700 ± 23% tonnes in 1990 to 9,800 ± 10% tonnes in 2011. This increase in imported P has significant implications for material accounting, environmental protection, and resource management, raising questions about how to monitor and manage the P consumption and associated pollution occurring in other nations.
In Austria, while biomass thermal plants have a long history, the biogas and biofuels industries are relatively new, emerging around 2002-2003 (excluding a few small, rural facilities). These sectors experienced rapid growth, with P consumption from crops increasing significantly from 1,200 ± 32% tonnes in 2004 to 8,600 ± 30% tonnes in 2011. Currently, they also process about 1,000 tP/yr of industrial organic waste and approximately 800 tP/yr of manure. Since biogas, bioethanol, and biodiesel themselves contain almost no phosphorus, the P from these processes is channeled in two main ways: some returns to agricultural land as biogas digestates, and the rest becomes by-products used as animal feed. This study estimated that these P-rich by-products contained 7,100 ± 31% tP in 2011, a substantial amount when compared to the total animal feed P consumption of 42,800 ± 12% tP. The rise of such P-rich animal feed, particularly from rapidly expanding biofuels production, has raised concerns in countries like the United States, as it could counteract efforts to reduce P loads in manure through feed management practices. It's important to note that for this study, only data on dried distiller's grains (bioethanol by-products) were directly available; estimates for biodiesel by-products and their end uses relied on indirect data and assumptions.
The total phosphorus (P) entering the Waste Management process, as depicted in Figure 8a, saw a consistent and substantial increase between 1990 and 2011. This input comprises various waste streams: solid waste from households, industry, and green spaces; fallen livestock from animal husbandry; all sewage sludge from wastewater treatment; ashes from biomass thermal plants; and imported waste. Notably, certain P-containing materials are excluded from this total, specifically home-composted kitchen and green residues, and organic by-products from the food industry directly repurposed for animal feed. These are instead tracked as internal flows within the Consumption and Industry processes, respectively. While population growth contributed to this trend, it doesn't fully account for the rise in waste P. Per capita waste P escalated from 1.2-1.5 kilograms in 1990 to 2.0-2.2 kilograms in 2011. The primary driver of this increase was sewage sludge, which grew from 3,000 ± 11% tP in 1990 to 7,100 ± 6% tP in 2011. This surge in sewage sludge was due to population expansion, higher connection rates to treatment plants, and significantly improved P removal efficiencies. Other important, though less critical, contributors included organic industrial waste, reflecting the expanding food industry, and disposed biomass ashes, indicating the growth of the bioenergy sector.
The analysis of phosphorus (P) recovery from waste reveals significant shifts and challenges over the study period. Figure 8b details the various pathways for waste P recovery, encompassing materials like meat and bone meal used as animal feed, sewage sludge, compost, and other materials applied to agricultural fields, recycled wood and paper, compost for gardens, and waste valorized in biogas and biomass thermal plants. Notably, P recovery from landscaping activities and exported waste is excluded from this assessment. Landscaping is omitted due to the uncertain utility of P recovery in this context, while exported waste is excluded because its final management or disposal information is often unavailable. A dramatic change in P recovery occurred in 2001 when the use of meat and bone meal as animal feed was banned. Prior to this, approximately 5,000 tonnes of P (tP) per year were recovered through this route in the 1990s, as depicted by the sharp decline in Figure 8b. Following the ban, total recovered P saw a rapid increase, largely driven by the application of processed animal fat and meal to agricultural land. However, despite this increase in absolute terms, the overall efficiency of P recovery from waste declined. The ratio of total recovered P to the total waste input dropped from 55-60% in 1990 to roughly 40% in 2011, indicating that waste generation, in terms of P content, outpaced recycling efforts. A substantial portion of the total waste P, averaging 40-45% (with annual fluctuations between 35% and 55%), was lost through disposal in cement kilns and landfills. P loss in cement kilns, primarily through the use of meat and bone meal as a secondary fuel, was negligible before 2001. After the ban on its use in animal feed, this flow surged to approximately 4,000 tP/yr, gradually decreasing to about 3,000 tP/yr in recent years (Figure 8c). Sewage sludge contributed only a minor amount to this loss. Conversely, the amount of P deposited in landfills remained relatively stable, ranging from 4,000 to 6,000 tP/yr throughout the study period (Figure 8d). However, the composition of materials contributing to this landfill P changed considerably. In 1990, direct disposal of sewage sludge, residual waste, and ashes from municipal waste incineration and sludge co-incineration were the primary sources. By 2011, the dominant sources were ashes from municipal waste incineration, sludge co-incineration, and thermal biomass plants, followed by mechanically-biologically stabilized sludge and municipal waste. The reliability of data varies across different waste management flows. Figure 8, which includes uncertainty ranges, highlights these differences. The total P input (Figure 8a) generally has lower uncertainty than P recovery (Figure 8b) and P loss in cement kilns (Figure 8c). This is attributed to more detailed reporting on waste generation compared to its subsequent management. The uncertainty associated with P loss in landfills (Figure 8d) is notably lower and has decreased over time, reflecting improved regulation and monitoring of organic waste and sewage sludge disposal.
Over a relatively short 22-year period, Austria's national phosphorus (P) budget experienced significant and sometimes sudden shifts across various system components. Notably, the bioenergy sector's rapid expansion in the last decade has led to substantial flows of raw materials, waste, and by-products. However, this growth has lacked adequate monitoring of these associated flows, a crucial oversight given their potential to negatively impact the P balance in agriculture, particularly in animal husbandry. While P recovery from household waste saw a considerable increase from 20% in 1990 to 60% in 2011, overall P recovery from total waste decreased from 55-60% to approximately 40%. A striking example of how seemingly unrelated events can influence the P budget is the substantial and abrupt diversion of P-rich meat and bone meal to cement kilns since 2001, a direct consequence of the Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy crisis.
When accounting for result uncertainty in temporal change analysis, significant shifts across the study period remain evident. However, for 50% of the flows, distinguishing between constant and moderately changing states proves challenging. The study highlights that identifying annual variations is highly susceptible to data set uncertainty, a crucial factor for effectively monitoring future policy and measure performance.
This study's multiyear approach offered methodological benefits by identifying systematic data inconsistencies and problems within the model. Such issues would likely have gone undetected in a single-year static analysis, thereby improving the overall model.
This study proves the practicality of creating detailed national budget time series. It highlights significant temporal shifts impacting environmental, resource, and waste management, while also revealing numerous methodological advantages and added benefits.
This study highlights the utility of national authorities establishing comparable resource accounting systems. Such systems offer several key benefits: they can identify detrimental trends in material flows, stocks, efficiencies, and recovery rates; they enable the monitoring of policy and regulatory effectiveness; and they assist in refining national data collection by pinpointing areas contributing most to system uncertainty.
