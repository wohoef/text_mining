This study explored how serum levels of trace elements and heavy metals are affected in cases of threatened abortion, a frequent early pregnancy complication. Prior research has suggested a link between altered trace element levels, elevated heavy metal concentrations, and spontaneous abortion, though this association requires further elucidation. To investigate this, 45 pregnant women diagnosed with threatened abortion were recruited as the patient group. A control group comprised 40 healthy, nonpregnant women. Blood samples were collected from all participants, and atomic absorption spectrophotometry was employed to quantify the serum concentrations of various trace elements and heavy metals. The findings revealed statistically significant reductions in serum zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), and manganese (Mn) in the threatened abortion group compared to controls (p < 0.001). Conversely, serum levels of copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb) were significantly elevated in the patient group (p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed in serum cobalt (Co) levels between the two groups (p > 0.001). In conclusion, the study suggests that imbalances in essential trace elements, particularly decreased Fe and Zn, coupled with elevated levels of certain toxic heavy metals in the blood, may serve as crucial diagnostic and prognostic indicators for threatened abortion.

Threatened abortion, a frequent complication in early pregnancy (prior to 20 weeks), is typically identified by mild to moderate vaginal bleeding with a closed cervix. Approximately 25% of pregnant women experience first-trimester vaginal bleeding, with about half of these pregnancies resulting in spontaneous abortion. This condition is linked to a higher likelihood of antepartum hemorrhage, preterm labor, and intrauterine growth retardation. Factors such as prior pregnancy loss, a history of congenital abnormalities in offspring, thrombophilia, diabetes, hypothyroidism, infertility treatments, advanced maternal age, uterine abnormalities, and environmental influences like nutrition, all elevate the risk of spontaneous abortion.
During pregnancy, essential trace elements are vital for fetal growth and development, participating in numerous biochemical pathways. Pregnancy itself causes shifts in the concentrations of these elements due to the fetus's demands and the mother's physiological changes. Imbalances in these micronutrients and their homeostasis during gestation can contribute to a range of adverse pregnancy outcomes. These include miscarriage, preterm labor, stillbirth, restricted fetal growth, birth defects, and premature rupture of membranes. Zinc (Zn) stands out as a critical essential trace element, particularly important during the embryonic phase. Its role in cellular multiplication, differentiation, and organogenesis is paramount. As a component of over 300 enzymes, Zn is involved in metabolism, nucleic acid synthesis, and antioxidant functions. Disruptions to zinc homeostasis can have severe consequences for pregnancy, potentially leading to fetal growth restriction or embryonic and fetal demise. Similarly, copper (Cu) and iron (Fe) are indispensable for many bodily biochemical processes, and their deficiencies during pregnancy can precipitate widespread complications. In contrast, cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb) are environmental heavy metals that are not essential for the body. Prolonged exposure to these toxic substances can interfere with fetal development and negatively affect pregnancy outcomes. Research indicates a link between heavy metals like Pb and Cd and spontaneous abortion, though the precise nature of this association requires further clarification.
This study investigated how serum trace element and heavy metal levels differ in threatened abortions compared to healthy controls. While these elements have been well-researched in normal pregnancies, their impact on conditions like threatened abortion remains less understood.
This prospective study involved 45 pregnant women diagnosed with threatened abortion, identified by vaginal bleeding and a closed cervix during the first trimester, with gestational ages ranging from 6 to 14 weeks. These participants were patients at the Van Gynecology and Obstetrics Clinic. A control group of 40 healthy, nonpregnant women was also included. Throughout their pregnancies, the women in the study group were monitored, and their pregnancy outcomes were recorded. Specifically, pregnant women who experienced preterm delivery (before 37 weeks of gestation) or abortion (before 24 weeks of gestation) were included in the final analysis.
Gestational age was determined using the last menstrual period and first-trimester ultrasound. When the self-reported last menstrual period differed by more than seven days from the ultrasonography-calculated date, the ultrasound measurement was prioritized. Several criteria led to exclusion from the study: multiple pregnancies, smoking, uterine or placental abnormalities, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, polyps, cervicitis, or cervical myomas. Additionally, women who had taken any vitamin supplements were not included. All participants received comprehensive information about the study and provided written consent. The research adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was granted by the Van Regional Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee for Non-interventional Clinical Researches on February 28, 2017.
To conduct the biochemical analyses, samples were processed at the Central Research Laboratory of Yuzuncu Yil University and the Biochemistry Laboratory of the Chemistry Department, Faculty of Science at Yuzuncu Yil University. Blood was collected from both pregnant patients and healthy nonpregnant controls simultaneously. Participants provided 5 mL of blood from the antecubital fossa vein after an overnight fast, using sterile, disposable needles. The venipuncture site was disinfected to ensure sample purity. Following collection, blood samples were allowed to clot for 1 to 2 hours at room temperature before being centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 minutes. The separated serum was then stored at -20°C for subsequent analysis. The concentrations of various trace elements and heavy metals, including manganese (Mn), magnesium (Mg), zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), cobalt (Co), lead (Pb), and cadmium (Cd), were measured using a UNICAM-929 atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS).
Continuous variables were described using mean ± SD. For comparisons between the two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed when data did not meet normality assumptions, while a t-test was used for normally distributed data. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value less than 0.05. All data analysis was conducted using SPSS 20 software.
Table 1 details the descriptive characteristics of the study participants. Age was comparable between the threatened abortion group and the control group. Importantly, neither the pregnant women nor the control subjects were taking iron, mineral, or multivitamin supplements, nor did they consume alcohol or smoke. The control group comprised healthy, nonpregnant volunteers. The pregnant patient cohort was further categorized. Ten patients experienced spontaneous abortions, while the remaining 35 had pregnancies that progressed beyond 24 weeks of gestation. Within the study, 24 patients were classified as preterm, delivering between 24 and 37 weeks of gestation. Specifically, 14 of these preterm deliveries occurred between 34 and 37 weeks, and 10 occurred between 28 and 34 weeks. Ten patients in the threatened abortion group were diagnosed with hereditary thrombophilia and were treated with low molecular weight heparin. Three patients were identified with Trisomy 21 via ultrasound in early gestation and were subsequently excluded from the study. While a detailed analysis of diet and lifestyle habits was not conducted, participants in both the study and control groups shared similar living conditions and socioeconomic backgrounds. All individuals resided in rural areas, and their socioeconomic status was considered low due to income levels.
Table 2 presents the concentrations of certain trace elements and heavy metals in the study participants. The results indicated a statistically significant decrease (p < 0.001) in serum zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), and manganese (Mn) levels among women experiencing threatened abortion compared to the healthy control group. Specifically, the patient group exhibited mean levels of Zn (87.90 ± 9.59 μg/dL), Fe (34.63 ± 14.9 μg/dL), Mg (31.16 ± 5.74 μg/dL), and Mn (16.8 ± 5.2 μg/dL), which were markedly lower than those in the control group (Zn: 152.8 ± 3.34 μg/dL; Fe: 74.9 ± 3.5 μg/dL; Mg: 61.59 ± 4.07 μg/dL; Mn: 22.9 ± 1.8 μg/dL). In contrast, serum levels of copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb) were found to be significantly elevated (p < 0.001) in the threatened abortion group. The patient group showed higher mean concentrations for Cu (177.9 ± 74.40 μg/dL), Cd (4.59 ± 0.44 μg/dL), and Pb (12.65 ± 0.51 μg/dL) when compared to the control group (Cu: 72.2 ± 10.2 μg/dL; Cd: 0.20 ± 0.03 μg/dL; Pb: 1.13 ± 0.02 μg/dL). No significant difference was observed in serum cobalt (Co) levels between the threatened abortion group (1.71 ± 0.14 μg/dL) and the control group (1.06 ± 0.02 μg/dL).
Trace elements are vital for health, with imbalances leading to disease. Pregnancy alters their concentrations due to fetal demands and maternal physiological changes, impacting overall health.
Thrombophilia, characterized by an increased risk of venous thrombosis, can be hereditary or acquired. During pregnancy, it may arise from genetic mutations like Factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A, or deficiencies in proteins C, S, and antithrombin. While the link between thrombophilia and pregnancy loss is established, the precise mechanism is not fully understood, though thrombosis in decidual vessels is a suspected cause. This study observed a significantly higher prevalence of thrombophilia among patients experiencing abortion. This finding might be influenced by regional factors such as increased consanguineous marriages and shared socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds among participants.
Iron (Fe) is a crucial trace element, integral to hemoglobin and myoglobin, and plays a vital role in oxygen transport, storage, and utilization. Pregnancy is particularly vulnerable to Fe deficiency, which has been linked to increased oxidative stress, placental and fetal hypoxia, and diminished immunity. This deficiency is a significant public health concern, especially in developing nations where it disproportionately affects pregnant women. Our research indicated that serum Fe levels were notably lower in women experiencing threatened abortion compared to a healthy control group. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating reduced Fe levels in patients who have miscarried. The implications of maternal Fe deficiency are severe, elevating risks of mortality and morbidity for both the mother and the developing fetus, leading to complications during prenatal and postnatal periods. Consequently, adherence to specialist recommendations for Fe supplementation during pregnancy is essential to mitigate adverse outcomes and ensure adequate fetal development.
Copper (Cu) is a vital trace element, indispensable for the creation of numerous enzymes crucial to bodily functions. During pregnancy, elevated maternal serum copper levels are linked to increased ceruloplasmin, a consequence of higher estrogen concentrations. This study observed significantly higher serum copper levels in women experiencing threatened abortions compared to healthy controls. These findings contrast with research by Alebic-Juretic et al., which reported lower copper concentrations in pathological pregnancies, including threatened abortions. Conversely, Borella et al.'s work supports our observation, indicating higher copper levels in threatened and spontaneous abortions. The metabolic pathways of copper and iron are closely intertwined during pregnancy, meaning a deficiency in one profoundly impacts the other. Specifically, maternal iron deficiency can lead to increased copper levels in the liver, which in turn elevates serum copper and ceruloplasmin activity in the mother.
Zinc is crucial for numerous enzymes, including DNA and RNA polymerase, and is involved in key metabolic processes. Low serum zinc levels can negatively impact embryonic and fetal development. This occurs through mechanisms such as diminished cell proliferation, reduced protein synthesis, heightened cellular oxidative damage, and increased apoptosis. Our study observed significantly lower serum zinc levels in threatened abortion cases compared to controls. Previous research indicates that zinc deficiency in early pregnancy can contribute to abortion and congenital malformations. Consequently, monitoring pregnant women's serum zinc levels is vital for ensuring healthy fetal development.
Magnesium (Mg) and manganese (Mn) are vital essential trace elements crucial for metabolic regulation and bone development. Mg's role extends to being a cofactor for numerous enzymes, and it significantly influences peripheral vasodilation and neurochemical transmission. During pregnancy, Mg deficiency has been linked to complications like hypertension, preeclampsia, placental dysfunction, and premature labor. This study observed lower serum Mg and Mn levels in women experiencing threatened abortion compared to healthy controls. Interestingly, prior research has reported no significant differences in Mg levels between pathological pregnancies and healthy individuals. While Mg supplementation during pregnancy has been suggested to mitigate complications such as preterm birth and intrauterine growth retardation, findings regarding Mn are mixed. One study found elevated serum Mn in women who had experienced miscarriage, whereas another noted higher, though not statistically significant, Mn levels in recurrent abortion cases.
Cobalt, a transition metal and a component of vitamin B12, can see its maternal concentrations fluctuate during pregnancy. Studies suggest women may accumulate more cobalt than men, potentially due to increased metabolic iron loss. While trace metal changes in pregnancy have been examined, cobalt has often been overlooked. This study found no significant difference in serum cobalt levels between the threatened abortion group and the healthy control group.
Cadmium (Cd) is a toxic heavy metal with metalloestrogenic properties, meaning it can bind to and stimulate estrogen receptor subtypes alpha and beta. This activity positions Cd as a potential contributor to the development of conditions such as breast and endometrial carcinoma, endometriosis, and spontaneous abortions. Research indicates that women generally exhibit higher concentrations of Cd in their blood, urine, and kidneys compared to men. A potential explanation for this elevated level in women is the significant increase in Cd absorption associated with iron deficiency. It is crucial to recognize that the toxic effects of Cd are amplified during pregnancy, posing risks to both the mother and the developing fetus. Within the general non-smoking population, dietary intake represents the primary source of Cd exposure, with other sources including contaminated drinking water, paints, welding by-products, and certain types of shellfish. In this study, serum Cd levels were notably higher in the threatened abortion group (4.59 μg/dL ± 0.44 μg/dL) when contrasted with the control group (0.20 μg/dL ± 0.03 μg/dL). These findings align with previous research, such as that by Ajayi et al., who reported elevated serum Cd levels in recurrent spontaneous abortion cases (4.58 ± 0.77 μg/dL) compared to controls (2.49 ± 0.09 μg/dL). Furthermore, Otebhi and Osadolor observed a significant rise in blood Cd levels among pregnant women with a history of pregnancy complications compared to those without such a history.
Lead (Pb) is an environmental toxic metal. In women, Pb levels around 10 µg/dL have been associated with increased risks of pregnancy hypertension, spontaneous abortion, and impaired fetal growth. While Pb can be highly toxic even at low doses, its direct impact on the reproductive system at these levels is not definitively established. Pb levels may also rise with age due to the release of previously stored Pb from bones. Our study observed significantly higher serum Pb levels in the threatened abortion group (12.65 ± 0.51 μg/dL) compared to the control group (1.13 ± 0.02 μg/dL). This finding aligns with research by Ajayi et al., who reported elevated Pb concentrations in recurrent spontaneous abortion cases. Conversely, Vigeh et al. found no significant difference in blood Pb levels between spontaneous abortion cases and ongoing pregnancies. The elevated heavy metal levels observed in the Eastern Turkey region may be attributable to environmental exposures, such as contaminated water and air pollution, coupled with lower educational and socioeconomic statuses.
This study has limitations, notably its small sample size, which warrants larger investigations to confirm the link between threatened abortion and trace element levels. Additionally, a detailed analysis of diet and lifestyle habits was not conducted. Despite these constraints, the research offers a valuable contribution to the existing body of knowledge on threatened abortion.
In conclusion, altered levels of essential trace elements, particularly decreased iron and zinc, alongside elevated toxic heavy metals in the blood, may play a significant role in the etiology, treatment, or prevention of threatened abortion. Given the frequent occurrence of threatened abortions, especially in developing nations, and their associated economic and psychological burdens, measuring these element concentrations early in pregnancy could serve as valuable diagnostic and prognostic indicators.
