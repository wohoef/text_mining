Effectively managing materials presents a two-fold challenge: meeting substantial and growing demands for goods while simultaneously managing waste and emissions in disposal sites. Understanding material flows and stocks is therefore crucial for both enhancing resource efficiency and safeguarding the environment. This paper specifically examines the urban context, an area infrequently explored in previous metal flow research. Our study employs substance flow analysis to comprehensively and transparently determine the urban copper (Cu) balances of Vienna (Europe) and Taipei (Asia), two cities representing different economic conditions. A primary distinction observed between the cities' Cu profiles is their stock levels. Vienna exhibits a near-saturated state with 180 kilograms per capita (kg/cap) and a modest annual growth rate of 2%. Conversely, Taipei's Cu stock, at 30 kg/cap, is expanding rapidly at 26% per year. Despite high Cu recycling rates in both urban centers, municipal solid waste incineration bottom ash represents an untapped Cu resource, contributing 1% to 5% of their yearly Cu demand. Furthermore, nonpoint sources are the dominant form of emissions, accounting for up to 50% of the Cu entering the sewer systems. The findings of this research are vital for shaping each city's Cu management strategies. They provide a foundation for identifying and recovering recyclable materials, and for channeling non-recyclable substances to suitable disposal sites, thereby preventing harm to sensitive environmental systems. This methodology is also highly applicable for comparing cities, provided adequate data exists.

Throughout 10,000 years of urban history, a primary challenge in managing human settlements has been ensuring adequate material supply and waste disposal. Human interaction with natural resources and the environment has constantly evolved, influenced by technological advancements and socioeconomic forces (Agudelo-Vera et al. 2011). Significant historical shifts include the emergence of cities and empires post-3000 B.C. and the onset of the industrial revolution in the 18th century. In the contemporary era, global population growth has coincided with the widespread adoption of resource-intensive lifestyles. Patrick Geddes (Geddes 2010) was an early visionary who highlighted the implications of escalating urbanization. During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, he drew attention to the substantial resource flows within cities and, by using input-output balancing to identify material losses, foreshadowed modern material flow modeling and accounting practices.
A variety of research frameworks have emerged to study anthropogenic metabolism since early material budgeting efforts. These include material flow analysis (MFA), physical input-output (I/O) analysis, and environmental-extended I/O analysis. Among these, substance flow analysis (SFA) has demonstrated particular utility for examining metal pathways within urban environments. SFA's strength lies in its ability to trace specific substances through various systems, such as households, businesses, cities, or entire regions. However, a review of existing literature reveals two key limitations regarding urban metal flow studies. Firstly, such studies are uncommon. A comprehensive review of over 350 SFA articles identified only five cities where metal metabolism had been investigated. Notably, Stockholm was the sole city with a focus on copper (Cu), despite Cu's significant resource and environmental implications, leaving many other cities unexplored in this context. Secondly, comparisons between individual city studies are challenging due to inconsistencies. An expanded literature search, encompassing 15 exemplary urban Cu studies, highlighted a common issue: these studies are often selective in their scope and exhibit considerable methodological variations in aspects like system boundaries, modeling techniques, and data collection and assignment.
This study addresses the scarcity of urban copper (Cu) studies and the lack of comparative city assessments by offering exploratory insights into urban Cu balances across different cities. Its objectives include developing a method for analyzing and evaluating urban Cu flows and stocks, presenting and comparing the findings of such an analysis for two specific cities, and discussing the observed differences using selected indicators. Ultimately, the research aims to test the hypothesis that comparing metabolic variations between cities can significantly enhance decision-making for resource management and environmental protection.
A case-study methodology was employed to achieve the research goals, focusing on Vienna and Taipei. These two cities, located in Europe and Asia respectively, were selected due to their significant variations in population density, economic status, cultural practices, and environmental conditions. The study utilized explorative data analysis and Material Flow Analysis (MFA) to present urban copper balances clearly and concisely. This approach simplifies the intricate details of copper flows and stocks, thereby enabling easier comparison between the cities.
This research advances industrial ecology by offering city planners and researchers a substance-specific perspective on urban resource dynamics. By analyzing comprehensive substance balances, including total flows from import to in-use stocks and exports, the study enables a holistic understanding of dynamic material turnover. While individual process details and indicators might seem limited, the overall approach supports enhanced resource efficiency and environmental performance within urban systems.

This is followed by the application of an accounting scheme to establish city-specific model equations, incorporating Monte Carlo simulation to account for input parameter uncertainty. Static modeling is then employed to balance flows and stocks, producing reconciled data presented as stock and flow charts. Subsequently, substance flow indicators are chosen and computed from these balanced charts. Finally, discrepancies in indicator scores serve as the foundation for interpreting and discussing unique urban patterns observed in each city.
Vienna and Taipei were selected as case studies due to their distinct characteristics across several domains. Geographically and environmentally, the cities present contrasting settings: Vienna is landlocked, draining into the distant Black Sea via the Danube, implying limited capacity for effluent disposal. In contrast, Taipei, an island city near the South China Sea, benefits from a substantial dilution potential for liquid emissions. Demographically, Taipei is significantly denser, with 96 inhabitants per hectare compared to Vienna's 40. Despite its larger area (41,487 ha vs. 27,180 ha), Vienna has a smaller population (1.7 million vs. 2.6 million). Future projections anticipate a 27% population increase for Vienna over the next five decades, while Taipei is expected to see a 19% decrease. Culturally, Vienna represents a traditional European urban environment, differing from Taipei's Asian context with minimal Western influence. Economically, both cities exhibit similar gross regional products per capita (Vienna: €43,900; Taipei: €34,800), influencing their consumption of goods and infrastructure needs for waste and wastewater management. Despite these differences, both cities share a modern, service-oriented economy, devoid of heavy industry, which shapes their anthropogenic activities.
Taipei and Vienna exemplify global urban patterns. Roughly 65% of people in coastal ecosystems reside in cities similar to Taipei, while about 45% of those in cultivated ecosystems live in urban areas akin to Vienna (Marcotullio et al. 2008).
Copper was chosen for its significance to both resource management and environmental concerns. As a vital technological metal, it underpins modern lifestyles in both Vienna and Taipei, being extensively utilized in consumer goods like electric appliances, transportation systems, and critical infrastructure for water, energy, and information. Its high production cost from raw ores makes copper recovery and recycling a widespread and appealing practice.
Copper serves as an environmental indicator, particularly for urban emissions. Studies show higher copper concentrations in Vienna's sewage sludge and soils compared to rural counterparts (Kroiss et al. 2008; Pfleiderer 2010). Modern cities increasingly contend with nonpoint metal emissions, which are challenging to regulate due to their numerous sources, unlike more controllable point sources (Sörme 2010).
Our methodology employs a static mass-balance approach, primarily due to two factors. Firstly, material accounting necessitates reported or measured data, which is generally more accessible and reliable for historical periods. This led us to choose 2008 for Vienna and 2009 for Taipei for our retrospective material flow assessment. Secondly, a descriptive framework is best suited given the limited understanding of precise connections between material inflows, stocks, and outflows. Methodologically, this combines substance flow analysis (SFA) with exploratory data analysis.
To facilitate comparison between Vienna and Taipei, the study aimed to develop a standardized stock and flow chart. This necessitated creating a model that, while accommodating the unique urban features of each city, would ultimately allow for data comparison. The model's scope was defined by the administrative city boundaries, with data collected annually. Urban systems, encompassing processes and flows, were established using insights from prior research, literature reviews, municipal reports, and expert consultations. To determine the copper (Cu) flux rate (mass per unit time) and the total Cu mass in stock, specific model equations are employed. The Cu flux rate, denoted as $\dot{m}_{flow}$, is a function of various input parameters, $p_1, p_2, p_3, \dots, p_i$. Similarly, the Cu stock, $m_{stock}$, is calculated as a function of its own set of input parameters, $q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots, q_i$. These input parameters ($p_i$ and $q_i$) are presumed to follow a normal distribution, characterized by a mean value ($m$) and a standard deviation ($s$). The determination of these statistical measures is a multi-step process. The mean values ($m$) for these parameters are established through a data mining approach, as outlined by Månsson (2010). This process prioritizes a bottom-up data acquisition strategy for both Vienna and Taipei. The availability, quantity, and quality of data exhibit variability not only between the two cities but also within each city, depending on the specific type of flow or stock being analyzed. Commonly, input parameters reflecting city characteristics are sourced from official statistics. Import data, for instance, are derived by downscaling national import and export statistics to the urban level. The allocation of these imports to internal city processes is based on the global sectoral share of Cu products, supplemented by estimations drawn from local waste statistics. Information on the Cu content within products is compiled from existing literature, local consumption data, and waste statistics. Waste flows are meticulously documented in statistics provided by both public and private waste management companies. Furthermore, Cu fluxes entering and exiting waste management facilities, such as incinerators and wastewater treatment plants, are recorded in scientific reports commissioned by city authorities. Emission flows are estimated by synthesizing data from literature and inventory databases, such as Ecoinvent. Estimates for Cu stocks within technical infrastructures are made by correlating network lengths with corresponding specific masses. For Vienna, building stock data are based on Swiss per capita figures, while for Taipei, proxy data from other Taiwanese cities are utilized. The standard deviation ($s$) for each parameter is derived using an uncertainty factor ($uf$), which is based on the data vagueness concept developed by Hedbrant and Sörme (2001). The formula for calculating $s_p$ (for flow parameters) and $s_q$ (for stock parameters) is given by $s_p = m_p \times \frac{uf-1}{2}$ and $s_q = m_q \times \frac{uf-1}{2}$, respectively. The uncertainty factor itself is calculated as $uf = 1 + 0.0036 \times e^{1.105 \times l}$. The uncertainty level ($l$) is an integer ranging from 1 to 5, and its value is assigned based on the classification of the data source. For example, official statistics collected at the local level are considered to have low uncertainty, corresponding to an $l$ value of 1. Conversely, official national statistics that are subsequently downscaled to the local level are assigned a higher uncertainty level, such as $l=3$.
Monte Carlo simulation is employed to determine the mean value and standard deviation for each flow and stock. This calculation incorporates the established model equations and the distribution functions of the input parameters.

Vienna-specific data, such as model equations and input parameters, are found in sections 2.2.2.2 and 2.2.2.3, while Taipei's background data is in section 2.2.2.4.
Our methodology employs a static model architecture, where the mass balance principle is applied to each process. This principle dictates that the sum of annual input flow, minus annual output flow, plus the alteration of stock, must equal zero. However, the diverse origins of our data lead to inconsistencies in both quality and quantity, often resulting in discrepancies when attempting to satisfy the mass balance criteria. To address these inconsistencies, we utilized the STAN freeware. This software effectively reconciles data through an algorithm that incorporates the law of error propagation, thereby resolving the challenges posed by heterogeneous data.

To facilitate a comparative assessment of copper (Cu) flow data, this study employs exploratory data analysis, a method designed to distill the essential features of datasets into an accessible format. This approach is integrated with a set of indicators, which are essentially observable variables used to represent unobservable realities, as defined by Loiseau et al. (2012). These indicators collectively illustrate how substances interact within and between human-made systems (anthroposphere) and natural environments. They also provide a foundation for informing policy and decision-making regarding substance management, recycling, and waste management practices. The study utilizes 13 indicators, organized into eight groups, with seven focusing on resource efficiency and six on environmental protection. These indicators are calculated using the final Cu balances for each city, which consist of 42 flows and four stocks.

Copper imports into Vienna and Taipei, both consumption-driven cities with high GDPs, are substantial, reflecting their demand and consumption patterns and economic status. Both cities heavily depend on external sources for copper, importing comparable amounts: Vienna at 12 ± 1 kg/cap/yr and Taipei at 11 ± 1 kg/cap/yr. This imported copper primarily serves infrastructure and consumer goods. Notably, these net import rates are six times the global average per capita consumption.
Urban copper stocks are assessed using three indicators: current stock, absolute change, and relative change.
A significant portion of imported copper (Cu) is integrated into urban infrastructure, forming substantial stocks. Vienna, for instance, possesses a Cu stock of 178 ± 23 kg per capita, while Taipei's stock is considerably lower at 28 ± 6 kg per capita. These figures align with observations in other European and Asian cities. However, the distribution of Cu within these stocks varies significantly between the two cities. In Vienna, a mature city, 80% of its Cu is held in long-term assets, primarily within heating systems and extensive electricity and telecommunication networks. This makes infrastructure and building components key areas for potential "urban mining" in Vienna. Conversely, Taipei, with its more recent urban development and absence of heating systems in buildings, stores only 48% of its Cu in long-term goods like infrastructure and buildings. A larger proportion, 52%, is tied to consumer goods such as air conditioners, cars, and scooters. This distinction highlights that in Taipei, consumer goods represent a more important source for recovering secondary Cu. Overall, the two cities exhibit clear differences in both the total quantity of Cu stocked and the relative importance of various sectors in holding these reserves.
The annual change in copper (Cu) stock, whether accumulation or depletion, reflects a city's economic and technological characteristics. A growing city typically sees more Cu input than output, leading to stock increases across categories like buildings, infrastructure, vehicles, and consumer goods. However, if a material is replaced or phased out, like cadmium, its input might fall below its output, causing stock depletion.
Taipei's copper stock grows annually by 7 ± 2 kg/cap, significantly more than Vienna's 3 ± 1 kg/cap increase. Both cities see rising consumer stocks, though Taipei's accumulation is primarily driven by household electronics and vehicle appliances, exceeding infrastructure and vehicle contributions.
Comparing the absolute change in copper stock to the existing stock reveals significant differences between the two cities. Taipei exhibits a much higher relative accumulation rate of copper at 26%, exceeding Vienna's 2% by over tenfold. This indicates that Vienna's copper stock is substantial and experiencing only modest growth. Conversely, Taipei's rapid stock increase is largely driven by unmet demand, particularly from private households, where copper consumption is five times greater than in Vienna. In Vienna, the copper turnover primarily involves the upkeep of its already extensive stocks.
Three indicators define this category: the quantity of copper in solid waste, encompassing scrap for recycling, and both intentional and unintentional emissions. Intentional emissions refer to copper deliberately introduced into the environment, for example, as an agricultural fungicide. In contrast, unintentional emissions are incidental byproducts from processes like industrial and household wastewater, copper released from vehicle brake pads, or catenary wires, all of which present potential environmental hazards.
Copper from wastes and emissions in Vienna is significantly higher than in Taipei, at 7.4 ± 0.4 kg/cap/yr compared to 0.9 ± 0.1 kg/cap/yr. In both urban centers, over 99% of these emissions are attributed to solid waste. Unintentional emissions account for less than 1% of the total. This aligns with a U.S. study by Lifset et al. (2012), which noted that recovering dissipative losses would only marginally boost recycling rates by 0.5%.
While household waste copper levels are comparable in Vienna (1.0 ± 0.06 kg/cap/yr) and Taipei (0.7 ± 0.02 kg/cap/yr), significant disparities emerge in copper waste from industrial, construction, and vehicle sources. Vienna's contribution from these sectors is substantially higher at 6.4 ± 0.4 kg/cap/yr, compared to Taipei's 0.2 ± 0.1 kg/cap/yr. This difference can be attributed to three factors: Vienna's extensive and aging copper infrastructure and buildings require ongoing replacement, generating considerable waste. Conversely, Taipei's "zero waste" policy aims to minimize copper waste from manufacturing. Additionally, variations in data collection methods for industrial waste statistics between the two cities introduce uncertainty.
Vienna's unintentional copper emissions, at 16 ± 2 g/cap/yr, significantly exceed Taipei's 5 ± 0.3 g/cap/yr. This disparity is largely due to Vienna's airborne emissions and surface runoff being roughly 12 times higher. Factors contributing to Vienna's elevated levels include greater car mileage and associated brake wear, the presence of catenary wear (absent in Taipei), and the use of copper in roofing and gutters.
Nonpoint emissions, originating from the operational use of products rather than industrial sources, are becoming increasingly significant, as highlighted by Bergbäck (2010) and Sörme et al. (2001b). Their research on Stockholm and Sweden revealed substantial nonpoint emissions. These diffuse releases, such as wear from brake pads or corrosion of roofs and gutters, are particularly challenging to regulate. Consequently, this study uses the proportion of nonpoint emissions to total emissions as a key indicator. The findings indicate a notable difference between cities: nonpoint emissions constitute 51% of total emissions in Vienna, compared to only 12% in Taipei (Figure 5). Nonpoint sources encompass waterborne copper in surface runoff, including contributions from roof runoff, vehicle brake and tire wear, catenary wear, and atmospheric deposition. In contrast, point sources involve waterborne emissions from industrial and household activities, such as urine, feces, consumer products, and pipe corrosion.
The majority of copper emissions in both Vienna (87%) and Taipei (97%) are captured by their respective wastewater systems. Within these systems, point sources contribute more copper than nonpoint sources, accounting for 57% in Vienna and 91% in Taipei. Vienna's point source emissions are primarily industrial (60%), with corroding water pipes, and household waste (feces, urine, consumer products, kitchen waste) each contributing about 20%. Specific point source contributions in Taipei remain unquantified due to data limitations.
Vienna's nonpoint copper emissions, originating from sources like brake and tire wear, catenary wear, and roof runoff, are significantly higher than Taipei's. Annually, Vienna's nonpoint Cu flow is 13 times greater (7.3 g/cap/yr) compared to Taipei's (0.6 g/cap/yr). This disparity is attributed to Vienna's extensive tram network, prevalent copper roofs and gutters, and increased emissions from low-duty vehicles. Consequently, Vienna's per capita copper flow into the soil is approximately 15 times larger than Taipei's.
For Vienna, nonpoint emission patterns are significant, aligning with findings from Swedish research (Sörme et al. 2001b; Hjortenkrans et al. 2007). This necessitates that urban authorities devise long-term strategies to mitigate these diffuse emissions. Furthermore, due to extensive sealed and drained surfaces, most copper flows are waterborne and enter the sewer system. Consequently, effective end-of-pipe technologies, like advanced sewage treatment plants, are essential for removing heavy metals and managing their impact on receiving waters.
Indicators for this study included both human-made and natural sinks. The vast majority of copper (over 99.9%) in both cities ended up in anthropogenic sinks. A negligible fraction, specifically less than 0.1% of the total processed output, accumulated in the environment (0.08% in Vienna and 0.03% in Taipei). Figure 6 visually represents these primary sink flows, distinguishing between anthropogenic sinks (measured in kilograms per capita per year) and environmental sinks (measured in grams per capita per year). The "External anthroposphere" encompasses the surrounding regions, including consumption, recycling, and underground storage outside Vienna and Taipei. "Receiving water" refers to the Danube River for Vienna and the Danshui River for Taipei.
Within the urban context, anthropogenic sinks encompass materials either contained in city landfills or processed externally. The internal anthroposphere includes landfills holding copper from municipal solid waste (MSW) and bottom ash generated by MSW incineration. Conversely, the external anthroposphere handles copper recovery from recyclables, as neither Vienna nor Taipei possesses internal copper recycling facilities. Most copper exits these cities as either products or solid waste. Notably, Taipei exports 78% of its copper as products, while Vienna exports 81% as waste, which incorporates recyclables.
The study considers receiving waters and urban soil within city boundaries as environmental sinks. The atmosphere is excluded due to copper's brief atmospheric residence time. The subsequent discussion on "Technical efficiency of the wastewater system" elaborates on how effectively the wastewater system directs copper to suitable sinks, a critical aspect for urban planning. Additionally, the "Accumulation in urban soil" indicator addresses copper buildup in soil from both intentional and unintentional emissions.
The accumulation of copper in urban soil, a phenomenon recognized for decades (Purves, 1966), highlights the importance of monitoring these environments for sustainable land management (Wong et al. 2006; Johnson et al. 2011). Studies have frequently observed higher copper concentrations in urban soil samples compared to rural counterparts (Pfleiderer 2010; Jien et al. 2011). Lacking systematic monitoring in Vienna and Taipei, this research employed a simplified indicator: the annual copper load relative to the existing copper stock in the top 30 cm of greenspace soil. This revealed a slow copper accumulation in both cities' soils, with Vienna exhibiting a slightly higher rate (0.07% annually) than Taipei (0.03% annually), possibly due to Vienna's greater atmospheric emissions. Despite these accumulations, neither city exceeds current legal thresholds for urban soil substance concentrations.
Given that both Vienna and Taipei are service-oriented, rather than industrial, cities, the Substance Flow Analysis (SFA) data suggests minimal copper accumulation in their soils, implying negligible long-term environmental impact. However, the absence of comprehensive monitoring programs raises concerns about potential localized copper hotspots, which could stem from specific emissions, natural geological variations, or historical human activities. Vienna's copper metabolism, characterized by greater copper presence on building surfaces and within transportation infrastructure, necessitates the immediate implementation of a robust monitoring program. This would help ensure that copper levels do not exceed established reference values, especially considering the ongoing development of site-specific geochemical background concentrations for the city.
The discharge of heavy metals into aquatic environments is significantly influenced by the design of sewer systems, their leakage rates, and wastewater treatment technologies. For instance, urban areas in Germany contribute approximately 35% of the copper (Cu) entering rivers (Blondzik et al. 2004). To assess how effectively wastewater systems remove Cu, this study calculated removal efficiencies by comparing the amount of Cu extracted by wastewater treatment (WWT) and contained in sewage sludge against the total Cu introduced into the sewer system. The analysis revealed that Taipei's WWT system achieved a Cu removal efficiency of 74%, while Vienna's was lower at 65%. This means that 26% of the Cu in Taipei and 35% in Vienna ultimately entered receiving waters. This discharge occurs through various pathways, including WWT effluent, surface runoff, and combined sewer overflow. Notably, both surface runoff collected by separate sewer systems and combined sewer overflow bypass treatment entirely before reaching receiving waters. Vienna's lower removal efficiency is likely attributable to higher levels of nonpoint emissions and additional Cu loads from its combined sewer overflow. These findings lead to several key conclusions regarding wastewater management. Firstly, in terms of WWT system design, reducing and collecting nonpoint Cu emissions, alongside achieving high removal rates at WWT plants, is critical for mitigating Cu loads into the hydrosphere. For service-oriented cities, where nonpoint sources are the primary contributors to emissions, a comprehensive approach to designing the entire wastewater collection and treatment system is paramount. Secondly, monitoring efforts are hampered by a scarcity of precise data, particularly concerning surface runoff, combined sewer overflow, and stormwater overflow. Few cities maintain comprehensive water balances that account for all collected, uncollected, treated, and untreated waters and wastewaters. Therefore, establishing such water balances is recommended as a foundational step for informed environmental management decisions. Berlin, for example, offers a sophisticated model for precipitation runoff that uses land use data to estimate location-specific surface runoff (City of Berlin 2010). Finally, environmental risk assessment highlights that cities rely on the dilution capacity of their surrounding natural environments. Vienna utilizes the Danube River, and Taipei the Danshui River, to dissipate Cu from purified sewage. Despite a significant portion (one-fourth to one-third) of sewage-borne Cu being released into these rivers, current environmental quality standards are generally met. However, there is a notable absence of both legal standards and consistent monitoring data for Cu concentrations in river and sea sediments (e.g., Kavka et al. 2000 for Vienna). To prevent future overloading, the development and application of environmental fate and impact models are crucial for predicting how heavy metal concentrations will evolve over time.
Waste-to-energy plants in both cities incinerate municipal solid waste, converting most of its copper content into bottom ash. Recovering this copper offers dual benefits: it conserves resources and reduces environmental contamination if the ash is landfilled or repurposed in construction.
Taipei utilizes bottom ash as a fine aggregate in asphalt and for road construction, while Vienna stabilizes it with cement before landfill disposal. The annual per capita copper flux from bottom ash in Vienna is significantly higher, at approximately 655 ± 43 g, compared to Taipei's 63 ± 21 g. This represents about 5% of Vienna's and 1% of Taipei's annual copper consumption. The market value of this copper is roughly US$8.8 million annually in Vienna and US$1.4 million in Taipei.
The high value of copper and other metals creates economic motivation for their recovery from bottom ash. Concentrations of these substances in bottom ash are significantly greater than in natural ores, and further enhancement is possible through treatment processes. Current research indicates that dry discharge of bottom ash is the preferred method for effectively recovering nonferrous metals. However, this advanced recovery technique has not yet been adopted in Vienna or Taipei.
Landfill mining, which involves excavating materials from older municipal solid waste (MSW) or bottom ash landfills, offers an alternative for recovering valuable metals. However, such recovery projects are seldom undertaken solely for resource extraction. Economic viability typically requires the creation of additional value, such as acquiring new land for construction or reducing long-term landfill maintenance costs. For instance, over a 20-year period, Viennese landfills have accumulated an estimated 24,000 tonnes of copper, valued at US$200 million, based on a daily MSW generation rate of one kilogram per person.
Beyond recovering copper from bottom ash and landfills, another method involves the separate collection of waste streams containing copper, like electronic and electrical equipment. This approach is prevalent and aligns with European waste policy. To optimize a comprehensive copper recovery system, it's essential to understand the primary sources of copper, alongside the effectiveness and economic implications of various recovery technologies. The challenge lies in identifying which recovery pathway best achieves both resource recovery and environmental protection goals while minimizing costs.
This research offers a foundational comparison of urban copper (Cu) metabolism, analyzing its flows and stocks in two distinct cities. It represents the first such comparative study and, to our knowledge, the most thorough urban-level Cu balance, tracing the substance from import through in-use stocks to export. The mass balance methodology employed here moves beyond a simplified "black-box" view of cities by detailing key processes, flows, and stocks. The findings enable a discussion of the variations in Cu balances between Vienna and Taipei, leading to recommendations for managing Cu as both a valuable resource and a potential environmental contaminant. This framework can also serve as a basis for future comparisons involving other cities and metals.
The copper (Cu) stock dynamics reveal distinct urban patterns. Younger cities like Taipei exhibit lower Cu stocks but experience significant annual increases due to rapid growth. Conversely, older cities such as Vienna possess substantial Cu stocks, leading to a comparatively smaller relative change. In Vienna, Cu demand and disposal primarily stem from maintenance and replacement needs, with consumer goods holding less relative importance for Cu.
Despite significant copper recycling efforts, untapped recovery potential remains. For instance, both Vienna and Taipei could extract more copper from incinerated municipal solid waste's bottom ash. This increased recovery is crucial for achieving sustainability objectives like resource conservation and environmental protection. Beyond copper, separating other metals like aluminum, gold, and silver from bottom ash can offer economic advantages. Furthermore, removing copper, chromium, lead, and cadmium from bottom ash lowers heavy metal concentrations, making the material safer and more suitable for use in construction.
Copper is released into the environment through the wear, corrosion, and weathering of infrastructure and transport systems. These diffuse emissions, often overlooked by urban authorities, partially enter wastewater systems and accumulate in urban soil. Despite the European Union's call for attention to nonpoint emissions, they remain outside the primary focus of urban governance. Drawing from studies in Stockholm, Vienna, and Taipei, it is advisable to monitor copper and other heavy metal concentrations in urban soils and sediments using a combination of Substance Flow Analysis (SFA) and direct measurements.
Minimizing heavy metal pollution, including copper, in receiving waters necessitates a comprehensive approach to urban wastewater systems. The overall efficacy in pollutant removal hinges on the combined performance of wastewater collection and treatment technologies. Therefore, controlling heavy metal flows into waterways depends more on the entire system's performance than on the wastewater treatment plant's efficiency in isolation.
Transnational collaboration, as demonstrated by this study, offers valuable insights into urban substance balances. Adopting a shared research framework for urban metabolism data allows authorities and researchers to mutually benefit. Therefore, we advocate for exchanging experiences, discussing methodologies, and developing common models and data acquisition protocols for urban metabolism studies.
