The increasing demand for reliable consumption-based material flow indicators, or material footprints (MFs), in international policy frameworks like the UN Sustainable Development Goals, has become prominent. However, a significant obstacle to their widespread policy adoption is the inconsistency in MFs for national economies when derived from different Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) databases. This paper aims to quantify how data discrepancies among GMRIO databases influence MF results. To achieve this, we employed structural decomposition analysis and structural production layer decomposition. These methods were applied to a pairwise comparison of three GMRIO databases—EXIOBASE, Eora, and the OECD Inter‐Country Input–Output (ICIO) database—all utilizing the same material extensions. While all three databases consistently indicate whether a country's final demand relies on net raw material imports or if it's a net exporter, they often exhibit substantial variations in the magnitude and makeup of material flows. Our analysis, which involved decomposing the effects from the Leontief matrices (representing economic structures), revealed that a mere handful of sectors at the initial stages of the supply chain—specifically, raw material extraction and basic processing—account for 60% of the total deviations originating from the technology matrices. Therefore, a crucial research priority should be the development of improved methods to harmonize GMRIO results, especially for material-intensive sectors and supply chains. This harmonization is essential for enhancing the integration of demand-based material flow indicators into resource policy.

Globalized supply chains for materials, goods, and services now extend across nations, separating where production occurs from where demand originates. This global interconnectedness means that consumption in one country increasingly fuels environmental and social issues in others, particularly in regions extracting raw materials or manufacturing products. Consequently, understanding these global "teleconnections" has become a key area of study in industrial ecology, as evidenced by recent dedicated journal issues.
To understand the global reach of supply chains, comprehensive indicators are essential. This paper adopts the "material footprint" (MF) to represent "raw material consumption" (RMC), aligning with established research. RMC quantifies the material extractions, both direct and indirect, necessary to fulfill a nation's domestic final demand, irrespective of where those materials were extracted. Over recent years, the MF indicator has been widely applied. It has been used to evaluate the raw materials embedded in international trade and the environmental impact of consumption throughout the life cycle for individual countries, including those in the EU. Furthermore, the MF has facilitated comparative analyses across different nations.
The demand for reliable resource efficiency metrics, particularly consumption-based material flow indicators (MFs), has grown significantly within international policy circles. These indicators are crucial for evaluating the effectiveness of policies aimed at enhancing national economic resource efficiency and mitigating environmental impacts. For instance, the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target 8.4 specifically calls for such robust indicators. The Inter-Agency Expert Group (IAEG) has chosen MF indicators to track progress for SDG targets 8.4 and 12.2. Furthermore, MFs play a role in the G7's resource efficiency initiative and the OECD's "Green Growth" framework for reporting demand-based material flow data. However, widespread adoption of MF indicators by policymakers is hindered by uncertainties surrounding their methodological development and data accuracy. Consequently, the IAEG has designated the footprint indicator as "tier 3" in the SDG classification, indicating that a global methodological standard is still under development, which impedes consistent national reporting.
The Material Footprint (MF) indicator can be determined using three distinct methodological approaches. These include environmentally extended input-output analysis (IOA), calculations based on material intensity coefficients from process analyses and life cycle assessment (LCA), and hybrid methods that integrate aspects of both IOA and LCA. The recent emergence of highly detailed Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) databases, such as Eora and EXIOBASE, has significantly advanced global footprint assessments.
When using demand-based indicators to inform policies on consumption's environmental impact across supply chains, it's crucial to understand their reliability and inherent uncertainties. While numerous studies have already compared Global Multiregional Input-Output (GMRIO) databases for carbon footprint indicators, pinpointing the sources of discrepancies, a similar comprehensive assessment for material footprints (MFs) has been lacking. Research on national MFs has shown that results vary significantly depending on the GMRIO database used. Furthermore, some studies have highlighted the critical role of initial processing stages in achieving accurate overall MF results.
This paper addresses a significant gap in research: the impact of variations within Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) databases on Material Footprint (MF) calculations. While carbon footprint discrepancies have been thoroughly examined, the effect on MFs remains largely unexplored. Our analysis aims to pinpoint the specific sectors within GMRIO systems responsible for disproportionate differences in MF outcomes. We achieve this by employing two methodologies: Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD). SDA helps quantify footprint variations attributable to major GMRIO data components, such as the Leontief inverse matrix or final demand. Subsequently, SPLD allows for a detailed examination of the Leontief inverse matrix, focusing on the technology matrix to identify interindustry inputs that most significantly influence MFs. This approach enables us to highlight areas within GMRIO databases where data refinement is crucial for improving the consistency of MF results.
This paper is organized into several sections. Section 2 details the data sources and methodologies employed. Section 3 presents the findings, beginning with an examination of Material Footprint (MF) discrepancies among Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) databases, followed by specific country and raw material group analyses using Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD). Section 4 explores strategies for enhancing the reliability of GMRIO-based MF calculations, and Section 5 offers concluding remarks.
This paper compares Material Footprint (MF) indicators using three Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) databases: EXIOBASE, Eora, and the OECD Inter-Country Input–Output (ICIO) database. Notably, this marks the ICIO database's debut in MF publications. Detailed information on the data sources and construction methods for these GMRIO databases is provided in Supporting Information S1, chapter 1, with a summary of their key characteristics in Table 1.
Among the GMRIO databases, Eora stands out as the most comprehensive, encompassing data for 189 countries. Its sector detail varies significantly by country, from 25 sectors in many developing nations to over 500 in some industrialized ones, with material extraction sectors ranging from 2 to 63. Eora's final GMRIO table exhibits a mixed structure, utilizing both supply-use and input-output tables depending on the region. In contrast, EXIOBASE, developed through European research projects, was specifically tailored for environmental applications. Version 3 of EXIOBASE features 200 products (and 163 industries) across its covered countries and regions, with 33 products (and 25 industries) dedicated to biotic and abiotic raw material extraction. It presents data as a multiregional input-output table, modeling all EU-28 countries, 16 key trading partners, and five aggregated "Rest-of-the-World" regions. The OECD's ICIO database, considered highly authoritative by national institutions, covers 61 countries. It models intersectoral trade flows for 34 industries, with only two specifically addressing extractive industries, and publishes its final GMRIO database as symmetric input-output tables.
All three GMRIO models utilize the industry-technology assumption to generate input-output tables from supply-use tables. However, developers prioritize different data components, such as national accounts or trade statistics, when balancing the GMRIO system. These varying balancing strategies can substantially modify the initial data to ensure global consistency.
This study utilizes two distinct aggregation levels. While absolute Material Footprints (MFs) are analyzed using the fully detailed models, the Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD) methods necessitate a standardized format. Consequently, the three multiregional input-output tables were consolidated into a "common classification (CC)." This CC includes 40 countries, a "Rest-of-the-World (RoW)" region, and 17 industries, a methodology consistent with previous research (Owen et al., 2014; Steen‐Olsen et al. 2014). The complete breakdown of countries/regions and industries within the CC is provided in Supporting Information S1, chapter 2, and the concordance matrices are available as an Excel file in Supporting Information S2. Models employing this common classification are denoted with a "cc" suffix.
When using the full GMRIO models, material extraction data are assigned to numerous primary sectors. However, for the Common Classification (CC), material extraction is limited to just two sectors: agriculture and mining, due to the constraints of the ICIO database.
To isolate the impact of monetary data discrepancies within GMRIO systems on material footprint indicators, this study utilized a single, harmonized dataset for global domestic material extraction. This approach addresses concerns raised by previous research (e.g., Owen et al., 2014) regarding the significant influence of environmental satellite data variations across different GMRIO databases. The chosen dataset, developed for UN Environment’s International Resource Panel (Schandl et al., 2017; UNEP, 2016), encompasses 44 material extraction categories globally. All analyses in this paper are based on data from 2010.
To quantify how variations in GMRIO model data influence Material Footprint (MF) indicators, we employ two complementary methods: Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD). Further details on these methods are provided in Supporting Information S1, Chapter 3.
Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) is an Input-Output (IO) model-based technique designed to dissect changes in a variable by identifying its underlying determinants (Dietzenbacher & Los, 1998). While commonly employed to trace changes in environmental indicators over time, such as energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions (Lenzen, 2016), this study utilizes SDA differently. Instead of analyzing temporal shifts, we apply SDA to pinpoint the factors contributing to variations in Material Footprint (MF) results when calculated using different Global Multiregional Input-Output (GMRIO) databases for the same year. This approach, similar to a carbon footprint study by Owen et al. (2014), helps reveal how data discrepancies within the Leontief matrix (L), aggregated final demand (Y), and total output (x) influence MF outcomes. Previous research has also leveraged SDA to explore MF drivers (Munoz & Hubacek, 2008; Plank et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2014; Weinzettel & Kovanda, 2011; Wenzlik et al., 2015).
The SPLD (structural production layer decomposition) method, a recent development by Wieland et al. (2018), is also employed in this study. Unlike SDA, which typically uses the Leontief inverse matrix, SPLD performs a structural decomposition of PLD (production layer decomposition) results by utilizing the transaction matrix. Its calculations involve the interindustry transaction matrix and various orders of technology matrices (A-matrices), representing different production layers. This allows SPLD to pinpoint how data discrepancies within specific components of the A-matrix, such as trade versus domestic blocks or inter-industry flows, contribute to variations in the material footprint (MF) indicator across different GMRIO models. Ultimately, SPLD helps identify the most influential elements within the technology matrix responsible for these MF differences.
This section begins by examining discrepancies in aggregated Material Footprint (MF) indicators derived from the three fully detailed Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) databases. Subsequently, two countries exhibiting significant MF deviations are chosen for an in-depth analysis using Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD) with the Common Classification (CC) versions of the GMRIO models. The analysis concludes by extending the SPLD findings to all studied countries, identifying key sectors and regions within the technology matrix that contribute to MF result variations.
A pairwise analysis of per capita Material Footprints (MFs) from EXIOBASE, Eora, and ICIO models for 2010 is presented in Figure 1. To ensure consistency, identical World Bank population data was used across all three models. A 15% discrepancy range was established for visual clarity, with a thick gray line (45°) signifying identical results. Countries falling between two thinner gray lines, but off the main diagonal, show MF deviations of no more than 15% between the compared models. Country codes are displayed for nations outside this 15% range and for key countries within it. Complete results for all countries are detailed in Chapter 4 of Supporting Information S1. Luxembourg is omitted from the figure due to its exceptionally high MF (exceeding 70 tons per capita in some models), largely attributed to its high GDP per capita. Similarly, the "Rest-of-the-World" group's value is not shown due to the diverse material use profiles of its constituent countries.
Across the three model comparisons, 46% of the countries examined (18 in total) fell within the 15% discrepancy range. This group included diverse European nations like Germany and France, OECD members such as the United States and Canada, and significant emerging economies, including China, India, and Brazil.
Significant variations in Material Footprint (MF) calculations across different models are evident for several nations. For instance, Taiwan's 2010 MF, as per EXIOBASE, was 21.9 tons per capita, considerably higher than Eora's 9.1 tons. Conversely, Eora's figure for Slovakia (37.5 tons) greatly exceeded EXIOBASE's (21.3 tons). The Netherlands also showed disparities, with ICIO reporting 13.9 tons per capita compared to Eora's 23.5 tons. For Russia, while Eora and ICIO results were similar (8.0 and 8.4 tons), EXIOBASE presented a roughly 50% larger MF at 12 tons per capita.
The study found that, regardless of the specific GMRIO database used, most countries consistently showed similar trends in their raw material trade balance (RTB). This means the databases generally agree on whether a nation's domestic consumption relies on more imported raw materials than it exports, or vice versa. Specifically, the United States, Japan, South Korea, and various European nations were consistently identified as major net importers. Conversely, Australia and the BRIICS economies (Brazil, Russia, India, Indonesia, China, and South Africa) were consistently categorized as significant net exporters of embodied raw materials. (Further details on RTB figures are available in Supporting Information S1, chapter 5).
Using the Common Classification (CC) models alters the Material Footprint (MF) indicator at the country level compared to analyses employing the full-detail Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) models. This aggregation leads to fewer countries falling within the 15% deviation corridor, with only 15 countries exhibiting such low discrepancies across all three model pairs (further details in Supporting Information S1, chapter 6). The effect of this aggregation from full detail to CC on a country's MF is elaborated in Supporting Information S1, chapter 4. ICIO shows the smallest average percentage deviation across countries at 1.5%, which is anticipated given its sector classification (34 industries) is most similar to the CC's 17 sectors. Eora and EXIOBASE exhibit higher average deviations of 7.7% and 8.3%, respectively. While some countries experience minimal changes due to aggregation, such as Korea (max 3%), Spain (4.5%), and China (5%), others see substantial shifts in their MF results. Notable examples include the United States (max 20%), Russia (34%), the Netherlands (66%), and Belgium (71%).
This paper prioritizes analyzing how variations in interindustry matrices affect Material Footprint (MF) indicators, thus not delving deeply into discrepancies caused by different aggregation levels. While the aggregation effect on MF indicators has been explored elsewhere (de Koning et al., 2015; Piñero et al., 2015), this study will address it briefly in Section 4, following the presentation of Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD) results.
To demonstrate the SDA and SPLD assessment outcomes, we chose the Netherlands and Russia for in-depth analysis. These nations exhibit substantial variations in their Material Footprint (MF) results across both the complete and common classification (CC) models. Both countries possess considerable economic output and are presumed to have reliable data. Furthermore, they present differing contributions of domestic versus foreign material extractions to their respective MFs. This paper details the findings for the Netherlands, while the comprehensive analysis for Russia is available in Supporting Information S1, chapter 7. Beyond overall MF indicator differences, the comparisons also differentiate between renewable (biomass) and non-renewable (fossil fuels, minerals, metal ores) materials.
Our initial step involves presenting the SDA outcomes for the Netherlands' per capita Material Footprint (MF), as depicted in Figure 2. This analysis helps pinpoint the primary factors within the GMRIO models responsible for the observed discrepancies. The most substantial deviations in MFs, specifically 15.6 and 17.6 tons per capita for the EXIOBASEcc–Eoracc and ICIOcc–Eoracc comparisons respectively, are attributed to variations in the Leontief inverse matrix (L-effect). Conversely, for the EXIOBASEcc–ICIOcc pairing, differences in final demand data (Y-effect) emerge as the most significant contributor. Across all three model comparisons, the x-effect consistently registers as the least impactful. These findings align with those observed for Russia, detailed in Supporting Information S1, Figures SI 1–3.
Subsequently, we utilized the SPLD method to delve deeper into the Leontief inverse matrix within the GMRIO data system. The SPLD analysis produced a matrix detailing how data discrepancies in each cell of the multiregional technology (A) matrix contribute to variations in the overall Material Footprint (MF) indicator. To present these findings, we aggregated the results in two ways. One aggregation adopted a geographical lens, showing all sectors within each country, where off-diagonal cells represented trade flows with supplying countries in rows and receiving countries in columns. The second aggregation focused on an industry perspective, combining data across all countries for each specific industry.
For our SPLD analysis, we chose the ICIOcc–Eoracc pair due to its prominent L-effect in the SDA. Additional SPLD visualizations for the remaining model pairs are available in Supporting Information S1, chapter 8.
Figure 3a illustrates how data deviations within the multiregional A-matrices of the ICIOcc and Eoracc models geographically influence the Netherlands' Material Footprint (MF). Specifically, this figure compares the impact of domestic and trade blocks. The diagonal cells of the matrix represent the domestic input-output tables for all countries included in the Common Classification (CC). Conversely, the off-diagonal cells denote import and export activities between various nations. The "bubbles" within the figure quantify the absolute data discrepancies between the domestic and trade blocks of the EXIOBASEcc and Eoracc A-matrices. They also show how significantly these discrepancies contribute to the differences observed in the Netherlands' MF, specifically those originating from the technology matrix. It's worth noting that as more production layers are broken down, the sum of these A-effect matrices progressively approaches the total L-effect (further details on the SPLD technique are in Supporting Information S1, chapter 3). Figure 3 presents these SPLD results for the Netherlands' MF, offering both a country (a) and sector (b) perspective for the ICIOcc–Eoracc pair in 2010. The bubble sizes are independently scaled for each graph to best highlight deviation patterns for specific aggregations and material groups, meaning direct comparisons of bubble sizes across different figures are not appropriate.
For the Netherlands, unlike Russia where domestic data is the primary source of variation, deviations in the Material Footprint (MF) are largely driven by numerous import blocks within the aggregated interindustry matrix, as shown in Figure 3a. The significant reliance of Dutch final demand on foreign imports is evident in the "Netherlands (NL)" column. Specifically, data discrepancies in trade blocks, such as imports from Belgium, Germany, France, Canada, and particularly the "Rest-of-the-World" region, substantially influence MF outcomes. These direct trade blocks collectively account for 64% of all A-matrix-related deviations.
For the Netherlands, a globally integrated economy, aligning MF results from ICIOcc and Eoracc requires focusing on more countries than for Russia. This is because discrepancies in the domestic data of several other nations, such as Belgium, Germany, the United States, India, Russia, and the "Rest of the World" region, also contribute to the overall deviations.
Analyzing the ICIOcc–Eoracc pair, 83% of the Netherlands' Material Footprint deviations linked to the A-matrix stem from non-renewable materials, while renewable materials account for only 17%. This covers all global supply chains meeting Dutch domestic final demand.
For the Netherlands' biomass footprint, deviations primarily stem from the agricultural sector's deliveries, notably to public administration (39%) and food production (15% of A-matrix deviations). The food product supply chain also contributes, with deliveries to public administration (13%) and sales (2%) being less significant factors.
Discrepancies in the non-renewable material footprint (MF) are significantly influenced by data deviations related to public administration, as shown in the bubble chart. Specifically, the largest deviations in the abiotic MF stem from the mining sector's deliveries to petroleum and chemicals (10% of total A-matrix effects), electricity/gas/water (6%), and public administration (32%). Additionally, deliveries from the petroleum and chemicals sector to public administration contribute 13% to these deviations. Generally, for all raw material types, data inconsistencies in downstream manufacturing and service sectors have only a minor impact on MF results.
The SDA and SPLD assessments were conducted across all countries in the Common Classification. Figure 4 visually presents the detailed SDA results for all countries within this classification for the year 2010.
Our analysis of the three model pairs showed no consistent dominance of any single effect (L-effect, Y-effect, or x-effect) in explaining material footprint (MF) discrepancies. Specifically, for the Eoracc–EXIOBASEcc comparison, the L-effect accounted for 37% of deviations across all countries, surpassing the other two effects (33% and 30%). In contrast, the EXIOBASEcc–ICIOcc pair saw the Y-effect as most significant at 36%, compared to the L-effect (33%) and x-effect (31%). The Eoracc–ICIOcc pair exhibited the strongest x-effect, contributing 45% to the differences, while the L- and Y-effects were 32% and 23%, respectively.
Figure 5 summarizes the aggregated SPLD results from a sector perspective, encompassing all countries and three model pairs, for both renewable and non-renewable raw materials. This overview allows for the identification of general patterns. Additionally, accompanying pie charts indicate whether domestic or foreign components of the interindustry matrix are more significant contributors to material footprint indicator deviations.
Aggregating data across all countries reveals that patterns observed in individual country examples hold true generally. Deviations in material footprint (MF) calculations, specifically those linked to the A matrix, are predominantly driven by the raw material extraction sectors of agriculture and mining, accounting for 60% of these discrepancies across model pairs. Conversely, data inconsistencies in downstream manufacturing and service sectors have a significantly lesser effect, a finding crucial for improving the reliability of GMRIO-based MF assessments.
When examining renewable raw materials, the agricultural sector's supply structure accounts for a significant 60% of the biomass footprint deviation originating from the A-matrix across the three model comparisons. The food sector contributes an additional 18%, leaving only 22% attributable to the interindustry relations of all other combined sectors. The agricultural sector's own consumption, alongside its supply patterns to various downstream sectors, heavily influences these results. This includes deliveries to the food (4) and textiles (5) industries, to sectors involved in processing non-renewable materials (specifically, petroleum/chemicals, sector 6), and to service sectors, notably construction (12) and retail (13). Furthermore, the food sector's provision of processed biomass impacts the biomass footprint differences, particularly through its own use of food products and deliveries to retailers. In certain countries, other deliveries, such as biotic materials supplied to construction, retail, or public administration, also play a role.
Differences in the abiotic material footprint (MF) primarily stem from the mining sector's supply structure to other industries, accounting for 59% of technology matrix deviations across the three model pairs. Specifically, discrepancies in mining's deliveries to petroleum/chemicals (sector 6) and metal products (sector 7) have the most significant impact. The petroleum/chemicals sector's supply structures to various manufacturing and service sectors contribute a further 16% to these deviations. The remaining 25% is attributed to all other sectors combined.
An examination of the geographical distribution of material footprint deviations (as shown in Figure 5's pie charts) indicates a clear distinction between renewable and non-renewable raw materials. For renewable resources, domestic economic activities, represented by domestic blocks, are generally more influential in contributing to A-matrix effects, accounting for an average of 63% across the three model comparisons. Conversely, for non-renewable raw materials, international trade flows (trade blocks) are more significant contributors to A-matrix deviations. Specifically, trade blocks explain 52% of deviations for the Eoracc–ICIOcc pair, 49% for Eoracc–EXIOBASEcc, and 36% for EXIOBASEcc–ICIOcc. This heightened importance of trade for non-renewable materials is logical, given that the extraction of fossil fuels and metal ores is geographically concentrated, resulting in substantial volumes moving through global supply chains. Consequently, even minor inaccuracies in trade data for these materials can lead to considerable discrepancies in the non-renewable material footprint.
Policymakers increasingly require reliable resource use indicators. Our research, showing that per capita Material Footprint (MF) varies by only 15% across Global Multiregional Input-Output (GMRIO) databases for many significant economies, boosts confidence in this indicator. This consistency mirrors findings for carbon footprints, where Moran and Wood (2014) noted less than 10% deviation for major economies after harmonizing greenhouse gas emissions data. Furthermore, GMRIO frameworks largely agree on a country's role in international raw material trade, identifying whether it's a net exporter or importer. Despite these points of agreement, the absolute per capita MF levels still show considerable variation when calculated using different databases for certain nations.
Our analysis reveals that discrepancies in Material Footprints (MFs) are primarily driven by specific, limited portions of the input-output matrices. While previous research (Ewing et al., 2012; Schoer et al., 2013) highlighted the significance of primary sectors, our study uniquely quantifies their numerical impact on MF variations. Consequently, future development of Global Multiregional Input-Output (GMRIO) datasets should prioritize these critical sectors. This includes focusing on agriculture and forestry, along with industries that process renewable raw materials. Additionally, the mining and quarrying sector, and its connections to basic metal, petroleum, and chemical industries, warrant particular attention in GMRIO model refinement.
The use of a Common Classification (CC) was a prerequisite for employing the Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Structural Production Layer Decomposition (SPLD) methods in this study. Consequently, the national Material Footprints (MFs) derived from this aggregated classification diverge from those obtained using the full-detail Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) models. This aggregation directly contributes to some of the observed discrepancies among GMRIO models, a phenomenon also noted in prior research (Owen et al., 2014). Our findings indicate that reducing sector resolution leads to an amplification of these deviations between models. For instance, the number of countries whose MF results fell within a 15% discrepancy range decreased from 18 (out of 40) in the full-detail models to 15 in the aggregated CC. Across all countries and the three model pairs, the aggregation effect resulted in an average increase in deviations of approximately 19%, shifting from about 2.9 tons per capita to 3.5 tons per capita. While aggregation might, in some cases, reduce discrepancies by averaging out effects, it can also exacerbate them by combining subsectors with disparate product compositions and supply structures. Although not the central focus of this paper, our results corroborate previous arguments (de Koning et al., 2015; Piñero et al., 2015) advocating for the disaggregation of material-intensive supply chain segments to enhance the reliability of MF results.
Even with highly aggregated sector classifications, SDA and SPLD analyses effectively pinpointed critical areas for future research and database enhancements. We posit that these identified sector and supply-chain priorities, crucial for resolving MF discrepancies, likely extend to fully detailed GMRIO models, though this is not directly verifiable with our current methods. This assumption stems from the fact that in comprehensive models, material extraction is exclusively assigned to primary sectors. Consequently, any variations in the supply structures of these primary sectors will disproportionately influence material footprint outcomes compared to manufacturing or service industries.
Achieving complete uniformity across existing GMRIO databases is neither practical nor beneficial, considering their diverse methodological and policy applications. These databases, including EXIOBASE, Eora, and ICIO, each offer unique strengths and perspectives on resource utilization. EXIOBASE, for example, prioritizes comprehensive coverage of environmentally sensitive activities. Eora, conversely, emphasizes global scope and granular sector detail. The ICIO database has primarily served analyses of trade-embodied value added and carbon footprints, with a strong focus on accurately representing trade flows. To enhance the quality, comparability, and interpretability of results, reduce uncertainties, and broaden policy relevance, transparent documentation of each GMRIO framework's construction processes and fundamental components is crucial.
To enhance the quality of results, harmonizing procedures across GMRIO databases is crucial. This involves standardizing main data sources and their classifications, along with improving metadata reporting. Agreed-upon methods are needed for integrating source data into input-output models, including converting to basic prices and transforming supply-use tables. Furthermore, aligning bilateral merchandise and services trade statistics with national and global supply-use tables is important. Developing best practices for structuring the "Rest-of-the-World" region, disaggregating environmentally sensitive sectors, and establishing guidelines for balancing the final tables are also key areas for procedural harmonization.
International organizations could significantly aid in harmonizing data and methods for material footprint indicators. The OECD, for example, has previously organized three expert workshops on MF indicators, with the most recent in September 2017. This workshop identified that while the necessary expertise for procedural harmonization exists among research institutes, statistical offices, environmental authorities, and international bodies, adequate funding is absent. Currently, it remains uncertain if the OECD or another international entity will lead this harmonization, or if collaborative, grassroots initiatives like the global virtual MRIO Laboratory will fill the void left by insufficient international coordination.
This study's emphasis on raw materials shapes its specific conclusions about which economic sectors and supply chains are crucial for reducing uncertainties in material flow indicators. However, applying the same analytical approach to other footprint indicators, like the carbon footprint (as demonstrated by Wieland et al., 2018 for the EU), would likely reveal different priority areas for data alignment. The reason for this divergence is that the monetary data deviations within the GMRIO system that significantly impact other environmental categories, such as the carbon footprint, are driven by different sectors and supply chains—for instance, major greenhouse gas emitters like the electricity sector (Owen et al., 2014, 2016).
This study primarily aimed to measure how discrepancies in data within various widely used Global Multiregional Input-Output (GMRIO) databases affect Material Footprint (MF) results. Our analysis revealed that the majority of variations in MF indicators, across all countries and material categories examined, stemmed from data inconsistencies in primary material extraction sectors (agriculture, forestry, and mining) and their immediate processing industries (food, petroleum, chemicals, metal products, and construction). When GMRIO databases show differing monetary values for the outputs of these sectors, both domestically and in international trade, it leads to substantial differences in the MF indicator. This is due to the significant volume of materials embedded in these early supply chain stages. Conversely, data differences in sectors further along the supply chain, like manufacturing or services, generally had only a minor influence on the demand-based material flow indicator. This trend was consistent across both developed and emerging economies.
Future research on Material Footprints (MFs) should prioritize the refinement of Global Multiregional Input–Output (GMRIO) models and databases, specifically focusing on material-intensive sectors and their supply chains. A promising avenue involves leveraging the increasing availability of global physical unit data to track primary material flows. This could potentially bypass problematic sectors in GMRIO databases that currently contribute most to MF discrepancies, by establishing detailed and dependable physical accounts for initial processing stages. This approach would complement efforts to standardize monetary MRIO modeling procedures.
To effectively inform environmental policy, material flow accounting indicators must be rapidly developed. This will enable them to serve as a practical evidence base, addressing the intricate global economic connections to the environment and natural resources. Such advancements will equip researchers to meet policymakers' demands for insights into complex international supply chains and their environmental impacts in a globalized world.
