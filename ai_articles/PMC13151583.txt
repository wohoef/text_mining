The Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) was tasked by the European Commission with providing an opinion on amending the specifications for basic methacrylate copolymer (BMC – E 1205), specifically concerning its definition and particle size limits as outlined in Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012. This request stemmed from a shift in BMC's manufacturing process, moving from bulk polymerization to solution polymerization. A key issue arose with the existing particle size specifications, which stipulated a limit of 5.1% to 5.5% for particles less than 0.1 μm. These limits were reportedly never achieved and could be misinterpreted as a requirement for a specific percentage of nanoparticles in the powder. To address this, the specifications were revised. The new limits state that at least 95% of particles should be smaller than 50 μm, at least 50% should be smaller than 20 μm, and no more than 10% should be smaller than 3 μm. Importantly, analysis of BMC batches revealed no particles with a diameter of 0.1 μm (100 nm) or less. The applicant provided a comprehensive description of the solution polymerization process, along with data on residual solvents, residual monomers, stability, and the substance's behavior in food. No new biological or toxicological data were submitted. Considering the available information and the fact that BMC was recently evaluated by EFSA (EFSA ANS Panel, 2010), the ANS Panel concluded that the previous safety assessment remained valid for BMC produced via the new manufacturing method. Consequently, the Panel accepted the request to amend the specifications and determined that the proposed changes do not pose a safety concern for the food additive BMC.

The Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) evaluated a request to amend the specifications for basic methacrylate copolymer (BMC – E 1205). This request, concerning the substance's definition and particle size, stems from a shift in its manufacturing process from bulk to solution polymerization in 2013. The Panel's assessment focused on whether these proposed amendments could introduce safety concerns.
The applicant detailed the solution polymerization process, supplying data on residual solvents, monomers, and the stability, reaction, and fate of the new basic methacrylate copolymer in food.
The applicant provided substantial analytical data. These data confirmed that, apart from the proposed changes, the product from the solution polymerization process met most existing BMC specifications. The Panel found no significant differences between products from the new solution polymerization process and those from the previous bulk polymerization process. Crucially, no new biological or toxicological data were submitted.
The EFSA ANS Panel previously evaluated BMC in 2010. At that time, considering its high molecular weight, lack of absorption, and low toxicity, the Panel deemed the safety margins adequate. Consequently, BMC's use as a glazing or coating agent in solid food supplements was deemed safe at proposed levels. The current Panel concluded that this assessment remains valid for BMC produced using the new manufacturing method.
To ensure the particle size distribution accurately reflected the data and to prevent any misunderstanding about potential nanoscale components, the Panel sought further clarification from the applicant. The applicant's provided information on particle size distribution allayed concerns, confirming the absence of detectable nanoscale material. Consequently, the specifications were revised. The updated limits stipulate that at least 95% of particles must be smaller than 50 μm, at least 50% must be smaller than 20 μm, and no more than 10% should be smaller than 3 μm.
The Panel concluded that proposed specification amendments for food additive BMC, stemming from manufacturing process changes, pose no safety concern.
Food additive usage in the EU is governed by Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008. Only additives on the Union list, specifically Annex II, can be marketed and used in food according to specified conditions. Furthermore, these additives must adhere to the specifications outlined in Article 14 of that Regulation and detailed in Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012.

EFSA is asked by the European Commission for a scientific opinion. This opinion will assess the safety of proposed specification amendments for the food additive BMC. The request follows Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008, which outlines a common authorization procedure for food additives.
To assess the safety of the proposed specification changes, the Panel determined that evaluating their impact on the product's characteristics and safety profile would suffice. Given the ANS Panel's recent scientific assessment of BMC in 2010, a complete re-evaluation was deemed unnecessary.

In 2010, the EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) conducted an evaluation of basic methacrylate copolymer (BMC) for its use as a glazing agent in solid food supplements and solid foods for special medical purposes. During this assessment, BMC did not present genotoxicity concerns. However, the Panel noted a lack of reproductive toxicity data and a limited database for developmental toxicity. Consequently, the Panel determined that an acceptable daily intake (ADI) for BMC could not be established. The available studies yielded the following no observed adverse effect levels (NOAELs): 1,000 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day from a single-dose developmental study in rats, and 2,000 mg/kg bw per day from a 26-week feeding study in rats, which represented the highest dose tested. For individuals who are heavy users of BMC, the estimated combined exposure from both food supplements and pharmaceuticals, assuming a coating level of 100 mg per tablet, was calculated to be 23.4 mg/kg bw per day for a 60 kg adult and 16 mg/kg bw per day for children aged 4–18 years. When considering exposure solely from food supplements, the calculated margins of safety (MoS) ranged from 85 to 171 for adults and 125 to 250 for children. Given BMC's high molecular weight, its inability to be absorbed, and its low toxicity profile, the Panel deemed these MoS to be sufficient. Ultimately, the ANS Panel concluded that the use of BMC as a glazing or coating agent in solid food supplements posed no safety concern at the proposed usage levels.
The Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) previously evaluated methyl methacrylate (MMA) and butyl methacrylate (BMA). They established a group TDI of 0.1 mg/kg body weight per day for these monomers, expressed as methacrylic acid.
Dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) was evaluated by the SCF in 1998 for food contact materials. However, no TDI was established because residues were undetectable. The SCF noted that DMAEMA readily hydrolyzes at neutral pH and above, making it unstable in many foods.
In 2010, the Panel calculated worst-case monomer exposure for adults at less than 50 μg/kg bw per day and for children at less than 32 μg/kg bw per day. These figures were significantly below the group TDI of 0.1 mg/kg bw per day for MMA, BMA, and DMAEMA.
A new dossier supplements the prior application, requesting amendments to BMC specifications. These changes concern its use as a protective coating for solid dosage forms like tablets and fine particles, providing mechanical stabilization, moisture protection, and masking taste or odor.
The ANS Panel evaluated the proposed manufacturing process change and definition amendments, including particle size, following the EFSA Guidance for food additive evaluations (2012).
The assessment adhered to EFSA's guidance on transparency in risk assessment (2009) and followed existing guidelines from the EFSA Scientific Committee.
BMC is a fully polymerized copolymer of acrylic and methacrylic acid esters. It is a cationic copolymer, composed of DMAEMA and neutral methacrylic esters (BMA and MMA).
The substance is designated Poly[(dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate)‐co‐(methyl methacrylate)‐co‐(butyl methacrylate)] according to IUPAC. Its molar ratio is 0.24:0.34:0.25, with a weight ratio of 49% methyl methacrylate, 25% butyl methacrylate, and 26% dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate. The CAS Registry Number is 24938‐16‐7. The applicant confirmed the substance's molar ratio aligns with this IUPAC designation.
Comparative data on weight average molecular weight (Mw) for BMC from both solution and bulk polymerization processes were provided, analyzed using gel permeation chromatography (GPC).
Comparative analysis of solution and bulk polymerisation products revealed nearly identical weight average molecular weights (Mw), averaging around 45,000–48,000 g/mol. Given the consistent manufacturing characteristics within specifications, the Panel deemed additional batch data unnecessary.
The number average molecular weight (Mn) is approximately 22,000 g/mol.
Production parameters and viscosity measurements ensured a constant molecular weight range. Figure 1 displays the representative structural formula for BMC.
The applicant states that cross-linking is impossible in the polymer. This is because the DMAEMA, BMA, and MMA monomers, after radical polymerization, form only chains. They each possess a single double bond for polymerization, lacking a second required for cross-linking.
BMC exhibits solubility in methanol, ethanol, 2-propanol, acetone, ethyl acetate, methylene chloride, and 1N hydrochloric acid, forming clear to slightly cloudy solutions. Additionally, a 12% BMC organic solution can be combined with these solvents and petroleum ether at a 1:1 ratio.
The solid substance is virtually insoluble in petroleum ether and water. A 12.5% solution precipitates when mixed with water at a 1:1 ratio.
BMC films dissolve in gastric juice and weakly acidic solutions (up to pH 5) by forming polymeric salts from their 2-dimethylaminoethyl groups.
Basic butylated methacrylate copolymer, also known as amino methacrylate copolymer, is a polymer of butyl methacrylate, dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate, and methyl methacrylate.
The applicant requested amendments to the 'Definition' and 'particle size' limits for BMC in EU specifications, stating other specifications remain unchanged from Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012.
The definition of BMC in EU specifications requires amendment due to a change in its manufacturing process from bulk to solution polymerization, as detailed in the manufacturing section.
The applicant emphasized that the chemical synthesis, a radical polymerization, remains the same. They stated that the product from the new solution polymerization process meets current E 1205 specifications and offers equivalent functionality, providing analytical data from validation lots to support this.
The Panel observed that the current specification for BMC powder (E 1205) in Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012 mandated a particle size of less than 0.1 μm for 5.1% to 5.5% of the material. The applicant explained this limit stemmed from a misinterpretation of their 2009 data and stated that commercial products have never met this requirement.
The current EU specification for E 1205 might imply a requirement for a specific proportion of nanoparticles within the powder. However, the applicant presented particle size distribution data for BMC produced through solution polymerization. Laser diffraction was employed for this analysis, with the instrument capable of measuring particles as small as 0.01 μm (10 nm). Crucially, the applicant's data revealed no particles measuring 0.1 μm (100 nm) or less. The applicant explicitly stated that BMC was not designed or anticipated to be a nanomaterial.

The Panel suggested a clearer description for the applicant's proposed particle size distribution: at least 5% larger than 50 μm, at least 50% larger than 20 μm, and at least 90% larger than 3 μm.
EFSA previously evaluated BMC produced by the bulk polymerization process in 2010. The applicant stated this older process was originally for industrial bulk polymers, later minimally adapted for pharmaceutical excipient guidelines and food manufacturing standards.
BMC manufacturing was modernized in 2013. It shifted from bulk polymerization, detailed in 2008, to a more controlled solution polymerization process.
Detailed information on BMC manufacturing via solution polymerization, still based on radical polymerization, has been submitted. This process change does not affect key copolymer properties, ensuring consistency with previous product characteristics.
Temperature-induced radical polymerization begins with the thermal decomposition of an initiator system. This process involves mixing monomers (DMAEMA, BMA, MMA), a solvent (propan-2-ol), a chain modifying agent (alkyl mercaptan), and the initiator.
In the new solution polymerization process, the initiator system was replaced with a comparable free radical donor initiator system. Thermal decomposition eliminates the initiator from the reaction mixture.
The applicant emphasized that the new initiator's low initial concentration and decomposition half-life during manufacturing ensure its complete consumption. Analytical data, according to the applicant, support this, showing initiator concentration in the polymer below the detection limit. This level is significantly lower than the maximum 0.2% recommended for food contact materials by the BfR (2010). Furthermore, the applicant highlighted that, unlike the initiator used in bulk polymerization, the solution polymerization initiator is recognized by BfR recommendations as suitable for polyacrylates and polymethacrylates in food contact applications.
Following polymerization, the viscous polymer solution is fed into an extruder for processing. Volatile substances like propan-2-ol are removed via vacuum degassing during extrusion. This process yields solid BMC granules, which are subsequently micronized into a powder form.
EFSA previously evaluated initiator decomposition and side reactions of the chain modifying agent in their 2010 assessment.
The applicant provided substantial analytical data confirming that the product from the solution polymerization process largely met existing BMC specifications, aside from the proposed changes, and offered equivalent functionality. The Panel found no significant differences between products from the new and old processes. While quantitative changes were observed, the Panel determined these did not introduce any new toxicological hazards.

To quantify BMC in solid dosage forms, the applicant detailed a method involving extraction with tetrahydrofuran (THF). Insoluble components are separated by centrifugation. The resulting clear liquid is then concentrated by boiling and oven-dried. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is used for final identification of the residue.
The applicant initially stated in 2008 that the polyethylene carbon backbone of the polymer prevented chain degradation, such as hydrolysis, under normal conditions. Furthermore, they reported high thermal and chemical stability during processing, specifically when using conventional coating methods like pan-coating and fluid-bed coating for solid dosage forms. The Panel, in its 2010 evaluation, concurred with these assessments.
The applicant previously noted that ionic interactions can occur between oppositely charged functional groups in BMC and other food components. As a cationic polymer, BMC can interact ionically with anionic substances like pharmaceutical ingredients, excipients, and food ingredients. This interaction, the applicant explained, is a known phenomenon in pharmaceutical applications and is not unique to polymethacrylates but rather characteristic of all ionic substances. The likelihood of complex formation is influenced by several factors, including the intensity of contact, the nature of the functional groups, steric limitations, and the frequency of contact due to the number of ionic groups. Furthermore, the applicant pointed out that these salt or complex formations through ionic interactions can be intentionally utilized for specific purposes, such as taste-masking.
The Panel agreed that previous considerations remained valid, as the applicant's data showed the solution polymerisation product maintained the same characteristics and quality as the bulk polymerisation product.
The applicant presented detailed stability data for basic methacrylate copolymer (BMC) produced via solution polymerization. Three validation lots were stored at ambient temperatures, adhering to US Pharmacopeia (USP 35–NF 30, 2012) guidelines. The powder form of BMC demonstrated stability for a minimum of 14 months. Further measurements are scheduled for 2 and 4 years post-storage. Concurrently, a routine storage stability program under standard warehouse conditions was initiated for all solution-polymerized BMC lots. Samples from this program will undergo analysis at two distinct points within the minimum stability period, as well as at its conclusion.
The applicant previously stated that low coating levels of 1–5 mg BMC/cm², or approximately 6–30 mg per 1,000 mg tablet, suffice for taste masking and moisture protection. Even with a maximum coating level of 100 mg per tablet, these use levels remain unchanged and are still considered valid.
In 2010, the Panel estimated that heavy users would have a combined exposure to BMC from food supplements and pharmaceuticals of 23.4 mg/kg body weight per day for adults and 16 mg/kg body weight per day for children aged 4-18. Concurrently, the worst-case exposure to the monomers MMA, BMA, and DMAEMA was calculated to be less than 50 μg/kg body weight per day for adults and less than 32 μg/kg body weight per day for children. These monomer exposures are substantially lower than the group TDI of 0.1 mg/kg body weight per day (expressed as methacrylic acid) established by the SCF. Since the applications and usage levels for BMC remain the same, this exposure assessment is still considered valid.
No new biological or toxicological data were submitted by the applicant for this request, consistent with the original 2008 application.
Since the manufacturing process changes revealed no new hazards, the 2010 toxicological evaluation for BMC remains valid.
