Achieving breast symmetry and a satisfactory cosmetic result in one-stage immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) relies heavily on accurate preoperative assessment of breast volume. This study aimed to compare the accuracy of breast volume estimations derived from three-dimensional (3D) surface imaging against magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Additionally, the research details the development and application of a 3D-printed mold designed to aid intraoperative autologous breast reconstruction. The study enrolled patients undergoing mastectomy with one-stage IBR, utilizing either autologous tissue transfer or direct implant, between 2016 and 2019. Both 3D surface imaging and MRI were employed to evaluate breast volume and shape. The accuracy of these measurements was subsequently validated by comparing them to the water displacement volume of the excised mastectomy specimens. A key component of the study involved designing a 3D-printed mold to facilitate autologous breast reconstruction. The findings included nineteen women who underwent a total of twenty mastectomies. A strong linear correlation was observed between the breast volumes measured by both 3D surface imaging and MRI, and the water displacement of the mastectomy specimens (Pearson correlation: 3D surface image, r = 0.925, p < 0.001; MRI, r = 0.915, p < 0.001). Bland-Altman plots indicated no proportional bias between the assessment methods. The coefficient of variation was 52.7% for 3D surface imaging and 59.9% for MRI. For the six breasts where both measurement methods were applied, the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.689 for 3D surface imaging (p = 0.043) and 0.743 for MRI (p = 0.028). In conclusion, 3D surface imaging offers a swift, efficient, and user-friendly approach for assessing breast shape and volume. The study demonstrated that the accuracy, reproducibility, and reliability of 3D surface imaging were comparable to MRI. Furthermore, the integration of 3D-printed molds was found to enhance symmetry and aesthetic outcomes in immediate autologous breast reconstructions.

Increased patient awareness and cancer screening lead to more early breast cancer diagnoses. Many patients opt for immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) to enhance psychological well-being and quality of life. Research indicates that neoadjuvant chemotherapy does not impact IBR complications, nor does IBR delay adjuvant therapy. Furthermore, autologous reconstruction, when combined with postmastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT), offers superior quality of life, sensory recovery, and fewer complications compared to implant-based reconstruction with PMRT.
Achieving breast symmetry and satisfactory outcomes in reconstructive and aesthetic surgery relies on accurate preoperative breast volume assessment. Because breast shape is dynamic, patient-position dependent, and highly variable, volumetric analysis methods must be versatile.
Since Bouman's initial breast volume measurement, numerous methods have emerged, including water displacement, negative molding, and various anthropomorphic measurements using 2D or 3D imaging. 3D surface scanning technology is also widely used. While MRI has shown the highest accuracy, 3D surface scanning is increasingly favored for its convenience and affordability. Despite these advancements, a definitive consensus on the most accurate method for breast volume estimation remains elusive.
Achieving optimal 3D shaping and orientation of the neo-breast is paramount in one-stage autologous immediate breast reconstruction (IBR). For unilateral reconstructions, a mirror image of the unaffected breast serves as an excellent template for natural contour and symmetry. Preoperative breast volume estimation can significantly aid in this process, potentially leading to reduced operative times and fewer revision procedures. This study sought to compare the accuracy of breast volume measurements obtained through 3D surface scanning and MRI. These estimations were then validated against the water displacement volume of mastectomy specimens. Additionally, the utility of a 3D-printed mold, created from the contralateral breast's mirror image, in facilitating autologous IBR is explored.
This prospective cohort study enrolled patients undergoing mastectomy and immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) at the Taipei Veterans General Hospital between 2016 and 2019. Participants were selected from those treated at the Department of Plastic Surgery and Comprehensive Breast Health Center, who were scheduled for either autologous tissue transfer or direct implant reconstruction. Within a month prior to their surgery, patients underwent both 3D surface scanning and MRI. Crucially, no surgical interventions occurred between these imaging sessions and the reconstruction, ensuring breast volume stability. The study included patients with available preoperative imaging (MRI or 3D scan) and water volume displacement data. Comprehensive data collection encompassed demographic information, preoperative calculations, water displacement measurements, and pre- and postoperative photographs. The study received approval from the Taipei Veterans General Hospital's medical ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.
For 3D surface scanning and subsequent mold fabrication, patients stood undressed. A plastic surgeon (C.J. Feng) palpated and marked the breast boundaries with tape, typically defining the superior limit at the second intercostal space, the medial limit at the lateral sternal line, the inferior border at the inframammary fold, and the lateral border at the anterior axillary line. A handheld Artec Eva scanner was then used to capture 3D surface images. During the scanning process, an experienced technician guided the patient to maintain a relaxed standing posture.
Following 3D surface data acquisition, postprocessing software reconstructed the breast shape in approximately 30 minutes. As the scanner could not detect the chest wall contour, it was treated as a flat plane. Breast volume was subsequently calculated using Meshmixer software.
To determine breast volume from MRI, Slicer4 software (version 4.11.0) was used to reconstruct 3D images. Individual slices, typically 0.8 to 1.2 mm thick, were segmented to isolate breast tissue. The pectoralis major fascia served as the posterior boundary. The total breast volume was calculated by summing the volumes of all segmented slices, assuming equal slice thickness.
To determine the volume of the mastectomy specimen, a water displacement method was employed. A specially designed metal tank in the operating room was filled with saline, and a drain hole was positioned laterally. The excised breast tissue was submerged in the tank, and the volume of saline that drained out was recorded as the specimen's volume. This water displacement measurement served as the validation for breast volumes calculated using either 3D surface imaging or MRI. Additionally, this technique was utilized to measure the volume of the autologous flap after it was harvested for reconstruction.
For the creation of the 3D-printed mold, Meshmixer version 3.4.35 software was utilized. To ensure a natural and symmetrical bilateral appearance during unilateral breast reconstruction, a mirror image of the contralateral breast was generated. Following this design phase, image slicing was performed using UltimakerCura 3.0.3 in preparation for 3D printing. The mold itself was printed using polylactic acid (PLA) material on a CR-10s 3D printer from Creality, China, with each print weighing approximately 160 grams and taking between 15 to 20 hours to complete. After printing, the mold underwent sterilization via a plasma sterilization technique. This custom-designed mold, derived from the contralateral breast's mirror image, proved instrumental in guiding the orientation and shaping of the neo-breast using an autologous flap during the intraoperative reconstruction. Excess tissue was then trimmed based on the predetermined neo-breast and flap target volume. To further enhance breast symmetry and achieve a natural contour, the patient was positioned in a sitting posture by bending the operating table during the flap inset.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 software. To evaluate the association between breast volume estimates from 3D surface imaging or MRI and water displacement measurements, Pearson correlation was employed. Linear regression was then used to define the relationships between these methods and water displacement, and to develop volume-predicting formulas. Agreement between the two assessment methods was examined using Bland-Altman plots, which helped identify relationships between bias magnitude and variation degree. A one-sample t test determined the mean difference between measurements. Confidence intervals were established by multiplying the standard deviation by 1.96 and adding or subtracting this value from the mean difference, defining the 95% limits of agreement for bias assessment. Reproducibility was assessed by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV), expressed as a percentage of the mean breast volume. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was also calculated to determine the most reliable boundary definition. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
A total of nineteen women participated in the study, undergoing twenty mastectomies, with eighteen being unilateral and one bilateral procedure. The primary reasons for mastectomy were malignancy (eighteen cases), prophylaxis (one case), and benign breast tumors (one case). Half of the mastectomies (ten breasts) were nipple-sparing, while the other half (ten breasts) were non-nipple-sparing. Prior to surgery, 3D surface imaging data was obtained for 70% (14 breasts) of the patients, and MRI data for 60% (12 breasts). Both imaging modalities were available for 30% (six breasts). For immediate breast reconstruction (IBR), 70% (14 breasts) utilized autologous tissue transfer, specifically deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flaps, while 30% (six breasts) opted for direct implant reconstruction. The patient cohort had a median age of 46 years (range, 33-64 years) and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 23.3 kg/m2 (range, 18.7-33.3 kg/m2). Complete pathological data was documented for all participants.
The average mastectomy volumes measured by 3D surface imaging and MRI were 411.5 ± 275 mL and 438.4 ± 254.4 mL, respectively, with no significant difference observed between these two groups. A robust linear correlation was found between the breast volumes determined by both 3D surface imaging and MRI, and the water displacement volumes of the mastectomy specimens. This strong association was confirmed by Pearson correlation coefficients of r = 0.925 (p < 0.001) for 3D surface imaging and r = 0.915 (p < 0.001) for MRI. Predictive equations were developed to estimate mastectomy mean volume: mastectomy mean volume = (3D scan mean volume × 1.03) − 70.68 and mastectomy mean volume = (MRI mean volume × 0.7) + 50.15. The graph's intersection point highlights an offset, indicating that the 3D scan method tends to slightly overestimate breast volume, which is accounted for by the constant 70.68 in its formula. The multiplication factor of 1.03 in the 3D scan equation signifies a slope very close to 45 degrees.
The 3D surface imaging method showed no significant absolute volume error (56.6 ± 104.8 mL, p = 0.064). In contrast, MRI systematically overestimated breast volume by an average of 113.8 ± 142.2 mL (p = 0.018). Bland-Altman plots confirmed no proportional bias between these assessment techniques.
Reproducibility was assessed using the coefficient of variation, which was 52.7% for 3D surface imaging and 59.9% for MRI. For six breasts measured by both methods pre-mastectomy, the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.689 for 3D surface imaging and 0.743 for MRI.
In nine out of fourteen DIEP flap reconstructions, a 3D-printed mold was utilized. This paper presents a case of one-stage immediate breast reconstruction (IBR) that employed preoperative volume estimation via 3D surface scanning and a 3D-printed mold. The patient, a 48-year-old woman, had previously been treated for left breast invasive ductal carcinoma with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, lumpectomy, and radiotherapy. This treatment resulted in asymmetry, deformity, and nipple-areola complex retraction in her left breast. Subsequently, she was diagnosed with local recurrence in the same breast. A nipple-sparing mastectomy and sentinel lymph node biopsy were planned. Following extensive consultation, she opted for autologous breast reconstruction using a DIEP flap. Preoperatively, bilateral breast volume and shape were assessed using 3D surface imaging while the patient was in a standing position. Based on this data, the volume of the healthy breast was calculated, and a corresponding 3D printing mold was designed. This mold was sterilized prior to surgery. Intraoperatively, the excess DIEP flap tissue was trimmed according to the calculated volume, and the mold aided in neo-breast orientation and shaping. The reconstruction aimed for symmetry and a natural contour by matching the intended volume and shape to the contralateral breast. The postoperative aesthetic outcome was satisfactory.
A 52-year-old patient with right breast invasive ductal carcinoma underwent nipple-sparing mastectomy and DIEP reconstruction, utilizing a 3D surface scan and printed mold. Preoperative 3D scanning estimated the healthy breast volume at 454 mL and the mastectomy side at 491 mL. The excised specimen measured 425 mL by water displacement. The harvested DIEP flap was 550 mL, with 102 mL discarded during the 3D mold-assisted inset, resulting in a final implanted flap volume of 448 mL. Two months post-operation, a satisfactory aesthetic outcome was observed.
A 32-year-old woman with a large phyllodes tumor presented a challenge for volume estimation in reconstruction. Employing the same 3D surface scan and printed mold technique for immediate breast reconstruction yielded satisfactory results. The right breast's estimated volume was 712 mL, while the left was 955 mL. The left mastectomy specimen's water displacement measured 940 mL, and the final implanted flap volume was 805 mL.
An effective method for one-stage immediate autologous breast reconstruction using 3D surface scanning and mold printing for volume estimation was introduced.
Immediate autologous breast reconstruction can be lengthy, increasing the risk of complications and reoperation. To mitigate this, pre-designed breast shaping, informed by objective contralateral breast volume estimation and 3D printed molds, can reduce operative time and the need for subsequent symmetrization procedures. This approach enhances surgeon confidence, particularly for those early in their careers, and allows reconstruction to commence promptly after mastectomy.
A systematic review by Choppin et al. evaluated various breast volume assessment methods. MRI scanning consistently showed the highest accuracy, with reported errors under 10% in three studies. MRI clearly delineates anatomical structures like the pectoralis major, mammary gland, subcutaneous tissue, and skin. However, MRI is performed with patients in a prone position, which is not suitable for flap inset during reconstruction surgery, limiting its utility for creating breast molds based on its scans. While MRI avoids radiation exposure associated with CT scans, its high cost and infrequent use in routine preoperative examinations are notable drawbacks.
Three-dimensional (3D) surface imaging represents the most recent and thoroughly investigated method for breast volumetric analysis. Pioneering work by Galdino et al.15 utilized 3D surface scanners to measure parameters like volume and shape, aiding in symmetry assessment. Various 3D surface imaging systems exist, with laser imaging, structured light, and stereophotogrammetry being the most commonly studied techniques.16 In this research, we employed the Artec Eva, a handheld 3D surface scanner. This device incorporates three cameras that utilize the structured light method, a technique known for its accuracy.17 Previous studies have demonstrated the precision of such systems; for instance, Modabber et al. reported a mean error of 0.23 ± 0.05 mm when measuring a scanned Lego brick attached to a patient's forehead.18 The literature also documents the use of 3D surface imaging in monitoring postoperative facial swelling following orthognathic surgery19 and in evaluating the volume and shape of lower extremity amputee stumps.20 A significant advantage of the Artec Eva is its mobility, allowing for 3D image acquisition from diverse locations. Furthermore, it offers a faster and more cost-effective alternative to other imaging systems. The scanner's ability to detect colors also facilitates the identification of anatomical landmarks. However, acquiring accurate 3D images necessitates considerable experience and training. Manual data processing is also a prerequisite for preparing the 3D image for analysis or surgical planning, a process that takes an experienced user several minutes. Consequently, imaging with the Artec Eva is often conducted by seasoned researchers. To our knowledge, this study is the first to integrate Artec Eva scanning with postprocessing software for the evaluation of breast shape and volume. Our findings indicate that the accuracy, reproducibility, and reliability of 3D surface imaging achieved with the Artec Eva system were comparable to those of MRI.
Previous systematic reviews indicate that while 3D surface imaging offers high accuracy for breast volume assessment, it can exhibit greater uncertainty in measuring absolute volumes. Factors contributing to this uncertainty include inconsistencies in defining breast landmarks, extended scanning times, and the inability to capture the chest wall contour. However, in this study, we mitigated these issues by having a single surgeon consistently define breast landmarks and ensuring stable patient positioning during image acquisition, thereby minimizing artifacts and variability. Our comparison of 3D surface scan breast volumes with water displacement measurements of mastectomy specimens demonstrated a strong correlation, validating our 3D scanning methodology and aligning with prior research by Yip et al.
A comparable technique was detailed by Tomita et al., utilizing 3D surface scanning in conjunction with a mold fabricated from acrylonitrile–butadiene–styrene copolymer (ABS). Both polylactic acid (PLA) and ABS are frequently employed 3D printing materials, exhibiting comparable density, tensile strength, and cost. PLA, owing to its lower printing temperature and proper cooling, demonstrates reduced warping and can produce finer details, down to 0.8 mm, making it preferable for aesthetically critical prints. Furthermore, PLA possesses inherent biocompatibility, unlike ABS, which requires surface modifications to achieve water impermeability, hydrophilicity, and biocompatibility for biomedical applications.
The designed mold incorporates holes, allowing for flap visibility during inset. This feature enables immediate detection of compromised blood flow by observing the flap's appearance through these openings. The random scattering of holes across the mold ensures that the entire flap can be accommodated, guiding the neo-breast to conform to the contralateral breast's mirrored image. The process of creating a mold involves 20 minutes for design, 10 minutes for slicing, 15 to 20 hours for printing, and approximately 60 minutes for support removal. The total cost for each mold ranges from 15 to 20 USD.
Our study's limitations include a relatively small sample size, which restricts the range of breast sizes and shapes examined. Defining breast borders during preoperative planning can be challenging for patients with a high body mass index (BMI), necessitating careful palpation. Scanning larger breasts in a supine position could potentially improve accuracy by reducing ptosis. Regarding mastectomy specimen measurements, the actual excised tissue may deviate from the planned area. Lymph node tissue should be excluded from specimens to prevent interference with volumetric analysis. The increasing popularity of nipple-sparing and skin-sparing mastectomies, which have demonstrated comparable oncologic safety and complication rates to traditional methods, presents another consideration. The volume of the skin flap or nipple-areola complex, though limited, could affect water displacement measurements. To address these points, we plan to conduct subgroup analyses based on BMI, breast size, ptosis grade, and mastectomy techniques to refine our findings. While our results indicate that 3D surface imaging is comparable to MRI for breast volume estimation, the inability of 3D scanning to detect chest wall contours is a limitation. Combining 3D surface scanning with MRI could potentially overcome this. Future research aims to develop a novel modality integrating both techniques. For most situations, 3D surface imaging is recommended for breast volume estimation, while MRI may be more suitable for cases involving chest wall deformities. Long-term follow-up, aesthetic outcome evaluations, and BREAST-Q questionnaires will be completed.
3D surface imaging offers a quick, effective, and convenient way to assess breast shape and volume. Our study found its accuracy, reproducibility, and reliability comparable to MRI. Furthermore, 3D-printed molds can enhance symmetry and aesthetic results in immediate autologous breast reconstruction.
