Electroshock Weapons (ESWs) are widely employed by law enforcement and military personnel globally as an escalation of force tool. These devices deliver a high-voltage, low-current electrical shock in a pulse burst designed to temporarily incapacitate a target, typically a human, for several seconds post-discharge. Ensuring the proper operation of ESWs is critical due to significant safety implications of malfunction. However, accurately measuring ESW output presents considerable challenges. The transient pulse outputs can exhibit frequency content exceeding 100 MHz while lasting over 10 seconds. Furthermore, the electrical impedance of a target can fluctuate between different targets and even within a single pulse burst for a given target. These factors make high-fidelity, reproducible measurements of ESW transient signals exceptionally difficult. Consequently, specialized measurement instruments are necessary to capture the required bandwidth, duration, and amplitude of these signals. Such accurate measurement capabilities are also vital for developing models and advancing the understanding of ESW exposure's physiological effects.

Electroshock weapons (ESWs) produce a pulse train burst, a series of numerous, nearly identical electrical pulses. This burst can last from under one second to over ten seconds. Individual pulses within the burst exhibit rapid transitions, varying from nanoseconds to microseconds in duration. These pulses can be bipolar and may display deviations, which are influenced by the electrical load connected to the ESW.
To accurately and reproducibly characterize Electroshock Weapon (ESW) output, waveform parameters must be extracted from the pulse train. Existing commercial instruments lack the necessary detail and duration capture capabilities for ESW current and high-voltage outputs. This paper details how measurement system limitations impact waveform fidelity and outlines minimum acceptable system performance. While a specialized measurement system has been developed, the presented information aims to encourage the creation of suitable systems for metrology and calibration labs, benefiting medical research and ESW developers.

Measuring an ESW's electrical output necessitates connecting it to a terminal electrical load that emulates its typical use. This testing requires both current and high-voltage (HV) measurements, thus mandating the use of one current transducer and one HV transducer.
The high-voltage (HV) transducer, connected in parallel to the load resistor, offers high input impedance and isolates the ESW's HV output from the waveform recorder's low-voltage input. Similarly, the current transducer isolates and senses current through the load, producing a proportional low-voltage output.
To accurately test electroshock weapons (ESWs), they must be connected to electrical loads that mimic those of a target. The ESW's output can vary depending on this load. Researchers have used materials like carbon-black loaded fluoropolymer, which matches the conductivity of human muscle tissue, and saline solutions to simulate typical target loads. These emulators help ensure the ESW's performance is assessed under relevant conditions.
Using high-voltage-rated resistors offers improved measurement reproducibility and simpler electrical connections compared to phantoms or saline solutions. While some ESW manufacturers assert their devices can dynamically adjust pulse output to maintain constant energy delivery by sensing target load changes, this is not universally true. Experimental evidence indicates that high-voltage exposure can alter impedance. For instance, one ESW model, as depicted in Figure 3, demonstrated an inability to adapt its output in real-time when its load was abruptly shifted from 400 Ω to 600 Ω between pulses.
Key ESW performance metrics include peak voltage/current amplitudes, total and partial charge (net, positive, negative), pulse duration, repetition rate, pulse count, and overall burst duration. Accurately measuring these parameters is compromised by noise, sampling rate limitations, and the measurement system's attenuation bandwidth. Furthermore, temporal and charge-related metrics are influenced by user-selected amplitude reference levels and the defined start and end points for calculations.
ESW electrical output is described using terms standardized by IEC 62792 and IEC 62754 (IEEE Std. 181). Measurements are single-shot events, and averaging successive events is often not feasible or recommended due to signal repeatability issues.
Pulse duration (TP) is calculated as the difference between two reference level instants: one for the initial pulse transition (t1,x%) and one for the second (t2,x%). This calculation, along with pulse separation and period, relies on these instants. Ultimately, TP's accuracy is influenced by user-defined reference levels, sampling rate, noise, and the measurement system's bandwidth.
The charge delivered to a load is a critical parameter for characterizing electroshock weapons (ESWs). This charge can be calculated as either the net charge, derived from the average of the entire current waveform, or as positive or negative charge, by averaging over specific user-defined portions of the waveform. The average value of a pulse burst is determined by averaging the waveform samples within each pulse sub-epoch. For a single sub-epoch, this average is calculated by summing all the waveform samples and dividing by the total number of samples. Subsequently, the net charge is computed by multiplying this average value by the duration of the waveform epoch or the user-defined summation interval. The accuracy of these charge calculations is influenced by the measurement process and the variables affecting both the waveform samples and the summation interval.
The net charge (Qnet) calculation is influenced by the user-defined duration of the waveform epoch or summation interval (T). Variables in the measurement and computation processes impacting yi and T directly affect Qnet. Consequently, user-defined epochs alter Qnet. Additionally, industry practice of setting amplitude thresholds to exclude yi from summation also impacts Qnet. Ultimately, Qnet's value depends on user-defined reference levels and thresholds, system attenuation bandwidth, noise, and sampling rate.

Insufficient sampling rates in ESW measurements can lead to the loss of critical output waveform details. This deficiency can compromise parameters reliant on precise temporal information. As illustrated in Figure 5, the sampling rate, or the number of waveform samples, directly impacts measured pulse duration, peak amplitude, and the summation of positive waveform values. Instrument and signal noise significantly impact ESW measurements of peak amplitude and pulse duration. As these are single-shot events, signal averaging is not possible for noise reduction. Noise introduces errors in peak amplitude, reference levels, and waveform instants, all crucial for parameter calculations.
Waveform parameter calculations for ESW outputs are influenced by user-defined settings. Specifically, the percentage reference levels and reference instants, as outlined in IEC 62792, directly impact the computed parameters. For instance, Figure 6 illustrates that raising the reference level, also known as the amplitude summation threshold, leads to a reduction in the sum of positive waveform values. This threshold is expressed as a percentage of the peak amplitude. Small increases in this threshold result in the most significant changes to the positive sum, particularly for waveforms with higher relative bandwidths. The sensitivity to summation threshold adjustments varies among different waveform profiles. Similarly, reference instants, which establish the summation interval, exert comparable influences.
Capturing the high-bandwidth, high-voltage pulse bursts from electroshock weapons, which can last several seconds, presents a unique measurement challenge. This complexity limits available measurement system choices for achieving waveform fidelity. Furthermore, insufficient measurement systems and ambiguous user-defined references can substantially diminish the accuracy of derived waveform parameters.
