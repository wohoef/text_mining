The exact cause of primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS), a systemic autoimmune disorder marked by dry eyes, dry mouth, and other systemic issues, is not fully understood. However, prior research indicates that apoptosis is a significant factor in pSS. This study aimed to explore the expression and impact of second mitochondria-derived activator of caspase (Smac) in pSS patients, linking its expression to clinical and pathological markers. Our investigation involved analyzing Smac expression in labial salivary gland samples from pSS patients using immunohistochemistry, RT-qPCR, and Western blot. We also quantified Smac levels in both the cytoplasm and mitochondria. Our findings revealed a significant increase in Smac mRNA, cytoplasmic Smac, and overall Smac protein in pSS patients compared to healthy controls (p < 0.05). Conversely, mitochondrial Smac levels were significantly lower in pSS patients (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the integral optical density of Smac protein showed a positive correlation with IgG (r = 0.7435, p < 0.05) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (r = 0.7925, p < 0.05). These results suggest a crucial role for Smac in the progression of pSS.

Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) is a prevalent systemic autoimmune condition, affecting around 0.5% of the general population. This disease manifests as either primary (pSS) or secondary (sSS) forms, with sSS occurring alongside other autoimmune disorders like rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. pSS, with a reported prevalence between 0.1% and 4.8%, involves the gradual lymphocytic infiltration and damage of exocrine glands, particularly the lachrymal and salivary glands. Additionally, pSS can present with extraglandular symptoms such as arthralgia, pulmonary issues, and renal tubular acidosis.
The precise mechanisms underlying pSS are complex, involving multiple contributing elements. Environmental factors like viral infections, hormonal fluctuations, and genetic predispositions are thought to play a role. Apoptosis, a process crucial for various biological functions including immunity and development, has been extensively investigated in pSS. While the endogenous apoptotic pathway, mediated by Fas/FasL interactions, has been observed in the salivary glands of pSS patients, the involvement of the exogenous apoptotic pathway in this condition remains to be fully elucidated.
Smac, a 29 kDa precursor protein found in mitochondria, undergoes proteolytic processing to become a 23 kDa mature protein. Following an apoptotic trigger, this mature protein is released from the mitochondrial intermembrane space into the cytosol. Functioning as a dimer, Smac facilitates caspase activation by binding to and sequestering inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs). Despite its known role, the specific alterations in Smac expression within pSS patients have not been thoroughly investigated. This study aimed to explore Smac expression in the labial salivary glands of pSS patients and healthy controls to understand its potential involvement in pSS development.
The School of Stomatology, China Medical University's ethics committee approved human tissue experiments, and written informed consent was secured from all patients contributing specimens.
The study enrolled 40 patients diagnosed with primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) based on the American-European Consensus Group Criteria, excluding those with other rheumatic conditions, infections, or malignancies. These participants, comprising 27 females and 13 males, had an average age of 43.67 ± 1.38 years and were recruited from the Department of Emergency and Oral Medicine at China Medical University between December 2015 and December 2016. A control group of 30 individuals (23 females, 7 males), with a mean age of 42.14 ± 0.92 years, was also included. Their specimens originated from lip trauma cases, and serologic tests confirmed the absence of autoimmune diseases. Detailed demographic information for all participants is presented in Table 1.
Following local anesthesia, a 0.6 cm x 0.6 cm section of lower lip labial gland tissue was excised from each participant. This fresh tissue was then divided: one portion was fixed in 4% formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, while the other was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -70°C.
Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted according to manufacturer guidelines. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by immersion in citrate buffer and treatment with 3% H2O2. Subsequently, sections were incubated with 5% goat serum before an overnight incubation at 4°C with a 1:1000 dilution of the primary anti-Smac antibody (Abcam). A biotinylated secondary antibody, part of the DAKO Real EnVision Kit (DAKO, Hamburg, Germany), was then applied, and sections were incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes. Staining was performed using 3,3´-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Maixin Biotech, Fuzhou, China), and counterstaining with commercial hematoxylin (Maixin Biotech) preceded microscopic examination. For semi-quantitative assessment of Smac protein levels in labial salivary gland samples from pSS patients and healthy controls, five random visual fields were analyzed at 400x magnification, with positive signal expression quantified by integral optical density (IOD) using Image-proplus software.
To assess Smac mRNA expression, total RNA was first isolated from tissue samples using Trizol kits (Sangon Biotech Corporation, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer's guidelines. The extracted RNA's quality and quantity were then verified with a UV spectrophotometer, and its concentration was standardized to 1 μg/μL for subsequent reverse transcription. For this process, RNA was converted into cDNA utilizing a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT kit (Toyobo Corporation, Osaka, Japan). The reverse transcription reaction mixture comprised 1 μg of RNA, 4 μL of 5× PrimeScript RT Master Mix, and RNase Free dH2O to achieve a total volume of 20 μL. This reaction proceeded at 37°C for 15 minutes, followed by 85°C for 5 minutes. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was subsequently conducted using a Light Cycler TaqMan Master kit (Toyobo Corporation), adhering to the provided instructions. The qPCR reaction setup included 10 μL of SYBR Premix Ex Taq II, 1 μL of cDNA, 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers, and 8 μL of sterile water. The thermal cycling protocol involved an initial 30-second denaturation at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles, each consisting of denaturation at 95°C for 5 seconds, annealing at 60°C for 30 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 2 minutes. The relative Smac expression levels were determined using the ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH gene expression serving as the normalization control for mRNA expression.
Western blot analysis commenced after protein concentrations were established using the bicinchoninic acid method. Fifty micrograms of each protein sample underwent 10% SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, then incubated with anti-Smac and anti-GAPDH primary antibodies (Abcam), and subsequently with secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Protein visualization was achieved using Thermo Pierce ECL (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Mitochondria were isolated from labial gland tissues following Zydowo's methods. After rinsing with cold saline, tissues were weighed, then homogenized in a 10% solution containing 0.1 mol/L Tris-HCl, 1 mol/L KCl, and 0.25 mol/L sucrose (pH 7.4). The homogenate underwent two centrifugation steps at low temperature: first at 600g for 15 minutes, then the supernatant was centrifuged again at 1800g. The resulting precipitate was resuspended in cold slurry medium, creating a mitochondrial suspension stored at -20°C.
Smac concentration was determined by first preparing a standard curve. This involved diluting 1 mL of an 80 mg/mL Smac standard, adding sodium sulfate while vibrating, and then measuring the sodium sulfate density at 520 nm. The standard's concentration was plotted against optical density, and this curve was used to calculate sample concentrations.
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS17.0 software, with a p-value below 0.05 indicating significance. The Mann–Whitney rank sum test compared Smac mRNA and protein levels between pSS patients and healthy controls. Furthermore, univariate analysis explored correlations between Smac protein's integral optical density (IOD) and various clinicopathological parameters.
Immunohistochemistry was employed to assess Smac expression in 70 labial gland samples, comprising 40 pSS cases and 30 healthy controls. All pSS samples showed clear Smac expression, predominantly in the cytoplasm of acinar and ductal epithelial cells, and within focal lymphocytic infiltrations, rather than the nucleus. A significant increase in Smac levels was observed in pSS tissues compared to control samples.
To assess Smac's expression, its mRNA levels were quantified using qPCR after total mRNA isolation. Figure 2A illustrates a significant 2.11-fold elevation in relative Smac mRNA expression in pSS patients compared to healthy controls (p < 0.05). Furthermore, Western blot analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed a statistically significant increase in Smac protein expression in pSS cases when contrasted with healthy controls (p < 0.05).
Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between groups. In pSS patients, cytoplasmic Smac content was 7.21 ± 0.22, notably higher than the control group's 1.62 ± 0.85. Conversely, mitochondrial Smac in pSS was 2.36 ± 1.32, significantly lower than the control group's 6.67 ± 1.79 (Fig. 3B).
This study also investigated how Smac expression relates to various clinical and pathological indicators in pSS patients, including SSA, SSB, ANA, ESR, CRP, RF, and IgG levels, using univariate analysis (Table 2). A significant positive correlation was identified between the integral optical density (IOD) of Smac protein levels and both ESR (r = 0.7435, p < 0.05) and IgG (r = 0.7925, p < 0.05) (Figs. 4A and 4B). No associations were observed with other factors. Furthermore, when patients were categorized by normal or abnormal test results, elevated Smac protein IOD levels were notably present in groups exhibiting high ANA titers (1.13 ± 0.45 vs 6.94 ± 0.43, p = 0.0025) or high RF concentrations (0.97 ± 1.16 vs 6.31 ± 2.54, p = 0.0012), as detailed in Table 2.
Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a chronic autoimmune condition, characterized by its complexity and diverse manifestations. It primarily involves localized lymphocytic inflammation of exocrine glands, which progressively impairs their secretory function. The prevalence of pSS varies, with estimates ranging from 0.2% to 2.7%, influenced by different study methodologies. The disease exhibits a significant female predisposition, with ratios from 9:1 to 20:1. Onset typically occurs between the fourth and fifth decades of life, while juvenile forms are exceedingly uncommon. Despite these observations, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying pSS remain largely undefined. Recent research has increasingly focused on the role of apoptosis. Studies have shown that endoplasmic reticulum stress is activated in minor salivary gland epithelial cells of pSS patients, leading to the relocation of Ro/SSA and La/SSB autoantigens to the cell surface and apoptotic blebs. Furthermore, efferocytosis is often compromised in pSS, mainly due to inhibitory IgG anti-ApoCell antibodies and impaired phagocyte function. Interestingly, while poly I:C can induce apoptosis in salivary gland epithelial cells in vitro, the in vivo prevalence of apoptosis in labial salivary gland ducts and alveoli appears to be relatively low.
The protein Smac, also referred to as a direct IAP-binding protein, acts as a natural inhibitor of IAP family proteins like XIAP, cIAP1, and cIAP2, which typically block apoptosis by binding caspases. Wild-type Smac (residues 1 to 184) forms a dimer in solution and engages with XIAP's BIR2 and BIR3 domains or its linker region. This competitive interaction between Smac and XIAP effectively reverses caspase inhibition.
Our investigation revealed a statistically significant increase in Smac mRNA expression within the labial glands of pSS patients compared to healthy controls (p < 0.05). Interestingly, while cytoplasmic Smac levels were elevated in pSS patients, mitochondrial Smac content was significantly lower than in the control group (p < 0.05). This suggests that under normal conditions, Smac protein resides primarily in the mitochondria with minimal cytoplasmic presence. However, upon receiving an apoptotic signal, the Smac protein's N-terminal signal peptide is cleaved, leading to its translocation from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm, where it plays a crucial role in promoting cellular apoptosis. This mechanism explains the observed shift in Smac distribution in pSS patients.
Our research indicates a strong link between Smac levels and the progression and severity of pSS in patients. Specifically, we observed a positive correlation between Smac protein levels and both ESR and IgG. When patients were categorized by normal or abnormal test results, those with abnormal ANA, RF, A-SSB, and IgA displayed significantly elevated Smac protein levels. Notably, higher integral optical density (IOD) of Smac protein was found in groups with abnormal parameters, with the exception of the anti-SSB group. This exception might be due to anti-SSB's role in inhibiting apoptosis. These findings offer new evidence suggesting that excessive Smac activity contributes to the disease's underlying mechanisms.
Several limitations in this study warrant consideration. The relatively small sample size could diminish the statistical power and the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the precise underlying mechanisms were not fully elucidated. Consequently, future research involving larger cohorts, alongside prospective animal studies incorporating histopathological and clinical correlations, would be beneficial to further investigate Smac's role in pSS.
Our research strongly indicates Smac's significant involvement in pSS development.
