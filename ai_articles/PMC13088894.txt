This report critically evaluates the 2015 Echinococcus multilocularis surveillance efforts in Finland, Ireland, Malta, the United Kingdom, and Norway, as detailed in their 2016 reports. The assessment focuses on the sampling strategies, collected data, and detection methods employed, all within the framework of Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011, which governs preventive health measures for E. multilocularis in dogs. The primary goal of this surveillance was to identify the parasite's presence, if any, within these nations. To achieve this, the 2015 surveillance reports from the four Member States and Norway underwent a thorough review. This involved scrutinizing the completeness of their surveillance system descriptions against established quality criteria for E. multilocularis reporting. Furthermore, the raw data from individual samples, submitted by each country through the EFSA Data Collection Framework (DCF), were analyzed. Significantly, none of the participating countries—Finland, Ireland, Malta, the UK, or Norway—reported any positive E. multilocularis samples in 2015. Statistical analyses were conducted to confirm adherence to legal requirements. Assuming unbiased and representative sampling, and accounting for the sensitivity of the diagnostic tests used, all five nations successfully implemented surveillance activities. These activities were capable of detecting E. multilocularis at a maximum prevalence of 1% with a 95% confidence level, thereby satisfying the stipulations of Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011.

Alveolar echinococcosis (AE), a severe zoonotic disease, originates from the larval stage of the fox tapeworm, *Echinococcus multilocularis* (EM). In natural environments, this parasite cycles between definitive hosts like foxes or dogs and small mammals. Humans, however, serve as atypical intermediate hosts. Within rodents, the larval mass grows quickly through external budding of germinative tissue, forming microvesicles filled with protoscolices in an alveolar pattern. In humans, this larval mass exhibits malignant-like characteristics, growing indefinitely by external budding and invading nearby tissues (Moro and Schantz, 2008). People contract AE by ingesting parasite eggs, which are shed in the feces of infected foxes and dogs. This can occur either directly through contact with definitive hosts or indirectly via contaminated food or water (Torgerson et al., 2010).
Before the 1990s, Echinococcus multilocularis (EM) was primarily recognized as endemic in a confined "core" region encompassing eastern France, southern Germany, and portions of Switzerland and Austria. However, since 1980, EM infections in animals have been documented in 17 central-eastern European countries, areas previously considered free of the parasite. Throughout the 1990s and beyond, both the prevalence of EM-infected animals and the density of host species saw an increase in regions like the Baltic areas, Denmark, the Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Slovakia, and Slovenia. The geographical spread of EM is uneven, exhibiting varying prevalence rates. Some areas, such as Denmark, northeast Germany, and Sweden, show prevalence near 0%, while others, including parts of France, southern Germany, and Switzerland, report rates approaching 50%. These variations in EM prevalence among foxes are often associated with landscape characteristics, which impact the distribution and abundance of rodent intermediate hosts, as well as microclimatic conditions crucial for the parasite's transmission and establishment.
Finland, Ireland, Malta, the UK, and Norway have never reported E. multilocularis infection in native wild hosts or as autochthonous human cases.
The European Commission and EFTA Surveillance Authority tasked EFSA to evaluate the sampling, data, and detection methods employed by Member States and Norway. This assessment concerned their *E. multilocularis* surveillance programs, specifically within the framework of Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011 on canine infection control.
Member States seeking inclusion in Annex I of Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011 must meet specific prerequisites. For a decade preceding their application, they must have enforced national laws mandating the compulsory notification of *E. multilocularis* infection in host animals. Additionally, an early detection system for this infection in animals must have been operational. These nations are also required to establish and execute a pathogen-specific surveillance program, adhering to the guidelines in Annex II. The findings from this program must be submitted to the Commission by May 31st after each 12-month surveillance cycle. The primary goal of this surveillance is to confirm the absence of *E. multilocularis* infection within these listed Member States. Notably, Norway's entire territory was added to the list of compliant countries on June 15, 2012, through Decision No 103/2012 of the EEA Joint Committee.
Annex II of Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011 outlines the mandatory elements for pathogen-specific surveillance programs. These programs must be structured to identify Echinococcus multilocularis with a maximum prevalence of 1% and a confidence level of at least 95% within each relevant geographical unit of a Member State. To achieve this, the surveillance must employ suitable sampling methods, either risk-based or representative, ensuring the parasite's detection across the Member State at the specified design prevalence. The program mandates continuous sample collection over a 12-month surveillance period. Samples should primarily come from wild definitive hosts. However, if there's proof that wild definitive hosts are absent in a particular area, domestic definitive hosts can be sampled instead. These samples are then analyzed using specific detection techniques. One method involves examining intestinal contents for E. multilocularis using the sedimentation and counting technique (SCT), or another method with comparable sensitivity and specificity. Alternatively, faeces can be analyzed for species-specific E. multilocularis DNA from tissue or eggs via polymerase chain reaction (PCR), or an equivalent technique.
Since January 1, 2012, Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011 mandates countries in Annex I to submit national surveillance reports annually by May 31st of the following year.
To safeguard Finland, Ireland, Malta, and the United Kingdom, which asserted freedom from E. multilocularis due to national regulations until December 31, 2011, the Commission implemented Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011 on July 14, 2011. This regulation established preventative health measures for controlling E. multilocularis in dogs. Subsequently, on June 15, 2012, Norway's entire territory was included in the list of compliant countries through Decision No 103/2012 of the EEA Joint Committee, aligning with Article 3 of the Regulation.
Member States and Norway must conduct a pathogen-specific surveillance program under this Regulation. The program aims to detect the parasite, if present, and has specific requirements for sampling, detection methods, and reporting.
The Commission must review this Regulation by December 2016, five years after its implementation. This review, considering scientific advancements in animal E. multilocularis infection, will then be presented to the European Parliament and the Council.

Finland's E. multilocularis surveillance employed a PCR method to analyze raccoon dog and red fox rectal samples for parasite eggs. This PCR technique, detailed by Isaksson et al. (2014) with a manual wash step modification, had an estimated sensitivity of 0.78. This sensitivity was determined by testing laboratory-prepared positive controls: 131 spiked specimens, each containing five inactivated E. multilocularis eggs in 3 mL of intestinal content, were examined in 2014. Of these, 78% (102 samples) tested positive via PCR. Statistical analysis using an exact binomial test suggested the true sensitivity could range from 0.70 to 0.85 (95% confidence level), a finding corroborated by a Bayesian approach. For a conservative estimate of the overall system sensitivity in the EFSA assessment, the lower bound of this credible interval (0.70) was adopted as a worst-case scenario.
Finland's surveillance system focused on raccoon dogs and red foxes as the primary wild definitive hosts. This selection was based on established scientific understanding: red foxes are recognized as the main European hosts for *E. multilocularis* (Deplazes, 2006), and raccoon dogs are also effective definitive hosts (Kapel et al., 2006). The EFSA's scientific opinion on *E. multilocularis* infection in animals (2015) further supports this choice. In Finland, raccoon dogs outnumber red foxes, with estimated populations of 230,000 and 150,000 respectively. These population figures, derived from a 2007 study by Kauhala, utilized various data sources such as radio tracking, hunting statistics, snow-track counts, and reproductive potential. While these estimates are scientifically sound, particularly given their minimal impact on sample size calculations, it's worth considering whether these 2007 population levels remain accurate or if an update is needed.
Kauhala's population estimates were deemed scientifically sound. The large population size, approaching infinite, meant that minor fluctuations would not significantly impact the sample size calculations for the surveillance program.
The target population's age and gender structures were not documented, though these factors seem irrelevant to EM's epidemiology or lifecycle. Surveillance focused on individual animals as the epidemiological unit.
Finland determined its sample size using the RiBESS tool, adhering to Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011's 1% design prevalence and a diagnostic sensitivity of 0.78. Voluntary hunters gather these samples, with information disseminated through Evira's website, emails, and direct communication with the Finnish Wildlife Agency, which then contacts local hunting groups.
In 2015, sampling efforts for *E. multilocularis* surveillance in Finland showed a geographical imbalance. Nearly half (48.6%) of the samples were collected from southeast Finland, a region with a long-standing rabies control program since 1990. This area is also considered at higher risk for *E. multilocularis* introduction due to its proximity to infected regions further south and its high density of raccoon dogs. Additionally, a significant number of fox samples came from Lapland, where red fox population control was underway to protect arctic foxes. To improve geographical representation, particularly in the southern and western parts of the country where foxes and raccoon dogs are plentiful, EVIRA, through the Finnish Wildlife Agency, issued an informational letter to hunters. This initiative led to an increase in samples from these areas. Conversely, Central Finland saw a relatively low number of samples because hunters there were not specifically solicited.
The majority of samples (73%) were gathered within the initial four months of the year, concluding by April. This concentration was partly driven by the fact that 94% of the substantial sample from the Lappi district was collected between January and March. Additionally, sampling efforts in the southern region were most intensive early in the year. Once enough samples were analyzed, testing ceased, leaving some year-end submissions unexamined.
No Echinococcus multilocularis was detected in any of the 611 samples collected from 273 foxes and 338 raccoon dogs, as all PCR tests yielded negative results.

Ireland's surveillance for *E. multilocularis* focused on red foxes, as they are the sole known wild definitive host species present across the country. This selection aligns with Regulation (EU) 1152/2011, which mandates pathogen-specific surveillance using suitable wildlife hosts. Raccoon dogs are not found in Ireland. The red fox population is estimated at 150,000 to 200,000 individuals.
The red fox population in Ireland exhibits seasonal breeding, with cubs born in spring and reaching near-adult size by seven months, leading to age structure variations throughout the year. Scientific literature provides limited data on the gender distribution of this population.
The distribution of red foxes across Ireland is well-documented, with earlier studies by Hayden and Harrington (2000) and Marnell et al. (2009) providing foundational data. Dr. Tomás Murray of the National Biodiversity Data Centre further updated this information in a 2015 report, offering more recent insights into the Irish red fox population's spread. This aligns with findings from Great Britain and Northern Ireland concerning the presence of definitive wild hosts.

(2009). The sample size needed to achieve a statistical confidence of under 1% prevalence at a given test sensitivity remains largely unaffected by minor population fluctuations when the population exceeds 10,000 individuals (EFSA, 2014). Consequently, changes to the previously estimated population of 150,000 do not substantially impact the required sample size (EFSA, 2014). Age and gender of the animals are not considered significant factors in the epidemiology or life cycle of *Echinococcus multilocularis* (EM) (EFSA, 2015). The individual animal, specifically post-mortem faeces, constituted the defined epidemiological unit.
In Ireland, fox samples were acquired through two primary methods: culling for pest and predator management, and incidental trapping during wildlife disease control efforts. To ensure comprehensive coverage, each of Ireland's 16 Regional Veterinary Offices was tasked with collecting a specific number of wild foxes. This quota was determined by the area's size and the local fox population density, aligning with figures derived from the 'Red fox (Vulpes vulpes) Species Distribution Model'. The total number of samples analyzed exceeded the minimum required to achieve the desired survey sensitivity. Sampling intensity was carefully calibrated to mirror the nationwide distribution of foxes and further refined to account for regional variations in population density. Sample collection spanned ten months of the year, with a notable increase in culling activities during October, November, and December. This seasonal adjustment aimed to prevent the culling of adult female foxes during the period when their cubs are dependent. Research from an endemic urban area in Switzerland (Hofer et al., 2000) suggests that a higher prevalence of *E. multilocularis* in foxes during winter months means this winter-focused sampling should not negatively impact the survey's sensitivity.
Sample size was calculated using a 1% design prevalence (Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011) and a test sensitivity of 0.78.

Malta's E. multilocularis surveillance employed the Microscopy/PCR RNAsn U1 method for analyzing live animal faecal samples. The sensitivity of this analytical technique was reported as 94% by Mathis et al. (1996), based on comparisons with parasitological findings from small intestine examinations. This 94% figure was derived from a direct ratio: 33 positive samples identified by the method out of a total of 35 positive samples. However, a more precise binomial test suggests the true sensitivity could range from 81% to 99% (with a 95% confidence level), a finding corroborated by a Bayesian analysis. For EFSA's assessment, the lower bound of this range (81%) was conservatively adopted to determine the overall system sensitivity.
Malta's National Veterinary Laboratory used flotation and concentration to examine fecal samples for worm eggs. Taenia spp. eggs, identified microscopically, were preserved in 75% alcohol for subsequent PCR identification. However, the flotation method employed by the lab is neither accredited nor validated.
Multiplex-PCR analysis was performed in Rome, Italy, at the Istituto Superiore di Sanità's Department of Infectious, Parasitic and Immunomediated Diseases. This was done to identify Echinococcus granulosus, E. multilocularis, and Taenia spp. eggs in fecal samples previously found positive for Taenia spp. eggs.
Malta lacks wild definitive hosts for E. multilocularis, such as foxes or raccoon dogs. The only carnivore found there is the weasel (Mustela nivalis), but its population is very small, and it's not considered a primary host for the parasite. The likelihood of disease transmission via weasels is minimal due to their nocturnal and secretive habits. The International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources (IUCN) confirms the absence of red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) in Malta, including Gozo, through their global species distribution maps. Given this absence of wild hosts, domestic dogs are recognized as potential definitive hosts for maintaining the parasite's life cycle, possibly through contact with rodents. Consequently, this study focused on dog populations, identifying "Rural" and "Stray" dogs as key risk groups. The estimated dog population was categorized into Pets, Rural dogs, and Stray dogs for risk assessment purposes.
Maltese dog owners must microchip and register their animals with the authorities, as mandated by Legal Notice LN 199/2011.
The National Veterinary Information System, managed by the Veterinary Regulation Department, registers identified dogs. However, it lacks categorization for pet, rural, or hunting dogs, meaning no such classification exists within the system.
In 2015, Malta's national microchipping database recorded 57,477 registered dogs. Of these, 29,191 were female and 28,286 were male. The age breakdown showed 6,525 young dogs (under two years old) and 50,952 adult dogs (over two years old).
Malta's high population density means dogs are distributed fairly evenly across the islands.
Malta's surveillance program deemed a geographical distribution of its rural dog population unnecessary, given the uniform spread of rural areas. This estimation was corroborated by data from various NGOs in Malta, which provide free neutering and microchipping services to dogs belonging to beneficiaries, farmers, factory workers, and hunters. Additionally, the six dog sanctuaries across the Maltese islands provided estimates for stray dogs, categorized as non-pet animals in this surveillance.
For the purpose of risk assessment, Malta's dog population was categorized into three groups, totaling 60,000 animals. This included 53,500 pets, 4,500 rural dogs, and 2,000 stray dogs. The rural dog estimate, ranging from 3,500 to 4,000, was derived by assuming two dogs per farm across the country's 2,061 farms (comprising 100 pig, 289 bovine, and 1,672 sheep/goat farms). This figure was corroborated by data from NGOs providing free neutering and microchipping for various working dogs. Similarly, the stray dog population, identified as non-pet animals in the surveillance, was estimated based on annual collection numbers (1,000-2,000) from the six local sanctuaries. Each individual animal, specifically their faeces, constituted an epidemiological unit.
Malta determined its required sample size using Winepiscope 2.0, aiming for a 1% prevalence detection with 95% confidence in the at-risk population. This resulted in 335 samples, comprising 150 from stray dogs (unknown history) and 185 from rural dogs (known history).
Malta's surveillance program identified hunting and rural dogs as higher-risk categories, assigning a relative risk (RR) of 1.2 to them. Additionally, the unknown history of sanctuary dogs was considered a risk factor for sample stratification, implying potential exposure to infected or high-risk areas. However, the report notes a lack of supporting evidence for both these assumed risk factors.
Malta's sampling strategy for E. multilocularis involved two distinct approaches. Dedicated teams collected samples from stray dogs and those in sanctuaries. Separately, sampling teams conducting routine farm inspections for Brucella, TB, and animal welfare also gathered samples. While repeat sampling of individual dogs couldn't be entirely ruled out, officers prioritized farm dogs to minimize this occurrence.
Sampling for the study occurred across both Malta and Gozo. On Gozo, eight out of fourteen localities were sampled, specifically targeting the primary rural areas. One of these Gozo localities also hosts a dog pound, which serves as a collection point for stray dogs from the entire island. Malta determined its Area Sensitivity using the RiBESS tool, based on a risk-based sampling approach and a test sensitivity (Se) of 0.94. This test sensitivity value of 0.94, whether applied to risk-based or simple random sampling, yielded an Area Sensitivity exceeding 0.95. For Great Britain (GB), the surveillance for *E. multilocularis* involved a PCR Cest1-Cest2 NAD1 test on rectal content from post-mortem samples. This detection method, detailed in Mathis et al. (1996) and Dinkel et al. (1998), extracts DNA from helminth eggs. The process begins with concentrating eggs from faecal samples through sequential sieving and flotation in zinc chloride solution. Subsequently, DNA from taeniid eggs captured by a 20-micron sieve undergoes alkaline lysis, followed by nested PCR using *E. multilocularis*-specific primers targeting the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene. The test sensitivity was supported by two studies. Mathis et al. reported a sensitivity derived from 33 positive detections out of 35 total positive samples. An exact binomial test suggested this value could range from 0.81 to 0.99 (95% CL), a finding corroborated by a Bayesian analysis. Dinkel et al. similarly presented a sensitivity based on 181 positive detections from 241 total positive samples. For this, an exact binomial test indicated a range of 0.70 to 0.80 (95% CL), also consistent with a Bayesian approach. In the EFSA assessment, the lowest reported sensitivity (0.7 or 70%) was adopted as a conservative estimate for the overall system sensitivity.
The Agri-Food and Biosciences Institute (AFBI) in Northern Ireland employed the Sedimentation and Counting Technique (SCT) to identify *E. multilocularis* eggs in individual intestinal samples. While the sensitivity of this egg counting method can vary across laboratories, a 99% sensitivity (Se) was adopted, aligning with EFSA's recommendation based on Eckert's 2003 suggestion to account for potential human errors.
The reported sensitivity for the Sedimentation and Counting Technique (SCT), attributed to Eckert (2003), actually draws from an earlier study by Hofer et al. (2000). However, the primary objective of the Hofer et al. research was to determine prevalence within a specific population, not to establish the SCT's sensitivity. While that study indicated that no samples identified as negative by SCT were subsequently found positive using the intestinal scraping technique (IST), suggesting a near 100% sensitivity for SCT, this conclusion is problematic. There was no information provided regarding the true contamination status of the samples, nor were details about the IST technique itself available. Consequently, the only valid inference is that IST's sensitivity is not superior to SCT's. The notion of SCT possessing almost perfect sensitivity remains an unproven assumption. A more prudent approach would be to adopt the EFSA's recommended test sensitivity of 0.78.
In the UK, including both Great Britain and Northern Ireland, the red fox (Vulpes vulpes) stands as the sole wild definitive host for E. multilocularis. These fox populations are geographically isolated, preventing the introduction of other wild definitive hosts from continental Europe.
Wildlife experts in Great Britain have estimated the pre-breeding adult fox population at 240,000, a figure published in 2013. While annual fluctuations occur, the overall population is considered stable or slightly growing. Urban areas account for approximately 33,000 foxes, representing about 13% of the total. Fox density varies significantly, likely influenced by food availability. For instance, Scottish hill regions may have one breeding pair per 40 km², contrasting sharply with urban areas where densities can reach 30 foxes per square kilometer. Despite these variations in abundance, the red fox population in both Great Britain and the island of Ireland exhibits considerable intermixing. This is evidenced by the swift spread of sarcoptic mange and the absence of geographical barriers. The typical range for a red fox in open farmland in the UK is between 200 and 600 hectares (2–6 km²). Data from BTO survey squares (primarily rural) confirm that the total fox abundance has remained consistent over the past decade (Wright et al., 2014). Furthermore, urban fox distribution has expanded, with nearly all urban areas now hosting foxes (Scott et al., 2014). Detailed maps illustrating fox distribution and abundance, derived from NBN data, published density information, and public sightings in urban areas, are available (Figure 12).
Northern Ireland's fox population is estimated at 14,000, averaging one fox per square kilometer. This density reflects the extensive rural landscape compared to urban areas, as detailed in Figure 5.

Norway's E. multilocularis surveillance employs a DNA-fishing technique, known as PCR 12S rRNA, for detection. This method, detailed by Isaksson et al. (2014), begins with magnetic capture mtDNA extraction from samples, utilizing specific DNA-hybridization. Subsequently, real-time PCR (CO1rtPCR), as described by Øines et al. (2014), is performed. To enhance sensitivity and mitigate operator-induced errors, samples undergo duplicate analysis during the detection phase. This duplication also helps minimize false negatives that can occur with very low target DNA. The primers used are 'EMrtCO1F', 'EMrtCO1Rew', and 'Zen probe'. Any positive results are further confirmed through PCR/sequencing of NAD1 (Trachsel et al., 2007) and an additional independent real-time PCR (Taq PCR/12S rDNA real-time, Isaksson et al., 2014).
In Norway, the red fox is the primary wild host for E. multilocularis. While tiny populations of wolves and arctic foxes exist, and raccoon dogs are rarely seen, four wolf samples examined in 2015 for forensic purposes all tested negative for the parasite.
Across Norway, red foxes are found throughout the country, though their population density varies geographically in spring. Mountain regions are estimated to have about 1 red fox per 10 km², while forest/marsh areas have roughly 3 red foxes per 10 km². The highest densities, around 10 red foxes per 10 km², are found in urban and agricultural areas such as Akershus, Vestfold, and Østfold, according to Professor Olav Hjeljord. While comprehensive scientific studies on the Norwegian red fox population size are lacking, an updated estimate of 151,000 foxes (up from a previous 70,000) was used for the surveillance program. This figure, partly derived from hunting statistics and habitat distribution, was provided by Professor Emeritus Olav Hjeljord. Approximately 21,000 red foxes are hunted in Norway each year.
In 2015, Norway aimed for approximately 600 red fox samples. EpiTools, an AusVet Animal Health Services calculator, confirmed this sample size was adequate. It ensured detection of Echinococcus multilocularis at a maximum 1% prevalence with 95% confidence, using hypergeometric approximation for population size.
In Norway, red fox hunters played a crucial role in collecting samples for the E. multilocularis surveillance program. Recruitment efforts targeted hunters nationwide, initially drawing from the Norwegian Register of Hunters. Those who had participated previously were also invited for the 2015 sampling season. Furthermore, the Norwegian Veterinary Institute and the Norwegian Association for Hunters and Anglers utilized their websites to enlist additional participants. The primary method for obtaining red fox carcasses was through firearms, though some were acquired via traps or road accidents. Following the animal's death, hunters immediately collected fecal samples from the rectum. Each hunter then completed a standardized form, detailing the location and date of the kill, along with the fox's sex and estimated age. These samples were then sent to the laboratory in prepaid envelopes, where they were frozen at -80°C for a minimum of three days before analysis. This volunteer-based sampling approach was deemed the most practical and representative method for the national red fox population, given Norway's challenging geographical and climatic conditions.
Following the detection of *E. multilocularis* in red foxes in southern Sweden in late 2011, Norwegian authorities adjusted their surveillance strategy. Hunters in southeastern Norway, particularly those near the Swedish border, were urged to intensify hunting efforts and submit more samples. This proactive measure was taken due to the perceived heightened risk of the parasite's introduction into Norway via foxes, given the proximity of the infection (Uddevalla, Sweden, is only about 80 km from the Norwegian border). The precise threat posed by Swedish foxes remains difficult to evaluate, however, as their migration patterns and habitat use are not fully understood. Despite this increased focus on border regions, the overall representativeness of the Norwegian sampling remained uncompromised.
In 2015, 523 red fox samples were collected; all tested negative for E. multilocularis via PCR.
Throughout 2015, samples were gathered across all 19 Norwegian NUTS3 regions. The uneven spatial distribution of these samples is attributed to Norway's mountainous terrain, which leads to dispersed settlements and voluntary hunter participation near their residences.
Mainland Norway's surveillance achieved an area sensitivity exceeding 0.95, calculated via the RiBESS tool. This assumed a representative sample for the entire region, with a test sensitivity of 0.63.
The 2015 surveillance data successfully met Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011's requirements, achieving a minimum 95% confidence level against a 1% design prevalence.
No E. multilocularis was found in any of the samples examined. Finland, Ireland, Malta, the United Kingdom, and Norway successfully conducted surveillance activities in 2015, meeting the requirements of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011. These efforts were designed to detect E. multilocularis at a maximum prevalence of 1% with a 95% confidence level, assuming unbiased representative sampling and appropriate test sensitivities. However, achieving truly representative sampling in wildlife populations presents challenges. Simple random sampling is often impractical, and detailed information on red fox population distribution at a regional level is often limited (EFSA, 2015). Despite this, both the UK and Ireland provided data on red fox population density. Ireland specifically aimed to mitigate geographical sampling bias by distributing its tested samples according to red fox density within NUTS3 regions. While a risk-based sampling approach is legally permissible and theoretically sound, its practical application is complex. Relative risk values found in scientific literature may not be directly applicable or validated for specific countries and their populations. Furthermore, political boundaries do not always align with epidemiologically relevant units, as they do not impede the movement of E. multilocularis hosts in wildlife (EFSA, 2015). For instance, the absence of a physical barrier between Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland suggests that surveillance for the entire island's fox population would be epidemiologically more accurate and resource-efficient. Therefore, defining epidemiologically relevant units should be independent of political borders (EFSA, 2015). All five participating countries also took part in the EURLP 2015 ring trial to evaluate their laboratory's E. multilocularis detection capabilities.
To improve data collection and estimate wildlife population characteristics (like density, distribution, age, and gender), collaboration between epidemiologists and wildlife experts is crucial. Sampling strategies or composite sample composition should account for population density distribution.
To enhance detection, research into epidemiological and geographical risk factors is needed. This would allow for better-informed, risk-based sampling strategies within host subpopulations.
For countries lacking definitive wildlife hosts, like Malta, routine surveillance of domestic dogs for *E. multilocularis* absence is not scientifically warranted. While these nations don't require dog surveillance to prove EM-freedom, waiving mandatory treatment for incoming dogs presents a public health concern. This relates to the potential for human infection from contaminated dog feces.
To accurately determine the sensitivity of diagnostic tests, a study is needed. This research should involve collecting a diverse range of samples from endemic regions, representing all infection stages and intensities. The European Union Reference Laboratory for Parasites could coordinate this effort, which should adhere to the guidelines outlined in Chapter 1.1.5 of the OIE Terrestrial Manual (OIE, 2012).
For upcoming surveys, in the absence of more comprehensive data, it's advised to conservatively establish diagnostic sensitivity. This involves selecting the lowest reported value from scientific literature concerning tests used by countries under Regulation (EU) No 1152/2011, while excluding the bottom 20th percentile. Consequently, a 78% diagnostic sensitivity is recommended for future assessments (EFSA, 2015).
Alveolar echinococcosis (AE) is a severe human illness caused by the larval stage (metacestode) of *E. multilocularis*. This condition is marked by an invasive, tumor-like growth, typically originating in the liver, which can lead to high mortality rates. For statistical purposes, the European Union developed the Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics (NUTS), a geocode standard, to categorize administrative divisions of countries. This standard divides the EU's territory into three hierarchical levels (NUTS 1, 2, and 3), moving from larger to smaller territorial units. The EFSA Data Collection Framework (DCF) is a web-based interface provided by EFSA. It allows data providers to submit their files using common web browsers, offering automated feedback on structural and content errors, along with confirmation of successful submissions. The Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) is a diagnostic method that leverages the immunological principle of an antigen binding to its specific antibody. This enables the detection of minute quantities of antigens, such as proteins, peptides, hormones, or antibodies, within a fluid sample. It achieves this by using enzyme-labeled antibodies or antigens and a chromogenic substrate that reacts with the enzyme to identify the target molecules. The Odds Ratio (OR) quantifies the likelihood of an event occurring in one group compared to another. It determines the probability of an event given exposure to a specific factor by assessing the probability of exposure when the event is present. The Risk-based Estimate of System Sensitivity (RiBESS) tool is an Excel-based application created by EFSA. Its purpose is to calculate the necessary sample size to confirm the absence of a particular disease and/or to determine the survey's sensitivity (confidence) after samples have been collected. The Sedimentation and Counting Technique (SCT) is employed for the quantitative evaluation of *E. multilocularis* parasite load in foxes and other definitive hosts. This technique involves repeatedly washing and settling intestinal material, followed by microscopic examination of the resulting sediment under a stereomicroscope to detect the parasite.
