This study aimed to investigate the role of Interleukin 32 (IL-32) in thyroid cancer (TC) cell migration, particularly in the context of its interaction with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). IL-32, a pro-inflammatory cytokine with various isoforms, has been previously identified in differentiated TC cells. While its role as a potent inducer of cell migration in other cancers is established, its specific impact on TC cell migration remained unexplored. Given that TAMs are known to facilitate cancer cell migration, a central objective was to determine if the interplay between TC cells and TAMs leads to elevated IL-32 expression in TC cells, and consequently, influences their migratory behavior. To address these questions, TPC-1 cells were co-cultured with either TC-induced or naive macrophages. Subsequent transcriptome analysis of the TPC-1 cells was conducted, and their supernatants were utilized to stimulate other TPC-1 cells. To further assess the direct effects of IL-32 isoforms, IL-32β and IL-32γ were exogenously overexpressed in TPC-1 cells via transient transfection. Following this, an in vitro gap closure assay was performed to evaluate cell migration, and the expression levels of relevant migratory factors were measured using RT-qPCR. The findings revealed that TC-induced macrophages indeed stimulated IL-32 expression in TC cells. Specifically, TAM-derived TNFα was identified as the primary driver behind the increased expression of the IL-32β isoform in TC cells. Interestingly, despite the induction, overexpression of either IL-32β or IL-32γ did not enhance TC cell migration; instead, it led to increased cell death. Furthermore, IL-32β overexpression was linked to an elevated mRNA expression of the pro-survival cytokine IL-8, while other migratory factors showed no significant changes. In conclusion, the study established that TAM-derived TNFα promotes the expression of IL-32β in TC cells. Although IL-32β itself does not appear to influence TC cell migration, the alternative splicing of IL-32 towards the IL-32β isoform might contribute to TC cell survival by inducing the pro-survival cytokine IL-8.

First identified in 2005, Interleukin 32 (IL-32) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine. Alternative splicing of IL-32γ pre-mRNA has led to the discovery of nine distinct IL-32 isoforms. This cytokine is implicated in various health conditions, including inflammatory, cardiovascular, and oncological diseases. Within the realm of cancer, IL-32's function is complex and appears contradictory. Some studies suggest a tumor-suppressive role, noting that IL-32β and IL-32γ can hinder melanoma and colon cancer cell proliferation and stimulate anti-tumor immune responses in prostate and colon cancers. Conversely, other research indicates a pro-tumorigenic role for IL-32, particularly in promoting invasion and migration in lung, breast, gastric cancers, and osteosarcoma, though these effects haven't been tied to a specific isoform. Therefore, IL-32's impact varies depending on the specific cell and cancer type, as well as the particular isoform involved.
Research into IL-32's function in differentiated, non-medullary thyroid cancer (TC) is limited. Our prior work established the presence of IL-32 protein in TC, with mRNA analysis confirming both IL-32β and IL-32γ isoforms in TC tissues. A specific single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the IL-32 promoter, which enhances IL-32γ production in immune cells, has been linked to a higher risk of TC development and reduced responsiveness to standard treatments. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that expressing IL-32β and IL-32γ can trigger caspase-8 dependent cell death in two distinct TC cell lines: FTC-133 (follicular, PTEN-deficient) and BCPAP (papillary, BRAF V600E mutated).
While IL-32 has been implicated as a strong promoter of cell migration in various cancers, its impact on thyroid cancer (TC) cell migration and metastasis remains unexplored. Metastasis is a multifaceted process, influenced by elements within the tumor microenvironment (TME), such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which can facilitate this spread. Poorly differentiated TCs exhibit significant TAM infiltration in their TME, a factor linked to unfavorable prognoses. The precise mechanisms governing IL-32 production in TC are currently unknown, and its role in pro-carcinogenic activities is not well understood. TAMs are known to release substantial quantities of cytokines, including TNFα, when interacting with TC cells. Notably, TNFα is a primary inducer of IL-32. This led us to hypothesize that the presence of activated immune cells, specifically TAMs, within the TME could elevate IL-32 expression in TC cells. Such an increase might then enhance IL-32-driven tumor cell migration, thereby contributing to the metastatic cascade. Consequently, this study aimed to investigate if the interaction between TC cells and innate immune cells stimulates higher IL-32 expression in TC cells, and to determine if this heightened expression influences TC cell migration or viability in differentiated TCs.
The study initially examined the impact of TAM-TC cell interactions using an in vitro co-culture model with human monocytes and TPC-1 cells. Subsequently, to investigate IL-32's role in TC cell migration, TPC-1 cells were transfected with IL-32β and IL-32γ. Following transfection, cell migration was evaluated via an in vitro gap closure assay. Additionally, mRNA expression levels of migration-related factors, including IL-8, MMPs, E-cadherin, and VEGF, were analyzed.
In vitro co-culture experiments utilized TPC-1 cells, a papillary thyroid cancer cell line characterized by RET/PTC rearrangement [22]. These cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium, a Dutch modification from Life Technologies, enriched with 50 μg/ml gentamycin, 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM GlutamMAX, and 10% Fetal Calf Serum (Gibco). Human monocytes were sourced from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), which were isolated from buffy coats obtained from Sanquin Bloodbank, Nijmegen, via Ficoll-plaque density gradient centrifugation. Ethical oversight for these procedures was provided by CMO Arnhem-Nijmegen (CMO 2010–104), ensuring adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. For transcriptome analysis, an additional purification step involving CD14-labelled magnetic beads was employed to yield a highly pure monocyte population.
For co-culture experiments involving TC cells and human monocytes, a trans-well system incorporating ThinCert™ cell culture inserts within a 24-well plate (Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Austria) was employed. Initially, 1.0 × 10^5 TPC-1 cells, suspended in 250 μl of culture medium, were placed into the upper compartments of this system. Control upper compartments contained only medium. The lower compartments received culture medium, and the entire setup was incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following this, cells were rinsed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Braun Melsungen, Germany). Subsequently, a fresh co-culture medium was introduced, formulated with a physiological glucose concentration: RPMI-1640 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA) supplemented with 5 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, 50 μg/ml gentamicin, and 10 mM HEPES (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). Cell counts were determined using a Coulter particle counter (Beckman Coulter Inc. Pasadena, CA, USA).
After isolation, monocytes were prepared in co-culture medium. These monocytes, numbering 5.0 × 10^5 in 500 μl, were introduced into the lower compartments of the trans-well system. Following a 1-hour adhesion period in the 24-well plates, any non-adherent cells were removed. The remaining adherent monocytes then underwent a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C in co-culture medium, either alongside TPC-1 cells or with medium only. Subsequently, these adherent monocytes, now designated as naïve monocytes or TC-induced macrophages, were stimulated for an additional 24 hours. This stimulation involved either RPMI-1640 or 10 ng/ml LPS (E. coli strain O55:B5, Sigma Chemical Co, St. Louis, MO, USA), serving as a substitute for endogenous TLR4 ligand signaling, while the trans-wells containing medium or TPC-1 cells remained undisturbed. Upon completion of this incubation, TPC-1 cells were lysed using 200 μl Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and preserved at -80 °C for transcriptome analysis. Supernatants were also collected and stored at -80 °C for later use in conditioned medium experiments. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates this experimental setup.
Following RNA isolation using Trizol, 50 ng of RNA was transformed into cDNA libraries with the TruSeq RNA preparation kit. These libraries underwent cluster generation on a cBot, enabling a 75 bp single-read rapid run on an Illumina HiSeq 1500 system.
Following base calling and de-multiplexing with CASAVA version 1.8, the 75 bp single-end reads underwent alignment to the UCSC human reference transcriptome hg38, utilizing kallisto v0.44.0 with its default settings. Subsequently, this data was imported into DESeq2 (v.1.10.1) via the TXimport (v1.2.0) package. DESeq2 then calculated normalized counts for each transcript, employing its default parameters. After this normalization, transcripts with a maximum mean across all groups below 10 were filtered out, leaving 17,099 genes for analysis. To address confounding factors like batch and preparation date, the sva package [23] was used to remove unwanted variation. The normalized rlog transformed expression values were further refined by adjusting for two surrogate variables identified by sva, using the removeBatchEffect function from the limma package [24]. Genes were deemed differentially expressed if their absolute fold change exceeded 1.5 and their adjusted p-value (FDR) was less than 0.05. A volcano plot depicted log2 fold changes against –log10 adjusted p-values, highlighting the top 10 up- and down-regulated genes. Sashimi plots were created to visualize aligned reads using the Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV) [25]. Furthermore, principal component analysis was conducted on all identified genes. Comprehensive data from this study is accessible in the GEO database under accession number GSE120391.
To investigate if paracrine factors from the co-culture medium induced IL-32 mRNA expression in TPC-1 cells, fresh TPC-1 cells were exposed to supernatants from TPC-1-monocyte co-culture experiments for 24 hours. Given that TNFα is a significant inducer of IL-32 and is plentiful in the co-culture medium, TPC-1 cells were additionally stimulated with 100 ng/ml TNFα for 24 hours. For these experiments, 500,000 cells per well were plated in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere for 18 hours. The medium was then replaced with either a 1:1 mixture of fresh medium and co-culture supernatants, or fresh medium containing 100 ng/ml TNFα. To broaden the investigation to other thyroid cancer (TC) cell lines, BC-PAP (papillary TC, BRAF V600E mutation) and FTC-133 (follicular TC, PTEN deficient) were also included. The involvement of TNFα was further verified by incorporating specific TNFα inhibitors, etanercept (a decoy receptor) and adalimumab (a monoclonal antibody), at 10 µg/ml in certain experiments.
To study the effects of specific IL-32 isoforms, pcDNA3 vectors were engineered for the expression of IL-32β and a modified IL-32γ. These expression plasmids incorporated a Kozak sequence preceding the ATG start codon, followed by the full-length cDNA for human IL-32β, IL-32γ, or eGFP, as well as an empty DNA control. The IL-32γmutant was specifically designed to prevent its splicing into other IL-32 isoforms, allowing for isolated investigation of IL-32γ's impact. This was achieved by altering a donor splice site from GU to AU.
For experimental purposes, TPC-1 cells were prepared by seeding them at a density of about 40,000 cells per cm² in 10 mm dishes. Following a 24-hour incubation, the culture medium was replaced. Subsequently, these cells underwent transfection with pCDNA3 plasmids, which carried either IL-32β, IL-32γmutant, or empty DNA, alongside eGFP-expressing pCDNA3 plasmids, maintaining a 10:1 ratio. The transfection was performed using Fugene, adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were harvested with trypsin/EDTA, and viable cells were quantified using a Bürker-Türk counter and Tryphan blue staining before being re-seeded for subsequent studies.
To assess transfection efficiency, cells were reseeded post-transfection at a density of 200,000 cells. At 40 and 64 hours, these cells underwent trypsinization, centrifugation, and fixation in Fix and Perm buffer for 45 minutes. Following a wash in Perm buffer, an anti-Ki-67 monoclonal antibody (SolA15, Invitrogen) was used to stain the cells for 30 minutes in the dark at 4°C, serving as a proliferation marker. Subsequently, cells were washed again and resuspended in PBS with 1% BSA for flow cytometry analysis. A Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Inc.) was employed, with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm (FITC channel), to quantify GFP-expressing (transfected) cells by recording 100,000 events. Ki-67 positive cells were identified using 488 nm excitation and 610 nm emission (ECD-A channel). The proportion of positive cells was derived from a fluorescence intensity histogram, which correlates event count with fluorescence intensity.
Cell toxicity was evaluated using a Cytotox96 assay, following the manufacturer's specified protocol.
IL-32 protein expression was evaluated by Western blot analysis. TPC-1 cells were plated at 300,000 cells per well in 24-well plates. Following transfection, at 40 and 64 hours, cells were lysed using a buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The extracted proteins underwent polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on a pre-cast 4–15% gel. Subsequently, proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. To detect IL-32, the membrane was incubated overnight at room temperature with a goat-anti-IL-32 antibody (AF3040). Actin detection, using a rabbit anti-actin antibody, served as a loading control. Protein visualization was achieved using polyclonal secondary antibodies and a Clarity Western ECL Blotting Substrate.
To analyze mRNA expression, TPC-1 cells were prepared by lysing 200,000 cells per well in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and then storing them at -80 °C. Following the manufacturer's protocol, RNA was isolated and subsequently converted into cDNA using an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad) via reverse transcription. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was then performed on a CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad), utilizing a Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). To ensure data consistency, expression levels were normalized against the housekeeping genes human β2M or GAPDH. Primers, obtained from Biolegio, had their sequences detailed in Supplementary Table S1. The study focused on evaluating the mRNA expression of IL-32 isoforms α, β, and γ during various stimulation experiments. Post-transfection, the mRNA levels of migration-related factors, such as IL-8, MMP2, 3, and 9, E-cadherin, and VEGF, were also measured. IL-32α mRNA was specifically monitored due to its potential upregulation as a splicing product of IL-32β and IL-32γ expression.
To evaluate cell migration, an in vitro gap closure assay was performed. TPC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 28,000 cells per chamber into silicone inserts placed on culture plates, allowing them to form a confluent layer overnight. Following adhesion, the inserts were carefully removed, and any non-adherent cells were washed away with PBS. Subsequently, RPMI-1640 culture medium, with either 0% or 10% FCS, was introduced. The extent of gap closure was then measured at various intervals using standard light microscopy and quantified with the MRI wound healing tool in ImageJ.
Statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism 5. The Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparison test, Mann-Whitney test, or paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test were employed to evaluate differences in mRNA expression, GFP and Ki-67 positivity, cytotoxicity, and gap closure. A p-value less than 0.05 or 0.01 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as means ± SEM.
To understand the transcriptional changes occurring in TPC-1 cells when co-cultured with either inactivated (RPMI-1640 stimulated) or TLR-4 activated (LPS stimulated) TC-induced macrophages, a comprehensive analysis was undertaken. This involved generating genome-wide transcriptome data from TPC-1 cells using quantitative RNA-sequencing, followed by detailed bioinformatic analysis, as outlined in Figure 1a. The initial unbiased principle component analysis (PCA), which considered 17,099 transcripts (Figure 1b), clearly demonstrated significant alterations in gene expression within TPC-1 cells following co-culture with TLR-4 activated TC-induced macrophages. Further in-depth differential gene expression analysis identified a total of 128 genes exhibiting significant changes (Figure 1c), with 98 genes showing upregulation and 30 genes showing downregulation, based on a fold change (FC) of ≥ 1.5. Notably, Interleukin 32 (IL-32) was identified as one of the top ten most significantly upregulated genes, a finding visually represented in the Volcano Plot (Figure 1d). To pinpoint the specific isoform of IL-32 involved, Sashimi plots were employed (Figure 1e). These plots, which visualize splice junctions in TPC-1 cells co-cultured with TC-induced macrophages after re-stimulation with either RPMI-1640 (control) or the TLR-4 ligand (LPS), strongly suggested that IL-32β was the predominant splice variant. The first panel of Figure 1f, specifically addressing IL-32α, further indicated that co-culture of TPC-1 cells with TC-induced monocytes indeed led to increased IL-32 expression. This systematic approach allowed for a global assessment of transcriptional differences and the identification of key genes and isoforms affected by the interaction between TPC-1 cells and activated macrophages.
Paracrine factors released by activated TC-induced macrophages were confirmed to upregulate IL-32 expression in TPC-1 cells. This was observed when TPC-1 cells were stimulated with supernatants originating from TC-induced macrophages, naive monocytes, and TPC-1 cells alone (Fig. 1f). Specifically, supernatants from TLR-4 stimulated TC-induced macrophages led to significantly elevated mRNA expression of IL-32α, IL-32β, and IL-32γ in TPC-1 cells (p = 0.0156), in contrast to those from RPMI-stimulated macrophages. The highest induction was seen for IL-32β, with a fold change of 16.77 ± 6.17 from TLR-4 stimulated TC-induced macrophages, significantly greater than the 9.23 ± 5.55 observed with naive, TLR-4 stimulated monocytes (p = 0.0313).
Our previous work demonstrated that TC-induced macrophages secrete elevated levels of TNFα and IL-6 when compared to naive monocytes [21]. Given that TNFα is a prominent inducer of IL-32, we posited that the substantial concentrations of TNFα present in the co-culture supernatants might be responsible for the observed increase in IL-32 mRNA expression within TC cells. To investigate this, TPC-1 cells were exposed to TNFα (100 ng/ml). This stimulation resulted in a notable increase in the mRNA expression of IL-32α (p = 0.0079) and IL-32β (p = 0.0079), while the expression of IL-32γ remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2a). To broaden our understanding, we also examined whether TNFα could elicit a similar IL-32 induction in other TC cell lines. Specifically, BC-PAP cells (a papillary TC line with a BRAF V600E mutation) and FTC-133 cells (a follicular TC line deficient in PTEN) were stimulated with TNFα. In both these cell lines, an upregulation of IL-32α and IL-32β mRNA expression was observed following TNFα stimulation, although the magnitude of this increase was less pronounced than in TPC-1 cells (Fig. 2a). These findings collectively suggest a role for TNFα in modulating IL-32 expression across different thyroid cancer cell types. Figure 2 provides a comprehensive overview of TNFα-mediated IL-32 mRNA and protein expression in these various TC cell lines.
TNFα's role in inducing IL-32 expression in thyroid cancer (TC) cells was confirmed at the protein level across three TC-derived cell lines via Western blot. TPC-1 cells exhibited the highest IL-32 levels, likely IL-32β given its 25.9 kDa size. Crucially, inhibiting TNFα with Humira or Enbrel prevented this IL-32 upregulation in TC cell lines, reinforcing TNFα's significance as an inducer of IL-32β mRNA and protein in TC.
Given that TPC-1 cells exhibited the highest levels of IL-32 mRNA and protein expression, the researchers proceeded to introduce exogenous IL-32β and IL-32γ into these cells. IL-32β was chosen for overexpression due to its prominent expression in thyroid cancer (TC). Additionally, IL-32γ was included in these experiments, recognizing its reputation as the most potent IL-32 isoform for various cellular functions [3]. To specifically isolate the effects of IL-32γ without interference from other splice variants, a modified IL-32γmutant vector was engineered, preventing its splicing into alternative IL-32 isoforms. The efficiency of this transfection process was evaluated by quantifying the percentage of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-positive cells at two distinct time points: 40 hours (marking the commencement of the gap closure assay) and 64 hours (signaling the conclusion of the assay). At the 40-hour mark, under serum-rich conditions (10% FCS), the average percentage of GFP-positive cells did not show significant variation across groups. However, it was observed to be lowest in the control transfected cells (46.2 ± 14.1%) and highest in the IL-32γmutant transfected cells (74.0 ± 9.2%) (as depicted in Fig. 3a). By 64 hours post-transfection, a marginal, non-significant increase in the mean percentage of GFP-positive cells was noted in both serum-rich (10% FCS) and serum-deprived (0% FCS) environments. Further analysis confirmed that transfection with either IL-32β or the IL-32γmutant successfully led to substantial protein expression of the respective IL-32 isoforms in TPC-1 cells at both measured time points (Fig. 3b illustrates results at 40 hours, with 64-hour data not shown). In contrast, control cells, transfected with an empty vector, displayed no detectable IL-32 protein expression. Interestingly, despite using the IL-32γmutant, both IL-32β and IL-32γ proteins were detected in the cells transfected with the 32γmutant.
We then evaluated the impact of IL-32 overexpression on cell toxicity. Transfection itself increased cell death across all experimental groups compared to untreated cells at 40 hours (p = 0.0006). By 64 hours post-transfection, the overall cytotoxicity attributed to the transfection process decreased. However, at this later time point, both IL-32β and IL-32γ overexpression significantly enhanced cytotoxicity, regardless of serum presence (p-values ranging from 0.0022 to 0.0411). Notably, IL-32γ overexpression led to the highest observed cell death in serum-rich conditions, reaching 25.5 ± 3.3% compared to 18.4 ± 0.5% in control-transfected cells.
The impact of external IL-32 overexpression on cellular proliferation was assessed by examining Ki-67 expression. All experimental conditions showed over 98% of cells positive for Ki-67, including all GFP-positive cells (Fig. 3d). While the average fluorescence intensity of Ki-67 was lower in cells overexpressing the IL-32γmutant compared to control or IL-32β transfected cells, this difference lacked statistical significance (Fig. 3e, f). Consequently, IL-32 overexpression did not alter the proportion of Ki-67-positive cells and had only a minor influence on Ki-67 intensity.
To evaluate the impact of exogenous IL-32β and IL-32γ overexpression on TC cell migration, an in vitro gap closure assay was performed. This assay, illustrated in Figure 4a, utilized IBIDI© inserts to establish a cell-free zone. The extent of gap closure was measured after 24 hours using the MRI wound healing tool in ImageJ. As anticipated, the highest rates of gap closure were observed under serum-rich conditions, which provide ample growth and migratory factors. However, when comparing control-transfected cells with those overexpressing either IL-32β or IL-32γ, no statistically significant differences in the percentage of gap closure were detected after 24 hours. This lack of significant difference was consistent across both serum-deprived (0% FCS) and serum-rich (10% FCS) environments. Figure 4b visually presents these findings, showing the percentage of the gap filled over the 24-hour period for IL-32β (n=5) and IL-32γ (n=3) overexpression. The experiments were conducted with 3 to 5 repetitions, each including 2 to 3 replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SEM.
Our investigation concluded with an analysis of mRNA expression levels for factors previously linked to IL-32 activity and cellular migration, specifically under serum-rich conditions. We observed a notable decrease in the mRNA expression of IL-32α, IL-8, and VEGF across all experimental conditions at 64 hours post-transfection, when compared to the levels recorded at 40 hours. Interestingly, IL-32β-transfected cells exhibited a significant upregulation of both IL-32α and IL-8 mRNA expression at the 64-hour mark, relative to control-transfected cells (p = 0.0003 and p = 0.0120, respectively). Furthermore, at 40 hours, the mRNA expression of IL-32α was significantly elevated in cells transfected with either IL-32β or IL-32γ compared to controls. In contrast, the overexpression of IL-32β and IL-32γ did not lead to any significant alterations in the mRNA expression of MMP2, MMP3, MMP9, E-cadherin, or VEGF at either time point when compared to control-transfected cells.
Our findings indicate that TNFα from tumor-associated macrophages significantly boosts IL-32 mRNA and protein levels in thyroid cancer (TC) cells, with IL-32β being the most prevalent isoform. While overexpressing IL-32β and IL-32γ in TC cells led to increased cell death, it did not alter the proliferation marker Ki-67. Notably, IL-32β overexpression specifically enhanced IL-8 mRNA expression, but neither IL-32β nor IL-32γ affected other cancer cell migration markers. Furthermore, in our in vitro model, neither IL-32β nor IL-32γ overexpression impacted TC cell migration.
A key discovery was the significant increase in IL-32 expression within TC cells following co-culture with TLR-4-activated, TC-induced macrophages. Specifically, the IL-32β isoform showed strong induction when TC cells were stimulated with supernatants from these activated macrophages. Given the high concentration of TNFα in supernatants from activated TC-induced monocytes and its similar effect on IL-32β induction in TC cells, it is plausible that TNFα acts as the primary paracrine agent driving IL-32β expression in these cells.
Our findings suggest that TNFα acts as a crucial regulator of IL-32 alternative splicing within thyroid cancer (TC) cells, primarily promoting the expression of IL-32β. This contrasts with its known role as a strong inducer of IL-32γ in human synovial fibroblasts. The shift towards IL-32β, and consequently increased IL-32α and reduced IL-32γ, appears to offer a protective mechanism for TC cells against cell death. Previous research has established IL-32γ as the most potent IL-32 isoform, with its predominant expression (achieved by blocking alternative splicing) leading to cell death in FTC-133 and BCPAP TC cell lines. Consistently, our study demonstrated that exogenously overexpressing IL-32γ significantly increased cytotoxicity in TPC-1 cells, reinforcing its potential to induce cell death in TC. Interestingly, while IL-32β overexpression in TPC-1 cells led to a modest reduction in cell death and a notable increase in the pro-survival cytokine IL-8 mRNA expression, IL-32γ did not exhibit this effect. The importance of IL-8 as a pro-survival factor is highlighted by its ability to counteract IL-32β-induced cell death when its signaling pathway is restored. Furthermore, IL-32γ overexpression slightly decreased Ki-67 expression, indicating a potential reduction in proliferation. These observations collectively support the idea that favoring IL-32β through alternative splicing could enhance the survival of TC cells.
Overexpression of IL-32β and IL-32γ in TPC-1 cells led to substantial protein levels of these specific isoforms. Unexpectedly, the overexpression of the IL-32γmutant also yielded IL-32β protein, despite the fact that the IL-32γmutant pre-mRNA is designed to be unspliced. This suggests that the IL-32γmutant protein might stimulate the transcription of the cell's own IL-32 mRNA, subsequently increasing IL-32β mRNA and protein levels at later stages. Consistent with this, IL-32α mRNA levels were lower in cells overexpressing the IL-32γmutant compared to those overexpressing IL-32β.
Our findings revealed that, contrary to our initial hypothesis, overexpressing IL-32β and IL-32γ did not influence TC cell migration in vitro. Furthermore, established factors linked to IL-32-mediated cell migration were not significantly altered in TC cells following IL-32β and IL-32γ overexpression. Notably, we observed a temporal decrease in IL-8 and VEGF mRNA expression, with significantly lower levels evident 64 hours post-transfection. This decline might be attributed to the continuous proliferation of TPC-1 cells, as evidenced by Ki-67 expression, leading to confluence and subsequent contact inhibition, which could induce a quiescent state and consequently lower IL-8 and VEGF expression.
This study primarily investigated IL-32's role in the interaction between tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and differentiated thyroid cancer (TC) cells. However, TAMs exert broader influence within the tumor microenvironment by releasing soluble factors like chemokines and cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNFα, which can affect other immune cells. These pro-inflammatory cytokines from TAMs in TC are known to stimulate adaptive immune responses. Furthermore, TNFα, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, has been linked to tumor dedifferentiation, leading to adaptive immune resistance. This suggests an indirect pathway through which TAMs can impact adaptive anti-tumor immunity in TC. Our findings showed that TAM-derived TNFα triggered IL-32 expression in TC cells. It's established that IL-32 expression in tumor cells influences adaptive immune responses in various other solid cancers. For instance, IL-32β has been shown in in vitro and animal models of colon and prostate cancer to enhance adaptive anti-tumor responses by boosting NK cell cytotoxicity, increasing T cell infiltration, and stimulating cytotoxic T cell activity. Given that these broader effects were outside the scope of our current research, further studies are needed to evaluate IL-32's impact on adaptive anti-tumor responses specifically in TC.
While previous research indicates a greater presence of TAMs in aggressive thyroid cancers like poorly differentiated and anaplastic TC, the functional interplay between TAMs and TC cells in other thyroid cancer subtypes remains largely unexplored. Our prior work demonstrated that co-culturing a follicular thyroid cancer cell line (FTC-133, PTEN-deficient) with TC-induced macrophages led to a more pronounced pro-inflammatory response compared to co-culture with a papillary TC cell line (TPC-1, RET/PTC rearrangement). This suggests that IL-32β induction might be more robust in follicular TC due to elevated levels of TAM-derived TNFα, implying a potentially greater role for IL-32β in this subtype. Further studies are essential to fully understand the functional interactions between TAMs and diverse TC subtypes, and to elucidate the impact of IL-32 in these other forms of thyroid cancer.
Our findings indicate that TNFα from tumor-associated macrophages primarily drives alternative splicing of IL-32 in thyroid cancer cells, leading to increased IL-32β expression. While IL-32β doesn't impact TC cell migration, this shift in splicing likely aids TC cell survival. This benefit stems from a reduction in IL-32γ-induced cell death and an increase in the pro-survival cytokine IL-8. Thus, targeting IL-32 alternative splicing could offer a new therapeutic approach for advanced TC.
