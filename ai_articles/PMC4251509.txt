This paper examines the material flow analysis (MFA) of two small, high-income island economies, Iceland and Trinidad and Tobago, over nearly fifty years. Both nations possess limited domestic resources, with their economies primarily driven by the export of one or two key commodities. Trinidad and Tobago's economy is heavily reliant on oil and natural gas extraction and associated petrochemical industries. Historically, Iceland's economy has centered on fisheries, but more recently, abundant hydropower and geothermal energy have fueled large, export-oriented metal processing industries. These industries, however, are entirely dependent on imported raw materials, though they utilize domestic renewable electricity. Both countries exhibit a significant reliance on their natural resources, making them susceptible to issues like overexploitation and price volatility. The study demonstrates how export-focused industries contribute to escalating per capita material and energy consumption, as well as increased carbon dioxide emissions. This is attributed to substantial processing waste and energy demands inherent in their production processes. The analysis of these small, open economies, characterized by relatively simple industrial structures focused on a few abundant resources, offers valuable insights. It highlights how resource availability, coupled with the presence or absence of infrastructure and specific institutional frameworks, shapes domestic resource utilization patterns. Furthermore, this research contributes to a more nuanced understanding and interpretation of MFA indicators, such as domestic material consumption.

Material flow accounts offer a summary of the physical aspects of national economies. Increasingly, researchers are examining long-term trends in material flow indicators through national case studies, aiming to understand how industrial metabolic profiles develop. Factors such as available resources, the structure of industry, and a nation's wealth all contribute to shaping how materials are used within an economy. In larger economies, the intricate nature of their physical systems often makes it challenging to pinpoint the specific drivers behind observed material flow patterns, leading to discussions of resource use that are often confined to broad trends like population growth and GDP changes. To gain a clearer insight into the formation of national material use patterns, this study focuses on two small island economies. These nations are characterized by relatively straightforward industrial production, trade, and resource utilization structures, making them ideal for in-depth analysis.
Island systems present a valuable and manageable focus for industrial ecology studies, as noted by Deschenes and Chertow (2004). These systems often function as self-contained entities with limited indigenous resources. However, when viewed through the lens of material flow analysis (MFA), islands frequently exhibit characteristics of open economies. They rely on extracting a select few primary resources for export while depending on imports to meet a significant portion of their domestic requirements and final consumption. This dual nature renders them particularly susceptible to global economic shifts and local environmental damage. The urgency of sustainability issues is often amplified on islands compared to continental landmasses, a point underscored by the cautionary tale of Nauru's phosphate-driven boom and subsequent decline (Gowdy and McDaniel 1999).
Islands have previously been examined by industrial ecologists, primarily concerning waste management and industrial symbiosis. This study, however, employs economy-wide material flow accounting to analyze the long-term trends in material extraction, trade, and consumption within two island nations: Iceland and the Republic of Trinidad and Tobago (TT). This analysis covers the period from 1961-1962 to 2008. Globally, these two nations are relatively small, with populations of 0.36 and 1.3 million respectively. Collectively, they represented only 0.02% of the world's population and contributed 0.07% to global GDP in 2008. Despite their size, these high-income economies exhibit distinctive resource utilization patterns and unique metabolic characteristics. Unusually for island states, both possess abundant energy resources, though of different kinds: Iceland benefits from renewable hydropower and geothermal heat, while TT relies on petroleum and natural gas deposits. Currently, both countries are notable for their high per capita resource consumption and emissions. They demonstrate exceptionally high levels of energy use and energy intensity within their economies. In 2005, TT and Iceland ranked among the highest globally for per capita primary energy consumption, with TT also being a top five per capita emitter of carbon dioxide (CO2). Together, they accounted for 0.16% of global CO2 emissions in 2008. These distinctive resource use patterns, coupled with their status as small economies with narrow yet significant resource bases and a high reliance on trade for both domestic supply and economic growth, make Iceland and TT compelling case studies for investigating resource use in conjunction with resource endowment, industrial production, and trade. Their structural similarities, alongside their differing resource endowments, biogeographic contexts, and economic histories, also render them valuable subjects for comparative analysis.
This article examines the evolution of material and energy flows in Trinidad and Tobago and Iceland over nearly five decades, from 1961 to 2008. Employing Material Flow Analysis (MFA) methods, the study quantifies direct material use, encompassing extraction, trade, and consumption of biomass, fossil fuels, ores, and nonmetallic minerals, and calculates aggregated MFA indicators. The economic structures of these two nations are characterized by a limited number of resource-intensive, export-focused industries. This simplicity facilitates an examination of the factors contributing to their notably high and distinct material and energy usage patterns. The research delves into the origins of their current "metabolic profiles" and the influence of specific biogeographic and socioeconomic elements on material flow development. It further investigates how these island nations leverage their constrained resource bases and manage these resources. Particular emphasis is placed on the impact of resource endowment, infrastructure, trade, and institutional frameworks on material flow dynamics. The experiences of these two small, open economies offer valuable perspectives on interpreting MFA indicators in the context of production and consumption patterns.

This detailed examination will focus on the evolution of material flows, with particular attention to the extraction, processing, and trade of key resources. The third section will then offer a comparative analysis of the metabolic profiles of both nations, contextualize them globally, and explore the implications of material use indicators for sustainable development. Material flow data for Trinidad and Tobago and Iceland spans from 1961-1962 to 2008. This dataset details direct material flows, encompassing domestic extraction (DE), imports, and exports. It also accounts for the physical trade balance (PTB), calculated as imports minus exports, and domestic material consumption (DMC), which is equivalent to DE plus PTB and is also referred to as material use. The data categorizes materials into 65 groups, subsequently aggregated into five primary categories: biomass, fossil energy carriers, metals, nonmetallic minerals, and unspecified materials (solely for trade). The compilation of these material flow accounts adheres to the methodological principles and guidelines established by Eurostat (2009). The process involves utilizing both national and international statistical data. For material flows not captured in existing statistics, standard estimation procedures are applied, drawing on work by Krausmann et al. (2009) and Fischer-Kowalski et al. (2011). To enable per capita calculations, population data was sourced from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Statistics Division (FAOSTAT) (2010). Furthermore, GDP data from the World Bank Group, expressed in constant 2005 USD, was used to determine material intensity, defined as DMC per unit of GDP.
To determine domestic extraction (DE) of biomass, agricultural and forestry statistics from FAOSTAT (2010) were utilized. For Iceland, supplementary data from its Statistical Office were incorporated to account for fish, hunted animals, and forage. Standard methodologies and regionally and temporally specific coefficients (Krausmann et al. 2008) were applied to estimate harvested crop residues and biomass grazed by livestock. Fossil energy carrier DE for Trinidad and Tobago (TT) was sourced from International Energy Agency (IEA) and United Nations (UN) energy statistics (IEA 2010; UNSD 2011). Iceland, however, did not extract any oil or gas during the study period. Data on ore and nonmetallic mineral extraction were obtained from U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) minerals yearbooks (e.g., USGS 2008) and UN industrial commodities statistics (UN 2010). Neither country exploited ores. The DE of nonmetallic minerals for construction presented a challenge due to limited statistical coverage. Therefore, estimates for sand, gravel, and crushed stone extraction and usage in cement, concrete, and asphalt production were derived from data on cement (e.g., Cembureau 1998) and bitumen (IEA 2010) production and consumption, supplemented by coefficients from prior research (Steinberger and Krausmann 2011; Krausmann et al. 2009; Eurostat 2009).
Trinidad and Tobago's export-oriented nitrogen fertilizer (ammonia) production presents a unique case for material flow accounting. Atmospheric nitrogen, the primary raw material, is typically classified as a "balancing item" and not included in domestic extraction (DE) figures. However, this study quantified the nitrogen utilized in fertilizer production based on its stoichiometric composition. This quantified nitrogen has been incorporated as a distinct component within Trinidad and Tobago's DE. The accompanying Figure 1 illustrates the material flows for Trinidad and Tobago, categorizing them by main material groups. It details domestic extraction, imports, exports, and domestic material consumption (DMC) in million tonnes annually. Additionally, the figure presents DMC on a per capita basis, alongside per capita income and DMC for biomass and mineral/fossil materials.
Trade data for agricultural and forestry products came from FAOSTAT (2010), while fossil energy trade relied on IEA (2010) data. Information on ores, metals, and nonmetallic minerals was sourced from the UN's Comtrade database (UNSD 2012). For Iceland, international trade figures were cross-referenced with data from Statistics Iceland (2011).
Trinidad and Tobago, a Caribbean archipelagic state, comprises two main islands. Trinidad, covering 94% of the nation's 5,100 square kilometers, hosts 96% of the population and all industrial activity. By 2008, its population reached 1.3 million, resulting in a high and increasing population density, which rose from 167 to 266 people per square kilometer between 1961 and 2008. This nation ranks among the wealthiest in the Latin American and Caribbean region, with a per capita GDP of US$24,000 in 2008, measured in purchasing power parity.
Total material extraction in Trinidad and Tobago increased from 12 to 46 million tonnes. This growth was primarily driven by fossil energy carriers, particularly petroleum and later natural gas, whose share in domestic extraction rose from 59% to 82%. Atmospheric nitrogen also contributed to this increase. Biomass, significant in the 1960s, became less important over time.
Trinidad and Tobago (TT) exhibits substantial import volumes, as depicted in Figure 1B. Fossil energy carriers constitute the primary component of these imports, though their proportion has decreased from 98% in the 1960s to between 40% and 50% in the 2000s. Beyond fossil fuels, TT imports significant quantities of metals, predominantly iron ore, to support its iron and steel industry, an initiative aimed at diversifying its oil-dependent economy. While biomass imports are considerably smaller than those of fossil fuels and metals, TT's reliance on imported biomass for food supply has steadily increased. Per capita food imports rose from 0.2 to 0.7 tonnes annually by 2008, reaching a level comparable to domestic biomass extraction. Exports from TT, illustrated in Figure 1D, mirror the trends observed in imports but are substantially larger, having tripled over the study period. Fossil energy carriers remain the dominant export, accounting for 98% to 80% of the total. In addition to fossil fuels, TT exports considerable mineral materials, primarily fertilizers (10% in 2008) and iron and steel products (9% in 2008). Per capita export volume nearly doubled, from 14 to 27 tonnes annually, and even surpassed domestic extraction until the late 1970s. TT has consistently been a net exporter of materials. However, the average price of its exports was relatively low, leading to a decline in an initial monetary trade surplus during the 1970s. A period of negative monetary trade balance persisted for nearly a decade due to falling oil prices in the 1980s. It was only in the last two decades, with the export of gas and related products and high energy prices, that TT began to achieve a substantial monetary surplus from its foreign trade.
Trinidad and Tobago's Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) has grown significantly, rising from 9 to 23 million tonnes annually. Initially, biomass and fossil materials were the primary components of DMC, with nonmetallic minerals becoming more prominent later. While biomass consumption decreased over time, the use of mineral and fossil materials tracked the country's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) closely. Trinidad and Tobago exhibits a notably high per capita DMC. This figure saw a dip during the economic downturn of the 1980s but subsequently surged with periods of robust economic expansion. From 1993 to 2008, per capita DMC more than doubled, reaching 17 tonnes per year, a level considerably exceeding the global average of 9 tonnes and even surpassing the European Union's average of 12 tonnes. The per capita consumption of fossil energy carriers is particularly high at 12 tonnes. Consequently, Trinidad and Tobago faces exceptionally high per capita carbon dioxide emissions, stemming from fossil fuel combustion and cement production, totaling 37 tonnes.
The economic trajectory of Trinidad and Tobago (TT) reveals a significant shift from a biomass-dependent economy to one centered on oil. Prior to 1958, when TT was a British colony, its economic foundation rested primarily on the export of sugar, supplemented by cacao and coffee. During this era, biomass was the dominant material in both extraction and utilization. By the commencement of the observed period in 1961, biomass still constituted over a third of TT's total domestic extraction. Petroleum emerged as the second crucial resource, with extraction commencing in the late 19th century. By the 1930s, petroleum extraction had surpassed one million tonnes. This figure rose to six million tonnes by 1961 and doubled to twelve million tonnes by 1977, marking TT's peak oil production. Following this peak, petroleum extraction saw a 35% decline. However, domestic petroleum reserves were not the sole catalyst for the expansion of TT's petroleum industries. A pivotal external factor was the United States' Mandatory Oil Import Quota Program, established in 1959. This program restricted oil imports from specific nations, particularly from the Near East, aiming to reduce U.S. reliance on foreign oil and safeguard its domestic industry. TT, however, was exempt from these restrictions. Its strategic advantages included a deep-sea harbor and a favorable geographical position in the Caribbean, offering proximity to the United States. Furthermore, following its independence, TT adopted a policy of "industrialization by invitation," encouraging industrial growth through investments from multinational corporations. Texaco, the first to capitalize on this approach in 1956, was soon followed by other companies like Shell. Texaco acquired the national Trinidad oil company and began importing oil from its fields in the Near East, processing it in TT's refineries into residual fuel oil. This fuel oil was then exported to the United States as a feedstock for its petrochemical sector. This development propelled TT into becoming a significant refining hub and one of the world's largest trans-shipment centers. Material Flow Analysis (MFA) data reflects this transformation, showing that by 1961, petroleum imports already exceeded domestic extraction, reaching fifteen million tonnes by 1970 – double the volume of domestically extracted petroleum. Despite these developments, the economic benefits were constrained, and the trade surplus began to diminish. The political landscape shifted significantly following the Black Power uprising in 1970. This event led to changes in foreign investment policies and the nationalization of key industries. Consequently, oil imports drastically decreased, approaching zero by the early 1980s, and there was an increasing reliance on domestic resources. While TT's economy benefited from the high oil prices of the 1970s, a combination of falling oil prices, depletion of domestic reserves, and a lack of new discoveries severely impacted the economy. This resulted in a severe recession between 1983 and 1989, during which income fell by a third. It was not until 2003 that income levels prior to the crisis were regained. Since then, the economy has experienced robust growth, primarily driven by substantial revenues from natural gas extraction.
For an extended period, natural gas in Trinidad and Tobago (TT) was largely considered a byproduct of petroleum extraction, lacking the necessary infrastructure for domestic utilization or export. This situation began to change in the late 1970s with the expansion of the fertilizer industry and other sectors that utilized natural gas. The opening of the first natural gas liquids (NGL) production plant in 1999 significantly enhanced TT's capacity for direct gas exports. Between 1998 and 2008, natural gas extraction saw a substantial increase, growing by a factor of 4.5. By 1999, natural gas extraction surpassed that of petroleum, reaching approximately 30 million tonnes (Mt) in 2008. Currently, TT ranks as the fifth-largest exporter of NGLs, with a significant portion, two-thirds, destined for the United States. A crucial natural gas-dependent industry for TT has been the production of nitrogen compounds for export. This sector experienced rapid growth, increasing from 0.5 Mt in 1980 to 5 Mt in 2008, a period when petroleum extraction stood at 8 Mt. By 1985, TT had become the world's second-largest exporter of nitrogen fertilizer, trailing only the USSR. In parallel, TT pursued industrial diversification in the 1970s and 1980s by establishing an iron and steel industry. This industry relies entirely on imported iron ore from Brazil, a fact reflected in the increasing volumes of both ore imports and metal exports. By 2007, iron ore imports reached 5 Mt, constituting 37% of TT's total imports. Approximately two-thirds of these imported iron ores and concentrates are re-exported as processed iron or steel billets, with the remaining portion generating waste within TT.
The ascendancy of the petroleum industry, coupled with escalating labor expenses and diminishing sugar prices, led to a swift contraction in sugar production and its export. Sugar cane harvests in Trinidad and Tobago (TT) were substantial, reaching around 2.5 million tonnes annually until the early 1970s, with sugar cane and its byproducts constituting 86% of biomass domestic extraction (DE). However, the sugar industry's decline saw this production fall to approximately 1 million tonnes by 1985, and further to a mere 0.4 million tonnes after 2002. Notably, during this latter period, TT transitioned into a net importer of sugar. This reduction in sugar output is the primary driver behind the overall decrease in biomass DE. By the 1970s, TT had already become a net importer of biomass, now sourcing the majority of its staple foods and wood from abroad. Per capita biomass imports rose to 0.7 tonnes, surpassing domestic biomass production. Consequently, per capita biomass domestic material consumption (DMC) saw a significant drop, from 4 to 5 tonnes per year in the 1960s to less than 0.5 tonnes in the 21st century. The high per capita biomass DMC observed in the 1960s was directly linked to export-oriented production. A mere 5% to 10% of harvested sugar cane was exported as refined sugar, while the substantial remaining residues stayed within the country, contributing to DMC through their use or combustion.
In 2008, Trinidad and Tobago (TT) exhibited a Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) of 17 tonnes per capita annually, a figure substantially exceeding the average for Latin America. This elevated material usage is not indicative of a particularly resource-intensive lifestyle or high household consumption. Instead, it is a direct consequence of TT's economic structure. The nation's economy relies on industries that are both material and energy-intensive. These industries leverage domestic energy resources and imported raw materials, with their output exclusively destined for export markets. This pattern of disproportionately high DMC has been a consistent feature of TT's metabolic profile, dating back to its colonial past when sugar exports led to elevated per capita biomass DMC. Currently, exports of steel, fertilizer, petroleum products, and natural gas liquids (NGLs) continue to drive this high per capita DMC. The export-oriented industries have been instrumental in TT's rapid economic development, contributing significantly to its GDP and resulting in some of the highest per capita incomes in Latin America, alongside low unemployment and poverty rates. However, these resource-intensive export industries also present considerable drawbacks. They render the economy highly susceptible to external shocks, as evidenced by the decline in per capita GDP during the 1980s due to falling oil prices. Furthermore, these industries incur substantial ecological costs. TT ranks among the countries with the highest per capita CO2 emissions globally. Additionally, oil and gas extraction, along with heavy industrial activities, generate extensive, often hazardous, waste streams and pose a significant risk of accidents and oil spills.
Located in the North Atlantic, Iceland is a Nordic European island nation. While significantly larger than Trinidad and Tobago at 103,000 km², its population was considerably smaller in 2008, numbering 315,000. This results in a very low population density, which rose from 1.7 to 3.0 people per square kilometer between 1961 and 2008. The country's cold climate limits agricultural potential. Iceland's geography, characterized by extensive inland glaciers feeding large glacial rivers and high geological activity, has led to abundant hydropower and geothermal resources. This natural endowment has contributed to Iceland's transformation from one of Europe's poorest nations to a high-income country, with a per capita GDP of US$33,200 (PPP) in 2008.
Iceland's domestic material extraction is primarily composed of biomass and nonmetallic minerals used for construction, with no extraction of ores or fossil energy carriers. Biomass extraction experienced a doubling from 1.6 to 3.2 million tonnes (Mt) by 1997, followed by a subsequent decline. This surge was exclusively driven by an increase in fish catch, which rose from 40% to 70% of total domestic extraction (DE) by 1997, though its proportion has since decreased. By 2008, fish catch still constituted 58% of biomass DE. The remaining biomass extraction largely consists of livestock grazing, with crop and vegetable production being minimal due to unfavorable climate conditions. Iceland relies heavily on imported biomass, similar to Trinidad and Tobago, with its food supply predominantly sourced from imported crops and livestock products. Per capita biomass imports increased from 0.7 to 1.8 tonnes per year (t/cap/yr), while biomass exports, almost entirely fish, ranged between 1.3 and 3 t/cap/yr. The extraction of nonmetallic minerals, mainly sand, gravel, and stones for construction, saw a significant increase between 1961 and 1975, rising from 0.7 to 2.2 Mt. Since then, this extraction has fluctuated between 1.5 and 2.2 Mt annually.
Iceland's trade volume, both imports and exports, expanded significantly, mirroring the country's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth. By 2008, imports had risen from 0.4 to 4.8 million tonnes (Mt), surpassing domestic extraction. Exports also saw a substantial increase, reaching 1.9 Mt from an initial 0.3 Mt. Consequently, Iceland has become a net importer of materials, with net imports growing to 2.8 Mt. This reliance on imports extends to numerous products and essential resources. Beyond food, which has already been noted as a significant import, Iceland also imports substantial quantities of fossil fuels, metals, and consumer goods. All of Iceland's fossil fuel needs are met through imports, primarily petroleum, which fuels the extensive fishing fleet and land transportation. Fossil fuel imports have grown considerably, from 2.2 to 4.3 tonnes per capita annually. The import of metals is also substantial, encompassing not only finished products like vehicles and vessels but also significant amounts of bauxite, accounting for 35% of total imports for alumina production, and iron for ferroalloy manufacturing. Iceland's exports, which reached 6.0 tonnes per capita in 2008, are predominantly composed of fish (36%), aluminum and ferrosilicon (55%), and certain nonmetallic minerals such as sand and diatomite. Despite being a considerable net importer of materials throughout the observed period, Iceland maintained a relatively balanced monetary trade account until the late 1990s. This stability was largely attributed to highly favorable terms of trade, with export prices significantly exceeding import prices.
Iceland's domestic material consumption (DMC) has more than tripled, reaching 7.2 million tonnes. This increase was concentrated in two distinct periods: 1961-1974 and 1995-2007. For two decades between these growth phases, from 1974 to 1995, DMC remained relatively stable. Consequently, per capita material use has doubled, rising from 12 to 23 tonnes per year. This places Iceland's per capita material use at a very high level internationally, significantly exceeding the European Union's average of 14 tonnes per year.
Iceland's economy, much like Trinidad and Tobago's, relies on the exploitation of a limited number of primary resources. Beyond its significant hydropower and geothermal energy reserves, the country possesses few other natural resources. This scarcity is evident in its material extraction patterns, where sand and gravel, grazed biomass, and fish collectively constitute 95% of domestic extraction. Historically, fishing has been the cornerstone of the Icelandic economy, still contributing 40% to export earnings and employing 5-7% of the workforce. While fishing remains a key industry, its prominence has decreased from 90% of export share in the 1960s to 40% by 2006. The Icelandic economy has diversified, with aluminum and ferrosilicon now being the most crucial export products alongside fish.
Iceland witnessed substantial economic growth during the observed period, with per capita income rising from $9,900 in 1961 to $38,200 by 2008. This transformation saw Iceland evolve from one of Europe's poorest nations to a leading global economy. Initial economic expansion from 1961 to 1987, fueled by the fishing industry and favorable fish prices, was punctuated by recessions in 1966-1969 and 1982-1983. A significant economic downturn occurred between 1988 and 1993, causing income to fall from $27,000 to $25,000. However, the economy rebounded in 1994 and experienced rapid growth until 2008. The 2008 global economic crisis severely impacted Iceland, leading to a two-year recession and a 10% GDP decline by 2010. Despite this, Iceland demonstrated a strong recovery, maintaining its position as the 25th wealthiest nation globally in 2011.
Historically, Iceland's economy relied heavily on its marine fish stocks, exporting both raw and processed fish. Fish catches rose from 0.6 million tonnes (Mt) in 1961 to 2.2 Mt in 1997, but have since decreased due to declining fish populations, reaching 1.2 Mt by 2008. Iceland consistently ranks among the top ten global fish exporters by mass. Per capita exports ranged from 1.3 to 3.0 tonnes, while domestic fish consumption per person increased to 60-90 kg annually, significantly exceeding the global average. Despite these export volumes, only 35% to 50% of the total fish catch is exported in terms of mass. This discrepancy is not due to high domestic consumption but rather a processing technique known as "reduction." This process transforms over half the catch into fishmeal, which has a much lower mass than fresh fish due to its significantly reduced moisture content.
Iceland's fish production saw an increase, driven by the expansion of fishing grounds. This expansion, however, led to international conflicts, notably the "Cod Wars" with Great Britain in 1961, 1973, and 1976. The fishing industry's reliance on fish stocks makes it highly susceptible to declines, as evidenced by significant fluctuations in catch volumes. Overfishing caused substantial drops in fish production and overall biomass domestic extraction in 1965, 1981, and 1988. The collapse of the Atlantic herring population in 1965 prompted a shift to capelin and cod, but these species also faced overfishing due to the industrialization of fishing practices, leading to further catch slumps in 1981 and 1988. Given the critical importance of fishing to Iceland's economy, these crises contributed to economic downturns. To address these issues, Iceland implemented an "Individual Transferable Quota" system, rooted in population ecology principles, which has since improved the situation. Furthermore, the fishing industry's large fleet requires substantial fossil fuel imports, accounting for 23% of Iceland's total fossil fuel imports.
Beyond its significant fishing industry, Iceland possesses abundant hydropower and geothermal resources, a natural endowment that has been utilized for centuries. These renewable sources currently meet all of Iceland's domestic electricity needs and over 90% of its household heating requirements. However, prior to the 1970s, the application of hydropower and geothermal heat was confined to limited domestic consumption. The export of electricity from Iceland is technically challenging and financially demanding due to the absence of a submarine cable connecting to the European mainland. To circumvent this limitation and leverage its energy resources for export, Iceland adopted an alternative strategy: fostering energy-intensive industries. The aluminum industry, in particular, requires substantial electricity. The Icelandic government actively encouraged foreign investment in this sector by offering attractive terms, including guaranteed power supply, low electricity rates, and favorable taxation and industrial location policies. This initiative led to the establishment of the first aluminum smelter by Alcoa in 1969. Consequently, Iceland began exporting energy in the form of processed aluminum and ferrosilicon. A critical aspect of this industrial development is Iceland's lack of domestic iron ore and bauxite deposits. Consequently, all necessary raw materials must be imported from countries like the United States, Australia, and Ireland. This reliance on imported raw materials is reflected in the significant increase in bauxite imports, which rose from 0.4 million tonnes to 1.6 million tonnes, with notable surges in 1998 and 2007 coinciding with the expansion of production capacity. Iron imports, while smaller, also reached 150,000 tonnes in 2008. Collectively, bauxite and iron for ferrosilicon production constituted between 47% and 76% of Iceland's total metal imports. The mass of the exported aluminum and ferrosilicon is considerably less than the imported raw materials, with approximately 60% of the imported mass being exported. The remaining material, comprising slag and by-products, contributes to a substantial Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) for metal products in Iceland, reaching 4 tonnes per capita in 2008, with roughly 2.7 tonnes of this attributed to production waste. Electricity is supplied to these industries at exceptionally low prices, and the exploitation of these energy sources is rapidly intensifying. While hydropower and geothermal heat are clean and renewable, their potential is finite. Current estimates suggest an electric power potential of 50,000 gigawatt-hours per year from these sources, considering feasibility and environmental factors. However, only about 20% of this potential is currently being utilized. Plans for further development, including the construction of an underwater cable to the United Kingdom, are under consideration but are met with controversy. The construction of new dams, power stations, and industrial facilities is anticipated to have significant impacts on Iceland's ecosystems and remaining wilderness areas. Despite Iceland's economy being largely built upon renewable energy for domestic use, the significant demands of its export-oriented industries place an environmental burden on these resources. This leads to emissions, problematic production wastes, overexploitation, and unsustainable management of ecosystem services, creating conflicts with nature conservation objectives. Consequently, even with its reliance on renewable energy for domestic supply, Iceland's per capita carbon dioxide emissions remain only slightly below European averages and considerably above the global average.
Trinidad and Tobago (TT) and Iceland, both small, open economies with limited domestic resources and relatively straightforward economic structures, exhibit distinct metabolic profiles. Their significant domestic material extraction is concentrated on a few key resources: natural gas, petroleum, and nitrogen for TT, and fish and nonmetallic minerals for construction in Iceland. In both nations, abundant energy resources have facilitated a transition from biomass-dependent industries to large-scale, energy- and material-intensive petrochemical and metal processing sectors. A substantial portion of both domestically extracted and imported materials is channeled into export production, resulting in high per capita export volumes of 26 tonnes per year in TT and 6 tonnes per year in Iceland. Despite their export-oriented production, both countries demonstrate exceptionally high per capita domestic material and energy consumption. Concurrently, they rely heavily on imports for their food and consumer goods needs. While these broad similarities exist, the socioeconomic and metabolic characteristics of TT and Iceland diverge significantly. Studying these two small nations, with their unusual metabolic patterns, offers valuable insights into how metabolic profiles develop, the contributing factors, and how to interpret aggregate Material Flow Analysis (MFA) indicators.
The material flow patterns observed in Trinidad and Tobago (TT) and Iceland highlight the crucial role of resource endowments in shaping both the quantity and type of material flows, from initial extraction through trade to domestic consumption. Both nations historically relied heavily on the extraction and export of renewable biomass. However, in the latter half of the twentieth century, a significant shift occurred towards exploiting mineral and fossil resources. In TT, initial material flows were dictated by fertile land suitable for sugar production. This later evolved to be dominated by extensive petroleum and natural gas deposits, which fueled material-intensive petrochemical and fertilizer industries. Iceland's material profile was similarly shaped by its rich fishing grounds, a cornerstone of its economy. This transitioned with the advent of abundant hydropower and geothermal energy, paving the way for material-intensive metal industries. These examples collectively underscore the significant influence of energy availability in driving material usage patterns.
The case studies of Trinidad and Tobago and Iceland reveal that resource endowment is not the sole determinant of material flow patterns. Infrastructure and specific institutional frameworks play equally crucial roles in enabling resource extraction, processing, and export. The presence or absence of vital infrastructure, such as pipelines and harbors for oil and gas exports, or a submarine cable for electricity, significantly influences how resources are exploited and utilized, thereby shaping the material flow profile in conjunction with resource availability. Beyond these technical considerations, institutional settings are paramount. In Trinidad and Tobago, resource utilization was initially shaped by colonial land ownership and later by its "industrialization by invitation" policy, coupled with US import restrictions. Iceland's trajectory was influenced by the expansion of fishing grounds, the management of fishing rights, and agreements with multinational corporations regarding power supply, electricity pricing, and taxation. These institutional arrangements dictate the sustainability of domestic resource exploitation and the distribution of profits. Consequently, specific technological choices and institutional frameworks can establish path dependencies with enduring impacts on the physical economy. Ultimately, institutional arrangements determine the sustainable exploitation of domestic resources and who benefits from them.
Both Trinidad and Tobago (TT) and Iceland exhibit a strong reliance on a limited number of key materials for both domestic extraction and export. A significant indirect impact on material usage stems from their abundant energy resources, namely natural gas in TT and hydropower in Iceland. These energy sources are leveraged to facilitate the extraction of other resources and to power energy-intensive industrial processes. Specifically, TT utilizes natural gas for fertilizer and iron and steel production, while Iceland's electricity powers alumina and ferroalloy manufacturing. These energy- and material-intensive operations not only shape extraction and export patterns but also significantly influence import levels and contribute to high domestic material consumption (DMC). Despite substantial portions of extracted and processed materials being exported, considerable waste streams persist domestically. These waste flows contribute to elevated DMC, increased energy consumption, and higher carbon dioxide emissions. Furthermore, the availability of certain resources can correlate with above-average domestic final consumption, as seen in Iceland's high per capita fish and household electricity usage.
The experiences of Trinidad and Tobago and Iceland highlight the intricate relationship between trade and sustainable development. Both nations leveraged their natural resources for export-oriented production, which in turn spurred economic growth, generated wealth, and facilitated the import of goods they lacked. While this approach hasn't always yielded consistent success, over time, both countries have enhanced their resource management and the advantages derived from them. Despite the considerable depletion of natural capital and associated ecological costs stemming from export-driven resource exploitation, there are arguments for potential global benefits. For instance, Iceland's aluminum industry, by operating with greater efficiency and lower CO2 emissions compared to global alternatives, may contribute to resource conservation on a worldwide scale.
Both Trinidad and Tobago and Iceland demonstrate how a dependence on a limited number of abundant resources makes small island economies highly susceptible to global market fluctuations. This reliance on a few key resources exposes them to risks from price volatility, market access issues, and the potential for overexploitation. Economic prosperity can surge with high resource prices, but conversely, falling prices or declining resource stocks can trigger severe economic crises. This vulnerability is compounded by their significant reliance on imported food and other essential consumer goods. Examples include the impact of fish prices and overfishing on Iceland's economy, and oil prices and peak oil concerns in Trinidad and Tobago, both of which have historically led to substantial economic downturns in these nations.
The findings from this study illuminate both the utility and limitations of aggregate Material Flow Analysis (MFA) indicators. A key observation is that the remarkably high per capita Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) in Trinidad and Tobago (TT) and Iceland is significantly influenced by waste generated from their resource-intensive export industries. Examples include bauxite waste, substantial petroleum and gas consumption by petrochemical operations, and the processing of fish. Conversely, these countries rely heavily on imports for nearly all their food and the majority of their consumer goods. The upstream resource demands associated with producing these imported items are not captured within their national DMC figures. While these balancing effects might be less pronounced in larger economies with more intricate production and trade networks, they can lead to exceptionally high or low DMC values in small, open economies like those examined here. It is crucial to remember that DMC represents apparent consumption, quantifying an economy's total resource utilization. However, it does not distinguish between materials used for domestic final consumption versus those destined for export, nor does it account for the resources embedded in imported goods. To accurately assess consumption-related resource use, additional analytical tools are necessary and are currently under development. Despite these limitations, the analysis clearly demonstrates that DMC offers valuable insights into the material use patterns stemming from domestic production structures. These structures have been instrumental in generating substantial economic wealth, providing employment, and supporting income for large segments of the population in both nations. Therefore, DMC serves as an indicator of the material flow patterns for which these countries bear immediate responsibility. These are patterns that can be directly influenced through national policies and technological advancements, and which ultimately contribute to domestic waste generation, positioning DMC as a measure of a country's domestic waste potential.
