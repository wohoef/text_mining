Maintaining dental implants long-term relies on supportive treatments, but professional cleaning can modify abutment surfaces, potentially causing negative biological reactions. This research investigated four common clinical cleaning methods, assessing their impact on abutment material surfaces and subsequent bacterial adhesion. Titanium and zirconia discs were prepared and assigned to five groups: treatment with a titanium curette, a carbon fiber reinforced plastic curette, ultrasonic scaling with a carbon fiber tip, air polishing with glycine powder, or a control group with no treatment. Following cleaning, surface roughness (Ra), hydrophilicity, and surface free energy were measured. Bacterial adhesion was then evaluated after a one-hour incubation with *Streptococcus mitis*, observed microscopically and quantified. Results showed that titanium curette treatment significantly altered titanium surfaces, increasing roughness, hydrophilicity, and surface free energy, which correlated with higher bacterial adhesion. In contrast, zirconia samples exhibited no significant differences in surface morphology, roughness, or bacterial adhesion across the tested cleaning procedures. The study concluded that zirconia is more resistant to surface changes from these cleaning methods than titanium. Consequently, titanium curettes should be used cautiously on titanium abutments.

Dental implants are widely employed for tooth restoration and can achieve long-term functionality with appropriate planning and execution. However, their widespread use has coincided with a rise in complications, particularly infection-related peri-implant diseases. Factors such as uncontrolled diabetes, existing periodontal disease, smoking, and poor oral hygiene are recognized as risk factors for peri-implant infections. Professional supportive care is therefore crucial for the sustained success of dental implants. If peri-implant mucositis, a soft tissue inflammation, is not detected and treated, it can advance to peri-implantitis, a destructive condition affecting hard tissues. Patients with early peri-implant mucositis who do not receive regular dental support face a significantly higher risk of disease progression. Advanced infections can result in implant loss and ridge deformities, complicating future reimplantation efforts. Consequently, early detection and management of these infections are paramount.
The precise protocols for supportive implant treatments remain undefined. A cornerstone of this care, beyond comprehensive examinations, is the removal of plaque and calculus surrounding the implant system. Both chemical and mechanical approaches have been explored. While agents like chlorhexidine, essential oils, and delmopinol have demonstrated efficacy against dental plaque on natural teeth, their application to implants has yielded less favorable outcomes. Mechanical methods, including ultrasonic scaling devices, metal and plastic curettes, titanium instruments, and air polishing devices, have been investigated with varying results. Conventional stainless steel Gracey curettes are generally not advised for routine implant supportive therapy due to their tendency to leave more biofilm compared to powered instruments and their propensity to damage implant surfaces. Plastic curettes, while safer for implant surfaces, offer limited cleaning effectiveness. Ultrasonic scaling is more efficient but can cause scratches on smooth surfaces. To mitigate abutment surface alterations, various scaling inserts have been developed, such as carbon, plastic, and metal tips with plastic sheaths. However, concerns persist regarding residue deposits left by these instruments on implants. Air polishing is a widely used method for plaque removal from both natural dentition and implants with minimal surface damage. Despite this, its additional benefits in cases of peri-mucositis have been modest, and an early report of submucosal emphysema following air polishing around an implant has raised questions about its use. Consequently, the optimal instrument for implant cleaning during supportive treatment continues to be a subject of discussion.
Long-term dental implant success is also influenced by the implant system's material properties. Choosing an abutment material that resists bacterial adhesion due to its chemical and physical characteristics is vital. While titanium has been the primary focus of research on professional cleaning around implant abutments, zirconia has gained prominence as an alternative abutment material. Zirconia offers advantages such as good mechanical properties, aesthetic appeal, biocompatibility, and inherent resistance to bacterial adhesion. However, the effects of professional cleaning on zirconia abutments have been less explored. This study investigated four common clinical cleaning methods: titanium curettes, carbon fiber reinforced plastic curettes, ultrasonic scaling with carbon fiber inserts, and air polishing with glycine powder. Both titanium and zirconia samples were used to assess how these cleaning procedures altered their surface characteristics and subsequent bacterial adhesion. Our hypothesis was that none of the tested cleaning procedures would change the surfaces of either titanium or zirconia abutments or influence bacterial adhesion.
The study utilized two dental implant abutment materials: commercially pure titanium (grade IV) and yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia. Round discs of each material were fabricated and polished to mimic clinical abutments. Sixty samples of each were prepared, sonicated with 75% ethanol, and then stored in a dry cabinet for 24 hours prior to testing.
Samples of titanium and zirconia were randomly assigned to one of five groups. Four clinically used implant cleaning instruments were tested: titanium curettes (Langer 5), carbon fiber reinforced plastic curettes (Implant Deplaquer), an ultrasonic scaler with a carbon fiber tip (PH1 tip with P5 Newtron), and an air polishing system using glycine powder (Air-flow powder Perio with Air-flow S1). A fifth group served as a control, receiving no surface treatment. These instruments were selected due to their widespread recognition for implant cleaning, particularly their purported ability to minimize surface alterations. Their ease of use and lack of requirement for specialized training also made them suitable for broad application by general dentists, thereby reducing potential operator-related study errors.
To ensure consistency, each sample was secured in a silicon mold to prevent movement during cleaning. A single, experienced periodontist executed all cleaning procedures. For titanium and plastic curette treatments, the curette blade was applied perpendicularly to the sample surface with overlapping strokes, without any fluid or lubricant. Ultrasonic scaling was performed with water irrigation at power level 2, following manufacturer guidelines, using smooth, overlapping movements across the entire surface. Air polishing utilized medium water and power settings, with the nozzle held 0.5 to 1 cm from the sample at a perpendicular angle. Glycine powder was replenished as recommended before each sample was treated. Control samples remained in a dry cabinet, receiving no instrumentation.
Following the cleaning procedures, samples were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and then sonicated in 75% ethanol to eliminate any debris. Surface morphology was initially examined using an optical microscope. For more detailed and precise evaluation of the surfaces where bacteria would adhere, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed. AFM's three-dimensional reconstruction images offered a clearer assessment. A substantial area of 50 µm × 50 µm was randomly scanned to account for bacterial size.
Surface roughness was measured using a profilometer. Three samples per group were analyzed, with three random areas on each sample measured. The arithmetical mean roughness (Ra) was calculated, using a cut-off length of 0.8 mm.
Hydrophilicity and surface free energy were assessed using a contact angle goniometer and its associated software. For each group, three samples were analyzed. Hydrophilicity was determined by measuring the contact angle of a deionized water droplet on the sample surface. Surface free energy was evaluated using the sessile drop method with deionized water and diiodomethane as polar and nonpolar liquids, respectively. The Owens-Wendt method was employed for the surface free energy calculations.
Streptococcus mitis (ATCC49456) was revived from a frozen pure strain. Cultured to its logarithmic phase in an anaerobic environment at 37°C using brain heart infusion medium, the bacteria suspension's optical density (OD) at 600 nm was measured. Using a spectrophotometer, the OD was adjusted to approximately 0.9 before use.
The bacterial adhesion test involved six samples per group. After disinfection with ultraviolet irradiation, each sample received 200 μL of a bacterial suspension. This study specifically examined initial bacterial adhesion, encompassing both reversible and irreversible attachment phases. While the precise timing of these phases can vary individually, an observation period of one hour was chosen for initial bacterial adhesion, rather than days. Following a 1-hour incubation period, the discs were gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate unadhered bacteria. One disc from each group was prepared for optical microscopic observation after Gram staining. Images were captured at ×500 magnification. To obtain quantitative data, the remaining five samples from each group were placed into wells of a new polystyrene culture test plate containing PBS. The adhered bacteria were then released into the PBS solution through sonication. Finally, bacterial quantification was performed using a turbidity test, measured by optical density (OD).
Statistical analysis involved a two-way ANOVA for surface roughness, hydrophilicity, surface free energy, and bacterial suspension OD. Comparisons between cleaning procedures were made using the Tukey HSD post-hoc test. All calculations were performed using SPSS statistics software, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.
Surface roughness (Ra) measurements revealed that titanium curette treatment resulted in the highest Ra for both titanium and zirconia samples. However, only the titanium samples showed a statistically significant increase in Ra compared to other groups (p < 0.05). For zirconia, while the titanium curette group had the highest Ra, no significant intergroup differences were found.
Table 2 presents the hydrophilicity of the tested samples. Zirconia exhibited greater hydrophilicity than titanium under identical treatments. Regarding cleaning effects, air polishing significantly decreased hydrophilicity for both materials compared to controls. Conversely, titanium curette and ultrasonic scaling treatments notably increased hydrophilicity.
Zirconia samples exhibited higher surface free energy than titanium samples. For both materials, titanium curette treatment resulted in the significantly highest surface free energy.
Table 4 presents the quantified bacterial adhesion results (OD values). For titanium samples, the titanium curette treatment group exhibited the highest bacterial adhesion (p < 0.05), with no significant differences among the other four groups. In contrast, no significant differences in bacterial adhesion were observed across any of the groups for zirconia samples.
Optical microscopy revealed distinct surface morphologies across the tested groups. Titanium samples in the control group exhibited a generally smooth surface with scattered, shallow carving marks. The air polishing group displayed a similar morphology to the control. In contrast, ultrasonic scaling resulted in extensive linear carving marks covering the entire surface. Plastic curette treatment also produced linear carving marks, though these were sparser and shallower. The titanium curette treatment group showed carving marks aligned similarly to the plastic curette group, but these were significantly deeper. For zirconia samples, shallow, randomly distributed carving marks were observed, along with evenly spaced pits potentially from manufacturing. No significant differences in surface morphology were apparent among the various treatment groups for zirconia.
Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) provided three-dimensional surface views (Fig. 2). Titanium samples treated with air polishing or left as controls exhibited similar, subtly waved surface morphologies. The plastic curette group showed minor scraping lines. Ultrasonic scaling and titanium curette treatments resulted in more pronounced surface waves, particularly with the titanium curette. In contrast, zirconia samples displayed no discernible differences in surface morphology across any cleaning treatments. All examined zirconia surfaces presented shallow, randomly oriented carving marks and remained relatively smooth.
After incubation with Streptococcus mitis, bacterial clusters were visible on all sample surfaces. Titanium samples treated with titanium curettes showed the highest density of adhered bacteria, followed by those treated with plastic curettes. Other titanium groups displayed no significant differences. For zirconia samples, bacterial distribution and density were consistent across all treatment groups.
Maintaining implant longevity necessitates consistent biofilm removal, yet this process can negatively impact abutment surfaces. Our study observed that cleaning procedures can lead to increased surface roughness, hydrophilicity, and surface free energy. Existing research indicates a positive correlation between these surface properties and bacterial adhesion. Specifically for titanium, titanium curette treatment resulted in elevated Ra, hydrophilicity, and surface free energy. These modifications facilitated bacterial adhesion, as evidenced by the significantly highest optical density of adhered bacteria in this group. Ultrasonic scaling also increased hydrophilicity and surface free energy, while air polishing and plastic curette treatments reduced these parameters. However, the surface roughness in these latter three groups remained comparable to the control, and bacterial adhesion did not reach a significant level. These findings suggest that while multiple surface characteristics influence bacterial adhesion, surface roughness appears to be a particularly critical factor.
The extent of surface changes on implant abutments due to cleaning procedures varied significantly, a finding supported by prior research. Studies have investigated the impact of different instruments on titanium surfaces. For instance, Fox and colleagues found that titanium curettes and stainless steel curettes caused significant alterations to titanium implant abutments, while plastic curettes had no significant effect. Similarly, Duarte and associates reported that metal curettes roughened titanium surfaces, whereas air polishing and plastic curettes did not substantially alter surface roughness. Sahrmann and team observed that ultrasonic treatment with metal tips and stainless steel curettes markedly changed implant surface morphology, while glycine powder air polishing had minimal impact. Schmage and collaborators, examining various instruments including plastic curettes, ultrasonic devices with carbon composite tips, and air polishing with glycine powder, concluded that none of these significantly increased the roughness of smooth surfaces. Collectively, these studies suggest that while non-metal ultrasonic inserts, plastic curettes, and glycine powder air polishing generally do not cause significant surface alterations, metal instruments pose a risk of damage. These prior findings align with the results of the present study.
In contrast to titanium, zirconia samples demonstrated no significant changes in surface roughness or morphology following any of the tested cleaning procedures. While titanium curette and plastic curette treatments did lead to a slight increase in hydrophilicity and surface free energy for zirconia, these alterations did not translate to significant differences in Ra or surface morphology. Consequently, no significant variations in the optical density of adhered bacteria were observed across any of the zirconia groups. This suggests that zirconia is more resilient to the mechanical cleaning methods employed compared to titanium. The superior wear resistance of zirconia is likely the reason for this difference. Material wear is primarily determined by hardness and surface roughness. Given that the initial Ra values for untreated titanium and zirconia were comparable, the distinct surface changes observed after cleaning can be attributed to their differing hardness. The Vickers hardness values for the titanium and zirconia samples in this study were 227 kg/mm² and 1410 kg/mm², respectively. This substantial difference in hardness likely accounts for their varied responses to the cleaning procedures. In clinical practice, over time with repeated cleanings during supportive care, zirconia's enhanced wear resistance could become even more pronounced, making zirconia abutments more resistant to bacterial adhesion than titanium ones.
During routine follow-up visits, it's generally assumed that implant patients have controlled acute infections and adequate hygiene skills, resulting in only minor inflammation and biofilm. Under these circumstances, preserving the abutment surface integrity is prioritized over aggressive cleaning. The study's findings suggest that most tested instruments are safe for both titanium and zirconia abutments, with titanium curettes on titanium being the sole exception. However, it's crucial to acknowledge that this study simulated a single cleaning event. In clinical practice, implants require lifelong supportive care, meaning cleaning procedures are repeated over time. These repeated interventions could lead to cumulative surface alterations and increased roughness. Therefore, careful selection and execution of cleaning procedures are essential for long-term implant maintenance.
In situations where patients present with significant calculus buildup, receding gums, or exposed implant fixtures, the objectives of supportive cleaning treatments may shift. Given the uncertainty surrounding the re-osseointegration of contaminated implant surfaces, smoothing out both macro-level features like thread patterns and micro-level irregularities from treatments such as sand-blasting or acid-etching can be a practical approach to improve patient hygiene. For these specific scenarios, more robust instruments like metal scaling tips, titanium curettes, titanium brushes, or diamond burs might be preferable. It is important to recognize that no single cleaning method is universally applicable. Clinicians should carefully choose the most suitable cleaning procedure based on their specific goals and a thorough understanding of both the implant system and the cleaning instruments themselves.
The study's findings led to the rejection of the null hypothesis, indicating that both the cleaning instruments and the abutment materials influence how cleaning procedures affect implant abutments. Specifically, titanium curettes applied to titanium samples resulted in deep scratches and increased surface roughness, which in turn promoted bacterial adhesion. Therefore, titanium curettes require cautious use during routine implant supportive treatment. In contrast, zirconia demonstrated superior resilience to damage from all tested cleaning procedures. Based on the experimental limitations, zirconia appears to be a favorable material for implant abutments, particularly concerning long-term maintenance.
