Tapeworms are widespread parasites, impacting millions of humans and livestock annually and contributing to two of the World Health Organization's prioritized neglected tropical diseases. Research into tapeworm biology and the diseases they cause is frequently hindered by the intricate life cycles of species that infect various hosts, including foxes, dogs, cattle, pigs, and humans. To circumvent these practical, ethical, and financial challenges, laboratory models offer a valuable alternative for tapeworm parasitologists. The rat intestinal tapeworm, *Hymenolepis diminuta*, is readily cultured in a laboratory setting. This accessibility positions it as a promising model for advanced molecular experiments, significantly enhancing our comprehension of tapeworm biology, particularly concerning growth and reproduction. In our pursuit of developing molecular tools for *H. diminuta* research, we have identified and characterized a range of lectins, antibodies, and common stains capable of marking diverse tapeworm tissues and organ structures. Employing confocal microscopy, we have constructed an "atlas" detailing the organ architecture of *H. diminuta*, which will serve as a valuable resource for helminthologists. The methods presented here are designed to simplify the analysis of loss-of-function mutations in *H. diminuta*. This comprehensive toolkit is expected to deepen our fundamental understanding of tapeworm biology, potentially revealing novel therapeutic targets for parasite eradication.

Tapeworms, belonging to the phylum Platyhelminthes and class Cestoda, are responsible for numerous diseases affecting both humans and livestock. The larval stages of the Taeniidae family, for instance, are the primary causes of hydatid disease and cysticercosis, conditions that can lead to severe health issues like organ failure and fatal seizures. In contrast, adult tapeworms, found predominantly in the intestine across all genera, typically induce less severe symptoms, including abdominal discomfort and diarrhea. These parasites are widespread globally, contributing to a significant human health burden estimated at one million disability-adjusted life years.
The availability of twelve sequenced tapeworm genomes (Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute, 2014; Tsai et al., 2013) offers a significant resource for comparative bioinformatics, aiding in the identification of potential targets for anthelminthic drug development. However, to fully understand the functions of parasite genes, tractable model systems are essential to complement these genomic efforts. Rodent tapeworms, particularly those in the genus *Hymenolepis*, present practical advantages as convenient laboratory models (Cunningham and Olson, 2010; Pouchkina-Stantcheva et al., 2013; Siles-Lucas and Hemphill, 2002). The rat intestinal tapeworm, *Hymenolepis diminuta*, stands out as particularly well-suited for parasite biology research due to its complete life cycle being reproducible both in vivo and in vitro (Evans, 1980; Roberts, 1980; Ubelaker, 1980). Research on *H. diminuta* allows for the investigation of the genetic control of tapeworm growth and reproduction, with findings potentially applicable to a broader spectrum of difficult-to-manipulate, disease-causing flatworms. Nevertheless, the full potential of *H. diminuta* as a model system hinges on the development of advanced molecular tools, such as RNA in situ hybridization and loss-of-function perturbation techniques, which are currently under development in our laboratory.
This research details the characterization of a collection of widely applicable reagents, including plant lectins and antibodies, alongside the development of a straightforward whole-mount staining method. Utilizing confocal microscopy with these markers, we have generated a comprehensive "atlas" illustrating the diverse tissues and organ structures within *Hymenolepis diminuta*. The selection of these stains was informed by their demonstrated efficacy in labeling tissues of other flatworms, such as *Schistosoma mansoni* and *Schmidtea mediterranea*, making them valuable for comparative studies across different flatworm species. Crucially, these staining techniques will prove instrumental in assessing the consequences of gene function disruptions.
To obtain adult *Hymenolepis diminuta* tapeworms, mealworm beetles (*Tenebrio molitor*) infected with cysticercoids were acquired. These cysticercoids were extracted in 0.85% NaCl and then administered to Sprague–Dawley rats via oral gavage. Adult tapeworms were collected from the rats between 6 and 21 days post-infection. For recovery, rats were euthanized, and their small intestines were removed. The intestinal contents were then flushed out using Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). The collected tapeworms were subsequently washed multiple times in fresh HBSS using a hooked stainless steel tool.
To initiate the life cycle, cysticercoids were extracted from infected mealworm beetles. These were then incubated in an acid-pepsin solution (10 mg/mL pepsin in pH 2.0, 0.85% NaCl) at 37°C for 10 minutes. Subsequently, the cysticercoids were transferred to a bile solution (10 mg/mL sodium tauroglycocholate, 5 mg/mL trypsin in HBSS, pH 7.4) for 30 minutes at 37°C. This process successfully induced excystment in over 80% of the cysticercoids. The resulting juveniles were washed in HBSS, and all Petri dishes were pre-coated with 0.5% bovine serum albumin to prevent adherence.
To prepare adult *Hymenolepis diminuta* for staining, worms were first heat-killed by immersion in 70–75 °C distilled water for approximately five seconds to straighten them. Immediately following this, they were fixed. The fixation process involved a solution of 4% formaldehyde, 0.1% NP40, and PBSTx (phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.3% Triton-X 100) and was carried out for two hours at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C. The length of adult worms, which can vary from 5 to 40 cm based on age, influenced subsequent steps. After fixation and rinsing with PBSTx, the worms were sectioned into roughly 0.5 cm pieces. These samples were then dehydrated using ethanol and stored at -20 °C. Prior to staining, rehydrated samples underwent a treatment with Proteinase K solution for 20 minutes to an hour, depending on sample size, followed by a 10-minute post-fixation in 4% formaldehyde/PBSTx. It's important to note that Proteinase K treatment was not required for most stains and should be avoided when using anti-synapsin antibodies and phalloidin. Finally, samples were blocked using a solution containing BSA, fish gelatin, and horse serum in PBSTx before primary staining, which was performed overnight at 4 °C.
A comprehensive list of primary stains and their dilutions can be found in Supplementary Table S1. Phalloidin and lectins were used directly as they were already conjugated to fluorophores. For antibody staining, tyramide signal amplification (TSA) was employed, utilizing in-house synthesized FAM-, TAMRA-, or DyLight 633-conjugated tyramides. This TSA detection involved incubation with secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies (1:1000 dilution) for two hours at room temperature. Subsequently, a ten-minute incubation in Amplification Diluent containing the specific fluorescent tyramide (at dilutions of 1:500 for FAM- or TAMRA-tyramide, or 1:250 for DyLight 633-tyramide), hydrogen peroxide, and 4-iodophenylboronic acid in dimethylformamide within TSA buffer was performed. DAPI staining was conducted overnight at 4 °C in PBSTx at a 1 μg/mL concentration. Finally, all samples were cleared in an 80% glycerol solution and mounted on slides for subsequent imaging.
Due to their small size and difficulty in tracking, newly excysted juvenile *H. diminuta* were stained in BSA-coated Eppendorf tubes. To facilitate solution changes, tubes were microcentrifuged briefly before supernatant removal. Dehydration and Proteinase K treatment were omitted for these juveniles.
Confocal microscopy was performed using a Zeiss LSM 710. A range of objectives were employed, including 10x, 20x, 40x water, and 63x oil. Fluorophores Alexa 488/FITC/FAM, TAMRA/Rhodamine, and DyLight 633 were excited at 488 nm, 561 nm, and 633 nm, respectively. Image processing utilized Zen 2009 or ImageJ software.
This study utilizes common stains, lectins, and antibodies to examine and delineate the organization of various tissues within adult *H. diminuta*. The objective is to demonstrate that most adult tapeworm tissues are discernible through straightforward staining techniques and reagents, thus rendering this species suitable for laboratory investigations. Our findings align with prior anatomical descriptions of *H. diminuta* derived from electron microscopy and histology. For a comprehensive understanding of *H. diminuta*'s ultrastructure, readers are directed to the comprehensive review by Lumsden and Specian (1980).
The adult *Hymenolepis diminuta* tapeworm is structurally organized into three main regions: the scolex, the germinative region (or neck), and the strobila. The scolex, positioned at the anterior end, features a hookless rostellum that protrudes centrally, surrounded by four muscular suckers. Posterior to the scolex lies the neck, a crucial growth zone from which the strobila develops. The strobila itself is composed of numerous segments known as proglottids, which are not separated by internal septa. These proglottids arise sequentially from the neck, meaning the anterior proglottids are the youngest, and the posterior ones are the oldest. Each proglottid is destined to develop both male and female reproductive organs. After fertilization, embryos are housed within the uterus of each proglottid. As the proglottids move towards the posterior, they become gravid. In these gravid proglottids, the uterus enlarges to contain the developing embryos, while the other reproductive structures undergo degeneration.
Within 2-3 weeks, *H. diminuta* achieves reproductive maturity and can persist in the rat intestine for the host's lifetime. During this period, the tapeworm attains an equilibrium length of approximately 60 cm, comprising around 2000 proglottids.
The life cycle of *H. diminuta* necessitates multiple hosts for completion. Mature, gravid proglottids detach from the adult tapeworm and are expelled from the rat host in feces. These shed proglottids contain embryos that must be ingested by an appropriate arthropod intermediate host, such as beetles, to undergo larval development. In laboratory settings, gravid proglottids are fed to mealworm beetles (*T. molitor*), which develop infective cysticercoids within 8 to 14 days. These cysticercoids can persist within the beetle's hemocoel until the beetle is consumed by a rat. To propagate the life cycle in the lab, cysticercoids are extracted from the beetles and given to rats through oral gavage. Upon reaching the rat's stomach, the cysticercoids excyst, releasing juvenile tapeworms. These newly excysted juveniles, characterized by a scolex and a rudimentary body lacking a strobila, then attach to the intestinal wall of the rat using their suckers, initiating growth towards reproductive maturity.
Parasitic tapeworms, including *H. diminuta*, have undergone genome reduction and body plan simplification to optimize their fitness within host environments. These adaptations include the evolution of unique structures and strategies essential for parasitism. Notably, *H. diminuta* lacks an internal gut. Instead, it relies on a specialized external surface, the tegument, for nutrient absorption and waste excretion. This tegument, a hallmark of parasitic flatworms, functions as both the parasite's skin and digestive system, and is further characterized by numerous fine projections known as microtriches.
Beyond parasite-specific features like the tegument, *H. diminuta* shares common bilaterian tissues including nervous, muscular, and osmoregulatory systems. A key distinction is that many tissues, such as lumenal ducts, the uterus, and the tegument, are syncytial rather than epithelial.
Upon excystment, juvenile *Hymenolepis diminuta* possess a rostellum and four suckers, both highly muscular structures visualized with phalloidin. These anterior muscular components persist throughout maturation and are the primary muscle groups in the worm's front end. The tapeworm's musculature, comprising longitudinal, transverse, and circular fibers, has been extensively detailed through ultrastructural studies and is identifiable with phalloidin staining.
The musculature of the strobila's body wall is arranged circumferentially, lying directly against the tegument. Anatomically, this musculature is divided into two cortical layers: the superficial and medullary muscle. Between these layers are the tegumentary cytons. The superficial muscle, comprised of fine longitudinal and circular fibers, is situated at the base of the tegument's distal cytoplasm. While both fiber types are present, the circular fibers stain less intensely with phalloidin than the longitudinal ones. The medullary muscle consists of thicker fibers located basal to the tegumentary cytons. Phalloidin staining highlights the longitudinal medullary fibers as the most prominent muscle elements throughout the worm's length. Additionally, cortical transverse fibers delineate the boundaries of each proglottid. Beyond the body wall, phalloidin also stains the contractile components of the reproductive organs.
Muscle cells in Hymenolepis diminuta, like other flatworms, feature offset nuclei linked by cytoplasmic bridges. This musculature is vital for the tapeworm's physiology, enabling intestinal locomotion, attachment, and mating. Furthermore, these muscle cells serve as a site for glycogen storage.
The adult *H. diminuta*, much like other flatworms, exhibits a nervous system comprising both central and peripheral elements. This system is readily visualized through anti-synapsin antibody staining (Fig. 3A–D), a technique previously employed to map the nervous systems of various flatworm species. The primary neural structure, termed the cephalic ganglia or "brain," is located at the base of the suckers. From these ganglia, two lateral nerve cords and two median nerves extend along the tapeworm's length. Notably, the fundamental organization of the cephalic ganglia and lateral nerve cords is established even in newly excysted juveniles, with complexity increasing as the tapeworm matures.
The cephalic ganglia, consisting of two large, laterally positioned ganglia linked by a transverse commissure, form the tapeworm's "brain." These ganglia extend anteriorly to the suckers and rostellum, with neuronal projections visible within the rostellar capsule and throughout the suckers. While synapsin staining effectively highlights general neuronal structures, it doesn't capture the full complexity of neuronal connections in *H. diminuta*. For instance, acetylcholinesterase (AChE) staining reveals distinct rings within the rostellar capsule, a feature not apparent with synapsin alone. Supplemental Movie S2 offers a detailed look at synapsin-stained innervations in the adult scolex. For more in-depth information on scolex innervation, consult prior studies utilizing AChE and paraldehyde-fuchsin staining.
In the strobila, the nervous system exhibits a consistent, stereotypical organization. Longitudinal nerve cords and associated nerves extend the full length of the strobila. Furthermore, each proglottid features three transverse commissures linking to the lateral nerve cords. While peripheral nerves permeate the parenchyma, resolving them with whole-mount synapsin antibody staining is challenging. Previous studies have successfully mapped specific nerve projections by employing high-resolution imaging of sections stained with neuronal markers, such as anti-serotonin sera.
Synapsin staining, when viewed at higher magnification, reveals finer details of the nervous system in *H. diminuta*. This technique allows for the visualization of minor nerve cords situated alongside the main lateral nerve cords, structures previously identified through acetylcholinesterase (AChE) staining. While synapsin antibodies provide a valuable general marker for the nervous system, they did not definitively show "motor end plates" at neuromuscular junctions on the lateral nerve cords, an observation made with AChE staining. Therefore, additional tools may be required to specifically delineate particular neuronal connections.
Specific points within the reproductive system of *H. diminuta* show clear innervation. Synapsin antibodies strongly label the cirrus pouch and genital atrium at the poral edge. Peripheral neurons also extend to the oviduct and vitelline duct, potentially aiding in the coordination of fertilization and the union of the zygote with a vitelline cell. While most other reproductive components, including gonads, uterus, and sperm ducts, do not stain with synapsin or other neuronal markers like AChE histochemistry, the possibility of innervation in these areas cannot be dismissed. This is particularly relevant given the known roles of neuropeptides in regulating reproductive development and function in planarians.
The tapeworm tegument's surface features sensory projections called sensilla. These ciliated, dendritic structures, depicted in Fig. 3G, are detectable with anti-acetylated α-tubulin antibodies (Fig. 3H, I). Widely present in tapeworm species, sensilla are believed to mediate chemosensation and mechanosensation.
The osmoregulatory system of *H. diminuta*, similar to protonephridia in other flatworms, comprises three key components. These include excretory canals, networks of fine tubules, and flame cells, which act as rudimentary nephrons for filtering waste.
The osmoregulatory system of *H. diminuta* includes excretory canals that can be visualized using Lens culinaris lectin (LCA) and antibodies against phospho serine/threonine and phospho tyrosine (Fig. 4A–E). Within the scolex, these canals reach into the rostellum (Fig. 4A). Across the adult worm's body, the excretory canals form a ladder-like structure with both longitudinal and transverse components (Fig. 4B, C). Paired dorso-ventrally and positioned laterally, the longitudinal canals are linked by a single transverse canal in each proglottid (Fig. 4B, C). A key distinction is that the dorsal excretory canal (DEC) is narrower than the ventral excretory canal (VEC) (Fig. 4D, D′), with transverse canals exclusively connecting to the VEC (Fig. 4D′). These canals then connect to a network of tubules that culminate in flame cells (Fig. 4E, F). These flame cells, found within the medullary muscle and parenchyma (Fig. 4F), directly link to collecting ducts.
Figure 4G illustrates the structure of flame cells and their associated collecting ducts. Different staining methods, as shown in Figures 4H-K, can differentiate components of these structures. Specifically, anti-acetylated α-tubulin antibodies highlight the flame cell and its cilia within the collecting duct. Lens culinaris lectin (LCA) stains the collecting duct's cytoplasm but not the flame cell itself. Jacalin, a lectin, marks the boundary between the flame cell and collecting duct near the rootlet. Lastly, anti-phospho tyrosine antibodies label the collecting duct and the ciliated flame, but not the flame cell itself.
Flame cells are thought to filter H. diminuta's body fluids, directing waste into the canal system via collecting ducts. While longitudinal canals can expel waste directly from the posterior proglottid, the tegument also contributes to excretion through vesicular transport.
The parasite *H. diminuta* possesses a hookless rostellum, which it can actively move. This maneuverability likely assists in its attachment to and movement along the intestinal wall. Large vesicles within the rostellum suggest potential secretory functions. While the rostellum shares the same tegument as the rest of the tapeworm, its surface exhibits distinct lectin-binding properties. Specifically, lectins like sWGA and PNA bind to the rostellar surface, whereas LCA does not. This differential lectin binding indicates that the tegument of the rostellum is a specialized structure. This specialization, observed in newly excysted juveniles, is maintained in adult worms.
Microtriches on the rostellar surface possess a unique fine structure, characterized by more elongated projections and filamentous tips compared to those on the body tegument. This difference in microtriche morphology, along with distinct lectin binding specificity, suggests a specialized carbohydrate composition and functional specialization of the rostellar tegument compared to the rest of the tapeworm's surface.
As *Hymenolepis diminuta* matures, each proglottid develops both male and female reproductive organs. The diagram in Figure 6A illustrates this reproductive system. Sperm originates in the testis lobules and travels via sperm ducts to the external seminal vesicle (ESV). From there, it moves to the internal seminal vesicle (ISV), which connects to the cirrus, the male ejaculatory organ. The cirrus can protrude from the proglottid to fertilize another proglottid through the genital atrium (GA), the female genital pore. Sperm is then introduced into the vagina (V) and stored in the seminal receptacle (SR). Due to protandrous development, sperm can be stored in the SR even before the ovary is mature enough to produce oocytes. Fertilization occurs when mature oocytes and sperm meet in the oviduct (OD). Subsequently, the zygote is joined by a vitelline cell from the vitelline gland (VG). Situated above the uterus, the vitelline gland provides cells that form protective layers around the developing embryo. Initially small, the uterus expands significantly as fertilization progresses to house the growing embryos.
The intricate reproductive organs of *H. diminuta* have been successfully labeled using a variety of lectins, antibodies, and stains. A simple DAPI stain, for instance, allows for a general assessment of each proglottid's reproductive maturity. This staining method clearly highlights the gonads and genital pore components due to their distinct morphologies. Furthermore, the external seminal vesicle, internal seminal vesicle, and seminal receptacle become visible with DAPI staining once they have accumulated sperm.
Anti-acetylated α-tubulin antibodies and sWGA effectively label the sperm ducts connecting the gonads and accessory reproductive structures. sWGA further identifies the cirrus and its link to the internal seminal vesicle, which phalloidin also stains. Additionally, anti-phospho tyrosine antibodies highlight the oviduct and its intricate connections to the ovary, seminal receptacle, vitelline gland, and uterus.
The vitelline gland's overlapping position with the uterus makes it challenging to discern with DAPI staining (Fig. 6B). However, lectins like PNA strongly label this structure (Fig. 6G–H’). Before fertilization, vitelline cells reside solely within the vitelline gland (Fig. 6G′). Post-fertilization, these cells are paired with zygotes as they move into the uterus. Consequently, PNA staining becomes evident within the uterus, indicated by the yellow dotted line (Fig. 6H′).
Within each proglottid, three testis lobules, a central ovary, and copulatory organs develop at one lateral surface, designated the "poral" edge. Generally, two testis lobules are located on the aporal side of the ovary, with one on the poral side, though minor variations exist among proglottids.
Oocytes are specified within the lobate ovary. Early in development, the ovaries are small and have few lobes, with abundant cell divisions (indicated by anti-phospho histone H3 staining) that produce female gametes. As development advances, the ovaries grow larger and become highly lobate, with a decrease in cell division frequency.
As development progresses, the testes grow larger and more intricate (Fig. 7D–F′). Male gametes originate from spermatogonial stem cells, undergoing four mitotic and two meiotic divisions to produce 64 spermatozoa (Kelsoe et al., 1977). These mature spermatozoa are elongated, featuring a flattened head and a long tail, measuring 250–300 μm (Kelsoe et al., 1977). Typically, rosettes of 8 or 16 spermatogonia can be observed on the outer surface of the testes lobule (Fig. 7F). Mature sperm is then released into the testes' lumen (Fig. 7F′). This testicular structure shares similarities with that of the planarian *S. mediterranea* (Wang et al., 2010), although direct comparisons require further molecular marker analysis.
Given the challenges of its multi-host life cycle, *H. diminuta* prioritizes rapid reproduction. Following fertilization, the uterus enlarges to accommodate developing embryos. Ultimately, reproductive organs degrade, leaving the uterus to dominate each proglottid. These embryos develop protective coatings, detectable with anti-phospho serine/threonine antibodies. The posterior strobila, containing gravid proglottids, is shed via apolysis. These proglottids are expelled with the host's stool, and the life cycle depends on arthropod hosts ingesting this embryo-rich material.
This study shows that common lab reagents can effectively label specific tissues and organs in *H. diminuta*. Confocal microscopy revealed tissue architecture consistent with prior anatomical descriptions. Consequently, we've established a robust, straightforward protocol for visualizing *H. diminuta* organ structures. These findings aim to provide a valuable resource for parasitologists.
The rat intestinal tapeworm, *Hymenolepis diminuta*, offers a valuable model for investigating fundamental parasite biology. Researchers can leverage this system to identify and characterize parasite-specific genes crucial for growth and reproduction. Furthermore, *H. diminuta* is suitable for drug screening to identify compounds that impair parasite survival. The staining methods detailed in this study will aid in analyzing the consequences of gene function loss in *H. diminuta*, even without requiring specific molecular markers. Ultimately, these advancements expand the resources available to scientists seeking novel anthelminthic drugs to combat widespread parasitic flatworm infections.
