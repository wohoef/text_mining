The additive, manganese hydroxychloride, is deemed an effective source of manganese, capable of fulfilling the manganese needs across all animal species and categories. This conclusion is supported by evidence from two studies on fattening chickens, which demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in manganese deposition in bone and liver, confirming its bioavailability. The Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP) has assessed manganese hydroxychloride, which contains at least 45% manganese. They determined that its use in animal nutrition is safe for consumers, provided that the authorized maximum total manganese content in complete feed is not exceeded (100 mg Mn/kg for fish and 150 mg Mn/kg for other species in the EU). This additive is expected to replace other manganese sources in feed and does not alter tissue deposition differently than manganese sulphate. Furthermore, its use is not anticipated to harm the environment. However, due to a lack of specific data, FEEDAP could not reach a conclusion regarding the safety for users handling the additive.

Manganese (Mn), a common element comprising 0.1% of Earth's crust, is found in over 100 minerals. While its elemental form is not natural, Mn2+ and Mn3+ are its key biological oxidation states.
Manganese is a vital dietary mineral for mammals, playing a crucial role in various biological processes. Its functions include acting as a component of metalloenzymes like superoxide dismutase, arginase, and pyruvate carboxylase, and participating in the metabolism of amino acids, lipids, and carbohydrates. Furthermore, enzymes such as glycosyltransferases and xylosyltransferases, essential for proteoglycan synthesis and bone formation, are influenced by manganese levels in animals. Insufficient manganese in livestock can lead to significant issues, including stunted growth, bone deformities, reduced reproductive capacity, ataxia in newborns, and metabolic dysfunctions affecting lipids and carbohydrates. Researchers have also examined the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity of manganese.
While manganese hydroxychloride is a new feed additive in the EU, other manganese compounds are already approved as nutritional trace element supplements under EU Commission Regulation (EC) No 1334/2003.
EFSA has previously assessed various manganese-based feed additives. As part of a re-evaluation process, the EFSA FEEDAP Panel issued four opinions on additives such as manganous chloride tetrahydrate, manganous oxide, manganous sulphate monohydrate, and manganese chelates of amino acids and glycine (2013a,b,c, 2016). Earlier, EFSA also provided an opinion in 2005 on the safety of chelated forms of iron, copper, manganese, and zinc with synthetic feed-grade glycine. Additionally, three opinions were published (2008a, 2009, 2010) regarding a manganese chelate of hydroxy analogue of methionine. Preceding these, the Scientific Committee on Animal Nutrition (SCAN) released a report in 2002 concerning manganomanganic oxide in animal feed.

Authorization is sought for manganese hydroxychloride, an inorganic manganese compound, as a nutritional additive (functional group: 'Compounds of trace elements') for all animal species/categories. This additive, currently unauthorized in the EU, would be incorporated into feedingstuffs up to the EU's maximum permitted manganese levels in complete feeds.
Within this opinion, "manganese hydroxychloride" refers to the manganese compound designed to provide the element for nutritional purposes in living organisms. This compound undergoes further manufacturing steps to become a formulated additive, consistently termed "the additive" in this scientific document. The complete manufacturing process is detailed in the technical dossier.
Manganese hydroxychloride, also known as tribasic manganese chloride (TBMC) or basic manganese chloride, has the molecular formula Mn2(OH)3Cl and a molecular weight of 196.35 Da. Its CAS number is 39438-40-9, and it theoretically contains 56% manganese.
The additive's specifications require over 44% manganese, 16–19% chlorine, and under 2% moisture. Analytical data from two sets of five batches each supported these specifications.
Analysis of the additive revealed an average manganese content of 45.9% (ranging from 45.2–46.8%) in the initial data set, and 45.6% (ranging from 44.9–46.1%) in the subsequent set. Moisture levels varied between 0.4% and 0.8% for the first data and 0.3% to 1.0% for the second. Chlorine content, reported only for the second data set, averaged 14.0% (13.4–14.4%), falling below the specified range. X-ray diffraction of one batch indicated 72.2% tribasic manganese chloride (TBMC). This TBMC concentration would yield only 40.4% manganese, less than the observed average of 45.8%. Therefore, it is presumed the additive also includes approximately 7% manganous oxide, a component of its manufacturing.
Analysis of the second set of five batches, using X-ray diffraction, revealed impurities originating from the manufacturing process's raw materials. The predominant impurities, quantified as oxides, included iron (5.42%), aluminum (4.84%), silicon (1.93%), potassium (0.89%), sodium (0.65%), and calcium (0.28%). Other analyzed impurities, such as barium, magnesium, cobalt, chromium, copper, molybdenum, nickel, titanium, and zinc (also expressed as oxides), collectively averaged 1.3%. Specific data on nickel content, requested due to potential concerns, showed levels between 0.03% and 0.04%.
Analysis of five batches revealed specific levels of heavy metals, arsenic, and fluorine. Cadmium ranged from 0.69–0.82 mg/kg, lead from 26–29 mg/kg, arsenic from 28–30 mg/kg, and fluorine from 2.6–4.1 mg/kg. Additionally, dioxins and the combined total of dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs were quantified in three batches. Dioxin levels were between 0.091–0.127 ng WHO-PCDD/F-TEQ/kg, while the sum of dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs was 0.128–0.139 ng WHO-PCDD/F-PCB-TEQ/kg. All these measured concentrations align with the safety limits established by Directive 2002/32/EC for trace element feed additives.
This granular, odorless additive is olive green to light brown. It exhibits low water solubility and has a bulk density of 830 kg/m3, with a tapped density of 937 kg/m3.
Sieve analysis of five batches revealed a mean particle size of 221–238 μm. Laser diffraction on three of these batches confirmed the absence of particles smaller than 90 μm in diameter.
Using the Stauber–Heubach method, the additive's dusting potential was assessed across three batches, averaging 95 mg/m³ (range: 75–110). Stability studies are typically unnecessary for inorganic trace element compounds.
To assess the additive's uniform distribution, a study was conducted using laying hen feed containing 24 mg/kg background manganese. After adding 100 mg Mn from TBMC per kg of complete feed, 15 subsamples were analyzed for total manganese. The resulting coefficient of variation was 7.5% of the total 111 mg Mn/kg.
Manganese hydroxychloride can be added to animal feed, either directly or through premixtures, for all species. Its use is limited by EU regulations to a maximum total manganese content of 100 mg/kg for fish and 150 mg/kg for other animals.
The FEEDAP Panel previously assessed manganese tolerance and requirements. The applicant submitted five studies to demonstrate the additive's safety.
Initially, the applicant provided four studies on chickens for fattening (two long-term, two short-term by Conly et al., 2012). However, these were deemed insufficient as tolerance studies. They lacked crucial endpoints like haematology, clinical chemistry, and gross pathology, as mandated by relevant guidelines (Regulation (EC) No 429/2008) and EFSA guidance. Additionally, two studies were too short. Consequently, EFSA requested, and the applicant subsequently supplied, a fifth study that satisfied the necessary tolerance study criteria.
A study involving 640 one-day-old male Cobb 500 chickens for fattening was conducted over 35 days. The chickens were divided into eight treatment groups, with each group consisting of 10 pens, and each pen housing eight birds, totaling 80 chickens per treatment. The experimental diets, based on maize-soya bean meal, included varying levels of supplemental manganese (Mn) from either manganese sulphate (MnSO4) or the additive being evaluated. These supplementation levels were 60, 120, 960, and 1,920 mg Mn/kg of complete feed. The 60 mg/kg dose represented the chickens' manganese requirements as per NRC (1994), while 1,920 mg/kg was close to the maximum tolerable level for poultry (2,000 mg Mn/kg feed, NRC 2005). Chickens had unrestricted access to feed and water throughout the study. A starter diet was provided for the initial 21 days, followed by a grower diet until the study concluded. Total manganese concentrations were measured in both starter and grower diets. At the end of the 35-day period, blood samples were collected from eight birds per treatment for haematology and blood chemistry analysis. These birds were then euthanized for macroscopic examination and tissue collection. Manganese content was assessed in bone (tibiotarsus) to determine bioavailability, and in edible tissues such as breast muscle and liver.
Before statistical analysis, an outlier test (Grubb's test) was performed, leading to the removal of one pen from each of the TBMC 60, 120, and 1,920 mg/kg treatment groups for feed consumption and feed/gain ratio analysis. The statistical unit for the study was the pen. A bi-factorial ANOVA was then used to analyze the data, considering the impact of both manganese source and supplementation level, along with their interaction. Comparisons between the main factor levels were conducted using the Tukey test, or Tukey–Kramer when outlier removal resulted in differing replicate numbers.
Performance metrics like average daily gain, feed intake, and feed-to-gain ratio generally showed no differences across treatment groups. However, chickens given the highest dose of tribasic manganese chloride (TBMC) exhibited reduced eating and growth compared to other treatments. At this elevated TBMC level, mortality appeared higher, and feed intake and weight gain significantly decreased, while the feed-to-gain ratio increased. In contrast, the highest supplemental manganese sulfate (MnSO4) level did not significantly impact these parameters. Both manganese sources led to a dose-dependent rise in bone manganese concentration, indicating similar bioavailability, though TBMC showed slightly lower values at the two higher doses. While breast muscle manganese levels remained unaffected by supplementation, the two highest manganese doses (960 and 1,920 mg/kg) significantly elevated manganese concentration in the liver.
Organ examination showed no treatment-induced alterations. Haematological data, however, could not be fully evaluated due to significant discrepancies between two laboratories (e.g., mean WBC counts of 7.1 vs. 1.2 x 10^3/μL). Statistical analysis relied on merged values, hindering further assessment of these blood parameters.
In the highest TBMC group, several serum enzymes (AP, AST, GLDH, LDH, and CK) were significantly reduced compared to both equivalent MnSO4 levels and lower TBMC manganese levels. Additionally, serum potassium concentrations averaged 6.9 mmol/L (ranging 6.4–7.7), exceeding the physiological maximum of 5 mmol/L for male fattening chickens of this age. However, the FEEDAP Panel determined these enzyme alterations did not intrinsically signify an adverse effect.
At the highest concentration, near the maximum tolerable level for poultry, TBMC was less safe for fattening chickens than manganese sulfate. However, at lower concentrations, no differences were observed between the two manganese sources in terms of zootechnical or clinical chemistry parameters.
The FEEDAP Panel determined that manganese hydroxychloride is a safe manganese source for all target species. This conclusion, based on a chicken tolerance study, applies when the additive is used within the EU's authorized maximum total manganese content in complete feed.
The FEEDAP Panel previously assessed the safety of manganese compounds as nutritional feed additives, considering their metabolic and toxicological profiles, as well as consumer exposure. These prior evaluations concluded that using manganese compounds in animal nutrition poses no consumer safety concerns, provided the authorized maximum total manganese levels in feed are maintained. Therefore, this report focuses solely on the specific additive currently under review.
Manganese deposition in breast muscle and liver was studied in chickens given varying doses (60, 120, 960, and 1,920 mg Mn/kg feed) of either TBMC or MnSO4. The experimental details are in Section 3.2.1, with results presented in Table 1.
Across eight experimental groups, breast muscle manganese content ranged from 0.9 to 2.3 mg/kg dry matter. No significant differences were observed due to manganese sources or supplementation levels.
Manganese levels in the liver were unaffected by the manganese source. However, higher supplementation rates significantly increased liver manganese concentrations, regardless of the source.
For chickens, manganese levels in muscle and liver did not rise when supplemented at or below the EU's maximum authorized feed levels. Furthermore, no significant differences in manganese deposition were observed between TBMC and manganese sulfate in these chickens. While specific data for TBMC in milk and eggs were unavailable, existing research suggests that manganese deposition in eggs is similar for both organic and inorganic sources. Additionally, inorganic manganese sources typically lead to only minor deposition in edible products like milk and eggs. The FEEDAP Panel concluded that manganese hydroxychloride does not significantly increase manganese levels in edible tissues.
While animal products contribute little to dietary manganese, feed supplements shouldn't significantly boost consumer oral exposure by increasing manganese in edible tissues.
The FEEDAP Panel determined that manganese hydroxychloride is safe for consumers, assuming authorized manganese levels in complete feed are maintained. This conclusion is based on the expectation that it will replace other manganese sources without altering tissue deposition compared to manganese sulfate, thus not increasing consumer manganese exposure. However, the Panel could not assess user safety due to a lack of specific studies.
Information regarding skin and eye irritation or sensitization from TBMC was absent. However, its material safety data sheet suggests that, as a metal chloride, TBMC could cause irritation to skin and eyes, potentially triggering allergic reactions.
The FEEDAP Panel could not definitively assess user safety regarding inhalation. While manganese is known to be toxic when inhaled, and the additive exhibited a dusting potential of approximately 95 mg Mn/m3, crucial data were missing. Specifically, the exact dust particle size and the manganese concentration within the dust were not determined, despite indications of a high particle size (no particles below 90 µm).

The FEEDAP Panel previously assessed the environmental safety of various manganese compounds as nutritional feed additives. Their conclusion was that using these compounds in animal feed for all species poses no environmental risk. This assessment remains unchanged, as manganese hydroxychloride is expected to simply replace other manganese compounds.
Efficacy was supported by two studies: a short-term chicken study comparing TBMC and MnSO4 bioavailability, and data from the tolerance study mentioned in Section 3.2.1.
A study was conducted over 21 days using 252 one-day-old Cobb x Cobb chickens of both sexes, randomly distributed into seven treatment groups. Each group consisted of six pens, with six birds per pen. For the initial nine days, all chickens received a basal maize-soya bean meal diet containing 45 mg Mn/kg, aiming to maximize manganese absorption. Subsequently, for the remaining 12 days, they were fed assigned treatment diets. These diets were supplemented with either TBMC or MnSO4 at levels of 30, 60, or 130 mg Mn/kg. Body weight gain and feed intake were monitored throughout the study. At the end of the 21-day period, all birds were euthanized. Tissue samples from the tibia and liver were collected from two birds per pen for mineral analysis using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, with pen-level averages used for statistical evaluation.
Manganese levels, but not their source, significantly impacted manganese content in the tibia and liver. Neither manganese source nor levels significantly affected feed intake or body weight gain.
Both short-term and long-term studies demonstrated that manganese hydroxychloride is a bioavailable manganese source. This was evidenced by a dose-dependent rise in manganese levels in bone and liver, confirming its efficacy.
Beyond standard Feed Hygiene Regulation and good manufacturing practices, the FEEDAP Panel deems no additional post-market monitoring necessary.
Manganese hydroxychloride is deemed a safe manganese source for all species. Its use is safe provided total manganese levels in complete feed do not exceed EU authorized limits (100 mg/kg for fish, 150 mg/kg for other species).
Manganese hydroxychloride is a safe consumer choice in animal nutrition, provided authorized manganese levels in feed are maintained. It can replace other manganese sources without altering tissue deposition compared to manganese sulfate.
Due to a lack of specific data, the FEEDAP Panel cannot determine the safety of manganese hydroxychloride for users during handling.
Manganese hydroxychloride, when used in animal feed for any species, is not anticipated to harm the environment.
Two studies in chickens for fattening showed manganese hydroxychloride is a bioavailable source, increasing manganese deposition in bone and liver in a dose-dependent manner. The FEEDAP Panel concluded this additive effectively meets manganese requirements for all animal species.
To distinguish it from other manganese chloride hydroxides, the FEEDAP Panel suggests "Di manganese chloride tri hydroxide" as the compound's name, mirroring the authorization of di copper chloride tri hydroxide.
Cadmium and arsenic specifications need revision to align with Directive 2002/32/EC. Additionally, the maximum manganous oxide content requires clear definition.

An application has been submitted for manganese hydroxychloride (Mn2(OH)3Cl) to be authorized as a feed additive for all animal species and categories. This brown, free-flowing crystalline powder, containing at least 44% total manganese, falls under the "nutritional additives" and "compounds of trace elements" functional group, as per Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, Annex I.
The applicant proposed maximum total manganese levels of 100-150 mg/kg in feedingstuffs. This feed additive, containing 16-19% chloride, is designed for incorporation into premixtures and feedingstuffs.
The applicant proposed using the EPA's ion chromatography method (300.0, Rev.2.1) for measuring chloride in the feed additive. However, the EURL advises employing the Community method, which relies on titration, to determine chlorine content in the additive.
For measuring total manganese in the feed additive, premixtures, and feedingstuffs, the applicant proposed the CEN method (EN 15510), which is ring-trial validated and uses Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES). The EURL had previously endorsed two other ring-trial validated methods during the manganese group evaluation: EN 15621, an ICP-AES method requiring pressure digestion, and EN ISO 6869, which employs Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS). The latter also includes a Community method, further validated by the UK Food Standards Agency.
For official control, the EURL endorses all previously mentioned methods for quantifying total manganese in feed additives, premixtures, and feedingstuffs, given their acceptable performance.
No further testing or validation of these methods by the National Reference Laboratories consortium (as per Article 10, Regulation (EC) No 378/2005) is deemed necessary.
