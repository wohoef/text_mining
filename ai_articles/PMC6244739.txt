Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) presents a significant challenge in the United States, contributing to a high mortality rate with an overall survival rate below 5%. This aggressive cancer is characterized by the presence of cancer stem cell (CSC) markers, such as the epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), which is frequently overexpressed in EAC and other epithelial carcinomas. CSCs are recognized for their role in initiating cancer, driving metastasis, promoting recurrence, and conferring resistance to therapies. Previous research suggests that elevated EpCAM levels in EAC may correlate with more aggressive disease and poorer clinical outcomes. This study aimed to investigate whether standard chemotherapeutic agents could induce EpCAM expression in EAC, potentially leading to enhanced malignant potential and increased drug resistance. To address this, EpCAM expression was examined in 20 primary human EAC tissues and their adjacent normal counterparts. Additionally, EpCAM levels were assessed in a human EAC cell line (OE-19), a pre-malignant Barrett’s Esophagus cell line (Bar-T), and a benign esophageal cell line (HET 1-A) using immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, and qRT-PCR, respectively. The research further explored drug-induced resistance in OE-19-derived cell spheres treated with a combination of adriamycin, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil (ACF), a common chemotherapeutic regimen. Survival, adhesion, and flow cytometric assays were employed to evaluate drug resistance. The tumor-forming capabilities of cells surviving ACF treatment were then assessed both in vitro through spheroid formation and in vivo via xenograft assays. The findings revealed high EpCAM expression in EAC tissues and the OE-19 EAC cell line, contrasting with its absence in benign esophageal tissues and cell lines (HET 1-A and Bar-T). OE-19 cell spheres exhibited drug resistance, with significantly induced EpCAM expression compared to non-sphere OE-19 cells. Exposure of these spheres to ACF resulted in further EpCAM mRNA and protein upregulation up to 48 hours, followed by a decrease at 72 hours. Crucially, downregulating EpCAM expression through RNA interference enhanced the efficacy of ACF in killing OE-19 cells. Moreover, increased EpCAM expression was found to coincide with the CSC marker CD90 and was associated with aggressive in vivo growth of OE-19 cell spheres. In conclusion, these results indicate that ACF-induced EpCAM expression in EAC reflects the selection of a CSC subpopulation, which contributes to tumor development and drug resistance. This suggests that EpCAM may serve as a critical marker and potential therapeutic target in EAC.

Esophageal cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related death, characterized by a poor 5-year survival rate of 10-15% and a rising incidence. Over recent decades, there has been a notable shift in diagnoses from esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) to esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC). A significant factor contributing to EAC's deadliness is late-stage presentation, often when the disease is advanced. This, combined with limited effective treatments for advanced EAC, exacerbates the poor prognosis. Despite numerous efforts using various chemotherapeutic agents (CTA) historically, the outcomes for advanced EAC treated with chemotherapy have remained unsatisfactory. Common CTA for EAC include cisplatin, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and anthracycline derivatives like adriamycin. These agents are frequently administered in combination, such as 5-FU with cisplatin, or as a triple regimen known as ACF (Adriamycin-Cisplatin-5-FU).
Epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), a transmembrane glycoprotein first identified by Kaprowski et al., is widely expressed across various epithelial cancers. Initially noted for its overexpression in nearly all colorectal adenocarcinomas, subsequent research has confirmed its presence in most major epithelial carcinomas, including Barrett's adenocarcinoma and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). The mechanisms by which EpCAM enhances malignant potential are thought to involve cell cycle signaling and the promotion of proto-oncogenic activities. EpCAM possesses an extracellular epidermal growth factor-like domain and plays a role in cell adhesion to the basement membrane. Furthermore, EpCAM has been linked to the Wnt signaling pathway, which can foster cancer stem cell (CSC) characteristics and contribute to chemotherapy resistance. Our prior work demonstrated that EpCAM expression increases in hepatocellular carcinoma cells following chemotherapeutic treatment, suggesting a role in cell survival. While EpCAM has been observed in esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) previously, its specific function in this cancer type remained unclear. A recent study indicated that standard chemotherapy treatment leads to increased EpCAM expression, correlating with the emergence of residual cells exhibiting a mesenchymal stem cell-like phenotype, which could explain enhanced drug resistance. Considering its widespread expression in epithelial cancers, EpCAM is now being investigated as a potential therapeutic target. This study aimed to ascertain whether standard chemotherapeutic agents can induce EpCAM expression in EAC, consequently elevating its malignant potential and drug resistance.
To investigate EpCAM expression in esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), researchers prospectively collected 40 tissue samples from 20 patients who had undergone tumor resection. These samples comprised paired sets of cancerous EAC tissue and adjacent benign esophageal tissue. The collection was facilitated by the James Graham Brown Cancer Center Bio-Repository at the University of Louisville, adhering to an approved Institutional Review Board (IRB) protocol. The collected tissues were preserved by fixation in 10% buffered formalin for 48 hours, followed by processing for paraffin embedding. Subsequently, 5 μm sections were prepared and mounted onto glass slides. Histopathological evaluation was performed by two independent pathologists, who were blinded to the clinical history of the subjects. These evaluations involved staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). EpCAM protein expression was subsequently assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC). The DAKO EnVision™+System Kit was employed according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The IHC procedure involved deparaffinization, hydration, and washing steps. Peroxidase blocking was carried out for 5 minutes, followed by another wash. Slides were then incubated with a monoclonal mouse anti-EpCAM antibody (1:100 dilution) for 60 minutes at room temperature. After rinsing, a labeled polymer was applied for 30 minutes at room temperature. Visualization was achieved using the chromogenic substrate diaminobenzidine, and the slides were counterstained with methyl green. Each run included a negative control sample. The demographics of the 20 study subjects are detailed in Table 1, indicating 18 males and 2 females with a mean age of 67 years. Notably, EpCAM staining was positive in all 20 EAC tissue specimens, while only 2 of the 20 adjacent benign tissues showed positive staining.
To represent benign esophageal mucosa, SV40 large T transfected HET 1-A esophageal epithelial cells were utilized. Barrett’s Esophagus mucosa was represented by human hTERT-immortalized nonneoplastic Bar-T cells, generously provided by R. Souza and S.J. Spechler. Esophageal adenocarcinoma was represented by OE-19 cells, sourced from a 72-year-old male patient with adenocarcinoma of the gastric/esophageal junction. These cell lines were cultured under specific conditions. HET 1-A cells were maintained in Bronchial Epithelial Cell medium (BEGM BulletKit) with penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/ml), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Bar-T cells were cultured in keratinocyte basal medium 2 (KBM-2), while OE-19 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Both Bar-T and OE-19 cell cultures were supplemented with penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/ml), and 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in 75 ml flasks. The authenticity of all cell lines was confirmed by Bio-Synthesis Inc.
For treating EAC, adriamycin, 5-FU, and cisplatin are commonly employed chemotherapeutic agents. The drug concentrations employed in this study were derived from considerations of both clinically relevant dosages and established pharmacokinetic profiles. Drug dosages were standardized by considering an average patient's height and referencing the median range from various dosing protocols. This standardized dosage, when multiplied by the average patient size, allowed for the calculation of the total milligrams administered. This value was then combined with the volume of distribution for each agent to estimate a theoretical concentration of the chemotherapeutic within the total body fluid. Finally, this concentration was divided by five to approximate the amount present in the extracellular fluid, representing the concentration that would directly interact with tumor cells.
To generate cell spheres, OE-19 cells were cultured in ultra-low attachment 60 mm dishes. The culture medium consisted of serum-free DMEM/F12 supplemented with insulin (5 μg/ml), human recombinant epidermal growth factor (20 ng/ml), basic fibroblast growth factor (10 ng/ml), and bovine serum albumin (0.4%). Once cell spheres formed, they were subjected to treatment with either culture medium alone or medium containing chemotherapeutic agents. These treatments were administered for varying durations, specifically 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours, to evaluate cell survival. The chemotherapeutic agents were applied as single agents or in combination, including adriamycin, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil (ACF), to replicate clinical treatment protocols for EAC. Cell survival was quantified using a MTT assay. Following treatment, both intact cell spheres and dissociated cells were collected and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with MTT (1 mg/ml in PBS) for 4 hours. After removing the MTT solution, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 10 minutes. The resulting absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an optical density plate reader. Background noise was accounted for by subtracting the absorbance of wells containing DMSO and MTT but no cells. The average optical density values from triplicate wells for each treatment condition served as the measure of cell survival. To corroborate the MTT assay findings, cell adhesion assays were performed. These assays utilized 6-well plates pre-coated with a 40 μg/ml Collagen I solution in PBS, a standard method for assessing the effects of drug treatment on cancer cells. After a 72-hour exposure to chemotherapeutic agents, the cell spheres were dissociated using 10 mM EDTA in DMEM. The dissociated cells were then washed and added to the Collagen I-coated wells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 20 minutes to facilitate adherence. Non-adherent cells were removed by washing four times with DMEM. Subsequently, DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added, and the cells were incubated for 48 hours to allow for recovery. Finally, cell counts and image capture were performed using an Olympus 1X51 microscope.
Following drug treatment, OE-19 cells were detached using trypsin-EDTA and collected via centrifugation. The cells were then washed and resuspended in a buffer containing fetal calf serum. Subsequently, a FITC-conjugated anti-EpCAM monoclonal antibody was added to the cell suspension and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. After a washing step, the cells were analyzed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo 7.6.1 software.
To analyze protein expression, total protein was extracted from samples on ice using a lysis buffer. An equal volume of loading buffer, containing Tris/HCl, SDS, glycerin, β-mercaptoethanol, and bromophenol blue, was added and mixed. The samples were then denatured at 95°C for 5 minutes before being loaded onto 10% SDS gels and electrophoresed at 100 V. Following transfer to a membrane, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with either a mouse monoclonal anti-EpCAM antibody or a mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH antibody. After washing, a secondary donkey anti-mouse HRP antibody was applied. Antibody-antigen complexes were visualized using an ECL Western blot detection kit, and the resulting protein bands were quantified through densitometry.
To investigate the relationship between drug treatment and EpCAM expression, we utilized small interfering RNA (siRNA). Qiagen synthesized a specific siRNA targeting EpCAM mRNA (TACSTD1) and a negative control siRNA. OE-19 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells per well. Transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent, following the manufacturer's protocol, with a total of 100 pmol/well siRNA. Following a 24-hour transfection period, the cells were subjected to drug exposure and subsequently analyzed by Western blot and cell adhesion assays.
Total RNA was extracted from cells post-drug treatment using TRIzol reagent. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was then synthesized from this RNA, following the manufacturer's protocol for the RNA PCR kit. Quantitative PCR was performed on an ABI7300 real-time PCR system to assess gene expression. Fold differences between samples were determined using the comparative cycle time (Ct) method, with EpCAM expression normalized to β-actin as an endogenous reference. The final results were presented as fold changes in gene expression.
To assess the cancer stem cell (CSC)-like nature of residual cells following chemotherapy, OE-19 cells were treated with adriamycin, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil (ACF), or individual agents, for 48 hours. These treated cells were then fixed and stained. FITC-conjugated anti-EpCAM, PE-conjugated anti-CD90, and anti-CD44 antibodies were used for immunocytochemical analysis, with images captured using an Olympus IX51-DP72 imaging system. For immunohistochemical staining of tumor tissue sections, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide and 5% goat serum. Subsequently, the tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies against EpCAM and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies were then applied to detect the primary antibodies. A peroxidase substrate DAB kit was used to develop a brown color, and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin or methyl green. Digital images were acquired at 20× magnification using an Olympus IX51 microscope with Olympus DP72 digital software.
To evaluate the tumor-initiating capacity of residual OE-19 cells following chemotherapy, a xenograft model was established using eight-week-old nude BALB/c mice. These mice were housed under standard conditions with access to commercial chow and tap water, maintained at 22 °C on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Prior to inoculation, OE-19 cell spheres underwent pretreatment with the ACF drug combination for varying durations (6, 12, 24, 48, or 72 hours). Following this treatment, the cell spheres were cultured to assess their ability to reform spheres. For xenografting, OE-19 cell spheres that had been pretreated with ACF for 48 hours were chosen. These cells, at a concentration of 10^6 cells per mouse, were inoculated into the right flank of eight mice, with all procedures conducted under sterile conditions. Tumor development was monitored over a five-week period. Tumor dimensions (length and width) were precisely measured using a digital caliper (accuracy of 0.01 mm) and used to calculate tumor volume via the formula: tumor volume = length × width × width/2. All experimental protocols adhered to the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Louisville, which is accredited by the American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care.
Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (n=3-6). Statistical significance (p < 0.05) was determined using ANOVA and Newman-Keuls' tests.
Our investigation confirmed that EpCAM is significantly overexpressed in esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) tissues when compared to adjacent benign esophageal epithelia, as demonstrated by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1a). This finding aligns with the established understanding of EAC progression, which involves a transition from benign conditions like GERD to Barrett's Esophagus and finally to malignancy. To further explore EpCAM expression in vitro, we utilized Western blotting on three distinct cell lines: HET 1-A, representing benign esophageal epithelium; Bar-T, representing pre-malignant Barrett's metaplasia; and OE-19, representing esophageal adenocarcinoma. The OE-19 cells exhibited a notably higher level of EpCAM expression compared to both HET 1-A and Bar-T cells (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, flow cytometry was employed to quantify the proportion of EpCAM-positive OE-19 cells, revealing that 9.79% of these cells expressed EpCAM (Fig. 1c). These initial findings established the OE-19 cell line as a suitable model for subsequent experiments aimed at investigating the role of EpCAM in cancer stem cells (CSCs) and their contribution to drug resistance in EAC.
Chemotherapeutic interventions, while effective at eliminating the majority of tumor cells, often leave behind resistant subpopulations that can lead to cancer recurrence. This phenomenon is widely attributed to the presence of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which possess inherent resistance mechanisms. To investigate this in esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), we initiated experiments using the OE-19 cell line, a model for EAC. Our initial step involved assessing the OE-19 cell line's capacity to form spheres, a characteristic indicative of CSCs. We observed that OE-19 cells readily formed spheres within 2-3 days when cultured in a specialized serum-free medium (DMEM/F12). Subsequently, we aimed to determine if these spheroid cultures exhibited drug resistance. The OE-19 cell spheres were exposed to individual chemotherapeutic agents—adriamycin, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)—as well as their combination, referred to as ACF, for varying durations (0 to 72 hours). Drug concentrations were carefully selected to reflect clinically relevant doses, as detailed in Table 2. Using MTT assays, we monitored cell viability. Our findings indicated that while individual drugs led to a noticeable increase in surviving cells between 6 and 24 hours, and continuing beyond 24 hours, the ACF combination showed a less pronounced survival surge at 24 hours, with a slight increase observed at 48 hours. To corroborate these results, cell adhesion assays were performed. A 48-hour treatment period was chosen as it represented a critical time point for observing drug resistance differences between single agents and the ACF regimen, based on the MTT assay data. The cell adhesion assay results were consistent with the MTT findings, further supporting the observed drug resistance patterns. Following these viability assessments, we employed flow cytometry to investigate the role of EpCAM-positive cells in mediating this drug resistance. OE-19 cell spheres were treated with individual drugs and the ACF combination for 48 hours. We observed a significant increase in the proportion of EpCAM-positive cells, rising from a baseline of 9.79% to 18.3% after treatment. Intriguingly, while both individual drugs and ACF treatment resulted in cell death at 48 hours, the percentage of EpCAM-positive cells was unexpectedly higher following ACF treatment (reaching 6%) compared to individual drug treatments, despite a lower overall cell survival. These collective findings suggest that chemotherapeutic agent (CTA) treatment may induce EpCAM expression, and this induction is associated with CSC survival and enhanced drug resistance. Building upon our prior findings, we investigated the dynamics of EpCAM expression during sphere formation and its correlation with cell survival following ACF treatment. Initially, Western blot analysis was employed to compare EpCAM levels in OE-19 cells and OE-19 cell spheres. This revealed a significant induction of EpCAM expression in the spheroid cells compared to their non-spheroid counterparts (Fig. 3a). Subsequently, OE-19 cell spheres were exposed to ACF at previously established sensitive concentrations. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) demonstrated fluctuations in EpCAM mRNA levels over the course of ACF treatment. Specifically, EpCAM expression was found to be upregulated by ACF from 6 to 48 hours, with a subsequent decrease observed at 72 hours (Fig. 3b). Parallel analysis of EpCAM protein levels in ACF-treated OE-19 cell spheres mirrored these mRNA findings. Protein levels initially increased from 6 to 24 hours, followed by a decline at 48 and 72 hours (Fig. 3c). These observed decreases in EpCAM protein levels were correlated with a reduction in cell viability. Collectively, these results suggest that alterations in EpCAM expression may contribute to the drug resistance exhibited by surviving cells after ACF treatment. Given that cell viability and flow cytometry data indicated a subpopulation of cells surviving ACF exposure at 48 hours, it is plausible that these residual cells had acquired cancer stem cell (CSC)-like characteristics. To further explore this, a small interfering RNA (siRNA) assay was conducted on OE-19 cells to ascertain whether reducing EpCAM expression would enhance the efficacy of ACF. The results demonstrated that RNA interference effectively reduced both EpCAM mRNA and protein levels to approximately one-third of that observed in the negative control (scramble) group (Fig. 3d). A subsequent cell adhesion assay revealed that following 48 hours of ACF treatment, the majority of non-spheroid OE-19 cells were eliminated, irrespective of EpCAM downregulation. In contrast, a more pronounced cell kill was achieved in the spheroid OE-19 cells (Fig. 3e).
To confirm the cancer stem cell (CSC)-like characteristics of cells surviving ACF treatment, immunocytochemical staining was employed. This technique assessed EpCAM expression alongside other established CSC surface markers, specifically CD44 and CD90, chosen based on prior research. Following a 48-hour exposure of OE-19 cell spheres to ACF, the surviving cells exhibited positive staining for both CD90 and EpCAM. Conversely, CD44 staining was absent. These findings suggest that the cells persisting after ACF treatment may represent CSC-like populations, contributing significantly to drug resistance. While the presence of CSC surface markers is a key indicator of CSCs, definitive confirmation of CSC identity necessitates the demonstration of functional attributes, such as the capacity for spheroid formation in vitro or tumor initiation in vivo.
To investigate the tumor-initiating potential of cells surviving ACF treatment, OE-19 cell spheres were exposed to the drug combination for varying durations (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours). Following ACF removal, the surviving cells were cultured in spheroid medium. Cells that endured 6, 12, and 24 hours of ACF treatment readily reformed spheres within three days. A longer exposure of 48 hours resulted in a slower sphere formation, taking approximately one week. After 72 hours of ACF treatment, very few cells survived, and these did not form spheres within a three-week observation period. To assess the in vivo tumor-forming capacity, OE-19 cell spheres that had been re-formed after a 48-hour ACF exposure were selected and inoculated into nude mice. These xenografts exhibited aggressive tumor growth, becoming evident within five weeks. Histological examination of the tumor masses revealed features consistent with adenocarcinoma. The aggressive nature of the tumors was characterized by substantial size and the presence of central necrotic areas. Further immunohistochemical analysis of these xenografted tumors confirmed EpCAM expression and indicated a high proliferation rate, as evidenced by numerous PCNA-positive cells. This experimental model, depicted in Figure 5, demonstrates the tumor-forming potential of residual EAC cells following chemotherapy.
Our study revealed a significant elevation in EpCAM expression within primary esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) tissues when compared to adjacent benign esophageal epithelia. This heightened EpCAM presence was further substantiated in vitro, where EAC-derived cell lines exhibited increased expression compared to non-malignant Bar-T and HET 1-A cells. We observed a correlation between this elevated EpCAM expression and the emergence of cancer stem cell (CSC) characteristics. Specifically, an increased proportion of EpCAM-positive cells was noted in OE-19 cell-derived spheres and in residual cells following chemotherapeutic treatment. This suggests that EpCAM may play a crucial role in the development of chemoresistance in EAC. This research marks the first demonstration that conventional chemotherapeutic agents (CTA), typically employed for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), can select for a subpopulation of EAC cells possessing CSC-like properties. These selected cells exhibit both chemoresistance and an aggressive malignant potential. Our findings indicate that CTA treatment not only induces increased EpCAM expression in the early stages but also favors the selection of more aggressive tumor cells that maintain elevated EpCAM levels even after treatment cessation. This observation carries significant implications for current chemotherapy practices, potentially impacting both treatment efficacy and patient prognosis. These findings align with previous research on ovarian cancer, where Latifi et al. reported a similar increase in EpCAM expression following cisplatin treatment. Their work proposed that EpCAM might contribute to decreased E-cadherin expression, a phenomenon linked to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and CSC transformation. Further mechanistic insights suggest that EpCAM can undergo regulated intramembrane proteolysis, releasing its intracellular domain (EpICD). This EpICD can then form a complex with β-catenin, translocating to the nucleus and activating the canonical Wnt signaling pathway. This pathway is known to be critical for maintaining stem cell pluripotency and can even facilitate the reprogramming of somatic cells. Evidence from HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinomas also supports EpCAM's role in inducing a CSC-like gene signature. In our study, we observed the co-expression of EpCAM and the CSC marker CD90 in OE-19 cells after ACF treatment. However, a comprehensive understanding of the precise mechanisms by which EAC cells acquire CSC features following CTA treatment, particularly concerning the role of these CSC-like cells in chemoresistance and tumor recurrence, necessitates further investigation.
EpCAM's widespread presence in epithelial carcinomas suggests its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, as well as a therapeutic target. Mitas et al. [17] have already incorporated EpCAM into a molecular scoring system for EAC. Research by Osta et al. [43] indicates that silencing EpCAM in breast adenocarcinoma cells using siRNA can reduce their malignant and metastatic potential and correct E-cadherin abnormalities. A monoclonal antibody against EpCAM, catumaxomab, has shown promise in phase II/III trials for malignant ascites. While its use in EAC as an infusion therapy requires further investigation, targeting EpCAM could be beneficial for patients with EpCAM-positive primary tumors. Such a strategy could aim to eliminate circulating cancer stem cells (CSCs), which are known for their high metastatic capabilities.
Our findings indicate elevated EpCAM expression in primary EAC tissues and EAC-derived cells. Furthermore, standard chemotherapeutic agents appear to induce higher EpCAM levels in these cells, correlating with enhanced malignant potential and drug resistance. Deeper understanding of EpCAM's role in drug resistance could pave the way for developing new, targeted EAC treatment strategies.
