Chromosome 20q gain is a significant factor in the progression of colorectal adenomas to carcinomas. Genes that drive this amplification are anticipated to exhibit correlated mRNA and protein expression with 20q DNA copy number, while also functionally impacting cancer processes. CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39, located on the 20q amplicon, are known to influence colorectal cancer processes like cell viability and anchorage-independent growth. This study investigated whether these genes are drivers of 20q amplification. To address this, protein expression levels were assessed using immunohistochemistry on tissue microarrays comprising colorectal adenoma and carcinoma samples. These samples had previously undergone genome-wide DNA and mRNA profiling. The results indicated that CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 mRNA expression levels correlated with chromosome 20q DNA copy number status. However, CSE1L protein expression did not correlate with 20q gain, although it was elevated in carcinomas compared to adenomas. DIDO1 and RBM39 protein expression was notably strong in most tumors, regardless of their 20q DNA copy number status. The absence of a correlation between protein expression levels and 20q DNA copy number status suggests that CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 function as passengers, rather than drivers, of chromosome 20q gain during colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression. Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in Western countries, contributing to approximately 600,000 deaths globally each year. The genesis of CRC begins with disruptions in the WNT signaling pathway in normal colon epithelium, leading to the formation of benign adenomas. These adenomas have a roughly 5% chance of progressing into invasive CRC. The carcinogenic process in CRC is propelled by the accumulation of numerous genetic alterations, often occurring in the context of genomic instability. While microsatellite instability (MSI), stemming from DNA mismatch repair system failures, affects about 15% of CRCs and leads to increased mutations, chromosomal instability (CIN) is far more prevalent, present in around 85% of cases. CIN is characterized by the gain and loss of chromosomal segments, resulting in the amplification or deletion of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, ultimately causing abnormal gene expression that fuels cancer-related biological processes.
Chromosome 20q gain is a common event in colorectal cancer (CRC), found in over 60% of carcinomas. This chromosomal alteration is linked to the progression from adenomas to carcinomas and is associated with a poorer prognosis in CRC patients. The amplification of large segments of the 20q arm suggests that multiple genes within this region contribute to CRC development. Our previous research has shown that various genes on chromosome 20q play a role in cancer-related processes. While AURKA and TPX2 have been identified as key drivers of 20q gain-driven progression, other genes like CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 also contribute to cancer processes. Specifically, CSE1L and RBM39 impact cell viability, and CSE1L and DIDO1 influence anchorage-independent growth. These findings suggest that CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 could potentially be drivers of tumor progression associated with 20q gain.
It is generally thought that chromosomal regions with increased copy numbers in tumors drive carcinogenesis by altering the expression of key "driver" genes at both the mRNA and protein levels. Consequently, genes whose mRNA and protein expression are influenced by their DNA copy number status are considered potential drivers of amplicon gain. Previous research indicates that elevated mRNA expression of genes within amplified regions is uncommon. This study aimed to determine if CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 act as drivers or passengers in 20q gain-driven colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression. To achieve this, we examined the mRNA and protein expression levels of these genes in colorectal adenomas and carcinomas, correlating them with chromosome 20q DNA copy number status.
Previous array CGH and mRNA expression microarray analyses provided data on DNA copy number and mRNA levels for the genes of interest in colorectal adenomas and carcinomas. Gene copy number ratios were calculated by comparing tumor DNA to normal DNA using BAC clones specific to each gene locus (e.g., RP1-155G6 for CSE1L, RP4-563E14 for DIDO1, and RP11-353C18 for RBM39). These smoothed ratios, treated as continuous variables, were then analyzed. The Mann–Whitney test was employed to identify differences in DNA copy number and mRNA expression between adenomas and carcinomas, as well as between tumors with and without 20q gain. Furthermore, Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between array CGH and mRNA expression data.
A series of 82 colorectal adenomas and 82 colorectal carcinomas, collected retrospectively between 2001 and 2008 from the VU University Medical Center in Amsterdam, were used to construct tissue microarrays. The use of this patient material adhered to institutional ethical regulations and the Dutch "Code for Proper Secondary Use of Human Tissue." These tumors had previously been characterized for microsatellite instability (MSI) status and chromosome 20q DNA copy number status. Further details regarding the histological and molecular characteristics of these tumors are available in Supplementary Table 1.
To assess the protein expression of CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39, immunohistochemical staining was conducted on tissue microarrays. The process involved deparaffinizing 4 μm paraffin sections in xylene and rehydrating them through graded alcohols. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited for 30 minutes using 0.3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol. Antigen retrieval was achieved through autoclave heating in 10 mM citrate buffer at pH 6.0. Primary antibodies used were CSE1L (mouse monoclonal, Novocastra) at a 1:30 dilution, DIDO1 (mouse monoclonal, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at a 1:100 dilution, and RBM39 (rabbit polyclonal, AtlasAntibodies) at a 1:75 dilution. The RBM39 antibody was specifically developed for the Human Protein Atlas project. All primary antibodies were incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Detection of the stainings was performed using an HRP-coupled polymer (ThermoScientific), visualized with diaminobenzidine plus (DAB Plus) (ThermoScientific), and counterstained with Mayer’s haematoxylin. Negative controls were established by omitting the primary antibody.
Protein expression for CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 was assessed in epithelial cells by evaluating staining intensity, categorizing it as weak, moderate, or strong. The highest score from evaluated cores was recorded for each tumor. For statistical purposes, tumors were grouped into either weak/moderate staining or strong staining based on this maximum score. The Chi-Square test was employed to compare protein expression between adenomas and carcinomas, as well as between tumors with and without 20q gain. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 15.0.
To assess if CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 contribute to the progression of colorectal adenomas to carcinomas driven by chromosome 20q gain, we conducted correlation analyses. These analyses examined the relationship between the DNA copy number status of these genes and their corresponding mRNA expression levels. We found significant correlations between DNA copy number ratios and mRNA expression for all three genes: CSE1L (R = 0.49, p = 0.0001), DIDO1 (R = 0.53, p = 0.00002), and RBM39 (R = 0.45, p = 0.0003). These findings are illustrated in Figure 1a-c. Subsequently, we focused on CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 mRNA expression in relation to chromosome 20q gain status specifically within the subset of chromosomally unstable tumors. To achieve this, we excluded microsatellite instability (MSI) tumors from our analysis. The remaining colorectal tumors, comprising 26 adenomas and 23 carcinomas, were then categorized into two groups: those with and those without 20q gain. Our investigation revealed that mRNA expression levels for CSE1L (p = 0.01), DIDO1 (p = 0.001), and RBM39 (p = 0.001) were significantly elevated in tumors exhibiting 20q gain compared to those without it. These results are depicted in Figure 1d-f.
Given that functionally significant genes in tumor progression typically exhibit elevated mRNA levels in carcinomas compared to adenomas, and knowing that chromosome 20q gain is linked to colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression, this study investigated whether the increased mRNA expression of CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 was a consequence of general tumor progression or directly driven by chromosome 20q DNA copy number status. Analysis revealed that for all three genes, the difference in median mRNA expression between tumors with and without 20q gain (CSE1L: 0.55, DIDO1: 0.47, RBM39: 0.60) was greater than the difference observed between adenomas and carcinomas with known 20q status (n=49) (CSE1L: 0.44, DIDO1: 0.40, RBM39: 0.31). Supplementary Figure 2 provides further comparisons of adenomas and carcinomas using the complete mRNA expression dataset. Notably, only CSE1L (p=0.02) and DIDO1 (p=0.004) showed significantly higher mRNA expression in CRCs compared to adenomas. These findings collectively suggest that the mRNA expression levels of CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 are primarily dictated by 20q DNA copy number status, rather than by general tumor progression.
To assess the relationship between chromosome 20q DNA copy number and the protein expression of CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 during colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression, immunohistochemistry was conducted. This analysis utilized tissue microarrays comprising 82 colorectal adenomas and 82 carcinomas. CSE1L protein was primarily detected in the nuclei of epithelial cells, with a minor cytoplasmic presence also noted. DIDO1 protein expression was exclusively nuclear and observed in both epithelial and stromal cells. For RBM39, nuclear protein expression was evident, accompanied by a subtle cytoplasmic staining. Figure 2 illustrates representative immunohistochemical stainings for these proteins. The staining intensity for CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 in epithelial cells was categorized as weak, moderate, or strong. For DIDO1, nuclear staining intensity was evaluated. In the case of RBM39, due to a scarcity of weakly stained samples, representative images of moderate and strong staining were presented.
When examining the protein expression of CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 in epithelial cells, no significant differences were found between tumors that had chromosome 20q gain and those that did not (Fig. 3a-c). However, CSE1L protein expression was notably higher in carcinomas than in adenomas (p = 0.001; Fig. 3d). For DIDO1 and RBM39, strong protein expression was prevalent in most tumors. Over 80% of adenomas showed strong DIDO1 staining, and more than 90% of adenomas were strongly positive for RBM39. While carcinomas exhibited slightly higher percentages of strong DIDO1 and RBM39 protein expression, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3e-f). The figures illustrate the correlation of protein expression with 20q DNA copy number status and adenoma/carcinoma status.
The progression of colorectal adenomas to carcinomas is significantly influenced by gains on chromosome 20q. Genes driving this 20q gain are expected to exhibit elevated DNA, mRNA, and protein expression, thereby impacting cancer processes. The 20q amplicon contains numerous genes contributing to CRC carcinogenesis, as evidenced by the frequent amplification of this chromosomal region. Previous research identified AURKA and TPX2 as key players in 20q gain-driven CRC progression. Additionally, CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 were found to affect cancer-related processes like cell viability and anchorage-independent growth in the same study. This investigation aimed to further elucidate the roles of CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 in colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression by examining their mRNA and protein levels in adenomas and carcinomas relative to 20q DNA copy number status.
CSE1L has been shown to impact cell viability and anchorage-independent growth, as indicated in Supplementary Figure 1. Existing research also implicates CSE1L in apoptosis, chromosomal instability, migration, invasion, and metastasis across various cancer types. Studies have documented elevated CSE1L DNA copy numbers, including high-level amplifications, along with increased mRNA and protein expression in colorectal cancer and other tumors. Furthermore, CSE1L overexpression has been associated with tumor progression. This study, for the first time, provides evidence of heightened CSE1L mRNA and protein levels in colorectal carcinomas when compared to adenomas. Considering CSE1L's functional contributions to cancer processes and its elevated expression in carcinomas, it is probable that CSE1L plays a role in colon tumor progression. The observed correlation between CSE1L's DNA copy number status on chromosome 20q and its mRNA expression suggests that chromosome 20q gain influences CSE1L mRNA transcription. However, CSE1L protein expression does not correlate with either 20q DNA copy number status or mRNA levels, as shown in Supplementary Figure 3. This indicates that CSE1L protein levels are regulated post-transcriptionally, independently of 20q gain. Consequently, due to the lack of correlation between CSE1L protein expression and chromosome 20q gain status, CSE1L is likely a passenger, rather than a driver, gene in the context of 20q gain-associated adenoma-to-carcinoma progression.
CSE1L, also known as Cellular Apoptosis Susceptibility (CAS), functions as a nuclear transport factor, facilitating the movement of proteins between the cytoplasm and the nucleus. This process is crucial for normal cellular operations. In the cytoplasm, proteins destined for the nucleus, identified by a nuclear localization signal, form a complex with importin-α. This complex then binds to importin-β, leading to its translocation into the nucleus where the complex disassembles. While RanGTP recycles importin-β back to the cytoplasm, CSE1L is responsible for the recycling of importin-α. The role of CSE1L in tumor progression is linked to its function in nuclear transport, which necessitates its own presence in the nucleus. Immunohistochemical analysis of colorectal tumors confirmed this nuclear localization. When CSE1L is overexpressed, it can lead to constitutive binding with importin-α. This binding depletes the available importin-α, a protein essential for the nuclear import of other proteins. Consequently, aberrant CSE1L expression can cause cellular proteins to be mislocalized, potentially contributing to tumor development. For instance, interfering with protein function by sequestering them in cellular compartments has been shown to lead to the functional inactivation of tumor suppressor genes like TP53. In colorectal adenoma and carcinoma samples, weak CSE1L protein expression was observed in the cytoplasm, with staining intensity correlating directly with nuclear staining levels. Some research highlights cytoplasmic and vesicular CSE1L staining, suggesting a role in vesicle translocation and protein secretion. Proteomic analyses of colorectal cancer cell lines' surfaces and secretomes support the presence of CSE1L protein associated with plasma membranes and secreted microvesicles. Additionally, cytoplasmic staining has been attributed to CSE1L's association with microtubules. CSE1L bound to microtubules might serve as an inactive reservoir or possess a specific function during mitosis, particularly in chromosome segregation, as its name implies. Deregulation of chromosome segregation, a sensitive process, can result in chromosomal aberrations. This potential role for CSE1L in chromosome segregation aligns with observations of impaired chromosome segregation in yeast cells with CSE1L mutations. Other genes involved in chromosomal instability, such as AURKA and TPX2, have also been identified as significant contributors to colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression.
Previous research demonstrated that reducing DIDO1 levels led to decreased anchorage-independent growth. DIDO1 mRNA expression was found to correlate with DNA copy number and chromosome 20q gain status, suggesting that amplification of the 20q region induces higher DIDO1 expression. However, the study found no discernible differences in DIDO1 protein expression between tumors with and without chromosome 20q gain. This lack of observed protein variation might stem from technical limitations. Specifically, tumors lacking 20q gain, despite exhibiting lower DIDO1 mRNA levels compared to those with the gain, already displayed substantial DIDO1 protein expression. Given that immunohistochemistry has limitations in quantifying protein expression beyond a certain threshold, further increases may not be detectable as more intense staining. Consequently, this study does not support DIDO1 acting as a driver gene in colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression. Instead, the elevated DIDO1 mRNA expression in tumors with 20q gain is likely a passenger effect, attributable to its location on the amplified 20q region.
The functional role of DIDO1 in tumor progression remains unclear. While DIDO1 (death inducer-obligator 1) is an early apoptosis regulator, it has been characterized as a tumor suppressor in hematological myeloid neoplasms. Following apoptosis induction, DIDO1 moves from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, enhancing caspase activity. However, reducing DIDO1 mRNA expression did not affect cell viability in colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines exposed to 5FU, suggesting it does not play a role in apoptosis induction in these cells. DIDO1 disruption has been linked to chromosomal instability due to a faulty mitotic checkpoint. Conversely, DIDO1 overexpression also leads to chromosomal instability, evidenced by asymmetric cell divisions and lagging chromosomes. Similar to DIDO1, inhibiting genes involved in chromosomal instability, such as AURKA and TPX2, has also been shown to reduce anchorage-independent growth.
Reducing RBM39 expression impacted cell viability in colorectal cancer cell lines. Like DIDO1, RBM39's mRNA levels showed a correlation with DNA copy number and chromosome 20q gain status. However, no differences in protein expression were observed between tumors with and without chromosome 20q gain. Furthermore, RBM39 mRNA and protein expression levels did not differ between adenomas and carcinomas. Therefore, while RBM39 mRNA expression is elevated due to 20q gain and might contribute to 20q gain-associated tumor progression, this study does not identify RBM39 as a driver gene in adenoma-to-carcinoma progression. Instead, its increased mRNA expression in 20q gain tumors is likely a consequence of its location on the 20q amplicon, acting as a passenger.
RBM39, also known as CAPER alpha, functions as a transcriptional coactivator, enhancing transcription regulated by progesterone and estrogen steroid hormone receptors. Furthermore, RBM39 plays a role in alternative splicing, a crucial process for generating mRNA, protein, and functional diversity through the modification of pre-mRNA processing. Dysregulation of proteins involved in alternative splicing has been implicated in tumor development and progression, as seen with AURKA and TPX2. RBM39 overexpression has been noted in small-cell lung cancer and breast cancer. In breast cancer, a shift in RBM39 expression from the cytoplasm to the nucleus occurs during the transition from pre-malignancy to carcinoma in situ. However, our study did not observe a similar shift in subcellular localization of RBM39 protein during the progression from colorectal adenomas to carcinomas, as strong nuclear staining was already present in pre-malignant adenomas.
While CSE1L, DIDO1, and RBM39 can impact tumor progression through their various cellular functions, they are unlikely drivers of 20q gain in colorectal cancer. Although their DNA copy number and mRNA expression correlate significantly, a lack of correlation at the protein level suggests their elevated mRNA expression is a consequence of being on the amplified 20q region, rather than a direct cause of progression.
