Driven by significant interest in vitamin D's health impact, our research aimed to clarify the link between oral supplementation and changes in serum vitamin D levels. We analyzed randomized, placebo-controlled trials published up to 2014, identifying 25 studies that provided valuable insights. These studies revealed a strong dose-response relationship, with an r2 of 0.61, indicating that increased oral supplementation correlates with higher serum vitamin D. This finding aligns with previous individual studies and meta-analyses, suggesting a consistent relationship largely unaffected by confounding factors.

Interest in vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) supplementation for disease prevention and health has recently increased. This renewed focus is evident in systematic reviews targeting specific populations, like individuals over 50 or those categorized by BMI. Research has also explored how vitamin D supplementation influences serum levels and its impact on various health risks, from bone health to cancer.
The discussion surrounding cholecalciferol supplementation, including its potential benefits and risks, intensified following a 2010 Institute of Medicine Report that established reference levels for dietary and supplemental Vitamin D. Cholecalciferol is distinctive from an epidemiological standpoint because it can be acquired through diet and supplements, as well as from ultraviolet radiation, specifically UVB. Consequently, some have advocated for increased sun exposure and avoiding sunscreen to elevate serum 25-(OH)D levels. However, this approach presents a challenge, as excessive UVB exposure carries the risk of increased skin cancer incidence, making the health implications of UV exposure potentially problematic.
Given the limited research on the dose-response of oral vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and its effect on serum vitamin D levels, a literature review was undertaken. This gap in understanding is significant, especially since oral supplementation offers a potentially safer and more controllable method than UVB exposure for optimizing vitamin D dosage.
Our literature review encompassed studies published between 1991 and 2013, sourced from personal collections and PubMed using keywords like "vitamin D supplementation" and "randomized trials of vitamin D." Initially, 36 studies met our inclusion criteria, after excluding those with incomplete or inaccessible data. For in-depth graphical analysis (Figure 1), we focused on 25 of these studies (Table 1). The remaining 11 studies (Table 2) were treated separately due to their unconventional dosing schedules, such as single-bolus interventions.
Our review selected studies from existing vitamin D literature based on specific criteria. These included the provision of oral cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) dosage, baseline and post-intervention 25-(OH)D serum levels, and the inclusion of placebo groups (Tables 1 and 2). While many studies explored vitamin D supplementation and diet's impact on disease outcomes, only a limited number provided the essential pre- and post-supplementation serum level data, with even fewer focusing solely on serum vitamin D response to oral supplementation. Of the studies meeting our criteria, sixteen involved healthy individuals [7-22]. The remaining nine focused on individuals with various conditions, such as vitamin D deficiency [23,24], diabetes [25-27], relapsing multiple sclerosis [28], early-stage prostate cancer [29], prior hip fractures [30], and Parkinson's Disease [31].
Our initial aim was to conduct a meta-analysis, but the diverse nature of the available data prevented a statistically sound evaluation. Several factors contributed to this heterogeneity, including differences in geographical location, participant age, existing health conditions, the amount and frequency of vitamin D dosage, and whether calcium was also supplemented. The duration of follow-up and the overall quality of individual studies also varied significantly. Furthermore, some studies lacked essential details, such as standard deviations or baseline vitamin D levels, likely because vitamin D was not their primary research focus. Consequently, studies that were not placebo-controlled, randomized, or had insufficient sample sizes (under 20 participants) were excluded from our primary analysis. It's important to note that studies employing weekly or single bolus doses of cholecalciferol were analyzed separately and not integrated into the daily dose findings.
To assess the relationship between oral vitamin D intake and changes in serum levels, we first calculated the difference between post-intervention and baseline serum vitamin D. This change was then correlated with the administered oral vitamin D dosage. Linear regression, incorporating both quadratic and linear terms for duration, was employed using SAS 9.3 to perform these calculations.
A clear trend emerges from the most robust data available. Figure 1, specifically depicting the twenty most reliable and homogeneous studies, illustrates a positive correlation between the quantity of oral vitamin D administered and subsequent changes in serum vitamin D levels. This figure focuses on the change in vitamin D serum levels from rigorous studies involving daily cholecalciferol doses. The correlation observed is highly significant, with a P-value less than 0.001 and an r2 of 0.61. Data presented in Table 2 were not subjected to statistical analysis due to insufficient quantity for meaningful interpretation.
The findings highlight a consistent relationship, even with our straightforward model, showing a robust dose-response between the quantity of vitamin D supplementation and alterations in serum vitamin D levels. This consistency was observed across studies, all of which were double-blind and featured sufficient sample sizes, indicating their generalizability. Notably, restricting the analysis to studies involving healthy participants or those with interventions lasting six months or longer did not significantly alter these results. Despite these clear trends, the considerable data scatter in Figure 1 underscores the necessity for further research to pinpoint the optimal vitamin D3 dosage required to achieve specific serum vitamin D concentrations. A significant gap exists due to the scarcity of randomized studies on cholecalciferol supplementation, emphasizing the urgent need for more placebo-controlled, double-blind trials. Such studies are crucial for understanding how various doses of vitamin D impact serum 25-(OH)D levels. A deeper comprehension of the dose-response is essential for evaluating cholecalciferol's effects on health and disease outcomes, as well as its clinical applicability, ultimately enabling the scientific community to grasp the full importance of vitamin D supplementation for health.
Our analysis has several limitations. We did not consider various factors influencing vitamin D levels, such as age, body mass index, activity, diet, or geographical climate zones, due to the diverse nature of the studies reviewed. Furthermore, the linear regression model derived from Figure 1 fails to capture the plateau effect observed in serum vitamin D levels at higher concentrations. Despite these limitations, our research offers a foundational understanding for future, more refined evaluations. This initial step is particularly crucial given the increasing interest in vitamin D's role in disease prevention, the aging US population, and the pressing need to clarify whether vitamin D is a marker of ill health or a contributor to improved well-being.
