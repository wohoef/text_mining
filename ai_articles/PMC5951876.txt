Glioblastoma (GBM), the most prevalent adult brain tumor, is characterized by a subpopulation of cancer stem cells (CSCs) believed to confer resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. While CD133 serves as a marker for identifying and isolating these GBM CSCs, its functional role and the optimal microenvironment for its study have remained unclear. This research aimed to investigate the impact of hypoxia on CD133 expression, as well as the expression of hypoxia-related factors HIF-1α and HIF-2α. Furthermore, the study explored the potential functional significance of CD133 in conferring chemoresistance to GBM cells. To achieve these objectives, mRNA expression levels of CD133, HIF-1α, HIF-2α, and VEGF, along with the control gene HPRT, were analyzed in GBM cell lines (U251, U87, and SNB19) cultured under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions using 2D and 3D models. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed for this analysis. Gene silencing was performed using siRNA to downregulate CD133, HIF-1α, and HIF-2α expression, with knockdown confirmed by flow cytometry and qRT-PCR, respectively. Drug sensitivity was assessed by determining IC50 values using an Alamar Blue cell viability assay and Graphpad Prism software. The study revealed that CD133 expression was upregulated under hypoxic conditions in both 2D and 3D GBM cell culture models. Concurrently, GBM cells cultured in hypoxia exhibited increased resistance to cisplatin, temozolomide, and etoposide compared to those in normoxia. Knockdown of either HIF-1α or HIF-2α resulted in decreased CD133 expression, with HIF-2α demonstrating a more sustained effect. Notably, HIF-2α downregulation led to greater sensitization of GBM cells to cisplatin than HIF-1α. However, CD133 knockdown had a more pronounced effect on cisplatin sensitization than the knockdown of either HIF factor, suggesting a HIF-independent mechanism of cisplatin resistance mediated by CD133. This CD133-mediated mechanism did not appear to influence temozolomide resistance, as only HIF-1α downregulation, not HIF-2α or CD133, sensitized GBM cells to this drug. In conclusion, the findings indicate that hypoxia-induced CD133-mediated cisplatin resistance mechanisms may offer valuable insights for developing novel GBM treatment strategies.

Glioblastoma (GBM), a World Health Organization (WHO) grade IV astrocytoma, represents the most aggressive form of glioma. Despite advancements, managing GBM patients remains a significant challenge, largely due to the tumor's inherent heterogeneity. Temozolomide, an oral alkylating agent capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier, has shown promise in standard GBM care. However, its effectiveness is ultimately limited by the development of resistance. Cisplatin has been employed as a neoadjuvant therapy alongside temozolomide and as an adjuvant therapy with carmustine for malignant gliomas. In patients with relapsed high-grade gliomas resistant to temozolomide alone, combined temozolomide and cisplatin therapy has demonstrated moderate efficacy. For chemotherapy-naïve patients with recurrent GBM, initial treatment with fractionated temozolomide and cisplatin resulted in a progression-free survival of 6 months. Nevertheless, resistance to cisplatin can emerge, potentially linked to elevated glutathione-S-transferase (GST) activity. Etoposide has also shown benefits in improving survival for high-grade glioma patients, but relapse remains a common outcome.
The tumor microenvironment, a complex interplay of factors such as extracellular matrix (ECM) composition and oxygen levels, significantly influences tumor biology. Variations in ECM can impact cancer cell proliferation and motility. Glioblastomas (GBMs) are notably characterized by hypoxic regions, typically ranging from 0.1% to 10% oxygen, which are believed to contribute to their aggressive nature. Consequently, hypoxia is recognized as a prognostic indicator of poor outcomes and is associated with resistance to radiation and chemotherapy, as well as increased tumor cell migration and invasion. These hypoxic areas are also known to harbor glioblastoma stem cell populations. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are considered the primary mediators of the cellular response to hypoxia. Both HIF-1α and HIF-2α can bind to the same DNA sequence, the hypoxia-responsive element (HRE), and form heterodimers with HIF-1β to activate target genes. Despite this shared function, HIF-1α and HIF-2α exhibit functional distinctions, including variations in protein-protein interactions and tissue-specific roles. HIF-1α has been shown to support the survival of glioma cancer stem cells under hypoxic conditions, while HIF-2α is thought to be linked to the GBM stem cell phenotype within the hypoxic niche. Furthermore, studies on neuroblastoma cells indicate that HIF-1α expression peaks and declines rapidly under hypoxia, whereas HIF-2α remains steadily upregulated, mediating a sustained response.
The identification and characterization of cancer stem cells (CSCs) are crucial for therapeutic development, as these cells are linked to tumor recurrence and treatment failure. Various markers are used to distinguish CSCs from other tumor cells, with CD133 being a common marker for CSCs in glioblastoma (GBM). However, the functional importance of CD133 in GBM remains underexplored. Furthermore, the optimal environment for studying GBM cells is debated. Current research often overlooks the impact of the tumor microenvironment (TME) on CSC populations and characteristics. GBM cells are typically cultured in vitro at 20% oxygen, a condition that may not accurately represent the in vivo tumor environment. This discrepancy could contribute to the high failure rate of anti-cancer drugs tested in vitro, with an estimated 95% not translating to clinical success.
This study investigated hypoxia's impact on CD133 expression and the regulatory role of HIFs. We also examined hypoxia's influence on etoposide and cisplatin chemo-resistance, and how downregulating HIFs and CD133 could sensitize GBM cells to chemotherapy. Our findings suggest that HIFs mediate CD133 upregulation under hypoxia, with HIF-2α exhibiting a more sustained effect. Furthermore, we discovered that CD133 knockdown sensitizes GBM cells to cisplatin independently of HIFs.
GBM cell lines U251 and SNB19 originated from the National Cancer Institute USA (NCI-60), while U87 cells came from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC). These cells were cultured in either standard 2D or Cultrex-based 3D formats, under normoxic (20% oxygen) or hypoxic (1% oxygen) conditions.
Quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed for RNA analysis, utilizing SYBR green for detection. Data normalization was achieved by referencing the housekeeping gene HPRT and applying the 2-∆∆Ct method. Specific primer sequences for HPRT, CD133, VEGF, HIF1A, and HIF2A were utilized in these analyses.
To reduce CD133, HIF-1α, and HIF-2α expression, GBM cells were transfected with specific siRNAs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. The sequences used were: CD133siRNA-GAUCAAAAGGAGUCGGAAA, HIFIAsiRNA-GCCACUUCGAAGUAGUGCU, and HIF2AsiRNA-GC GACAGCUGGAGUAUGAA.
Cultrex BME was diluted to 3 mg/ml on ice with RPMI-1640 medium. GBM cells were then resuspended at appropriate densities and seeded into prewarmed, 37°C, 96-well culture plates.
To assess drug sensitivity, a cisplatin stock solution was diluted to the required concentrations. Glioblastoma (GBM) cells were then exposed to these drugs for 48 hours. Following this incubation period, an Alamar Blue cell viability assay was performed at 37°C for 1 hour. The fluorescence generated was measured using a plate reader. Graphpad prism software was employed to calculate the IC50 values, which represent the drug concentration required to inhibit cell viability by 50%, relative to untreated control cells. Drug sensitivities were quantified as percentages of these untreated controls. IC50 curves were generated and values determined using GraphPad Prism 6, utilizing a nonlinear curve fit.
GBM cells were harvested and pelleted. They were then resuspended in buffer with an FcR blocker. Following this, either an anti-CD133/1-PE antibody or a mouse IgG-PE isotype control was added per manufacturer instructions. The cells were subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry, and the resulting data processed with Weasel software.
All data were analyzed using Graphpad Prism 6. Comparisons employed Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA with Turkey's multiple comparison test as appropriate.
To investigate the impact of hypoxia on CD133 expression, we utilized three glioblastoma (GBM) cell lines: U251, U87, and SNB19. These cell lines were cultured under both normoxic (20% oxygen) and hypoxic (1% oxygen) conditions, employing both two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) culture systems. Our initial assessment confirmed that all three cell lines maintained viability in both culture formats and under both oxygen conditions, as detailed in Supplemental Figure 1. A key finding was the elevated expression of CD133 under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic environments. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) revealed a significant upregulation of CD133 mRNA in U251 and SNB19 cells cultured in 2D, and in SNB19 cells in the 3D model, after 48 hours of hypoxic exposure. Concurrently, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) mRNA expression was observed to be induced, as indicated in Supplemental Table 1A–C. These findings underscore the significant influence of the GBM microenvironment on CD133 expression. Further validation of the hypoxia-induced increase in CD133 expression over time was achieved at the protein level through flow cytometry in the 2D culture models (Figure 1d, Supplemental Figure 2). However, assessing CD133 protein expression in 3D cultures presented technical challenges due to the time-intensive process of extracting cells from the 3D matrix. In U251 cells cultured in 2D, we observed a rapid decline in CD133 protein expression following harvesting, with measurements taken at 0, 15 minutes, and 2 hours. Specifically, CD133 protein levels in normoxic conditions decreased from 14.9% to 6.9% and then to 4.4% over these time points. In hypoxic conditions, the corresponding percentages were 24.4%, 12.9%, and 5.1% (Figure 2a, b). These observations highlight the dynamic nature of CD133 protein expression and potential issues in its assessment, particularly in complex 3D culture systems.
Following the observation that CD133 expression increases under hypoxic conditions in both 2D and 3D glioblastoma (GBM) cell models, the subsequent investigation focused on elucidating the regulatory mechanisms behind this upregulation. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), specifically HIF-1α and HIF-2α, are recognized for their significant roles in tumor progression under low-oxygen environments, yet their precise influence on CD133 expression had not been extensively documented. To explore this, GBM cells were subjected to siRNA-mediated knockdown of HIF-1α and HIF-2α, achieving a reduction of over 57% in mRNA expression in both 2D and 3D cultures. Subsequently, CD133 expression was analyzed under hypoxic conditions. In U251 cells cultured in 2D and exposed to mild/acute hypoxia for 24 hours, HIF-1α knockdown resulted in a 63% decrease in CD133 expression, while HIF-2α knockdown had a minimal impact. However, as hypoxia prolonged, indicating chronic exposure, a shift in the regulatory influence was observed. In cells with silenced HIF-1α, CD133 expression began to revert towards baseline levels. Conversely, in cells where HIF-2α was downregulated, the reduction in CD133 expression progressively increased, reaching 39% at 48 hours and 69% by 72 hours. These time-dependent effects were corroborated in U251 cells grown in 3D cultures and in U87 cells under similar conditions. Concurrently, the induction of VEGF expression was noted following HIF-1α or HIF-2α knockdown in the 3D models. This parallel observation suggests a gradual transition from a predominant HIF-1α-mediated effect to a more sustained HIF-2α-dependent regulation of gene expression as hypoxia becomes chronic.
The study investigated how hypoxia influences the sensitivity of glioblastoma (GBM) cells to various chemotherapeutic agents, specifically cisplatin, temozolomide, and etoposide. Across three GBM cell lines, exposure to hypoxic conditions generally led to increased resistance to these drugs when compared to cells cultured under normoxic conditions. For cisplatin, this increased resistance was observed in all three cell lines, though it reached statistical significance only in the U251 and SNB19 cell lines. Temozolomide resistance also showed an upward trend in U87 and SNB19 cells under hypoxia, but these increases were not statistically significant. A more pronounced effect was seen with etoposide. In hypoxic environments, it was impossible to determine IC50 values for etoposide, even at the highest tested concentration of 267 μM. This indicated that all three GBM cell lines exhibited more than a two-fold increase in resistance to etoposide when cultured under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions. These findings highlight the significant impact of the tumor microenvironment, particularly oxygen levels, on the efficacy of standard GBM chemotherapies.
Given the established role of HIFs as primary responders to hypoxia, the study proceeded to examine how reducing HIF-1α and HIF-2α levels impacted GBM cell sensitivity to drugs. Due to the inability to determine etoposide IC50 values under hypoxic conditions, the focus shifted to cisplatin and temozolomide. The research revealed that suppressing HIF-2α, but not HIF-1α, significantly increased the sensitivity of U251 and U87 cells to cisplatin. Conversely, reducing HIF-1α, rather than HIF-2α, markedly enhanced the sensitivity of these GBM cells to temozolomide, with over 47% and 48% sensitization observed in U251 and U87 cells, respectively.
While HIF-1α and HIF-2α were implicated in regulating CD133 expression under hypoxic conditions, their knockdown did not entirely eliminate CD133 expression. This suggests that other mechanisms might also control the gene encoding CD133. Furthermore, our findings indicated distinct roles for these hypoxia-inducible factors in drug sensitivity: HIF-1α influenced temozolomide sensitivity in glioblastoma (GBM) cells, whereas HIF-2α knockdown specifically impacted their sensitivity to cisplatin. These observations prompted us to investigate whether CD133 itself plays a role in resistance to both temozolomide and cisplatin, independent of the HIF pathways. To explore this, we reduced CD133 expression using siRNA. Flow cytometry confirmed a twofold decrease in CD133 protein levels following this intervention (as depicted in Fig. 5a). When these CD133-silenced GBM cells were subsequently treated with temozolomide, no significant sensitization was observed across the different cell lines tested (Fig. 5b, Supplemental Fig. 5A). This indicates that CD133 downregulation does not appear to enhance the efficacy of temozolomide. In contrast, the impact on cisplatin sensitivity was notable. Interestingly, a significant fivefold increase in sensitivity to cisplatin was observed in U251 cells (Fig. 5c, Supplemental Table 3). Similarly, U87 and SNB19 cells exhibited a twofold increase in their sensitivity to cisplatin following CD133 downregulation (Supplemental Fig. 5B, C; Supplemental Table 3). These collective results strongly suggest that reducing CD133 expression enhances the effectiveness of cisplatin in treating GBM. This finding points towards CD133 as a potential mediator of cisplatin resistance, acting independently of the HIF signaling pathways.
A significant shift in cancer research has occurred, recognizing the crucial role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in tumor development. Locally advanced tumors often exhibit hypoxia, a condition arising from compromised blood vessel structure and function, leading to increased distances between vessels and tumor cells. Hypoxia is a prominent characteristic of the glioblastoma (GBM) TME, and cells residing in these hypoxic regions are known to be chemo-resistant. Our study demonstrated an increase in CD133 expression in GBM cells grown in both 2D and 3D cultures under hypoxic conditions. While these findings align with existing literature, we observed variations in CD133 levels across different GBM cell lines. Previous research has also reported CD133 upregulation in response to hypoxia. Our results confirm that hypoxia influences GBM cells regardless of their culture format (2D or 3D). Although the 3D model did not show statistically significant CD133 upregulation, it consistently displayed higher CD133 expression compared to normoxic conditions, mirroring findings from studies using 3D scaffolds. We also confirmed CD133 protein expression in 2D cultures via flow cytometry. However, we noted a rapid decline in protein expression post-harvesting. This technical challenge complicates the assessment of CD133 in 3D cultures, which necessitate further steps to isolate and break down cell clusters. This observation might also shed light on previous inconsistencies in CD133 positive cell analysis and sorting, and explain how seemingly CD133-negative cells can generate CD133-positive cells.
In both 2D and 3D GBM cell models, reducing HIF-1α and HIF-2α levels resulted in decreased CD133 expression. The impact of HIF-1α silencing on CD133 expression was short-lived, whereas HIF-2α silencing showed a progressively stronger effect over time, mirroring its influence on VEGF expression, a known HIF target. This observation aligns with prior research on neuroblastoma cells. Potential mechanisms for this phenomenon include the hypoxia-associated factor HAF, which is upregulated during acute hypoxia and targets HIF-1α for degradation while promoting HIF-2α stability and transactivation. Additionally, the histone deacetylase SIRT1 may contribute by deacetylating HIF-1α, reducing its activity, and simultaneously enhancing HIF-2α-mediated transcription through a HIF-dependent pathway.
Chemo-resistance is a significant factor in the progression of Glioblastoma (GBM), with cells in hypoxic areas exhibiting increased resistance and contributing to tumor recurrence. Our findings demonstrate that GBM cells develop greater resistance to temozolomide, cisplatin, and etoposide when cultured under hypoxic conditions. This observation, however, is not universally consistent across all cancer types. For instance, while some studies report resistance to various chemotherapeutic agents in breast cancer cells exposed to very low oxygen levels (<0.1%), others have observed varied responses. Notably, cisplatin has shown greater efficacy in hypoxic breast cancer, small cell lung cancer, and lymphoma cells compared to their normoxic counterparts. The mechanisms driving this drug resistance appear to be cell-type specific. In breast cancer, HIF-1α has been implicated in enhanced cisplatin resistance under hypoxia. Conversely, in osteosarcoma cells, hypoxia-induced resistance to cisplatin, doxorubicin, and etoposide has been attributed to a HIF-independent pathway involving the attenuation of p53 activation. Our research specifically revealed that reducing HIF-1α expression through siRNA significantly sensitized GBM cells to temozolomide, a finding not observed with HIF-2α knockdown. This aligns with previous research indicating that HIF-1α downregulation can enhance GBM cell sensitivity to temozolomide, as demonstrated by studies involving BMP2 and in vivo experiments.
The differential roles of HIF-1α and HIF-2α in tumor development were further supported by our findings that HIF-2α downregulation more effectively sensitized GBM cells to cisplatin than HIF-1α downregulation. However, the impact of HIF downregulation on drug sensitivity was comparatively minor when contrasted with the effect of CD133 downregulation. Silencing CD133 led to a substantial increase in cisplatin sensitivity, ranging from at least a 2-fold improvement across all tested GBM cell lines to as much as a 7-fold increase in U251 cells. Conversely, CD133 downregulation did not significantly alter the sensitivity of GBM cells to temozolomide. This observation aligns with the findings of Perazzoli et al., who concluded that CD133 is not associated with temozolomide resistance. This contrasts with other studies indicating that CD133-positive GBM cells exhibit resistance to temozolomide. Our results suggest that the increased cisplatin sensitivity observed in GBM cells under hypoxic conditions may operate independently of HIFs and could be directly or indirectly triggered by CD133 activation. A potential mechanism for CD133-dependent resistance involves the activation of the Erk pathway, which has been documented following CD133 overexpression in GBM cells. Furthermore, phosphorylation at the tyrosine-828 residue within the C-terminal cytoplasmic domain of CD133 has been shown to facilitate direct interactions with the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) 85 kDa regulatory subunit (p85). This interaction results in a preferential activation of the PI3K/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway in GBM stem cells compared to non-stem cells. Consequently, the upregulation of CD133 in GBM cells under hypoxic conditions may lead to the activation of the anti-apoptotic Akt pathway, thereby contributing to drug resistance.
Cisplatin has been investigated as a neoadjuvant therapy, both independently and in combination with agents like temozolomide, showing some benefits. However, resistance can develop, leading to poor clinical outcomes. While prior research has established HIF-dependent and independent pathways for cisplatin resistance, this study reveals a new mechanism of hypoxia-induced resistance mediated by CD133. This finding could partially account for the link between CD133 expression and a worse prognosis, suggesting CD133 functions as a key molecule beyond just a cancer stem cell marker. Further exploration of this hypoxia-driven CD133-mediated cisplatin resistance mechanism could lead to the development of combination therapies to enhance cisplatin's effectiveness in neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatments.
