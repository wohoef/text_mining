This study investigated the effectiveness and safety of a 12-week regimen of sofosbuvir (SOF) 400 mg/day and daclatasvir (DCV) 60 mg/day, with or without ribavirin (RBV), for Taiwanese patients with genotype-2 chronic hepatitis C (CHC). The research aimed to address the suboptimal efficacy of the current Bureau of National Health Insurance (BNHI) reimbursed treatment (SOF plus RBV) for difficult-to-treat patients, including those with liver cirrhosis, prior interferon (IFN) treatment failure, and high viral loads. Between March 2017 and December 2018, 50 consecutive genotype-2 CHC patients were enrolled. When RBV was included in the treatment, a weight-based dosage (800-1200 mg/day) was administered. Sustained virological response at 12 weeks post-treatment (SVR12) was defined as undetectable HCV RNA (<15 IU/mL). The patient cohort had a mean age of 62.0 ± 11.4 years, with 32.0% being male. A significant portion (40.0%) had previously failed IFN treatment. Liver disease severity varied, with 24.0% having ≤F2 fibrosis, 40.0% F3 fibrosis, 30.0% Child-Pugh A cirrhosis, and 6.0% Child-Pugh B-C cirrhosis. The average baseline HCV RNA level was 6.19 ± 0.91 log10 IU/mL, and 60.0% of patients presented with a baseline HCV RNA of ≥ 2 million IU/mL. Treatment outcomes demonstrated high efficacy: undetectable HCV RNA rates were 40% at week 2, 94% at week 4, and 100% at the end of treatment. Notably, most patients (66.7%) with detectable HCV RNA at week 2 exhibited low-level viremia (<50 IU/mL). Adverse events (AEs) were generally mild, and all patients completed the therapy without interruption. Subjective AEs and laboratory abnormalities were more prevalent in patients receiving RBV. Ultimately, all 50 patients (100%) achieved SVR12. In conclusion, this real-world study from Taiwan indicates that the 12-week SOF/DCV-based treatment is highly effective and well-tolerated for genotype-2 CHC patients, irrespective of the presence of liver cirrhosis.

Chronic hepatitis C (CHC) affects about 71.1 million individuals globally, leading to 700,000 annual deaths and posing a significant worldwide health challenge. The disease can advance to severe liver conditions like cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. In Taiwan, CHC prevalence ranges from 3% to 4.4%, with regional variations. Genotype-2 is the second most common form in Taiwan, after genotype-1b, accounting for 31% to 65% of infections and being particularly prevalent in the southern region.
The long-term prognosis for patients with chronic hepatitis C (CHC) significantly improves with successful antiviral treatment, evidenced by a marked decrease in both liver-related and overall mortality rates. While pegylated interferon (PEG-IFN) combined with ribavirin (RBV) for 16–24 weeks achieved sustained virological response (SVR) rates of 80% to 94% for genotype-2 CHC, this IFN-based therapy was associated with frequent side effects. Moreover, many patients were either unable or unwilling to undergo IFN-based treatment. The landscape of CHC treatment has since advanced rapidly with the development of IFN-free oral direct antiviral agents (DAAs), which offer superior efficacy, shorter treatment durations, and fewer adverse events compared to IFN-based regimens.
Initially approved in the USA in 2013 and subsequently globally for chronic hepatitis C (CHC) treatment, Sofosbuvir (SOF) is a pan-genotypic direct-acting antiviral (DAA) that targets HCV RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Large-scale phase III trials examined the effectiveness and safety of a 12-week regimen of SOF combined with weight-based ribavirin (RBV) for genotype-2 CHC. These trials demonstrated high sustained virological response (SVR) rates: 97% for treatment-naïve patients and 90-92% for those who previously failed interferon (IFN) therapy, provided they did not have liver cirrhosis. However, SVR rates were consistently lower in patients with liver cirrhosis. For instance, one study found an SVR rate of only 60% with 12 weeks of SOF plus RBV in genotype-2 cirrhotic patients who had failed prior IFN-based treatment. Extending the treatment to 16 or 24 weeks improved SVR rates to 78-87% and 100%, respectively. Consequently, academic guidelines have indicated that a 12-week SOF plus RBV regimen may not be optimal for all genotype-2 CHC patients.
Clinical trials have assessed the efficacy of daclatasvir (DCV), a potent pan-genotypic HCV NS5A protein inhibitor, when combined with sofosbuvir (SOF) for genotype-2 chronic hepatitis C (CHC). One study involving 26 treatment-naïve patients demonstrated 92% SVR rates with 24 weeks of SOF/DCV, with or without ribavirin (RBV). Subsequently, Mangia et al. observed 100% SVR rates in genotype-2 CHC patients unable to tolerate RBV, using a 12-week SOF/DCV regimen for 8 non-cirrhotic patients and a 24-week course for 11 cirrhotic patients. Consequently, academic societies endorsed SOF/DCV as a standard treatment for genotype-2 CHC.
After January 2018, Taiwan's BNHI covered a 12-week SOF plus RBV regimen for genotype-2 CHC. However, this standard treatment was recognized as potentially inadequate for real-world patients, particularly those with challenging characteristics like liver cirrhosis, prior IFN treatment failure, or elevated baseline HCV RNA. Consequently, this study aimed to assess the safety and effectiveness of SOF/DCV, with or without RBV, in Taiwanese genotype-2 CHC patients.
From March 2017 to December 2018, investigators at Taipei Veterans General Hospital recruited 50 consecutive patients with genotype-2 chronic hepatitis C (CHC) for this study. These individuals had all completed a 12-week treatment course of sofosbuvir (400 mg/day) and daclatasvir (60 mg/day), with or without ribavirin. To be eligible, patients needed to be adults (20 years or older) with a confirmed genotype-2 CHC infection, evidenced by detectable HCV antibodies and quantifiable serum HCV RNA (above 15 IU/mL) for at least six months. Exclusion criteria included mixed genotypes, co-infection with hepatitis B or HIV, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², previous direct-acting antiviral (DAA) exposure, active hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), or a history of organ transplantation. Patients unwilling to provide informed consent were also excluded. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement.
This single-center, retrospective cohort study gathered comprehensive patient information. Data collected included baseline demographics, prior virological responses to PEG-IFN/RBV therapy, and various laboratory parameters such as hemogram, international normalized ratio (INR), and serum biochemical profiles (albumin, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, ALT, AST, creatinine, eGFR). HCV RNA levels, anti-HCV status, and HCV genotype, determined using a commercial assay (Cobas HCV GT, Roche Diagnostics GmbH), were also recorded. Liver fibrosis was assessed using two methods: the FIB-4 index, calculated as age (years) × AST (U/L) / [PLT(109/L) × ALT1/2 (U/L)], and transient elastography (FibroScan, Echosens, Paris, France). FibroScan results defined fibrosis stages as F0-F1 (≤7.0 kPa), F2 (7.1-9.4 kPa), F3 (9.5-12.4 kPa), and F4 (≥12.5 kPa). Cirrhosis was identified by a FibroScan reading of ≥12.5 kPa or through typical clinical or radiological findings. Decompensation was indicated by a Child-Pugh score of 7 or higher.
For a duration of 12 weeks, all patients received a daily dose of 400 mg of Sofosbuvir (Sovaldi) and 60 mg of Daclatasvir (Daklinza). The inclusion of Ribavirin (Robatrol) in the treatment regimen varied based on the date. Prior to January 1, 2018, patients self-funded the SOF/DCV therapy without RBV. However, from January 1, 2018, onward, RBV was added to the SOF/DCV regimen, with SOF and RBV costs covered by BNHI, while DCV remained self-funded. RBV dosage was determined by patient weight: 1200 mg/day for those ≥75 kg, 1000 mg/day for 50-75 kg, and 800 mg/day for <50 kg, with adjustments made for eGFR. If a patient's eGFR dropped to ≤30 mL/min/1.73 m2 during treatment, the RBV dose was reduced to 200-400 mg/day.
Prior to SOF/DCV therapy, patients' regular medications were thoroughly reviewed for potential drug-to-drug interactions (DDI). Physicians either discontinued, switched, or initiated these DDI-prone medications at their lowest effective dose. If serum HCV RNA became undetectable via real-time PCR and hemoglobin dropped by more than 2.0 g/dL from baseline after week 4, the RBV dose could be lowered by 200 mg/day.
Virological response was assessed by measuring quantitative HCV RNA levels at weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, and 12 weeks post-treatment. Undetectable HCV RNA (<15 IU/mL) at the end of treatment and 12 weeks later defined SVR12. Patients without SVR12 data were considered non-responders.
Throughout the treatment, physicians monitored patients at weeks 1 and 2, then bi-weekly or more frequently if adverse effects arose, until therapy concluded. All adverse events were graded using CTCAE version 4.0. Recorded data included subjective patient reports, physical exam results, and laboratory tests covering biochemistry, hematology, and coagulation.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 18.0. Continuous variables were analyzed with independent t-tests, while categorical variables employed χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Baseline patient characteristics were presented as means with standard deviations or percentages. For analysis, quantitative HCV RNA levels were logarithmically transformed. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance defined as p < 0.05.
Fifty patients were included in this study. The average age was 62.0 ± 11.4 years, with 16 (32.0%) being male. Liver cirrhosis was diagnosed in 18 (36.0%) patients, and among these, three (6%) had decompensated cirrhosis. The mean baseline HCV RNA level was 6.19 ± 0.91 log10 IU/mL. Baseline HCV RNA levels were distributed as follows: 32.0% had ≤800,000 IU/mL; 8.0% had between 800,000 and 2,000,000 IU/mL; 30.0% had between 2,000,000 and 6,000,000 IU/mL; and 30.0% had ≥6,000,000 IU/mL. Of the 20 patients who previously failed PEG-IFN plus RBV treatment, 55.0% (11/20) were relapsers, 40.0% (8/20) were partial or null responders, and 5.0% (1/20) experienced intolerance and early termination. The treatment regimen involved 13 patients (26.0%) receiving SOF/DCV without RBV, while 37 patients (74.0%) received SOF/DCV with weight-based RBV for 12 weeks. Most cirrhotic patients (14, 77.8%) and noncirrhotic patients (23, 71.8%) were treated with SOF/DCV plus RBV.
Liver disease severity in patients was assessed using FibroScan data and clinical parameters. Fibrosis stages included 24.0% with ≤F2, 40.0% with F3, 30.0% with Child-Pugh A cirrhosis, and 6.0% with Child-Pugh B-C cirrhosis. The average FIB-4 score was 4.23 ± 3.53, and a significant positive correlation was observed between FibroScan and FIB-4 results (γ = 0.34; p = 0.012).
Following SOF/DCV-based treatment, all 50 patients completed the 12-week therapy without interruption and achieved a 100% SVR12 rate after post-treatment follow-up. HCV RNA levels, measured by real-time PCR, became undetectable (<15 IU/mL) in 40.0% of patients by week 2, rising to 94.0% by week 4, and reaching 100% by week 12. Of the 30 patients (60.0%) who still had detectable serum HCV RNA at week 2, most (20 individuals) showed low-level viremia (15-50 IU/mL), with only seven exceeding 100 IU/mL (Table 2). The proportion of patients with undetectable HCV RNA at weeks 2 and 4 was similar, regardless of whether RBV was included in their treatment regimen.
Overall, 31 patients (62.0%) experienced at least one adverse event (AE). The most frequently reported subjective AEs across all participants were fatigue, pruritus, dizziness, skin rashes, and insomnia (Table 3). When comparing treatment groups, patients receiving SOF/DCV plus RBV exhibited a higher incidence of fatigue, pruritus, asthenia, and dizziness than those on SOF/DCV therapy alone (p < 0.05). For the SOF/DCV-only group, fatigue, insomnia, pruritus, and skin rashes were the primary AEs. Conversely, fatigue, pruritus, dizziness, and skin rashes were most common in the SOF/DCV with RBV group. All subjective AEs were generally mild and manageable with medication.
In terms of laboratory adverse events (AEs), a notable difference was observed in hemoglobin (Hgb) decline. Patients receiving SOF/DCV with ribavirin (RBV) experienced a mean Hgb reduction of 1.62 ± 1.34 g/dL, significantly greater than the 0.24 ± 0.12 g/dL decrease seen in those on SOF/DCV alone (p < 0.0001). Anemia was also a concern; 14.0% (7/50) of all patients developed grade 2 anemia (Hgb: 8.0-10.0 g/dL), and 4.0% (2/50) had grade 3 anemia (Hgb < 8.0 g/dL). Notably, all cases of grade 3 anemia occurred in the SOF/DCV plus RBV group. Hyperbilirubinemia, specifically unconjugated, was exclusively seen in patients treated with SOF/DCV and RBV, with 13.5% (5/37) experiencing grade 2 (1.5-3.0 × ULN) levels. This condition, however, gradually resolved with continued therapy. No patients developed grade 3 or 4 hyperbilirubinemia or signs of hepatic decompensation. Throughout the study, no elevation in ALT was detected, and serum creatinine and eGFR remained stable, with no patient's eGFR dropping below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2.
Chronic hepatitis C (CHC) is a leading cause of chronic liver disease globally, including in Taiwan. A limited course of antiviral therapy can cure CHC, significantly improving patients' long-term outcomes. Studies have shown that achieving a sustained virological response (SVR) reduces all-cause mortality by 50% in the general CHC population and by approximately 74% in CHC patients with cirrhosis. Furthermore, advanced fibrotic CHC patients who previously failed IFN-based therapy face significantly higher rates of all-cause mortality, liver-related mortality, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma compared to those who achieve SVR. These findings, consistent even in the era of IFN-free direct-acting antivirals, underscore the critical need for effective antiviral treatments, especially for CHC patients with advanced fibrosis or prior treatment failures, to improve long-term prognosis.
The advent of IFN-free direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has revolutionized HCV treatment, offering superior efficacy and safety. Among these, sofosbuvir (SOF) combined with ribavirin (RBV) was the initial IFN-free regimen for chronic hepatitis C (CHC). While registration trials (17–20) demonstrated high effectiveness for genotype-2 patients without liver cirrhosis, sustained virological response (SVR) rates were notably lower in cirrhotic patients, particularly those with prior IFN treatment failure (18, 20). Real-world studies have further explored the effectiveness of SOF plus RBV for genotype-2 CHC. For instance, the HCV-TARGET study (27), a prospective longitudinal investigation across 57 North American and four European centers, reported SVR12 rates for 12 or 16 weeks of SOF plus RBV. Non-cirrhotic patients achieved SVR12 rates of 91.0% and 92.9% for 12 and 16 weeks of therapy, respectively. In cirrhotic patients, SVR12 rates were 79.0% and 83% for the same durations. Consistent with these findings, other real-world investigations (28, 29) indicated that SVR rates in real-life scenarios are generally lower than those observed in clinical trials. A study of 823 U.S. veterans with genotype-2 CHC (28) showed SVR rates of 81.6% for treatment-naïve and 70.9% for treatment-experienced patients after a 12-week course of SOF plus RBV. Similarly, a European study (29) of 236 genotype-2 patients treated with 12 weeks of SOF and RBV reported SVR rates of only 80% in treatment-experienced patients, 74% in cirrhotics, and 75% in patients with HCV RNA levels ≥ 6 million IU/mL.
A meta-analysis published last year examined the real-world efficacy of SOF plus RBV for genotype-2 CHC in Asia, encompassing 2208 patients across 13 studies. This analysis revealed that cirrhotic patients experienced an 8.7% lower SVR12 rate compared to non-cirrhotic individuals. Furthermore, SVR12 was 7.2% lower in treatment-experienced patients than in treatment-naïve patients. The lowest SVR12 rate, at 84.5%, was observed in cirrhotic patients who had prior treatment experience. These findings, consistent with academic guidelines and studies from Western countries, suggest that the SOF plus RBV regimen may be inadequate for real-world populations, particularly those with challenging characteristics like liver cirrhosis, previous IFN treatment failure, and elevated baseline HCV RNA levels.
The combination of sofosbuvir (SOF) and daclatasvir (DCV) has gained broad approval for treating various chronic hepatitis C (CHC) infections. This is due to its demonstrated high efficacy and tolerability in clinical trials. For instance, in genotype-2 CHC patients, with or without cirrhosis, SOF/DCV therapy proved highly effective. Furthermore, this pangenotypic regimen achieved excellent SVR12 rates in genotype-1 CHC patients, specifically 98% for subtype 1a and 100% for subtype 1b, even in those who were treatment-naïve or had failed prior interferon therapy. The SOF/DCV combination also showed SVR12 rates exceeding 90% in difficult-to-treat populations, such as those with advanced liver disease, hepatic decompensation, HIV/HCV coinfection, genotype-3 infection, and post-liver transplant HCV recurrence.
The optimal treatment for genotype-2 chronic hepatitis C (CHC) is still evolving, despite advancements in direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapies. Current guidelines from various organizations offer different recommendations. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) suggests sofosbuvir (SOF) plus velpatasvir (VEL) or glecaprevir plus pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) for both treatment-naïve and interferon (IFN)-failure patients, with SOF/daclatasvir (DCV) as an alternative. The 2016 European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guideline recommended SOF/DCV or SOF/VEL for these patient groups, with GLE/PIB included in its 2018 update. Meanwhile, the 2016 Asian-Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) advised a 12-week course of SOF and weight-based ribavirin (RBV) for treatment-naïve patients, extending treatment to 16 or 24 weeks for those who previously failed IFN. For patients unable to tolerate RBV, APASL suggested SOF/DCV, SOF plus ledipasvir, or SOF/VEL.
The effectiveness and safety of SOF/DCV for genotype-2 CHC patients in real-world Asian settings have been scarcely documented. Unlike the stringent conditions of registration trials, real-life studies offer crucial insights into how new treatments perform in routine clinical practice. Our current study, for instance, revealed a highly effective antiviral response to SOF/DCV, even among challenging patient groups. This cohort included 76.0% of individuals with advanced fibrosis (≥F3), 40.0% who had previously failed PEG-IFN plus RBV treatment, and 60.0% with high baseline HCV RNA levels (≥ 2 million IU/mL). After just two weeks of SOF/DCV therapy, 40.0% of patients achieved undetectable HCV RNA (<15 IU/mL), a figure that rose to 94.0% by week four. Notably, among the 66.7% of patients who still had detectable HCV RNA at week two, the viremia was at a low level (<50 IU/mL). All patients completed the SOF/DCV treatment without interruption, and a 100% SVR12 rate was observed after post-treatment follow-up. These findings align with a recent study from southern Taiwan.
Currently, no randomized controlled trials exist to directly compare the effectiveness of SOF/DCV versus SOF/RBV for genotype-2 CHC patients. However, real-world data from Belperio et al. on 255 U.S. Veterans treated with SOF/DCV demonstrated superior SVR12 rates: 94.6% for treatment-naïve, 94.3% for treatment-experienced, and 92.0% for cirrhotic patients. These figures represent a significant improvement over previous findings from the same database, where a 12-week SOF/RBV regimen yielded SVR12 rates of only 81.6% for treatment-naïve and 70.9% for treatment-experienced patients. Combining these observations with our study's results, it is evident that SOF/DCV offers a more effective treatment alternative for genotype-2 CHC than SOF plus RBV. This suggests that combination DAA therapy, similar to other genotypes, is likely the preferred future approach. Our study's findings are expected to contribute valuable insights for future updates to regional treatment guidelines.
The addition of RBV did not significantly influence SVR12 rates, despite its association with increased adverse events like fatigue, dizziness, pruritus, asthenia, and laboratory abnormalities (decreased Hgb, hyperbilirubinemia). This aligns with findings from the U.S. Veterans study, suggesting further research is needed to clarify RBV's role in combination DAA therapies.
This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective, single-center study, and the sample size was relatively small, particularly for patients with decompensated liver disease. Furthermore, the efficacy of SOF in combination with VEL was not assessed, as this treatment is not approved in Taiwan. Finally, the study did not explore the influence of baseline NS5A or NS5B resistance-associated substitutions on treatment outcomes.
A 12-week SOF/DCV-based treatment proved highly effective and well-tolerated for Taiwanese genotype-2 CHC patients, including those with advanced fibrosis (76%), prior PEG-IFN plus RBV failure (40%), and high baseline HCV RNA (60%), regardless of liver cirrhosis presence.
