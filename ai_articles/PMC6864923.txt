This study investigated the role of Golgi phosphoprotein 3 (GOLPH3), a recently identified oncogene, in endometrial carcinoma (EC), a context where its specific contributions were previously unexplored. The research aimed to assess GOLPH3 expression within EC and delineate its impact on EC cell proliferation, migration, and survival. To achieve this, GOLPH3 expression was analyzed in EC patient samples and four EC cell lines (HEC-1A, KLE, RL95-2, and Ishikawa) using qRT-PCR, western blotting, and immunohistochemistry. Subsequently, EC cell lines were engineered for either GOLPH3 overexpression or knockdown. The resulting effects on proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and migration were then evaluated through in vitro cell viability and transwell assays, as well as in vivo experiments involving cell injection into mice. The findings revealed a significant upregulation of GOLPH3 in EC tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (P< 0.05), with its expression correlating with tumor grade (P< 0.05). All four EC cell lines exhibited higher GOLPH3 expression than endometrial stromal cells (ESCs) (P< 0.05), and notably, GOLPH3 levels were elevated in EC cell lines with high invasive potential compared to non-invasive ones (P< 0.05). Experimental manipulation showed that GOLPH3 knockdown suppressed EC cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis in vitro. Furthermore, GOLPH3 knockdown also impeded EC cell invasion and migration both in vitro and in vivo, a process linked to the regulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Conversely, GOLPH3 overexpression stimulated proliferation and migration. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that GOLPH3 facilitates EMT and metastasis in EC cells, suggesting its potential as a prognostic indicator for EC progression and a promising therapeutic target for this cancer.

Endometrial carcinoma (EC), the most prevalent female reproductive tract cancer in developed nations, is experiencing a global rise in incidence, exacerbating health risks for women. Although many cases have a favorable prognosis, 22% of patients succumb to the disease. While early diagnosis is common, approximately one-third of cases involve regional or distant metastasis. Despite advancements in therapy, metastatic EC continues to be associated with a poor 5-year survival rate and frequent recurrence. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms driving EC development and progression is crucial for identifying prognostic indicators and developing new diagnostics and targeted treatments.
GOLPH3, a 34-kDa trans-Golgi matrix membrane protein, plays a role in cellular processes such as receptor recycling, glycosylation, and protein trafficking. This highly conserved protein has been implicated as an oncogene in various cancers, where its expression correlates with tumor growth, survival, clinical stage, and metastasis. These cancers include small cell lung, breast, ovarian epithelial, oral tongue, liver, rectal, prostate, bladder, esophageal squamous cell, gastric, and kidney cancers. GOLPH3 is thought to drive tumorigenesis and migration by activating key signaling pathways like mTOR, Wnt, and NF-κB. Despite its established role in other malignancies, the specific functions of GOLPH3 in endometrial carcinoma development remain unexplored.
This study explored GOLPH3's role in EC by analyzing its expression in patient tissues and cell lines. We further investigated how altering GOLPH3 levels in EC cell lines, both in lab settings and animal models, affected cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, migration, and EMT.
To determine GOLPH3's role in endometrial carcinoma (EC) development, its expression was assessed in EC tissues and cell lines using qRT-PCR, western blot, and immunohistochemistry. Subsequently, the impact of GOLPH3 on EC cell behavior was investigated. This involved creating GOLPH3 knockdown (shRNA) and overexpression (pcDNA3.1 (+)-GOLPH3 plasmid) models in KLE and Ishikawa EC cell lines, respectively. Researchers then evaluated GOLPH3's effects on cell proliferation via CCK-8 assays, migration and invasion through transwell assays and a xenograft tumor model, and apoptosis using Annexin V FITC detection. Additionally, qRT-PCR and western blot were employed to analyze EMT-related gene expression in these modified EC cells, aiming to clarify the mechanisms behind GOLPH3's influence on cell migration and invasion.
From January 2014 to December 2015, thirty endometrial carcinoma (EC) tissue samples, along with their adjacent non-cancerous counterparts, were randomly procured from patients undergoing curative resection at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University. Additionally, an endometrial tissue specimen was sourced from an endometriotic patient, specifically chosen because she had no prior history of endometrial hyperplasia or neoplasia and had abstained from anti-inflammatory or hormonal medications for at least three months before her surgery. All study procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University. Prior to surgical resection, both patients and their families provided informed written consent.
A tissue chip, obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co. Ltd. (CGT: HUteA060CS01, Lot: XT15-033), was utilized. This chip comprised 60 fixed pores, containing 34 endometrial carcinoma tissues and 26 normal endometrial tissues, sourced from 34 distinct cases.

All cell cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Endometrial stromal cells (ESCs) were isolated from an endometriotic patient's endometrial tissue, following the method by Hou et al. The human endometrial carcinoma (EC) cell lines HEC-1A, KLE, RL95-2, and Ishikawa were sourced from ATCC. Both cell types were grown in either DMEM or RPMI-1640 media, enriched with 10% FBS.
To achieve GOLPH3 overexpression, full-length human GOLPH3 cDNA was amplified via PCR and then subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 (+) plasmid. An empty vector served as the negative control for these experiments. Conversely, for GOLPH3 knockdown, specific short hairpin RNA (shRNA) oligonucleotide sequences targeting human GOLPH3 were cloned into the pSuper-retro-puro vector. Two distinct shRNA sequences, RNAi-1 (5’-GCAGCGCCTCATCAAGAAAGT-3’) and RNAi-2 (5’-GCATGTTAAGGAAACTCAGCC-3’), were utilized, with a scrambled shRNA (5’-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTT-3’) acting as the negative control. Plasmid transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent, and successful transfection was confirmed through qRT-PCR and Western blotting.
For gene expression analysis, cells were first harvested by trypsinization. Total RNA was then extracted using TRIzol reagent, followed by cDNA synthesis with SuperScript II reverse transcriptase. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed on an ABI PRISM 7900HT system, utilizing primers designed with Primer Premier 5.0 (sequences detailed in Table 1) and GAPDH as a reference. Expression levels were determined via the 2-ΔΔCT method.
The sequences for primers utilized in quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) are detailed in Table 1. This table lists the forward and reverse primer sequences (5’-3’) for several genes, including GOLPH3, Ki67, N-cadherin, Vimentin, E-cadherin, α-catenin, and GAPDH. These primers were crucial for detecting the expression levels of these specific genes in the study.
Following washing, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer. The resulting cell lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatant proteins were combined with loading buffer before being boiled for ten minutes. These protein samples then underwent separation via SDS-PAGE and subsequent transfer to PVDF membranes. After a one-hour room temperature block with non-fat milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies for twelve hours at 4°C, followed by a one-hour incubation with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature. Protein expression was finally assessed by densitometry with Image J, after visualization of bands using enhanced chemiluminescence.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) involved several steps. First, the paraffin tissue chip was dehydrated using PBS. Antigen retrieval followed, employing a sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0). Next, the chip was blocked for two hours at room temperature with 10% normal serum and 1% BSA in TBS. Incubation with an Anti-GOLPH3 antibody occurred for 12 hours at 4°C, followed by a one-hour incubation at room temperature with a peroxidase-labeled anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) antibody. Finally, the chip was exposed to DAB chromogen for 1-10 minutes until optimal staining, then counterstained with hematoxylin.
Cell viability was assessed by seeding cells at 3 × 10^5 per well in 100 μL of 10% FBS-supplemented culture medium in 96-well plates. After 24, 48, 72, or 96 hours, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was introduced to each well. Following a 1-4 hour incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, the plates were gently agitated for one minute to ensure uniform color distribution. Absorbance was then quantified at 450 nm using a Beckman Coulter ultraviolet spectrometer.
To assess apoptosis, cells were initially plated at a density of 1 x 10^6 per 10-cm plate and incubated for 24 hours, with their morphology observed via phase-contrast microscopy. Following this, cells were collected using trypsin-EDTA, rinsed twice with PBS, and then suspended in binding buffer at a concentration of 10^6 cells/ml. Annexin V and propidium iodide (5 µl each per 10^5 cells) were added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 15 minutes. Apoptotic cell percentages were subsequently determined using an EPICS XL flow cytometer.
To assess cell invasion, Matrigel-coated transwell inserts from Costar, equipped with 8-μm pore polycarbonate filters, were utilized. Following manufacturer guidelines, each insert was prepared with 50 μl of 1 mg/ml Matrigel matrix. Cells were introduced to the upper chamber at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells in 200 μl of serum-free medium, while the lower chamber received 600 μl of medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After a 24-hour incubation period, cells that had successfully invaded to the membrane's lower surface were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution. An Olympus fluorescence microscope, set at ×100 magnification, was then used to count the migrated cells across five randomly selected fields on each membrane. Migration assays followed an identical procedure, with the sole exception that the transwell inserts were not coated with Matrigel.
For in vivo experiments, 14 female Balb/c nude mice, weighing 18-20g and aged 4-5 weeks, were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Third Medical University. These animals were maintained under humane conditions in barrier facilities, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and unrestricted access to food and water. Control or GOLPH3-knockdown cells, prepared as previously described, were suspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 x 10^7 cells/ml. A 0.1 ml aliquot of this suspension was then administered via tail vein injection to each mouse, with 7 mice per experimental group. Thirty days post-inoculation, the animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Tumor volume was subsequently determined using the formula (length × width^2)/2, with measurements taken using calipers. All procedures adhered to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Third Medical University.

Statistical analysis was conducted using Graph Pad Prism software (version 7.0). Numerical data, presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), were compared between two groups using Student’s two-tailed t-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was applied. The relationship between GOLPH3 and clinic-pathological factors was assessed using Mann-Whitney analysis or the Chi-square test, with enumeration data expressed as counts. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted by asterisks in figures (p*< 0.05; p**< 0.01).
To understand GOLPH3's role in endometrial carcinoma (EC), its mRNA and protein levels were assessed in four EC cell lines using qRT-PCR and western blotting, respectively, with Ki67 serving as a positive control. All EC cell lines exhibited significantly elevated GOLPH3 expression compared to non-malignant endometrial stromal cells (ESCs) (Fig. 1A and B). Further investigation into the correlation between GOLPH3 expression and metastatic potential in EC cell lines, specifically through transwell migration assays, indicated higher GOLPH3 levels in the more invasive KLE and HEC-1A cell lines compared to the less invasive RL95-2 and Ishikawa lines (Fig. 1A and B). Analysis of patient samples also confirmed this upregulation: both mRNA and protein expression of GOLPH3 were markedly higher in 30 EC tissue samples and 6 EC/normal adjacent tissue (NAT) pairs, respectively, when compared to NAT (Fig. 1C and D). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed that GOLPH3 was overexpressed and localized in the cytoplasm of carcinoma tissues (Fig. 1E). Moreover, a relationship was observed between GOLPH3 expression levels and the grade of endometrial carcinoma. Nevertheless, no association was found between GOLPH3 expression and other clinical characteristics, including patient age, disease stage, or lymph node metastasis (Table 2).
GOLPH3 protein expression in endometrial carcinoma tissues (n=34) correlated with tumor grade, but not age, stage, or lymph node metastasis.
GOLPH3 expression correlated with tumor grade (P<0.05), but not with patient age, cancer stage, or lymph node metastasis. These correlations were determined using Chi-square or Mann-Whitney analyses.
To assess GOLPH3's impact on endometrial carcinoma (EC) cell behavior, the highly invasive KLE cell line was manipulated. GOLPH3 expression was effectively suppressed in KLE cells through transfection with shGOLPH3, as confirmed by significantly reduced mRNA and protein levels compared to control cells receiving an empty vector (Fig. 2A and B). This GOLPH3 knockdown led to several notable changes in vitro. Proliferation, measured by CCK-8 assay, was markedly slower in shGOLPH3-transfected cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, transwell assays indicated a significant decrease in both migration and invasion capabilities (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). Investigating cell survival, flow cytometry with an Annexin V FITC apoptosis detection kit revealed a substantially higher rate of apoptosis in KLE cells with suppressed GOLPH3 (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that inhibiting GOLPH3 expression in EC cells curtails their proliferation, migration, and invasion, while simultaneously promoting apoptosis.
Figure 4 illustrates that GOLPH3 downregulation lessens distant EC cell metastasis in living organisms. After 30 days, nude mice inoculated with GOLPH3-knockdown (KLE-shGOLPH3) EC cells, compared to controls, exhibited reduced tumor volume growth curves, tumor weights, and tumor images. These findings are the mean ± SD of three independent experiments, with statistical significance indicated.
To investigate GOLPH3's role, the non-invasive Ishikawa cell line was transiently transfected with a pcDNA3.1 (+)-GOLPH3 plasmid. Successful transfection was verified by qRT-PCR and western blot. Ishikawa cells overexpressing GOLPH3 showed significantly higher GOLPH3 mRNA and protein levels compared to empty vector controls (Fig. 3A and B). This overexpression led to increased proliferation, migration, and invasion (p< 0.05) (Fig. 3C and D), and a reduced apoptosis rate in vitro (Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that GOLPH3 overexpression drives aggressive EC cell behavior.
Figure 5 illustrates GOLPH3's role in regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in EC cells. When GOLPH3 was knocked down in KLE cells, mesenchymal markers decreased, while epithelial markers increased, indicating mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET). Conversely, ectopic GOLPH3 overexpression in Ishikawa cells led to an upregulation of mesenchymal markers and a downregulation of epithelial markers. These expression changes were assessed using qRT-PCR and western blotting, with all results representing the mean ± SD from three independent experiments and statistically significant differences marked.
The in vivo role of GOLPH3 in metastasis was subsequently explored. Nude mice were inoculated via tail vein with either control KLE cells or KLE cells with GOLPH3 knockdown (KLE-shGOLPH3). After 30 days, the animals were euthanized. The group receiving GOLPH3 knockdown cells showed a significant reduction in distant metastases, with only 4 out of 7 mice developing them, compared to all 7 mice in the control group. Furthermore, both tumor volume and weight were markedly lower in the GOLPH3 knockdown group, indicating that high GOLPH3 expression is crucial for EC cell metastasis in living organisms.
To substantiate the role of GOLPH3 in promoting EC cell migration and invasion, we investigated the expression of EMT-related genes. Through qRT-PCR and western blotting, we analyzed both mRNA and protein levels in EC cells with either GOLPH3 overexpression or knockdown. Our findings revealed that GOLPH3 knockdown in KLE cells led to a decrease in mesenchymal markers like N-cadherin and vimentin, while simultaneously boosting epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and α-catenin (Fig. 5A and B). Conversely, ectopic GOLPH3 overexpression in Ishikawa cells resulted in elevated mesenchymal marker expression and reduced epithelial marker expression (Fig. 5C and D), aligning with the idea that EMT is crucial for cancer cell invasiveness.
The most prevalent cancer affecting the female reproductive system in developed nations is endometrial carcinoma (EC), and its occurrence is on the rise. A significant factor contributing to poor patient outcomes is the frequent and early metastasis observed in approximately a quarter of high-risk EC cases. Consequently, discovering new molecules that control EC metastasis is crucial for pinpointing molecular prognostic indicators and devising more effective treatment approaches. Substantial research highlights that epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) empowers epithelial cancer cells, including EC cells, with increased mobility and invasiveness, thus promoting distant metastasis.
Our investigation reveals, for the first time, that GOLPH3 is significantly overexpressed in endometrial carcinoma (EC) tissues and cells when compared to healthy adjacent tissues. We observed GOLPH3's cytoplasmic localization within carcinoma cells, and its expression level correlated with tumor grade. This overexpression of GOLPH3 proved crucial for the heightened proliferation, increased invasiveness, and diminished apoptosis rates characteristic of cancer cells, both in laboratory settings and in animal models. Conversely, reducing GOLPH3 expression in typically aggressive and invasive EC cells suppressed their proliferative and metastatic capabilities, both in vitro and in vivo. We further established that GOLPH3 facilitates EC cell metastasis by modulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). EC cells with high GOLPH3 expression exhibited an EMT phenotype, while its downregulation in aggressive EC cells induced mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET). These findings strongly suggest that GOLPH3 overexpression is vital for EC cell tumorigenicity, aggressiveness, and metastatic potential. The reversibility of these GOLPH3-driven processes through its suppression highlights GOLPH3 as a promising novel diagnostic and therapeutic target for EC, meriting further research.
Research indicates GOLPH3 promotes cancer cell proliferation and metastasis through Wnt/β-catenin, mTOR, and NF-kB pathway activation. These findings align with our observations of GOLPH3's oncogenic role in EC cells, though the specific signaling mechanisms and downstream targets in EC require further investigation.
This study concludes that GOLPH3 overexpression in EC cells leads to an aggressive cancer phenotype, marked by high proliferation, strong invasiveness, and reduced apoptosis. Crucially, inhibiting GOLPH3 expression can reverse these aggressive traits, suggesting its therapeutic potential. However, the study lacked bioluminescence imaging of xenografted tumors and intensity measurements, necessitating further clinical validation in human patients.
