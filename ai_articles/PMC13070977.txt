This research outlines the opportunities and challenges encountered by a diverse team of academics as they designed an innovative closed-loop system. Their goal was to minimize e-waste and reclaim precious metals, using mobile phones as a specific example. The paper contrasts this interdisciplinary methodology with the prevailing top-down strategies for transitioning to a circular economy. A core objective of this study is the creation of a product service system (PSS) aimed at streamlining the retrieval of valuable functional components and metals from mobile phone circuit boards. To achieve a comprehensive solution and mitigate unforeseen negative effects, the paper examines various aspects beyond just technological advancements. These include suitable component lifespans, the often-overlooked role of individuals in the circular economy, how customers engage with the PSS, environmental life cycle assessment, and the social ramifications of the proposed PSS. The development of supportive technologies and materials to aid component and metal recovery, alongside the creation of an emotionally durable outer casing, is also detailed. Furthermore, the study emphasizes the critical need for a broad, interdisciplinary understanding of value within the circular economy.

Historically, people valued their possessions, maintaining them and using them until they were no longer functional (Fromm 2013, 61). However, the post-World War II era saw a dramatic increase in consumer goods production and ownership in the Global North, driven by manufacturing mechanization and automation. The North American economy, for instance, became dependent on continuous production growth, a model epitomized by Ford's assembly line, which is crucial for modern capitalist systems. As the 20th century progressed and production began to outpace demand, the sustained economic growth faced a challenge. This led to the emergence of marketing professionals. These marketers, drawing inspiration from the fashion industry's success in prompting purchases through new styles before items wore out, introduced a similar strategy across various sectors, from household goods to automobiles. A key tactic was planned obsolescence, manifesting in functional, aesthetic, psychological, and technological forms (London 2016; Packard 1963; Slade 2006; Cooper 2010). This marked a shift from products designed for long-term service and maintenance to the creation of increasingly short product lifespans. Consequently, a "throw away culture" took root, expanding globally over the next fifty years and remaining a fundamental aspect of today's economies (Cooper 2005).
In contemporary society, mobile electronic devices like smartphones and tablets are often discarded prematurely, before their functional lifespan is exhausted. For instance, in the UK and US, a mobile phone's initial use typically lasts under two years (Green Alliance 2016). This rapid turnover is fueled by manufacturers continually launching new, often sleeker and more powerful, models, leading to technological, cosmetic, or stylistic obsolescence. Such design choices frequently hinder device upgrades or maintenance. A prime example is the practice of gluing batteries into phones and tablets to conserve space, which, despite its intention, restricts the device's operational life to that of its battery, often the sole component with an intrinsically brief lifespan (Takeno et al. 2005).
The current management of electronic waste (e-waste) presents significant challenges, as only a small fraction is effectively recycled or reused at the component level. Instead, the majority of e-waste is either incinerated, sent to landfills, kept in disused storage, or exported to developing nations. For instance, in 2010, the United States alone saw 141 million smartphones discarded, with 89% ending up in landfills. Global e-waste figures from 2009 ranged from 20 to 40 million tonnes annually. Regardless of the precise amount, this trend has severe environmental and social consequences. These impacts stem from both the initial extraction of vital metals like tantalum from coltan ore for electronic components and the subsequent disposal and unregulated recycling practices in countries such as China, Nigeria, Pakistan, and Ghana.
E-waste has significant global social consequences, affecting economies, cultures, and public health. Studies, such as Kirby and Lora-Wainwright's (2015) work on China and Japan, highlight e-waste's contribution to East Asian economic growth. However, this sector's informal, unregulated, and insecure nature also leads to severe negative health impacts for the thousands who depend on it for their livelihood in East Asia and other regions.
The linear "take-make-waste" economic model faces growing challenges due to material scarcity and fluctuating prices. Consequently, there's a heightened interest in resource circularity, prominently discussed within the framework of the circular economy (CE). Key organizations like the European Commission (EC) and the Ellen MacArthur Foundation (EMF) are driving CE initiatives, primarily aiming to reshape how designers and manufacturers approach product creation, usage, and disposal. Beyond industrial practices, there's a growing recognition of the crucial role citizens play in the CE. This includes fostering consumer acceptance of novel consumption patterns and addressing the broader societal and cultural implications of proposed circular production-consumption systems.
The "Closed Loop Emotionally Valuable E-waste Recovery" (CLEVER) project emerged from a research "sandpit" hosted by the UK Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council. This initiative brought together diverse academic fields to foster innovative solutions for enhancing resource efficiency. The CLEVER team, comprising specialists in chemistry, material science, engineering, life cycle analysis, social science, and product design, aims to establish circular material flows within consumer electronics. Their approach considers various technical, economic, social, and environmental factors. This paper details a proposed product service system (PSS) designed to aid this transition, outlining the challenges and opportunities encountered during the development of this closed-loop system, along with key advancements made in addressing these issues.
The Circular Economy (CE) is often characterized by the cyclical movement of materials, aiming to lessen both the intake of new resources and the generation of waste (Haas et al. 2015). An alternative perspective views the CE as a method for preserving the inherent value of products, their constituent parts, and the materials they are made from. A key principle supported by CE advocates is designing for extended product lifespans (Great Recovery 2016; Park 2016). The environmental benefits of increased product longevity are contingent upon the interplay between impacts throughout a product's life cycle and its end-of-life management (Kwak and Kim 2012; Cooper 2010). Therefore, sustainable manufacturing necessitates that products are conceived with a defined useful life to minimize resource consumption and environmental harm, aligned with their intended end-of-life strategy (Kara et al. 2008). Considering mobile phones, a significant portion—74% on average—of their lifetime carbon dioxide emissions stems from material extraction and manufacturing (Suckling and Lee 2015). To enhance resource efficiency and diminish e-waste, consumers could be motivated to keep their devices for longer periods and return them once their functional life concludes (Cooper 1994; Van Nes et al. 2016; Chalkley et al. 2016; Wilhelm 2012). Nevertheless, convincing individuals to retain products for longer proves challenging. This difficulty arises from perceived technological obsolescence, social pressures, and minor product damage. These elements are largely influenced by marketing tactics of companies that depend on quick product turnover, especially as growth in the saturated smartphone market decelerates. Despite these challenges, implementing circular flows for components and materials has the potential to substantially lower material and manufacturing expenses (EMF 2016), thereby mitigating the economic drawbacks associated with reduced sales.
Instead of viewing a device as a monolithic entity, a more refined strategy involves evaluating the ideal lifespan and inherent "value" of its individual components (Kara et al. 2008; Ji et al. 2013). This perspective is supported by a performance economy framework, which prioritizes sustaining the quality of existing assets over merely managing the flow of components or materials within the economy (Lee et al. 2016). Such an approach prompts questions like whether maximum value is achieved through phone reuse or by recovering and repurposing its constituent parts, and identifying the most effective points for intervention in the product's lifecycle. This detailed assessment of asset value is crucial for developing a Product Service System (PSS), as any interventions, whether component upgrades or full unit replacements, necessitate user involvement. Furthermore, this method demands an early design-stage understanding of anticipated component lifespans. This ensures that replaceable parts are easily accessible when needed and are engineered for suitable durability. The feasibility of repairs and upgrades hinges on several factors: the expense of the procedure, the product's total cost, the current price of replacement parts, and, critically, the owner's inclination to upgrade components versus buying a new model. While costs fluctuate over a product's lifetime, designers can significantly boost the potential for component replacement by incorporating design for disassembly principles (Harjula et al. 1996) and fostering emotional durability (Chapman 2015).
Manufacturers stand to gain clear economic advantages from the circular economy (CE), notably through decreased dependence on unstable global material supply chains. However, recent studies emphasize that cultural and social values are crucial for a successful and consumer-beneficial transition to the CE. The adoption of product-service systems (PSSs), for instance, often hinges on individual preferences and societal norms, sometimes conflicting with them. This occurs when consumers must change their consumption habits, such as opting to rent instead of buy. Consequently, integrating consumers into these new business models presents a more significant hurdle than often acknowledged in discussions about the CE.
Current policy and governance strategies for promoting a circular economy (CE) often overlook a critical aspect: the active role of citizens. These approaches typically portray consumers as passive recipients of new product-service systems (PSSs), with the primary emphasis on designing products for post-use value recovery through novel business models. However, extensive research on sustainable lifestyles in the Global North reveals a crucial need to critically examine existing consumption values and practices, rather than merely embedding sustainability within products and services. While business models can independently enhance material circularity, there's a significant risk of rebound effects in wider consumption patterns if consumers remain disengaged and unaware of the CE's core objectives. Therefore, the CE should be understood not just as a business, scientific, or technological undertaking, but equally as a social and political initiative.
Smartphones, as a type of mobile electronic device, offer a significant yet complex case study for implementing a circular economy (CE). The magnitude of the current issue is highlighted by estimates suggesting that between 28 and 125 million mobile phones are currently unused in UK households (Green Alliance 2016). Research from Wilson et al. (2017) involving 181 mobile phone owners aged 18-25 revealed that while each participant owned their phone for nearly five years, almost three of those years were spent with the device in storage, unused. These "hibernating" devices represent a loss of valuable materials, which in turn drives the need for greater raw material extraction and production to satisfy market demand. The value of these stored phones also decreases over time, as depicted in Figure 1, making their recovery a time-sensitive matter (Wilson et al. 2017). To achieve a closed-loop system, facilitate the recovery and recycling of precious resources, and mitigate premature disposal and its environmental consequences, it is crucial to incentivize consumers to return their devices. This would allow functional components to remain within the manufacturing cycle. Figure 1 visually represents the functional and emotional value of a mobile phone's distinct parts—skin, skeleton, and organs—suggesting that component lifetimes are influenced by factors such as performance degradation, technological advancements (for skeleton and organs), or accidental damage and cosmetic obsolescence (for the skin).
Recent advancements in mobile phone design have explored solutions for various challenges, though none offer a comprehensive answer to the problems of manufacturing and disposal. Some projects, like Fairphone, prioritize ethical sourcing, material origin, and social responsibility, demonstrating that complex supply chains can be effectively managed. However, Fairphone's approach largely overlooks the crucial aspect of recovering valuable metals at the end-of-life stage to reintroduce them into the manufacturing cycle. Other innovations, such as Project Ara (formerly Phonebloks), PuzzlePhone, and nexpaq, focus on modular designs to facilitate personalization and component upgrades, thereby extending product life without needing to replace the entire device. Despite this, there's a notable absence of information regarding the recovery processes for these modular components and their potential re-entry into the manufacturing loop. A concern with modular designs is the risk of increased e-waste due to frequent component turnover, which could also diminish user attachment to the device by emphasizing its functional and content-driven value over its physical form.
The CLEVER project distinguishes itself from other initiatives by proposing a hypothetical Product Service System (PSS) aimed at minimizing e-waste. This PSS comprehensively addresses the entire product lifecycle, with a specific emphasis on component recovery and material reintroduction into the manufacturing process, areas often overlooked by other projects. A PSS itself is a business model centered on function, blending service and ownership. It presents an effective strategy for decreasing material consumption by reorienting business priorities from merely designing and selling physical goods to developing and marketing integrated systems of products and services that collectively meet specific customer needs.
The CLEVER Product Service System (PSS) design began by recognizing that mobile phones are not monolithic but consist of various components. These components possess distinct longevity requirements, different value-chain lifespans, and diverse pathways for recovery, disposal, or recycling. This inherent variability necessitates varied levels of consumer awareness and engagement. Instead of treating the mobile phone as a singular item with uniform emotional and functional value, the project conceptualized the device as having three distinct parts: the "skin" (outer casing, directly interacting with the user), the "skeleton" (critical internal support components like the circuit board), and "organs" (electronic components providing core functionality, such as the processor). This segmentation aims to simplify the recovery of subassemblies and individual components when optimal.
The CLEVER project conceptualized mobile phones as devices containing components with varying lifespans. Based on this understanding, they created a hypothetical Product Service System (PSS) to exemplify best practices. This PSS aimed to outline the service offering, user engagement, and the various stakeholders involved, while also assessing its environmental, social, and economic benefits (Vezzoli et al. 2016). The PSS was conceived during a "top-down" workshop, driven by the insights of CLEVER researchers and investigators. The diverse expertise of these participants spanned several areas: material testing, characterization, and aging; social and environmental change, along with multi-level environmental governance; social life cycle assessment (LCA) methodologies and social planetary boundaries; the development of functional materials from renewable resources, especially cellulose; and user-centered sustainable design strategies to inform the creation of less resource-intensive products. A sustainable design expert, experienced in service and PSS design, led the workshop. The workshop established clear goals for the PSS: it needed to establish a closed-loop system to optimize material flow, efficiently recover valuable resources, and maximize component remanufacturing and reuse. Furthermore, the PSS was designed to empower and engage users by offering advantages like data security and backup options, rather than relying solely on technological solutions. It also had to address social, economic, and environmental impacts, actively working to prevent negative rebound effects. While presented as a hypothetical model, the PSS was developed with an eye toward commercial viability, demonstrating scalability and a consistent, predictable stream for remanufacturing that would ensure value retention beyond initial use.
The workshop utilized business origami, a creative and generative approach, to develop a system model. This methodology, often employed by multidisciplinary teams in the initial stages of design, involved the project team manipulating two-dimensional paper tokens on a table. These tokens symbolized various system elements, such as factories, countries, different modes of transport, phone components (skeletons, organs, skins), people, infrastructure, and users. Paper arrows were continuously added and adjusted to illustrate the interactions between these elements, guided by ongoing discussions. Post-It notes served to annotate the evolving system and record questions. Following this, environmental and social hotspots were identified, and the service proposition was refined in accordance with the workshop's objectives.
The CLEVER Product Service System (PSS), as conceptualized during the workshop, prioritized hardware upgrades over complete phone replacement, leading to distinct ownership models and component lifespans. This PSS combines elements of both use-oriented and product-oriented approaches (Tukker 2004). For the "skeleton" and "organs"—components with low emotional value but requiring frequent technical updates—the service provider retains ownership and leases their functionality to the customer. These parts are designed for a short initial lifespan, with a strong emphasis on their recovery for reuse, remanufacturing, or targeted material recovery. This arrangement positions the service provider to efficiently manage the phased recovery of valuable components and metals at their peak value (Lee et al. 2016; Suckling and Lee 2015). Conversely, the "skin" component is owned by the customer and is intended to foster emotional attachment. This emotional value is cultivated to encourage users to maintain the device and return it for strategic refurbishment and internal upgrades of the skeleton and organs. The skin materials are specifically engineered to develop unique surface variations over time, creating intrigue and a sense of personalization (Ludden et al. 2012). This design challenges the modern expectation of perpetual perfection (Maffei and Fisher 2013) by allowing the object's surface patina to reflect the passage of time and accumulated experiences (Giaccardi et al. 2014; Odom and Pierce 2016; Chapman 2009; Rognoli and Karana 2014; Pedgley 2016). The goal is to imbue the device with deeper personal significance (Turner and Turner 2013, 404), strengthening the emotional bond as the product gains self-expressive value (Chapman 2009; Lacey 2009; Mugge et al. 2005; Van Nes and Cramer 2006; Mugge et al. 2009).
While a Product Service System (PSS) that fully recovers and replaces both the device's exterior ("skin") and its internal functional components might seem more advantageous and simpler, such a pure leasing model has drawbacks. It diminishes the potential for active citizen participation in the circular economy, relegating them to passive consumers. Furthermore, without direct feedback on the consequences of their actions, consumers may not be incentivized to learn or modify their behavior. Evidence also suggests that leased items tend to receive less diligent care than owned ones. For instance, a study on car-sharing revealed that Zipcar users felt less personal responsibility for the vehicles, leading to a reduced sense of stewardship, as exemplified by a user's comment about parking a Zipcar in a tighter spot because it wasn't their own.
The proposed Product Service System (PSS) outlines a circular model for mobile phones, beginning with the user acquiring a service contract and the device itself. This initial purchase can occur through diverse channels, such as online retailers, physical stores, specialized upgrade centers, local franchises (like those found in coffee shops), independent community repair shops, or even vending machines. A key feature of this PSS is the gradual personalization of the phone's external casing, or "skin," over time. This evolving aesthetic is intended to foster an emotional connection, encouraging owners to retain their device rather than seeking a full replacement. When internal upgrades become necessary or desired to combat technological obsolescence, the user can return their phone through any of the aforementioned distribution platforms. The device then undergoes internal hardware and software upgrades as requested. To ensure consumer satisfaction, especially given the "panic and anxiety" some users experience when separated from their phones (Vincent 2006, 39), these "organ" replacements are designed to be swift, ideally completed in a very short timeframe. Following the upgrade, the phone, now equipped with new internal components, is returned to the user, who retains their personalized external "skin." The process continues as reclaimed components from various platforms are collected and sorted. These "organ" and "skeleton" components and subassemblies are then either prepared for reassembly and reuse or directed towards material recovery and metal refining. Existing mixed metal recycling streams can feed into this refining process, optimizing resource utilization and minimizing losses. The raw materials derived from these recovery and refining stages are subsequently remanufactured into new "organ" and "skeleton" components. Finally, these recovered and new components and subassemblies are integrated into hardware upgrades or entirely new phone assemblies with fresh "skins," before being redistributed to the various platforms, thus completing the circular loop (figure 2).
Implementing the proposed PSS presents various interdisciplinary challenges and opportunities. A collaborative approach, spanning multiple fields, is crucial for a successful transition to a broader Circular Economy.
Our strategy to encourage phone returns for functional upgrades, rather than disposal, centers on fostering an emotional bond between the user and the phone's exterior. This necessitated a collaborative effort between a product designer and a materials engineer to develop and test materials, understand experiential design, and conduct user studies.
Materials like wood, leather, stone, and certain metals are often seen to "age gracefully," acquiring a patina that increases their perceived value over time. For instance, the green verdigris on weathered copper is highly prized in construction, and wood's grain becomes more pronounced visually and texturally with weathering. Conversely, artificial materials frequently evoke negative feelings once they lose their newness; people often feel "disgust" towards worn or degraded plastic items, prompting their disposal. This difference is crucial in product design, especially for consumer electronics, which typically feature smooth, synthetic, and scratch-free polymer surfaces. Damage or wear to this pristine exterior often leads to the quick replacement of these devices.
A user study investigated how natural materials, known for "aging gracefully," would be perceived in consumer electronics. Researchers explored tactile and aesthetic reactions to new and artificially aged mobile phone cases crafted from various materials, including bamboo, walnut, cork, leather, brushed titanium, plastic, and rubber (Bridgens et al. 2015; Lilley et al. 2016). Although some participants appreciated the aesthetic changes in simulated-aged natural materials like bamboo and walnut, the most favored materials were durable, man-made options with a smooth, shiny finish, with titanium emerging as the top choice. This indicated that people's preferences are heavily shaped by their expectations for materials within a specific product category. For mobile phones, consumers anticipated sleek, uniform surfaces, and consequently, natural materials were often deemed inappropriate.
A key design objective for the phone's "skin" emerged: creating a material that initially satisfies consumer expectations for a new product with a smooth, uniform surface, but then evolves gracefully with use, offering novelty and personalization. Achieving this necessitates understanding how users physically interact with the product (handling, carrying, storing) to facilitate accelerated wear testing of prototype materials, thereby avoiding costly and lengthy long-term user studies. A significant hurdle for designing for longevity and circularity is the current lack of information regarding how these products wear and sustain damage during actual use. Existing manufacturer testing for electronic devices primarily targets preventing functional failure, not assessing gradual wear or long-term durability. Furthermore, despite some research into phone usage habits and proximity, there are no published methods or standards for accelerated wear testing specifically for this product type. This means current design and testing protocols for consumer products prioritize their initial, pristine state rather than their evolution over time.
To simulate real-world usage, researchers devised new test methods informed by typical phone handling. They categorized potential degradation into two primary interaction types: wear and damage. Wear, representing careful use like carrying a phone in a pocket, gradually polishes the material. To replicate this, existing research on human skin's friction, roughness, and contact pressure (Chen et al. 2009; Derler et al. 2009; Tomlinson et al. 2009; Derler and Gerhardt 2011; Skedung et al. 2011; Wongsriruksa et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2013) guided the selection of Lorica, a material tribologically similar to human skin. This allowed for controlled polishing of test materials. Damage, simulating less careful use such as drops or carrying with keys, was based on manufacturers' durability tests. In this method, a mobile phone is attached to a rotating cylinder alongside keys and coins. The extent of damage is controlled by the number of cylinder revolutions.
To achieve a unique aging process, a layered surface finish was selected. This design aimed for an initial uniform appearance, meeting consumer expectations for new products. Over time, as the phone is used, this layered surface would wear away, revealing underlying colored layers in distinct patterns that reflect the user's interaction. To validate this concept, candidate coatings underwent rigorous testing. This involved accelerated aging methods, coupled with adhesion testing (ASTM D 3359 with a cross-hatch tester). The goal was to ensure the coatings possessed sufficient strength and adherence to resist chipping or cracking from drops or scratches, yet were delicate enough to gradually abrade and expose the layers beneath during normal use.
A user study involving thirty-six individuals aged 18-25 explored reactions to a layered surface coating on mobile phone cases as it aged through use. Participants were given Apple iPhone 4 cases, designed to fit over their personal phones, enabling normal interaction. The study aimed to observe the effects of gradual change and acclimatization over time, contrasting with studies that elicit immediate, visceral responses to material samples. Three groups of twelve participants were formed: a control group received uncoated cases, another group received coated cases without prior knowledge of potential material changes, and a third group was explicitly informed that their coated cases were designed to change. The study was planned for a six-month duration, with interviews and photographs scheduled at two, four, and six months. However, after four months, the study was halted because the layered surface was not performing as expected. Instead of a gradual, intended transformation, the coating was chipping and flaking, and the underlying plastic case was also showing damage. This outcome, illustrated in Figure 4, indicated that the anticipated gradual wear was not occurring, and instead, the cases exhibited chipping and scratching, particularly on the angular edges of the iPhone 4.
The observed damage to the layered phone surface revealed a discrepancy between accelerated aging tests and real-world mobile phone wear. This highlights a significant knowledge gap regarding user interaction with possessions, impeding the creation of materials designed for specific aging or change patterns to enhance product longevity.
After four months, the study was concluded, and participants were presented with visual representations, including images and video, of the layered case's simulated aging process. The reactions were varied, though generally positive. Some participants expressed enthusiasm, stating, for instance, that they would find it "cool to keep using it" and "really cool to use that cover," and that such a design was something they "would maybe buy." However, there were also negative comments, with one participant finding it "too ugly" due to "too many dull colours mixed together." This mixed feedback indicates that the concept of using materials that "age spectacularly" to foster emotional attachment holds potential, provided the layered material surface undergoes further refinement.
For the PSS to be implemented, devices must be easily disassembled for upgrades. This ease of disassembly is also crucial for component recovery and recycling, leading to the development of various design-for-disassembly strategies. While active disassembly using shape memory polymers or alloys is appealing, even products designed for disassembly typically yield functional units rather than individual components.
Circuit boards, a prime example, are currently made from highly durable epoxy resins, glass fiber, and conductive metal layers. When separated from other functional parts, these boards typically undergo existing recycling methods. These processes often involve pyrolysis and the creation of metal slags, followed by refining and electro-winning to retrieve valuable metals, with a particular emphasis on gold (Hagelüken and Corti 2010). The recovery of valuable metals from circuit boards usually relies on destructive techniques. These include pyrolysis, often followed by copper recovery through the "black copper smelting method" (Ghodrat et al. 2016), and hydrometallurgical leaching. While both traditional and more recent technologies have been examined (Zhang and Xu 2016), most still necessitate high-energy approaches to break down the circuit board's structural components. However, less environmentally impactful methods, like comminution and flotation, are becoming more prevalent (Estrada-Ruiz et al. 2016). Enhanced metal recovery and more efficient separation processes could be achieved if circuit boards were designed for triggered degradation. This would allow for the release of individual components and metals, such as gold in contacts and gold and silver in circuits. The concept of triggered disassembly is observed in nature, where robust materials biodegrade easily at the end of their life.
The CLEVER project chose cellulose, a significant renewable biopolymer and plant cell wall component, as the foundation for novel skeleton materials. Although cellulose is largely insoluble, numerous organisms have evolved enzymes that break it down into sugars for energy. While pure cellulose isn't suitable for electronic component supports or circuit boards, cellulose-based composites can be engineered to possess crucial properties like stiffness, fire retardancy, water resistance, and printability.
To create the cellulose-based "skeleton" support materials, a specific manufacturing process was employed. This involved dissolving cellulose using 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, an ionic liquid chosen for its efficiency and low hazard profile, in conjunction with various co-solvents. Following this, the cellulose was regenerated, a method based on the work of Rogers and colleagues. To achieve the desired material properties for these composites, the process incorporated various blended fillers and surface treatments. Given the cost of the ionic liquid, its recycling is a crucial aspect of the manufacturing process. The development of these composites necessitated an iterative approach involving repeated cycles of material production, characterization, and testing.
Through numerous iterative steps, a cellulose composite was developed for the "skeleton" material. This composite incorporated 10% ammonium polyphosphate filler and a thin, moisture-resistant ethylcyanoacrylate surface layer (less than 0.1% by weight).
To facilitate triggered degradation, enzymatic breakdown of the materials was necessary, utilizing readily available and affordable enzyme cocktails developed by the second-generation biofuel industry for cellulose saccharification. This enzymatic approach offers significant recovery and recycling benefits by operating under very mild conditions. It enables the recovery of small, individual components like transistors. Furthermore, applying the concept of triggered degradation to flexible circuitry and other connecting components could substantially decrease the loss of valuable metals during initial preprocessing stages such as shredding and separation.
An environmental analysis was performed to pinpoint the primary negative environmental effects stemming from the creation of a novel skeleton material. This analysis utilized data gathered from laboratory-scale material preparation. Its purpose was to offer actionable insights early in the development phase, rather than serving as a replacement for a comprehensive Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). Instead, it underscored specific processes and materials that warrant careful evaluation, potential modification, or substitution during development. Energy consumption emerged as the most significant environmental impact. However, given that laboratory-scale processes are not optimized for efficiency, this finding may not accurately represent the impacts within a full-scale manufacturing setting. The subsequent largest impact originated from the production of purified cellulose raw material. This suggests that employing less rigorously purified celluloses would be advantageous, a relatively straightforward adjustment since a substantial portion of cellulose production's environmental impact comes from processes aimed at achieving a bright white material, whereas residual color is acceptable for skeleton materials. Furthermore, the composite production process itself was intentionally designed to enable the recovery and recycling of nearly all solvents and processing aids.
The CLEVER project integrated environmental and social considerations throughout its PSS development. A product designer, materials engineer, and chemist collaborated on development, with iterative assessments by LCA and social science experts. This interdisciplinary approach ensured the proposed systems offered genuine benefits.
When products and systems are not fully defined, comprehensive environmental Life Cycle Assessments (ELCAs) can be time-consuming and uncertain. While streamlined scoping studies, often based on ISO 2006 standards, don't aim for precise impact calculations, they are valuable for fostering a deeper understanding of the systems under review. Essentially, these studies serve as indicators, pointing to areas within a product's life cycle that require more detailed examination. To optimize the development of a Product Service System (PSS), a combined approach of value chain analysis (considering both economic and non-economic value) and an ELCA-type study can effectively identify strategies that maximize benefits while minimizing negative impacts.
Assessing the social and economic consequences of modified Product Service Systems (PSSs) presents a significant hurdle. While methodologies for social Life Cycle Assessments (LCAs) have emerged and been applied to existing electronics like laptops, revealing the importance of evaluating labor conditions and wider social benefits in manufacturing, their reliability is debated. This contention stems from difficulties in gathering robust data on social impacts at production sites and identifying the most critical social and economic impact areas. The complexities escalate when attempting to evaluate the potential social and economic effects of hypothetical PSSs. Further research is crucial to ensure the Circular Economy (CE) fosters social sustainability alongside environmental benefits. A pertinent example illustrating this challenge is whether the health improvements for Chinese workers, should informal e-waste recycling centers be eliminated by a new PSS concentrating recycling in the Global North, would justify the economic impact on their livelihoods.
Evaluating a new or proposed Product Service System (PSS) involves considering both the criteria used for assessment and the extent of its implementation. The "scale" of a PSS refers to two key aspects: its market penetration and the geographical boundaries of its "closed loop" operations. For instance, policy documents often advocate for keeping materials and processes localized, such as within the European Union. Furthermore, the efficiency and impact of technical processes like chemical processing, manufacturing, and material recovery are influenced by their scale of implementation. Similarly, the social acceptance and popularity of a technology, style, or brand are tied to its adoption rate. Consequently, the numerous hypothetical scenarios and variables associated with a proposed PSS often hinder a thorough analysis of its social impact.
While the shift from a linear "take-make-waste" economy to a circular economy (CE) is broadly recognized as advantageous for environmental protection and manufacturing stability, its broader societal consequences remain largely unexplored. Policymakers and CE proponents have yet to adequately consider these social implications, which are complex and challenging to quantify, despite their importance for a successful transition.
Transitioning to a Circular Economy (CE) presents a greater challenge than merely evaluating its effects. Decades of industrial design, which largely neglected disassembly, repair, component reuse, and material recovery, must be counteracted by overcoming significant technical hurdles. While current CE strategies are often top-down, driven by designers and manufacturers addressing these technical issues, successful implementation critically depends on understanding and engaging citizen-consumers to foster willing participation and prevent unforeseen negative outcomes.
Our proposed Product Service System (PSS) aims to improve e-waste recovery by fostering consumer engagement with their devices, moving beyond purely financial incentives. We address technological obsolescence through upgrades of functional components. To combat cosmetic or stylistic obsolescence, the PSS cultivates an emotional connection between the owner and the device's exterior. Notably, the external enclosure, despite its minimal monetary value and environmental impact, is designed for maximum durability within this PSS. The objective is to build an emotional bond with the user, rather than solely reducing the production of external enclosures.
Transitioning effectively and beneficially to a Circular Economy necessitates a broad multidisciplinary approach. The project discussed, for instance, required close collaboration among experts in chemistry, material science, engineering, LCA, social science, and product design to achieve a coherent outcome. Such extensive cross-discipline collaboration remains unusual and presents challenges. Building effective communication and productive working relationships demands time, indicating that long-term research grants or industry partnerships are essential for developing innovative circular systems that can deliver consumer products with significantly reduced environmental impacts.
