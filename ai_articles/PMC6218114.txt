This study investigated the role of Krüppel-like factor 13 (KLF13) in prostate cancer (PCa), a member of the KLF family known to influence immunological diseases and tumor development, but whose specific function in PCa was previously unexplored. Our methods involved analyzing KLF13 expression in prostate tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues using immunohistochemistry. To assess the impact of increased KLF13, we conducted CCK8 assays, cell colony formation experiments, flow cytometry, and measured tumor proliferation in nude mice. Western blot analysis was employed to examine signaling pathways. The results revealed a novel finding: KLF13 is downregulated in prostate tumor tissues compared to matched non-tumor tissues. Furthermore, overexpressing KLF13 significantly reduced cell proliferation and triggered apoptosis in human PCa cells by inhibiting the AKT pathway. In living organisms, the introduction of KLF13 effectively postponed the development of PC3 xenografts and suppressed tumor growth. These findings suggest that KLF13 acts as a tumor suppressor in PCa, indicating that its pharmacological activation could be a promising therapeutic strategy.

Prostate cancer (PCa) is a prevalent and deadly malignancy, ranking as the most common carcinoma and second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among American males. Its incidence is also increasing in developing nations like China. While androgen deprivation therapy can manage localized PCa, many patients experience relapse and develop castration-resistant disease. Therefore, understanding PCa's molecular mechanisms and discovering new therapeutic targets is crucial.
KLF13, also identified as RFLAT-1, BTEB3, or FKLF-2, is a crucial component of the KLF protein family, playing vital roles in cellular processes like proliferation, migration, and differentiation. This family comprises 17 members, categorized into three functional groups. KLF13 is part of subgroup 3, alongside KLF9, 10, 11, 14, and 16, a group generally associated with repressive functions through their interaction with the transcriptional co-repressor Sin3A. Beyond its general cellular functions, KLF13 has been shown to influence the human papillomavirus life cycle in cervical keratinocytes and regulate adipocyte differentiation via PPARγ activation. Furthermore, KLF13 demonstrates tumor-suppressive characteristics by inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis in pancreatic cancer cells, and by downregulating MYB and BCL2 expression in acute lymphoblastic leukemia. While other KLF members, such as KLF6, have been implicated in prostate cancer development, with KLF6 mutations suggesting a tumor suppressor role, the specific biological and clinical impact of KLF13 in prostate cancer has not yet been explored.
This study aimed to uncover a new mechanism for KLF13's role in prostate cancer. We observed that KLF13 levels are reduced in PCa tissues. When KLF13 was overexpressed, it significantly suppressed AKT phosphorylation and triggered apoptosis. Remarkably, restoring KLF13 led to substantial tumor regression in living organisms. These findings suggest that increasing KLF13 could be a new treatment approach for this cancer.
The study utilized a human prostate tissue microarray (HProA180PG03), acquired from Shanghai Outdo Biotech, comprising 90 normal, 73 primary prostate cancer, and 17 metastatic prostate cancer tissue samples. Further details on this microarray are accessible online.
DU145 and PC3 human prostate cancer cell lines were acquired from Renji Hospital Clinical Stem Cell Research Center (Shanghai, China). These cells were cultured in DMEM medium (HyClone, Logan, UT) enriched with 10% FBS (HyClone, Logan, UT), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 mg/ml streptomycin, at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.
Gene expression levels were quantified using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) on an ABI7300 System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa). First, RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Master Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Relative expression was determined by the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primers for KLF13 were 5’-GTTTACGGGAAATCTTCGCA-3’ (forward) and 5’-GCGAACTTCTTGTTGCAGTC-3’ (reverse), while GAPDH primers were 5’-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3’ (forward) and 5’-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3’ (reverse).
For protein analysis, cells were initially rinsed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being harvested with lysis buffer. The protein content of these lysates was quantified using a BCA assay. A 50 µg aliquot of protein was then subjected to separation via 12% or 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by transfer to nitrocellulose membranes. These membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA in TBST. Overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting KLF13, Phospho-Akt-Ser473, Akt, Phospho-S6 Ribosomal Protein (235/236), S6 Ribosomal Protein, PARP1, BAX, and GAPDH was performed. Subsequently, IRDye secondary antibodies were applied for 1.5 hours at room temperature. Finally, the resulting immunoreactive bands were visualized using an Odyssey Infrared Imaging System.
Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of tumor tissue biochips began with deparaffinization using xylene, followed by rehydration through an alcohol gradient. To prepare the tissue for antibody binding, it was treated with 1 mM EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) for 30 minutes, and then endogenous peroxidases were blocked. The biochip was incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-KLF13 antibody (1:200, LifeSpan), followed by a 60-minute incubation at room temperature with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Sections were then stained with diaminobenzidine substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 minutes, and mounted in neutral balsam for microscopic examination. Staining was quantified by grading the percentage of stained tumor cells (0 to 4, representing increasing percentages) and the staining intensity (0 to 3, from negative to strong). A total IHC score was derived by multiplying these percentage and intensity scores.
To evaluate cell growth, 1500 cells per well were plated in 96-well plates. After 24 hours, doxycycline (0.25 mg/mL) was introduced. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK8 reagent was added to 100 µL of DMEM medium with 10% FBS in each well, incubated for 2 hours at 37°C, and then absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a TECAN instrument.
For colony formation assays, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1000 cells/well). After 24 hours, doxycycline (0.25 mg/mL) was introduced, with media changes every three days. Colonies were then fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, scanned, and counted.
To analyze cellular apoptosis rates, cells were first collected and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, they were resuspended in binding buffer and stained for 15 minutes at room temperature with 1 μL/mL of Annexin V and 7-AAD, before being analyzed via flow cytometry.
PC3 and DU145 cells were stably transformed by transduction with either pTRIPZ-lenti-KLF13 or pTRIPZ-lenti-Vector, followed by 7 days of puromycin selection. This process, using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent and HEK293T cells, adhered to manufacturer guidelines.
To investigate tumor growth, 5-6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice from SLAC Laboratory, Shanghai, were subcutaneously injected in their lower flanks with either PC3/KLF13 or PC3/CON cells (5.0 × 10^6). Following implantation, both groups received doxycycline (2 mg/mL) in their drinking water. Tumor volume, determined weekly using the formula V = length × (width)^2/2, and tumor onset time were meticulously documented. Kaplan-Meier analysis was employed to assess the tumor-free survival of these mice. After eight weeks, the animals were euthanized, and the resected tumor xenografts underwent imaging, followed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and IHC staining. All experimental protocols received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University.
GraphPad Prism v7.0 software facilitated all statistical analyses. Tumor-free survival was assessed via Kaplan-Meier analysis. Student's t-test compared two groups, while an unpaired two-sided Student's t-test handled other comparisons. Results are presented as the mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

While KLF13's role in various human cancers has been documented, its specific biological functions in prostate carcinoma (PCa) remained unexplored. To address this, we initiated our investigation by examining KLF13 expression in PCa and normal tissues using two databases, TCGA and GSE62827. Both datasets consistently showed a significant downregulation of KLF13 mRNA in PCa tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1A, B). This finding was further corroborated by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of KLF13 in 90 pairs of human prostate tissues, which also demonstrated reduced KLF13 expression in PCa (Fig. 1C, D). For IHC staining controls, we utilized tumor xenografts derived from PC3/CON and PC3/KLF13 cells. Notably, KLF13 staining levels were even lower in patients with metastatic PCa or those with high Gleason scores (Fig. 1E, F). These collective findings strongly suggest that KLF13 likely functions as a tumor suppressor gene in PCa.
Given KLF13's reduced presence in PCa tissues, implying a role in suppressing disease progression, we investigated the impact of its overexpression. We successfully induced KLF13 protein expression using doxycycline (Tet-on). Our experiments, including the CCK8 assay, revealed a significant reduction in cell proliferation by day 7 in KLF13-expressing cells (Tet+) compared to controls (Tet-). Furthermore, the colony formation assay demonstrated that KLF13 substantially diminished the ability of cells to form colonies. These findings collectively indicate that increasing KLF13 levels in PCa cells effectively hinders their proliferative capacity.
To investigate KLF13's role in cell proliferation and apoptosis, we first used flow cytometry, revealing that KLF13 overexpression significantly triggered apoptosis in PC3 and DU145 cells (Fig. 3A and B). This apoptotic effect was corroborated by Western blot analysis, which showed a notable increase in the protein levels of cleaved PARP and BAX (Fig. 3C). Given the known high activation of the AKT pathway in prostate cancer and its contribution to tumor progression, we further examined its involvement. Our Western blot assessment of AKT pathway proteins demonstrated that KLF13 overexpression inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473. Additionally, the level of phosphorylated S6 protein, a direct downstream target of active AKT, was also reduced. These collective findings indicate that KLF13 overexpression promotes apoptosis and simultaneously suppresses AKT activation in PCa cells.
The impact of KLF13 on PCa cell proliferation was further investigated using tumor xenograft models. Overexpression of KLF13 led to a significant reduction in tumor size and inhibited overall tumor growth, as depicted in Fig. 4A and B. Furthermore, KLF13 overexpression extended the time until tumor appearance and enhanced the tumor-free survival rates in mice (Fig. 4C). In vivo, KLF13's suppressive effect on cell proliferation was confirmed by Ki67 IHC staining (Fig. 4D), while the TUNEL assay demonstrated that KLF13 overexpression also induced apoptosis (Fig. 4E). These results collectively indicate that KLF13 effectively hinders tumor growth and tumorigenicity within a living organism.
The role of Krüppel-like factor 13 (KLF13) in prostate cancer (PCa) has been largely unexplored, despite its known involvement in tumor cell differentiation and survival. While some studies suggest KLF13 functions as an oncogene in oral and cervical cancers, others indicate a tumor suppressive role, inhibiting growth and inducing apoptosis in pancreatic cancer and repressing BCL2 in acute lymphoblastic leukemia. This ambiguity highlights the need to clarify its specific function in PCa. Previous research has established several other KLF family members as tumor suppressors in PCa. For instance, KLF4, when overexpressed, reduces cell viability, clonogenicity, invasion, and migration in PC3 cells, compensating for its downregulation in PCa tissues. Similarly, KLF9 contributes to apoptosis in LNcap cells, and KLF5 deletion in PTEN-deficient mouse prostate tumors can enhance angiogenesis by impacting PI3K/AKT signaling. Our current investigation marks the first time KLF13's physiological function and clinical significance in PCa have been examined. We observed a moderate downregulation of KLF13 in PCa tissues, with even lower expression in metastatic and high-grade cases. Significantly, introducing exogenous KLF13 into human PCa cells suppressed proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, and also induced apoptosis. These findings strongly suggest that KLF13 acts as a tumor suppressor gene in PCa.
The AKT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in prostate cancer (PCa) progression, with alterations observed in 42% of primary PCa cases and all metastatic tumors. A key indicator of this pathway's activity, phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473, is associated with a high risk of disease recurrence. Specifically, in LNCaP cells, activated AKT reduces p27 expression, thereby accelerating prostate tumor growth in xenograft models. Other Krüppel-like factors, KLF5 and KLF9, have been shown to inhibit PCa cell proliferation by modulating AKT signaling. Interestingly, KLF13's potential to suppress AKT activation is suggested by its known binding to the CYP1A1 promoter, which in turn suppresses CYP1A expression; CYP1A1 has been linked to AKT upregulation in breast cancer. Our current findings demonstrate that KLF13 overexpression in PC3 and DU145 cells leads to a suppression of the AKT pathway, and this reduced AKT phosphorylation may contribute to increased cell apoptosis. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which KLF13 influences PCa progression.
This study demonstrates KLF13's downregulation in PCa tissues, where it acts as a tumor suppressor. Further research into KLF13's exact role in AKT signaling and PCa pathogenesis could lead to new therapeutic approaches for the disease.
