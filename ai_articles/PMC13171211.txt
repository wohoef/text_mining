To enhance cancer care for Veterans, the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) has launched the Veterans Precision Oncology Data Commons (VPODC). This initiative, a partnership with the Center for Translational Data Science at the University of Chicago and the Open Commons Consortium, aims to accelerate discoveries by providing a data sharing platform. The VPODC makes VA's clinical, genomic, and imaging data accessible to the broader research community. This paper outlines the rationale for data sharing, details the VPODC's structure, and describes its collaborative model. By converting VA data into a national precision oncology research resource, the VPODC promotes innovation, collaboration, and resource sharing, ultimately striving for better cancer outcomes for Veterans.

Cancer is a significant health concern for US Veterans, ranking as the fourth leading cause of death. The Veterans Affairs (VA) Central Cancer Registry (VACCR) reports an annual cancer incidence of roughly 40,000 to 50,000 among Veterans. Despite a notable increase in Veterans utilizing VA benefits and services, rising from 38% to 48% between 2007 and 2016, the quality of cancer care provided by the VA has remained consistently high. A study by Keating (2001-2004) found VA care comparable to fee-based care, excelling in areas like early colon cancer detection and curative surgery rates. Furthermore, a 2015 report highlighted the VA's strengths in early cancer detection and superior adherence to evidence-based practices compared to private healthcare.
To accelerate progress beyond the pace of traditional randomized controlled trials, the VA has adopted Rapid Learning Health System (LHS) principles, leveraging data gathered during routine patient care. This strategic shift, despite the VA's existing success with evidence-based cancer care, is crucial for faster advancements. A key component of the VA's LHS roadmap for oncology (illustrated in Fig. 1) is the creation of a data and knowledge repository, built upon the foundation of its precision oncology program (POP) and research precision oncology program (RePOP). Recognizing data's potential for innovation, Rachel Ramoni, the VA’s Chief Research and Development Officer, has made transforming VA data into a national resource a strategic priority. Consequently, data sharing is fundamental to the oncology LHS roadmap. This paper will elaborate on the rationale for data sharing, introduce the Veterans Precision Oncology Data Commons (VPODC), and detail our collaborative framework.
President Kennedy's famous "moonshot" declaration underscored the power of a difficult, unifying objective. This challenge, accepted and pursued with unwavering determination, served to focus and evaluate the nation's best efforts. The collaborative spirit it fostered was crucial to the Apollo Space Program's ultimate success.
The successful 1969 Apollo 11 lunar landing and the modern American Space Program were spurred by President Kennedy's challenge. This ambitious goal fostered a collaborative organization, which Kennedy cited as a primary reason for his proclamation. The Apollo Space Program's success was fundamentally driven by this collaboration, involving over 400,000 individuals, numerous universities, and more than 20,000 companies working together to achieve the moon landing.
The Cancer Moonshot currently drives oncology in the United States, aiming to enhance cancer detection, treatment, and patient outcomes. This initiative involves tailoring therapies to the distinct molecular characteristics of individual cancer cases, necessitating a deep understanding of numerous clinically actionable genetic variants within the human genome's 3 billion genetic bases. While the FDA estimates individuals possess 3–3.5 million genetic variants, by 2014, fewer than 200 were considered clinically actionable after genetic screening. A significant challenge remains: the clinical importance of most variants is unknown, with approximately half a million being rare or previously unstudied.
Precision oncology faces significant challenges, not only clinically and biologically but also computationally, due to the unique molecular profile of each cancer and the vast number of genetic variants in an individual's genome. Overcoming these hurdles necessitates cross-disciplinary collaboration and data sharing. Innovation in precision oncology, much like the successful moon landing, requires government, academia, and industry to unite in sharing data and resources.
Studying cancer effectively requires a new approach, as traditional observational and interventional studies fall short. Even within a single tumor type, diverse genetic mutations exist, creating a high-dimensional space with rare subtypes, especially since many solid tumor driver mutations have low prevalence (1-2%). This complexity necessitates a paradigm shift, employing multiple simultaneous experiments to maximize learning within the dynamic treatment environment.
The VPODC is being developed to align with the US Cancer Moonshot's vision for a national cancer ecosystem. This ecosystem will leverage a federated electronic health record system to learn from every patient interaction within the Department of Veterans Healthcare system, the largest single-payer system in the US. As the VA's data platform, the VPODC manages data for sharing with partners in the Applied Proteogenomics Organizational Learning and Outcome (APOLLO) Network, a tri-agency Cancer Moonshot collaboration.
The VPODC employs a data commons strategy to foster widespread, rapid learning in oncology and other fields. This approach allows for the harmonization of data formats, robust data quality management, and clear provenance for clinical and genomic data, all while safeguarding patient confidentiality. It also facilitates data sharing across diverse studies, institutions, and healthcare systems. Ultimately, this enables the VA to optimize modern oncology practices, overcome barriers between healthcare centers, enhance learning from real-world clinical scenarios, adapt clinical guidelines, and expand access to relevant clinical trials for VA patients.
The VPODC, currently undergoing beta testing with pilot projects, is a collaborative effort. It unites the Boston VA's RePOP (funded by CSP), the University of Chicago's Center for Translational Data Science, and the Open Commons Consortium, a non-profit organization.
The VPODC is integral to the VA's Office of Research and Development's broader multicloud strategy. This initiative aims to utilize national resources, expertise, and technological platforms to extract insights from every patient interaction. The VA's on-premise computing infrastructure gathers and organizes extensive EHR, imaging, and genomic datasets. These datasets are then made accessible to application pipelines and computing environments used by public and federal agencies, allowing specialized experts to perform analyses. This framework ultimately facilitates data sharing with the wider research community through a data commons approach.
The VPODC leverages the open-source Gen3 data commons platform. This platform has also been instrumental in creating other significant data commons. For instance, it underpins the BloodPAC Data Commons, a public-private collaboration focused on liquid biopsy data for cancer. Additionally, Gen3 supports the Kids First Data Resource, an NIH-funded pediatric research initiative aimed at deciphering the genetic connections between childhood cancer and birth defects, specifically managing its controlled access genomic data.
The Gen3 data commons platform is fundamentally structured around a defined data model. This model dictates how data is organized and managed within the system. Once this data model is established, the Gen3 software automatically generates several key components to facilitate data interaction. These include a portal for data submission, another portal for data exploration and the creation of synthetic cohorts for research, and Jupyter notebook-based workspaces designed for data analysis. Furthermore, an Application Programming Interface (API) is created, enabling the data commons to integrate with external applications and other data platforms. A crucial aspect of the Gen3 data commons is its configurable data access model. Each instance can tailor its security and compliance policies, which are reflected in the applications and services exposed via its APIs. For instance, aggregated data might be openly accessible, whereas individual genomic data would require controlled access. Similarly, highly sensitive data might restrict Jupyter notebook analysis to a select few users, while others could only analyze data through a query gateway that processes pre-approved requests and returns only the results, without direct data access. This API-driven approach, adhering to specific security and compliance policies, allows a Gen3 data commons to function effectively within a broader data ecosystem.
Gen3 data commons leverage cloud computing for the scalability necessary to handle extensive biomedical datasets. These platforms manage both large, unstructured data objects like BAM, CRAM, and image files, assigning them globally unique digital IDs (GUIDs). These GUID-identified objects can be stored across various public or private cloud environments and accessed through an API. Additionally, Gen3 data commons accommodate structured data, such as clinical or biospecimen information, which is maintained within a database. An API, supporting GraphQL-based queries, facilitates access to all data managed by the Gen3 system, underpinning applications like the data submission portal, exploration portal, and analytical workspaces.
As of March 2019, the VPODC, accessible via a Gen3-built data portal (Fig. 3), holds genomic, clinical, and imaging data for 945 cancer patients. This patient count is projected to increase. The platform includes roughly 985 patient variables, though some are sparsely populated. Figure 4 illustrates the core Gen3 software architecture.
The Data Commons Framework Services (DCFS) is an open-source software framework, implemented by Gen3, that underpins several significant data commons, including the VPODC, the BloodPAC Data Commons, and the Kids First Data Resource. It offers a suite of essential services, such as user authentication and authorization for data access, assigning unique digital IDs to datasets, and enabling data retrieval from both public and private cloud environments using these IDs. DCFS also facilitates the definition of data models for various biomedical data types (genomic, clinical, imaging) and supports the import and querying of clinical data. This framework also serves as the foundation for the National Cancer Institute's (NCI) Cancer Research Data Commons, aligning with the Cancer Moonshot's goal of a national cancer ecosystem. Adopting DCFS offers multiple benefits over creating standalone data commons. It promotes interoperability between different data commons, provided their compliance and security policies permit. Furthermore, applications developed for one DCFS-compliant data commons can be readily utilized by others. This approach accelerates the establishment and operation of new data commons, allowing teams to concentrate on data modeling, data import, and developing analytical applications, rather than building fundamental infrastructure.
Developing the VPODC's data model and importing compliant data proved to be a labor-intensive process, especially when contrasted with the "data lake" approach where data are viewed as opaque objects with metadata. However, this investment yields significant benefits for researchers. By employing a common data model and standardized bioinformatics pipelines, or "data harmonization," the complexity of analyzing data within the commons is reduced. This harmonization is especially crucial for genomic data, as varying analysis pipelines and settings can otherwise complicate interpretation.
Ensuring patient privacy and data security is paramount in the VPODC's design. All clinical and genomic data undergo rigorous deidentification, overseen by the VA Privacy Officer. Access is granted only to certified users who adhere to strict policies preventing patient re-identification.
The Boston Institutional Review Board approved the VPODC, enabling it to host data from precision oncology patients and deceased individuals, following a data use agreement with the University of Chicago. A VA Privacy Officer de-identifies and reviews all VPODC data. This agreement was also vetted by an Information Systems Security Officer, the Privacy Officer, and the Boston Chief of Research and Development.
External investigators seeking to use VPODC data must first submit a one-page proposal. A committee reviews this proposal to verify alignment with the VA's mission of improving Veteran healthcare. Approved applicants then proceed by either providing an IRB-approved protocol from their own institution or collaborating with a VA researcher under a CRADA to submit through the VA IRB. The Boston CSP Informatics center possesses an existing IRB protocol suitable for much of the oncology data science research related to risk and prognosis.
The Open Commons Consortium contributed to both the VPODC's security and compliance framework, as well as its overarching data governance best practices. This model was a collaborative effort, also involving the Boston VA's Research Precision Oncology Program (RePOP) and the University of Chicago's Center for Translational Data Science. While not a federal system, the VPODC adheres to the policies and controls outlined in NIST Special Publication 800–53 Revision 4, appropriate for FISMA Moderate information systems.
VPODC users access aggregated data, including through Jupyter notebooks, via data portals and workspaces. For less aggregated or controlled genomic and clinical data, approved researchers utilize query gateways. These gateways execute preapproved queries or analysis pipelines on selected datasets, returning results to the researcher without providing direct data access.
The VPODC integrates various data types, including targeted sequencing (VCFs, FASTQs) and medical images, alongside electronic health record (EHR) data sourced from the VA’s Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW). The CDW is a massive repository, holding over 2 trillion rows of data across more than 22 million unique Veterans, 6 million of whom are deceased and eligible for inclusion in the VPODC. It comprises over 68 domains, 840 tables, and 22,000 columns. Currently, the VPODC incorporates eight key CDW domains: surgery, oncology, outpatient prescriptions, laboratory test results, orders, medications, and both inpatient and outpatient records, with additional domains being integrated based on research needs.
The VA utilizes the Veterans Health Information System and Technology Architecture (VistA) as its Electronic Healthcare Record (EHR) across its extensive network, which includes 150 medical centers, 800 outpatient clinics, and over 135 nursing homes. However, VistA Imaging, the system's imaging component, operates as a federated system, meaning there isn't a single, central repository for images. Instead, each medical center within the network independently manages its locally acquired images. The infrastructure of VistA Imaging relies on a network of PACS servers connected to Enterprise Routers, with every VA medical center having both a PACS server and an Enterprise Router. To gather medical images, a semi-automated process involves pinging other network routers from a local router. Once images are identified, the local PACS administrator initiates their transfer to a designated landing zone, where they undergo de-identification before being stored in the VPODC.
The VPODC's Gen3 data model is under continuous development, with multiple iterations anticipated in the coming 1-2 years. Currently, it integrates genomic, clinical, and imaging data, comprising approximately 985 variables, though some are sparsely populated.
While the VPODC's data model addresses key issues of data quality and harmonization, its implementation introduces certain complexities. A significant challenge lies in the potential for information loss and reduced design flexibility when mapping data to a common model, especially when integrating information from sequencing pipelines and electronic health records. This mapping process can render some data "unmappable," necessitating careful decisions on how to handle such elements during model creation. Furthermore, the inherent data abstraction involved in defining mapping extent and relationships can also contribute to information loss. The implications of these limitations, particularly within EHR systems, warrant further investigation. To mitigate these issues during data commons development, transparency in the data model is crucial, especially concerning decision points. It's also vital to incorporate metrics for assessing data "mappability" and to foster collaboration with contributing institutions and cloud providers to ensure the data model's interoperability and integration. Many common data models, including those in EHR systems, are designed with a "fitness-for-purpose" paradigm, prioritizing data portability, which can inadvertently lead to data distortions. Given the dynamic nature of genomics and precision oncology, the data model's purpose within the commons is likely to change over time. Therefore, when selecting a data model, it's essential to prioritize flexibility and expandability, alongside establishing a clear framework for evaluating its functionality.
To assess the current best methods for predictive modeling in specific clinical areas, the VA can invite private companies and academic institutions with shared interests to participate. These partners, some of whom may possess proprietary algorithms, can then evaluate their methods against real-world VA data via the VPODC and an honest broker. The honest broker facilitates this by running the participants' algorithms on the data and subsequently summarizing the results for all involved. For highly sensitive data, such as Protected Health Information, the honest broker would execute the algorithms on behalf of the participants. This collaborative process ultimately provides the VA and its partners with an evaluation of the most effective methods available.
The VA aims to foster the creation of open-source algorithms for cancer care in Veterans, aligning with its clinical operational guidelines. This initiative mirrors the state-of-the-field collaboration model but shifts focus from evaluating proprietary methods. Crowdsourcing, exemplified by successful Hackathons and the long-running DREAM challenge (since 2007), has proven effective in driving scientific innovation. The DREAM challenge, for instance, has facilitated the development of numerous predictive models for prostate cancer.
The Veterans Precision Oncology Data Commons (VPODC) addresses a significant hurdle in applying modern predictive models and decision support tools to Veteran care. While many such tools exist to enhance personalized patient care, their effective translation into clinical practice necessitates rigorous validation and calibration within the specific target population. This is especially crucial for the VA, as the Veteran population presents unique characteristics, such as service-connected exposures, comorbidities not commonly seen in the general public, and greater racial diversity compared to many academic medical centers. Consequently, predictive models developed in other healthcare systems often don't directly apply to Veterans. Previously, external validation of these models or the development of new ones using VA data faced substantial practical barriers to access. The VPODC now streamlines this process, offering a timely and accessible platform for researchers to adapt or create tools specifically tailored for the Veteran population, thereby increasing their suitability for use by VA healthcare providers.
The VA aims to improve cancer care for Veterans by making VA cancer data a national resource. This objective will be achieved through the creation of a learning healthcare system within the VA and the VPODC, fostering collaborative data and expertise sharing across government, academia, and industry.
The VPODC, currently running pilot projects, enhances internal VA multicloud solutions by integrating extensive genomic and imaging data with detailed EHR information. This platform's strength lies in its ability to combine breadth and depth, fostering continuous improvement, collaboration, and innovation. It aligns science, informatics, technology, and culture to tackle complex research questions.
