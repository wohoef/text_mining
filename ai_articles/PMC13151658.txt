This scientific opinion from the EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials, Enzymes, Flavourings and Processing Aids (CEF) addresses the safety of potassium metabisulfite (FCM substance No. 1056) when used as an active component to prolong the shelf life of wine. The active agent is sulfur dioxide (SO2, E 220), which is produced on-site from potassium metabisulfite (E 224). Other authorized sulfites, commonly used as food preservatives, can also serve as alternative precursors for SO2 generation. The system utilizes a gel matrix made of agar (E 406) to house both the potassium metabisulfite and the resulting sulfur dioxide. This mixture is enclosed within plastic containers sealed by a polyethersulfone (PES) membrane. Upon contact with the water present in the agar gel, the precursor releases sulfur dioxide. The PES membrane functions as a microporous barrier, facilitating the controlled diffusion of SO2 from the active cap into the wine. The Panel anticipates no significant interactions or reactions between the active components and the PES, agar, or plastic containers, and therefore no release of reaction products into the wine is expected. The CEF Panel concluded that potassium metabisulfite (E 224), along with other authorized sulfites (E 221, E 222, E 223, E 226, E 227, and E 228), poses no safety risk to consumers when employed as precursors for SO2 release into wine under the evaluated conditions. Crucially, the SO2 release from these active caps should not result in exceeding the maximum permitted levels (MPLs) for total SO2 in wine, as stipulated by Regulations (EC) No 606/2009 and (EC) No 203/2012. Furthermore, the opinion emphasizes the importance of considering the conclusions and recommendations from the EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources Added to Food (ANS) regarding the re-evaluation of sulfur dioxide and sulfites.

Regulation (EC) No 450/2009 provides specific rules for active and intelligent food contact materials, complementing the general requirements of Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004. Active materials are designed to deliberately include components that intentionally release or absorb substances to extend shelf life or improve the condition of packaged food. This interaction can occur with the food itself or its surrounding environment.
To be included on a positive list, the substances providing active or intelligent functions must undergo a safety evaluation by EFSA. This evaluation is conducted according to the procedures outlined in the aforementioned regulations, after which the Commission can add them to the list.
Under this procedure, industry applications are first submitted to Member State competent authorities. These authorities then forward the applications to EFSA for evaluation. The industry supports each application with a technical dossier, prepared according to EFSA's guidelines for the safety evaluation of active or intelligent substances in food contact materials.
EFSA received an application from the Direction Génerale de la Concurrence, de la Consommation et de la Repression des Fraudes to evaluate Biocork® sulfites-active caps.
Under Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004, EFSA is tasked with assessing the risks associated with the intended use of substances in food contact materials. Following this assessment, EFSA will provide a scientific opinion.
A dossier was submitted requesting authorization for potassium metabisulfite in active food contact materials. The evaluation utilized non-toxicological data and information provided by the applicant.
The dossier included chemical identity, manufacturing, properties, intended use, existing authorizations, migration data, and toxicological information.
Limited toxicity data for sulfur dioxide as a food additive was provided.
EFSA's safety evaluation of active or intelligent substances in food contact materials follows the general framework for monomers and additives in plastics. This methodology is outlined in the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) guidelines (European Commission, 2001) and is applied to active and intelligent substances as detailed in EFSA CEF Panel guidance (2009).
The safety assessment followed the principles of Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 concerning food contact materials. This regulation advises applicants to refer to the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) Guidelines for presenting safety assessment applications for substances intended for FCMs before authorization. These guidelines, published in 2001, detail the necessary data. The applicant's submitted dossier adhered to these SCF guidelines.
The safety assessment methodology involves characterizing the substance under review, including its impurities and any reaction or degradation products. It also entails evaluating potential exposure through migration and determining the necessary toxicity data for assessment. For active substances designed to be released into food, or to trigger the release of other substances, specific safety considerations and their status as direct food additives are crucial. These detailed considerations are outlined in the EFSA guidelines for active or intelligent substances.
Active or intelligent substances, beyond their intended release, can also lead to unintentional migration of other substances. Assessing the safety of ingesting these unintentionally released compounds requires combining toxicological data on potential hazards with likely human exposure data. Exposure is calculated based on migration studies into food or food simulants, assuming a daily consumption of up to 1 kg of food in contact with the relevant food contact material.
Increased migration necessitates more toxicological data for safety assessments. Three tiers, based on migration thresholds, dictate the level of toxicological information required.
Extensive toxicological data is required for high migration levels (5–60 mg/kg food). A reduced dataset may be sufficient for migration between 0.05 and 5 mg/kg food. For low migration (< 0.05 mg/kg food), only a limited dataset is necessary.
SCF guidelines (European Commission, 2001) detail required data.
The risk assessment followed EFSA's transparency guidance (2009) and incorporated existing EFSA Scientific Committee guidelines, ensuring a robust scientific evaluation.
The applicant states that sulfites-active caps are designed as active capsules primarily for extending the shelf life of wine. These caps are intended for use on various food containers, including boxes, jars, pouches, bags, and barrels. They are small devices that attach to container stoppers and are engineered to release sulfur dioxide gradually through a specialized membrane into the foodstuff. This controlled diffusion mechanism helps maintain an optimal, low level of free SO2, thereby protecting the wine from oxidation and spoilage during storage. The gradual release effectively compensates for the natural decline in SO2 levels that occurs over time due to reactions within the wine. The caps are available in various designs and sizes to ensure controlled release and appropriate dosing for different types and volumes of wine containers.
Sulfur dioxide (SO2, E 220) serves as the active substance, generated on-site from potassium metabisulfite (K2S2O5, E 224) for this application. Other authorized sulfite food preservatives can also act as precursors for SO2. Agar (E 406) in gel form supports the potassium metabisulfite and the resulting sulfur dioxide. This composition is housed in plastic containers of varying sizes and shapes, sealed with a polyethersulfone (PES) synthetic membrane.
In active cap manufacturing, E 224 (potassium metabisulfite) powder reacts with water in the agar gel. This interaction generates sulfur dioxide, the active substance released: K2S2O5(s)→K2O(s)+2SO2(g)↑.
Within the gel's aqueous environment, molecular sulfur dioxide (SO2) exists in equilibrium with bisulfite (HSO3-) and sulfite (SO32-) anions, collectively termed 'free SO2'. The containers and their lids are constructed from polyethylene (PE) or polypropylene (PP).
The cap is sealed with a PES membrane. This microporous material allows water and gas to pass through, facilitating SO2 diffusion from the active cap into the wine. Sealing is achieved through physical methods like radio frequency, ultrasonic, or heat welding, without the use of adhesives.
Agar (E 406), an unbranched polysaccharide derived from red seaweed cell walls, functions as a gel support. Its network structure confines sulfur dioxide (SO2) and regulates its diffusion rate into wine.
The applicant suggests that these sulfites-active caps are primarily intended for use with white wines, rosés, and sweet wines. Strict regulations govern the maximum levels of total sulfur dioxide (SO2), encompassing both free and combined forms, in wine, as outlined in Regulations (EC) No 606/2009 and (EC) No 203/2012. The submitted data indicated that the release characteristics of the caps can be adjusted to ensure compliance with these regulations. Furthermore, the applicant stated that the use of these active caps allows winemakers to significantly decrease the amount of sulfites added to wine before bottling and to select an appropriate diffusion profile suited to the specific wine and market.
The Panel reviewed data showing the polyethersulfone (PES) membranes are inert to various substances, including acids, bases, and organic solvents. Therefore, interactions between the PES and the active ingredients, leading to reaction products entering the wine, are not anticipated. Likewise, the Panel found no expectation of undesirable interactions between the active ingredients and the polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP) containers, or the agar gel.
While the applicant's toxicity data for sulfur dioxide was limited, the Panel did not require more. This decision was based on sulfur dioxide and potassium metabisulfite already being authorized food additives. Furthermore, these substances, along with other sulfites (E 220–228), have undergone recent re-evaluation.
In 2016, the EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS) re-evaluated sulfur dioxide (E 220) and its related sulfites (E 221-228) as food additives. These substances are permitted in the EU across 40 food categories, with maximum permitted levels (MPLs) varying between 20 and 2,000 mg/kg, as detailed in Annex II of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008. Their use in wines and liquors is specifically governed by Annex IB of Regulation (EC) No 606/2009.
The ANS Panel observed that sulfur dioxide, bisulfite, and sulfite ions exist in equilibrium in solution, allowing for a read-across assessment between various sulfites used as food additives.
The EFSA ANS Panel concluded that the existing group ADI of 0.7 mg SO2 equivalent/kg bw per day is adequate but temporary, pending toxicity database improvements. These recommended studies are estimated to take five years to complete.
The EFSA ANS Panel assessed chronic exposure to sulfur dioxide and sulfites (E 220–228) across various age groups, including infants, toddlers, children, adolescents, adults, and the elderly. When employing a regulatory maximum permitted level (MPL) exposure assessment scenario, wine and meat preparations emerged as the primary sources of exposure for adults and the elderly. Even with more refined exposure assessment methods, wine remained the principal contributor to sulfur dioxide–sulfite exposure in these adult and elderly populations. The panel observed that exposure estimates under the MPL scenario consistently exceeded the current group acceptable daily intake (ADI) for all age groups. Furthermore, using more refined scenarios, exposure estimates frequently approached or surpassed the group ADI.
The applicant provided data for the sulfites-active caps, demonstrating their release properties can be adjusted to comply with maximum permitted level (MPL) regulations for wine. As no information was supplied for other food groups where sulfur dioxide-sulfites are permitted, this opinion is confined to the use of these active caps in wine. The Panel observed that employing this active packaging would replace the direct addition of SO2-sulfites to wine before bottling, rather than introducing an additional exposure source. Consequently, the Panel concluded that consumers' SO2 exposure from wine utilizing this active packaging is unlikely to exceed, and may even be lower than, that from wine conventionally dosed with SO2-sulfites.
The applicant states that sulfur dioxide is produced on-site from potassium metabisulfite (E 224) for this application. However, other authorized sulfite preservatives can also serve as precursors. Because the chemically inert polyethersulfone (PES) membrane isolates the wine from the sulfur dioxide source, the Panel deems any authorized sulfite (E 221, E 222, E 223, E 224, E 226, E 227, and E 228) to be a suitable precursor, provided it adheres to the specifications outlined in Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012.
Based on the information reviewed, the CEF Panel concludes that potassium metabisulfite (E 224) and other authorized sulfites (E 221-223, E 226-228) pose no safety concern. When used as precursors to release sulfur dioxide into wine under the specified conditions, consumers will be protected.
The release of sulfur dioxide must not exceed the maximum permitted levels for total SO2 in wine, as stipulated by Regulations (EC) No 606/2009 and (EC) No 203/2012. Additionally, the EFSA ANS Panel's (2016) conclusions and recommendations should be taken into account.
BIO-CORK Sarl, France, submitted the dossier for Biocork® sulphites-active caps in January 2015, with additional data provided in August and October of the same year.
The Panel on Food Contact Materials, Enzymes, Flavourings and Processing Aids (CEF) and the Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources Added to Food (ANS) are European bodies. They evaluate substances for food contact materials (FCMs) and food additives, considering factors like acceptable daily intake (ADI) and maximum permitted levels (MPLs).
