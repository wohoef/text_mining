Syngenta Crop Protection AG submitted an application to France, the evaluating Member State, under Article 6 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. This application sought to establish import tolerances for benzovindiflupyr across various crops and specific animal products. EFSA determined that sufficient data existed to propose Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) for most imported crops, with the exception of inedible-peel cucurbits. For coffee beans and the liver of ruminants and horses, EFSA outlined several risk management options. The agency confirmed the availability of suitable analytical methods to monitor benzovindiflupyr residues in both plant and animal products. Ultimately, EFSA concluded that the proposed uses of benzovindiflupyr for these products would not lead to consumer exposure exceeding toxicological limits, thus posing no significant health risk.

Syngenta Crop Protection AG applied to France, the evaluating Member State, to establish import tolerances for benzovindiflupyr. This application, made under Article 6 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, covered various crops from Canada, USA, Mexico, Paraguay, and Brazil.
France initiated a proposal to establish maximum residue levels (MRLs) for a range of imported agricultural products. These included various fruits like pome fruits and wine grapes, several root and tuber vegetables, solanaceae, cucurbits, pulses, sweet corn, oilseeds, cereals, coffee beans, and ginger and turmeric roots. Additionally, MRLs were proposed for animal products such as muscle, fat, liver, and kidney from ruminants. The application also sought MRL changes for imported soybean and sugarcane, but these were not evaluated because their proposed levels matched existing EU MRLs. France prepared an evaluation report, as mandated by Article 8 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, which was then sent to the European Commission and subsequently to EFSA on February 26, 2016.
EFSA's assessment relies on several key documents. These include the updated evaluation report from the EMS, the draft assessment report (DAR) and its revision, the Commission's review report on benzovindiflupyr, and EFSA's own conclusion from the peer review of the pesticide's risk assessment.
Under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, benzovindiflupyr's toxicological profile was evaluated. Sufficient data established an acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0.05 mg/kg body weight/day and an acute reference dose (ARfD) of 0.1 mg/kg body weight.
Studies on primary crops, specifically fruit, cereal, and pulse/oilseed groups, examined benzovindiflupyr's metabolism after foliar application. This led to establishing benzovindiflupyr as the residue definition for enforcement and risk assessment across all relevant crops.
EFSA determined that the submitted residue trials generally provide enough data to propose Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) for the imported crops, with the exception of cucurbits that have inedible peels. For coffee beans, the data suggests that the MRL currently established in the country of origin might be surpassed. Consequently, EFSA presented three potential risk management strategies for coffee bean MRLs: maintaining the current MRL, aligning the import tolerance with the country of origin's MRL, or setting it based on the OECD calculator. While effective analytical methods exist for monitoring benzovindiflupyr residues in most plant products at a 0.01 mg/kg LOQ, their suitability for dried ginseng and turmeric roots still requires verification.
The residue definition for processed goods aligns with that of raw commodities. Robust processing factors were established for several apple and grape products, including juice, sauce, pomace, must, wine, and raisins, based on provided studies.
Since benzovindiflupyr is used on imported crops, residue studies on rotational crops are unnecessary.
For animal-derived foods, the peer review established a residue definition for enforcement as benzovindiflupyr. For risk assessment, the definition includes the total of benzovindiflupyr and its monohydroxylated form (SYN546039), both free and conjugated, expressed as benzovindiflupyr, and applies to all animal products. No changes to existing Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) are necessary when considering benzovindiflupyr's use in European feed products and residues in imported feed and by-products. However, the applicant sought import tolerances for animal products from the USA and Canada. These tolerances should reflect livestock exposure from benzovindiflupyr residues in feed from those countries. Due to a lack of complete information on authorized uses and feed residues in the USA and Canada, EFSA cannot confirm the suitability of their established MRLs. Therefore, a risk management decision is needed regarding whether to increase the current MRL for liver in bovine, sheep, goats, and horses to the US level of 0.06 mg/kg. Reliable analytical methods exist to detect benzovindiflupyr residues in animal tissues, with a validated limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.01 mg/kg.
EFSA conducted a consumer risk assessment using PRIMo (revision 2), incorporating current benzovindiflupyr applications, its use on imported crops, and MRLs set at the LOQ. For ruminant and horse liver, two exposure scenarios were modeled: one based on existing EU MRLs, and another assuming MRLs matched those in the countries of origin. Neither scenario indicated consumer intake risks. The maximum chronic intake reached 2.8% of the ADI (Dutch and German child diets), while the highest acute exposure was 39% of the ARfD for pepper.
EFSA determined that benzovindiflupyr's approved applications for these products won't lead to consumer exposure above toxicological limits. Thus, it's improbable that these uses pose a health risk to consumers.

The European Union's Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 outlines the framework for establishing maximum residue levels (MRLs) for pesticides. Under Article 6 of this Regulation, commercial entities like manufacturers or importers are permitted to apply for the establishment of import tolerances, as detailed in Article 7.
Syngenta Crop Protection AG applied to France, the evaluating Member State (EMS), for import tolerances for benzovindiflupyr in products from Canada, USA, Mexico, Paraguay, and Brazil. France then assessed this application, submitted under Article 8 of the MRL Regulation, and notified both the European Commission and EFSA.
On February 26, 2016, the evaluating Member State (EMS) sent its updated evaluation report to the European Commission and EFSA. This application, concerning the establishment of new MRLs for benzovindiflupyr in various commodities, was logged in the EFSA Register of Questions under reference EFSA-Q-2016-00164.
France suggested specific MRLs for benzovindiflupyr. They proposed 0.01 mg/kg for sweet corn and peanuts, and 0.02 mg/kg for potatoes, maize, tropical root and tuber vegetables, and Jerusalem artichokes. Soybeans and sugar cane were set at 0.04 mg/kg. Higher MRLs were proposed for various fruits, vegetables, and cereals, ranging from 0.08 mg/kg for edible peel cucurbits to 1.5 mg/kg for wine grapes, barley, and oats.
France suggested adjusting MRLs for specific animal products. This included 0.02 mg/kg for ruminant muscle and kidney, 0.03 mg/kg for ruminant fat, 0.07 mg/kg for ruminant liver, and 0.01 mg/kg for pig liver.
EFSA assessed the application and evaluation report, as mandated by Article 10 of the Regulation.
EFSA must assess consumer risks from the application, as per Article 10 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. This assessment is based on the evaluating Member State's report.
The reasoned opinion is supported by the publicly available evaluation report from France (2015b) and exposure calculations performed using the EFSA Pesticide Residues Intake Model (PRIMo).
The reasoned opinion must be delivered promptly, within three months of receiving the application, as per Article 11 of the Regulation. This deadline can be extended to six months for more extensive evaluations. Should EFSA require additional information, the timeframe will be paused until the requested data is supplied.

Notably, the MRLs of 0.04 mg/kg for imported soybeans and sugar canes are already set in the EU MRL Regulation. The Codex Alimentarius has set maximum residue limits (CXLs) for benzovindiflupyr. These are 0.05 mg/kg for soybeans and the limit of quantification (LOQ) for animal products such as meat, fat, offal, milk, and eggs. EFSA evaluated these CXLs.
Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) for the crops were established in the United States (US), Canada (CA), Mexico (MX), and Brazil (BR).

EFSA's evaluation was informed by several key documents. These included the updated report from the evaluating Member State (France, 2015b), along with the draft assessment report (DAR) and its revisions, prepared under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (France, 2014, 2015a). Additionally, the Commission's review report on benzovindiflupyr (European Commission, 2015b) and EFSA's peer review conclusion on the pesticide's risk assessment (EFSA, 2015a) were consulted. This assessment adhered to the legal framework of the Uniform Principles for the Evaluation and Authorisation of Plant Protection Products (Regulation (EU) No 546/2011) and relevant guidance for consumer risk assessment of pesticide residues (European Commission, various years; OECD, 2011).
Validated analytical methods, including HPLC-MS/MS and QuEChERS, were deemed suitable for monitoring benzovindiflupyr residues in various matrices. These methods, with a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.01 mg/kg, were confirmed for high-water, high-acid, high-oil, dry/starch commodities, and coffee beans during the Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 peer review. However, their effectiveness for dried ginger and turmeric roots still requires demonstration.
Analytical methods, specifically HPLC–MS/MS and QuEChERS, were deemed valid for detecting benzovindiflupyr residues in animal products like meat, fat, liver, kidney, milk, and eggs. These methods can quantify residues down to 0.01 mg/kg, as confirmed by a peer review under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009.
Adequate analytical methods exist to enforce proposed benzovindiflupyr MRLs in animal products, as validated by EFSA.

During the peer review, the metabolite SYN546039, part of the residue definition for animal product risk assessment, underwent toxicological studies. Its toxicity was deemed adequately addressed by the toxicological endpoints established for the active substance.
Benzovindiflupyr's metabolism in primary crops was assessed during the peer review for Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. This evaluation covered fruit, cereal, and pulse/oilseed crop groups, with details of these metabolism studies provided in Table 2.
Following foliar applications, parent benzovindiflupyr was suggested as the universal residue definition for both monitoring and risk assessment, according to metabolism studies. This definition aligns with the enforcement residue definition established during the peer review and currently specified in Regulation (EC) No 396/2005.
The peer review noted that existing studies didn't examine how plant metabolism affects benzovindiflupyr's isomer ratio, and further research is ideally needed. EFSA affirms the peer-reviewed residue definition for enforcement and risk assessment applies to the crops in question.
Residue trials from the USA, Canada, and Brazil (specifically for coffee) were provided to support the MRL application. All trials incorporated an adjuvant in the spray mixture. Although some supervised field trials for crops like potatoes and tomatoes were conducted in a single season, rather than the usual minimum of two, EFSA deemed them adequately representative. This was due to their execution across diverse geographical regions with varying climatic conditions, eliminating the need for trials from additional years. Side-by-side bridging trials demonstrated that both emulsifiable concentrate (EC) and water-dispersible granule (WG) formulations resulted in similar residue levels. For MRL calculations, EFSA utilized the highest residue value from these bridging studies. Samples from all trials were analyzed for benzovindiflupyr and its metabolite SYN546039. The metabolite's levels were generally at or near the limit of quantification (LOQ), with the exception of barley grains, where levels ranged from below 0.01 to 0.35 mg/kg.
The EU's food classification (Regulation (EU) No 752/2014) varies from those used in Canada and the US. To establish MRL proposals, EFSA aligned crops with EU classifications, then applied EU guidelines for trial numbers and extrapolations (SANCO/7525/VI/95, European Commission, 2015a).
Eighteen apple and twelve pear residue trials, adhering to Good Agricultural Practices, were provided. For MRL calculation, the highest residue value was chosen from two non-independent apple and pear trials that shared application details.
For pome fruits, a Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) of 0.2 mg/kg is proposed, aligning with levels in originating countries. This MRL was derived by pooling data from apple and pear trials, as their residue levels were statistically comparable and individual MRL values fell within the same MRL category, allowing for extrapolation to the entire pome fruit group.
Seventeen residue trials, compliant with Good Agricultural Practices, were provided. Using the OECD MRL calculator, a Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) of 1.5 mg/kg was calculated, exceeding the 1 mg/kg MRL in the countries of origin (Canada/USA). The previous EU methodology yielded a 0.9 mg/kg MRL. Adhering to the EU policy of not exceeding the country of origin's MRL for import tolerances, EFSA suggests an MRL of 1 mg/kg.
Fifteen trials were conducted for benzovindiflupyr. Twelve trials involved an in-furrow soil application followed by four foliar sprays, adhering to Good Agricultural Practices (GAP). The interval between soil and first spray application varied from 55 to 95 days. Five of these trials, including one examining residues after 14 days, showed residue levels slightly exceeding the Limit of Quantification (LOQ). The remaining three trials separately applied single in-furrow and four foliar treatments in adjacent plots. One sample from these foliar applications also had residues above the LOQ.
Despite the combined experimental design, the potato trials are deemed acceptable for foliar applications. The earlier soil treatment at planting is not anticipated to notably influence residue levels from subsequent foliar applications. Consequently, a Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) of 0.02 mg/kg is proposed for potatoes, aligning with the MRL in their countries of origin.
In the US and Canada, a maximum residue level (MRL) of 0.02 mg/kg is established for the 'tuberous and corm vegetables, subgroup 1C,' which includes cassava, sweet potatoes, yams, arrowroots, ginger, turmeric, and Jerusalem artichokes. However, the EU classifies these crops differently. Tropical root and tuber vegetables (code 212000) encompass most, while Jerusalem artichokes have a distinct classification (code 213050) within other root and tuber vegetables. Ginger and turmeric roots are addressed separately.
The MRL proposal of 0.02 mg/kg for tropical root and tuber vegetables and Jerusalem artichokes is supported by data from potato trials. This extrapolation is consistent with EU guidance. The proposed MRL aligns with the established values in the countries of origin for these vegetable groups.
In the US food classification system, ginger and turmeric roots are categorized under 'tuberous and corm vegetables, subgroup 1C'. For these, the US and Canada have established a Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) or tolerance of 0.02 mg/kg, derived by extrapolating from potato residue data. Conversely, within the EU classification, ginger and turmeric are listed as 'spices, root and rhizome' (codes 0840020 and 0840030), meaning their MRLs apply to the dried form. While European regulations permit extrapolation from potato residues, it necessitates applying a suitable concentration factor for drying. Consequently, EFSA recalculated the MRL for dried ginger and turmeric roots to 0.15 mg/kg, utilizing a dehydration factor of 7.5 based on residue trials in fresh potatoes.
A MRL of 0.7 mg/kg for tomatoes was determined from 12 residue trials. This MRL can be extended to aubergines, according to EU guidelines (European Commission, 2015a).
For tomatoes and aubergines, the OECD calculator suggests a sufficient MRL of 0.7 mg/kg. This is lower than the 1.5 mg/kg MRL established in the countries of origin for these crops.
A maximum residue level (MRL) of 1 mg/kg for peppers was established from nine compliant trials. This MRL can be extended to okra, as per EU guidelines.
For peppers and okra, the OECD calculator indicates a sufficient MRL of 1 mg/kg. This is lower than the 1.5 mg/kg MRL established in the countries of origin for these crops.
Eleven residue trials, compliant with Good Agricultural Practices (GAP), were conducted, comprising six on cucumbers and five on courgettes. Following EU guidelines, these trials were combined to propose a Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) of 0.08 mg/kg for cucurbits with edible peel.
For cucurbits with edible peel, an MRL of 0.08 mg/kg is deemed adequate, according to the OECD calculator. This contrasts with the 0.3 mg/kg MRL established in the countries of origin for the entire cucurbit group.
For melons, six residue trials were submitted, but these were deemed insufficient to establish an import tolerance. Melons are considered a major global crop, necessitating more extensive data. EFSA concurred with the evaluating Member State that at least two more melon trials are needed to propose an MRL for melons and to extend these findings to all cucurbits with inedible peel.
A Maximum Residue Level (MRL) of 0.01 mg/kg (LOQ) is suggested, aligning with the tolerance established in the country of origin, based on 12 compliant residue trials.
The submitted data included eight pea trials and twelve bean trials, all adhering to Good Agricultural Practices (GAP). While residue levels in peas and beans were statistically similar, their individual MRL proposals varied significantly, preventing data pooling for the entire dry pulse group, despite EU guidance allowing such extrapolation.
For dry peas, an MRL of 0.08 mg/kg is proposed, calculated from residue data using the OECD method.
For beans, the OECD calculator suggested an MRL of 0.3 mg/kg. However, this exceeds the 0.2 mg/kg MRL set in the originating countries (Canada/US). The European Union's previous MRL calculation method (Rmax) also yielded a 0.2 mg/kg proposal. Adhering to the EU's policy against setting import tolerances higher than those in the country of origin, EFSA recommends a 0.2 mg/kg MRL for pulses (excluding peas). A lower MRL of 0.08 mg/kg is proposed specifically for peas.
Fifteen residue trials involving three foliar applications at elevated rates (95–104 g/ha) were conducted, showing residues below the LOQ at the pre-harvest interval. While no trials replicated the critical GAP's combined soil and foliar treatments, EFSA concurred with the EMS that these were unnecessary. The initial soil application is unlikely to influence the absence of residues observed after subsequent foliar treatments.
Overdosed trials, despite residues below the LOQ, support a 0.01 mg/kg MRL for peanuts, matching the country of origin's established tolerance.
Thirteen residue trials, compliant with Good Agricultural Practices (GAP), were provided for oilseed rapes. Due to two pairs of trials sharing the same location, application dates, and rates, only the highest value from each duplicate pair was used for MRL calculation. Following EU extrapolation guidelines, these oilseed rape trial results can be extended to the entire oilseed group, including those for which the applicant sought import tolerance.
A Maximum Residue Level (MRL) of 0.15 mg/kg is proposed for rapeseeds, linseeds, poppy seeds, mustard seeds, and gold of pleasure seeds, aligning with values in their countries of origin.
For cotton, 16 residue trials compliant with Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) yielded a Maximum Residue Level (MRL) of 0.15 mg/kg, matching the US tolerance.
For maize, 24 residue trials compliant with Good Agricultural Practices led to a proposed MRL of 0.02 mg/kg, matching the country of origin's tolerance.
For wheat, 36 residue trials adhering to Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) were provided. Grain samples were taken at typical maturity or 28 to 54 days post-application. This data supports a 0.1 mg/kg MRL for wheat, which aligns with origin country values and can be extended to rye.
Twenty residue trials for barley, adhering to Good Agricultural Practices, were provided. Grain samples were gathered at typical maturity or 22 to 41 days post-application. This data supports a 1.5 mg/kg MRL for barley, which aligns with the value in the countries of origin and can be extended to oats.
Six residue trials were submitted, all designed as decline studies, using a WG formulation applied three times at 50 g/ha. While the final treatment occurred at BBCH growth stages 78-83, a slight deviation from the authorized BBCH 72, the trials were deemed acceptable. This is because the application rate fell within a 25% margin, and the growth stage difference was anticipated to result in only marginally elevated residue levels compared to the Good Agricultural Practice (GAP). At the pre-harvest interval (PHI), 21 days post-application, residues were below the limit of quantification (LOQ) in most cases, with only three trials showing detectable residues of 0.02 and 0.07 mg/kg.
To supplement the data, two additional trials with higher application rates (150 g/ha) and three applications, showing residues below the LOQ, were suggested.
EFSA's proposed MRL of 0.1 mg/kg, derived using the OECD calculator, aligns with France's proposal. However, this level exceeds the 0.03 mg/kg MRL established in the country of origin. The existing data suggest the origin country's MRL might be inadequate for the specified Good Agricultural Practice. Therefore, additional risk management decisions are necessary to determine the optimal MRL.

Benzovindiflupyr demonstrated stability for a minimum of 24 months at -18°C across various commodities, including those with high water, acid, or oil content, as well as dry goods. Notably, it remained stable in coffee beans for at least 4 months at the same temperature.
The residue data are considered valid concerning storage stability. This is because trial samples, including coffee beans (stored for 3 months), were kept for under 24 months in conditions proven to maintain their integrity.
France (the EMS) confirmed that the analytical methods employed for residue trial samples were adequately validated and suitable for their intended purpose.
EFSA has determined that the residue trials provided are adequate for establishing MRL proposals for most imported crops, with the exception of cucurbits with inedible peel. For coffee beans, the data indicates that the MRL currently established in the country of origin is inadequate. Consequently, a risk management decision is necessary regarding imported coffee beans. One option is to deny the MRL request entirely (option 1), due to the perceived inappropriateness of the origin country's MRL. Should an import tolerance be granted, risk managers have two further choices: either align the MRL with that of the country of origin (option 2: 0.03 mg/kg) or set it according to the OECD calculator's result (option 3: 0.1 mg/kg). The consumer risk assessment, detailed in Section 4, remains unaffected by the selection between options 2 and 3.
During peer review, studies on benzovindiflupyr residues under standard hydrolysis conditions confirmed its hydrolytic stability during pasteurization, boiling/cooking, and sterilization. Consequently, the residue definition for processed commodities remains consistent with that for raw agricultural commodities.
During the peer review, the levels of benzovindiflupyr residues in processed products like cereals, soybeans, and sugarcane were examined, leading to the derivation of several processing factors.
This MRL application included further processing studies on various crops like apples, grape vines, potatoes, tomatoes, cotton, rapeseeds, peanuts, maize, and coffee. All samples were analyzed for benzovindiflupyr and its metabolite SYN546039. Studies where residues in the raw agricultural commodity were at or near the limit of quantification were excluded from processing factor calculations.
Benzovindiflupyr residues decreased with washing, peeling, juicing, canning, and jam production. However, residues concentrated in oils, pomace, and dehydrated products.
Sufficient processing studies allowed for the derivation of reliable processing factors (PFs) for several products. These include apple juice, sauce, wet and dry apple pomace, canned apple fruits, red and white grape must, wines, grape juice, and raisins. For other items, such as tomato products (juice, paste, puree, canned, and sun-dried tomatoes), cotton, peanut and rapeseed meal and oil, cotton hulls, and peanut butter, only indicative PFs were established. The PF for coffee requires further validation through storage stability data spanning the entire sample storage period (1.5 months) before analysis. Furthermore, PFs for potato and maize processed products, based on a single trial, are not robust enough for definitive recommendations.

To determine the dietary burden for livestock, EFSA employed the animal feedstuff table from OECD guidance No. 64 (Series on Pesticides No. 32, 2009) and its own animal model calculator. Expected residue concentrations in feed items were sourced from the peer review (for soybeans, barley, oats, rye, and wheat straw) and the current MRL application (Table 3). For processed products lacking processing factors (PFs), default PFs were utilized to estimate residue levels. The input data for these dietary burden calculations are presented in Table 6.
The new dietary burden calculations, summarized in Table 7, show that the maximum dietary burden (MDB) for ruminants is comparable to that found in the peer review. However, the trigger value of 0.1 mg/kg dry matter (DM) is now surpassed for pigs and poultry as well. For ruminants, cereal straw from existing European uses is the primary source of residue exposure. Conversely, for poultry and pigs, the main contributors to exposure are residues in imported barley and potato by-products.
During the peer review, benzovindiflupyr's metabolism in livestock, specifically goats and hens, was investigated. These studies led EFSA to define the residue for monitoring as parent benzovindiflupyr. For risk assessment in all animal products, the residue definition includes the sum of benzovindiflupyr and its monohydroxylated form (SYN546039), both free and conjugated, expressed as benzovindiflupyr. A conversion factor of 2 was suggested for risk assessment.
A cattle feeding study, reviewed by EFSA, evaluated benzovindiflupyr residue levels. Doses of 0.08, 0.43, and 0.80 mg/kg body weight per day were administered. Residue definitions for both enforcement and risk assessment were used for reporting results. Due to the low molecular weight conversion factor of 1.04, SYN546039 residues (free and conjugated) were summed directly, without converting them to benzovindiflupyr equivalents.
At the lowest feeding level of 0.08 mg/kg body weight, residues in all matrices were at or below the 0.01 mg/kg limit of quantification. This dose represents 1.5–14N compared to maximum estimated intakes for ruminants and pigs. Additionally, muscle and kidney residue concentrations remained at or below the LOQ even at the second feeding level of 0.43 mg/kg body weight.
In the cattle feeding study, samples were analyzed for benzovindiflupyr and its free and conjugated metabolite, as defined for risk assessment. Conversion factors (CFs) for animal matrices were calculated, aligning with the CF of 2 previously established during the peer review.
Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs), highest residue (HR), and supervised trials median residue (STMR) for animal products are presented in Table 8. These values were determined based on the estimated dietary burden, following recommendations from OECD (2013) and FAO (2016).
Considering only EU-authorized uses and imported feed contributions, EFSA determined that current MRLs for animal products do not require modification. This applies to ruminant, equine, and other farmed terrestrial animal MRLs (0.02 mg/kg for fat, 0.03 mg/kg for liver, and 0.01 mg/kg LOQ for other products), as well as pig tissues (at LOQ).
Despite the dietary burden calculation for poultry exceeding the 0.1 mg/kg dry matter trigger value, EFSA determined that no MRLs are necessary for poultry products in EU legislation. This conclusion was reached in the absence of specific poultry feeding studies. Instead, a metabolism study from the peer review, which involved a dose rate of approximately 18 mg/kg dry matter (1.5 mg/kg body weight) over 14 days, indicated expected residue levels. The highest total radioactive residue, around 0.02 mg equivalent/kg, was found in egg yolk, skin, and subcutaneous fat.
The applicant and evaluating Member State proposed increasing current EU MRLs, aligning them with existing US/Canadian tolerance/MRLs for the same products, as detailed in Table 9.
For animal products intended for import, the maximum residue levels (MRLs) should correspond to the dietary exposure of livestock in their originating countries, specifically the US and Canada. However, EFSA cannot confirm if the MRLs set in these countries are suitable. Consequently, a decision is needed regarding whether to increase the current MRLs for liver in bovine, sheep, goats, and horses to the US-established level of 0.06 mg/kg (option 2). Alternatively, the MRLs could remain unchanged, reflecting EU uses and residues in imported feed (option 1). Both possibilities will be examined in the risk assessment detailed in Section 4.
EFSA utilized revision 2 of the PRIMo model for consumer risk assessment, which incorporates European food consumption data across various EU population subgroups. Two scenarios were developed by EFSA to determine chronic exposure.
For scenario 1 of the chronic exposure assessment, EFSA utilized specific residue data. For the imported crops in question, the supervised trials median residue (STMR) values from Table 3 were applied. Additionally, STMR values for soybeans and sugar cane, as detailed in a previous EFSA conclusion (EFSA, 2015a), were incorporated. For all other plant-based commodities, the existing Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) set at the Limit of Quantification (LOQ) in the MRL Regulation served as input. For animal products, STMR values for ruminant fat and liver, reflecting European authorized uses and contributions from imported crops and by-products (Table 8), were used. For other animal products, existing MRLs were multiplied by conversion factors (CFs) for risk assessment.
In scenario 2, the MRL for ruminant and equine liver, as established in the US, was used after being adjusted by the risk assessment conversion factor.
For both scenarios, the acute exposure assessment focused solely on the specific commodities being evaluated. This assessment assumed a high consumption of these food items, as indicated by national food surveys. In Scenario 1, it was presumed that these items contained residues at their highest residue (HR) or supervised trials median residue (STMR) levels. Scenario 2, however, used the Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) established in the US for ruminant and equine liver, adjusted for risk assessment. A variability factor was incorporated into the calculations to account for uneven residue distribution among individual consumed items, where necessary. The specific input values for these dietary exposure calculations are detailed in Table 10.
The calculated exposure was evaluated against benzovindiflupyr's toxicological reference values (Table 1). The intake calculation results from the EFSA PRIMo model are publicly accessible, serving as a crucial background document for this reasoned opinion.
During the peer review's risk assessment, a correction factor of 2 was applied to input values. This factor addressed the potential for preferential metabolism of benzovindiflupyr isomers in plants and animals, compensating for insufficient data on whether toxicologically relevant effects stem from a single property.
EFSA's current risk assessment did not account for potential changes in isomer ratios resulting from plant or livestock metabolism. However, the active substance's isomer ratio is set at 1:1, and toxicological studies adhered to these specifications. While a shift in residue isomer ratios could, in a worst-case scenario, double the toxicological burden, this is not a concern if exposure calculations remain below 50% of the ADI or ARfD. Therefore, any potential alteration in isomer ratios in the final residue does not impact the safety of the reviewed uses.
No long-term consumer intake concerns were identified across any European diets within the EFSA PRIMo model. The highest chronic intake observed was 2.8% of the Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) in Dutch and German child diets. Residues in the assessed products contributed minimally to overall consumer exposure. Specifically, milk and milk products accounted for a maximum of 1.6% of the ADI (French toddler diet), apples for 1.2% (German child diet), and tomatoes and barley for 0.5% (WHO Cluster diet B and Irish adult diet, respectively), with other products contributing even less.
No acute consumer risk was found for the MRL proposals in either scenario. The highest acute exposure was 39% of the ARfD for peppers (German toddler diet), followed by 25% for tomatoes.
Even when substituting the US MRL of 0.06 mg/kg for ruminant and horse liver (adjusted by a conversion factor of 3 for risk assessment) into the calculations, the overall exposure assessment remains largely unaffected. For chronic risk, consumption data for bovine liver showed a minor increase in the highest intake, rising from under 0.01% (scenario 1) to 0.05% (scenario 2) of the ADI for British adults. Similarly, for sheep liver, the increase was from 0.01% (scenario 1) to 0.08% (scenario 2) of the ADI for Irish adults. In acute risk assessments, bovine liver consumption data indicated a rise in the highest intake from 0.3% (scenario 1) to 1.5% (scenario 2) of the ARfD for British infants.
EFSA determined that the proposed uses of benzovindiflupyr for these products are unlikely to harm consumers. This is because consumer exposure will not surpass established toxicological limits, indicating no health risk.
The submitted data allowed for the proposal of Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) for various commodities, with benzovindiflupyr as the enforcement residue definition. For pome fruits, including apples, pears, quinces, medlars, loquats, and other pome fruits, the existing EU MRL of 0.01 mg/kg is proposed to be increased to 0.2 mg/kg. This import tolerance for Canada and the US was extrapolated from trials conducted on apples and pears. Wine grapes, currently at 0.01 mg/kg, are proposed to have an MRL of 1 mg/kg. While the OECD methodology suggests an MRL of 1.5 mg/kg, which is higher than the Canadian/US MRL of 1 mg/kg, EFSA recommends aligning with the country of origin's MRL. This proposed MRL is also close to the 0.9 mg/kg calculated using the previous EU methodology (Rber). For potatoes, cassava roots/manioc, sweet potatoes, yams, arrowroots, other tropical root/tuber vegetables, and Jerusalem artichokes, the MRL is proposed to increase from 0.01 mg/kg to 0.02 mg/kg. This import tolerance for Canada and the US was extrapolated from potato trials. Tomatoes and aubergines, currently at 0.01 mg/kg, are proposed to have an MRL of 0.7 mg/kg. This import tolerance for Canada and the US is supported by tomato trials, indicating a lower MRL than the 1.5 mg/kg set in those countries. Sweet peppers/bell peppers and okra/lady's fingers, also at 0.01 mg/kg, are proposed to have an MRL of 1 mg/kg. Pepper trials support this import tolerance for Canada and the US, which is lower than the 1.5 mg/kg MRL in those countries. Cucumbers, gherkins, courgettes, and other cucurbits with edible peel, currently at 0.01 mg/kg, are proposed to have an MRL of 0.08 mg/kg. This import tolerance for Canada and the US was extrapolated from trials on cucumbers and courgettes, supporting a lower MRL than the 0.3 mg/kg in those countries. However, for cucurbits with inedible peel, the MRL remains at 0.01 mg/kg due to insufficient data, requiring two additional trials on melons for a proposal. Sweet corn, with an existing MRL of 0.01 mg/kg, will retain this value as an import tolerance for the US. For beans, lentils, lupins/lupini beans, and other pulses, the MRL is proposed to be 0.2 mg/kg, up from 0.01 mg/kg. This import tolerance for Canada and the US is extrapolated from bean trials. While the OECD methodology suggests 0.3 mg/kg for beans, EFSA proposes to match the 0.2 mg/kg MRL in the countries of origin, which is also close to the Rmax calculation from the previous EU methodology. For peas, a lower MRL of 0.08 mg/kg is proposed, based on trials supporting a value lower than the 0.2 mg/kg in Canada/US. Linseeds, poppy seeds, rapeseeds/canola, mustard seeds, and gold of pleasure seeds, currently at 0.01 mg/kg, are proposed to have an MRL of 0.15 mg/kg. This import tolerance for Canada and the US is extrapolated from trials on oilseed rapes. Peanuts/groundnuts will retain their 0.01 mg/kg MRL as an import tolerance for the US. Cotton seeds are proposed to have an MRL of 0.15 mg/kg, an import tolerance for the US. For coffee beans, the existing EU MRL is 0.05 mg/kg. EFSA presents three risk management options: no change (0.05 mg/kg), setting it at 0.03 mg/kg (as in Brazil), or 0.1 mg/kg. Ginger and turmeric/curcuma, currently at 0.05 mg/kg, are proposed to have an MRL of 0.15 mg/kg. This document defines various abbreviations used in the context of pesticide residue analysis and risk assessment. Key terms include ADI (acceptable daily intake) and ARfD (acute reference dose), which are toxicological reference values. MRL (maximum residue level) refers to the legal limit for pesticide residues in food, while LOQ (limit of quantification) is the lowest concentration that can be reliably measured. The paper also mentions the EMS (evaluating Member State), which is France in this case, and EFSA (European Food Safety Authority), responsible for assessments. Analytical methods like HPLC-MS/MS and QuEChERS are noted, along with international bodies such as OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) and FAO (Food and Agriculture Organization).
The provided table details the Good Agricultural Practices (GAPs) for benzovindiflupyr across various crops and regions. For instance, in Canada and the United States, barley, oats, wheat, and rye are treated with benzovindiflupyr as a fungicide. This involves foliar spray applications of either EC75 or WG150 formulations, with 2 to 14 treatments applied until the BBCH 71 growth stage. The application rate ranges from 19 to 280 g/ha, with a pre-harvest interval (PHI) of 75 days. Pome fruits in Canada and the US also receive fungicide treatment via foliar spray (EC75 or WG150) until BBCH 87, with 4 to 7 applications and a PHI of 50 days. Wine grapes in the same regions are similarly treated with foliar sprays (EC75 or WG150) until BBCH 88, involving 4 to 7 applications and a PHI of 75 days. Potatoes, tropical root and tuber vegetables, Jerusalem artichokes, ginger, and turmeric in Canada and the US are treated with foliar sprays (EC75 or WG150) until BBCH 96, with 4 to 7 applications and a PHI of 75 days. Additionally, in the US, potatoes can receive an in-furrow application (EC100) at BBCH 00-02, with 1 to 2 treatments and a PHI of 100 days. Tomatoes, peppers, and other fruiting solanaceous crops in Canada and the US are treated with foliar sprays (EC75 or WG150) until BBCH 89, with 4 to 7 applications and a PHI of 75 days (0 days in US, 1 day in CA). Cucurbits, maize, popcorn, and sweet corn in the US receive foliar sprays (EC75 or WG150) until BBCH 99, with 4 to 7 applications and a PHI of 75 days. Pulses in Canada and the US are treated with foliar sprays (EC75 or WG150) until BBCH 95, with 2 to 14 applications and a PHI of 75 days. Cotton in Mexico and the US is treated with foliar sprays (EC100 or WG150) until BBCH 89, with 3 to 10 applications and a PHI of 75 days, with aerial application also possible. Peanuts in the US receive foliar sprays (EC100) with 3 to 14 applications and a PHI of 75 days, with a possible soil application 3-4 months before PHI (EC75).
