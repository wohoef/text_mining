This study investigated the development of resistance and cross-resistance to various tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in cancer cells. The research built upon prior findings that sunitinib resistance might stem from enhanced lysosomal sequestration, leading to greater intracellular lysosomal storage and drug inactivation. The current work aimed to explore how other TKIs contribute to this resistance. To achieve this, TKI resistance was artificially induced in cancer cell lines by continuously exposing them to escalating TKI concentrations over three to four months. Cross-resistance was assessed through MTT cell proliferation assays. Intracellular TKI levels were quantified using LC-MS/MS, and the expression of lysosome-associated membrane protein-1 and -2 (LAMP1/2) was determined via Western blotting. The results showed that sunitinib-resistant renal (786-O) and colorectal (HT-29) cancer cells, previously developed, exhibited cross-resistance to pazopanib, erlotinib, and lapatinib, but not to sorafenib. Furthermore, prolonged exposure of 786-O and HT-29 cells to sorafenib, pazopanib, or erlotinib for three to four months led to resistance against pazopanib and erlotinib, but not sorafenib. Notably, intracellular drug accumulation increased in cells exposed to pazopanib and erlotinib, but not sorafenib. Lysosomal capacity, indicated by LAMP1/2 expression, was elevated in resistant cells and proved to be transient. No cross-resistance to the mTOR inhibitor everolimus was observed. In conclusion, the findings suggest that tumor cells can develop TKI resistance, characterized by increased intracellular drug accumulation and lysosomal storage. This transient resistance was evident for several TKIs but not for everolimus, implying that transitioning from a TKI to an mTOR inhibitor could be a promising treatment strategy.

The human kinome contains 518 protein kinases, many of which are implicated in cancer deregulation [1]. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like sunitinib, sorafenib, and pazopanib target receptor tyrosine kinases within the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathway, making these kinases validated clinical anticancer targets [2]. While these multi-targeted TKIs offer clinical benefits as monotherapy in renal cell cancer and are being explored for other tumor types [3], complete remissions are uncommon. Patients often experience disease progression over time, necessitating treatment discontinuation. When progression or intolerable toxicity occurs with one TKI, switching to an alternative TKI is a recognized treatment strategy [4, 5]. This sequential approach can be effective, as evidenced by the clinical activity of pazopanib and sorafenib after sunitinib failure, which implies that cross-resistance might not be absolute [5, 6].
Our understanding of how tumor cells develop resistance or cross-resistance to multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) during prolonged exposure remains limited. This contrasts with single-targeted TKIs, such as vemurafenib for BRAF V600E melanoma or gefitinib for activating EGFR mutations in lung cancer, where acquired resistance is frequently attributed to secondary drug-resistant kinase mutations. The mechanisms by which tumor cells respond to long-term kinase inhibitor treatment, like the continuous administration of sunitinib that typically yields only partial responses, are not well-defined. To investigate this, we previously simulated extended sunitinib exposure in vitro. Our studies demonstrated that continuous sunitinib treatment over several months could induce resistance in 786-O renal cell cancer and HT-29 colorectal cancer cell lines. Notably, this sunitinib-resistant HT-29 cell line maintained its resistance when grown as xenografts in mice. We also observed that this sunitinib resistance was linked to an increased capacity for lysosomal storage and was transient, reversing within weeks of drug withdrawal. This suggests that this form of resistance might be an adaptive response to either partial inhibition of multiple kinases or a disruption in lysosomal function, rather than a permanent genetic change. These findings align with preliminary clinical reports suggesting that reintroducing sunitinib after a recovery period could be a viable treatment strategy.
This study investigated how long-term exposure to sunitinib and other TKIs affects tumor cell sensitivity to subsequent treatments. We analyzed resistance and cross-resistance patterns in tumor cells against various multi-targeted TKIs and the mTOR inhibitor everolimus. Given existing preclinical and clinical data suggesting synergistic effects between EGFR and VEGFR inhibitors, erlotinib and lapatinib were also included. Our findings showed that sunitinib-resistant tumor cells developed cross-resistance to certain TKIs, but not all, a phenomenon linked to increased intracellular drug accumulation. Similarly, continuous exposure of 786-O and HT-29 cells to some TKIs also induced resistance, mirroring the cross-resistance observations. Additionally, resistant cells exhibited an enlarged lysosomal compartment, indicated by elevated LAMP1/2 expression.
Sunitinib malate was a gift from Pfizer Oncology. Other drugs, including sorafenib, pazopanib, erlotinib hydrochloride, lapatinib di-p-toluenesulfonate, and everolimus, were acquired from LC Laboratories. Erlotinib hydrochloride was prepared as a 10 mM stock in 96% DMSO/4% H2O, while all other drugs were 20 mM stock solutions in DMSO. All stock solutions were kept at -80 °C.
To investigate drug resistance, 786-O renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and HT-29 colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines were utilized. These cell lines, obtained from the American Tissue Culture Collection (ATCC) and verified via STR profiling by Baseclear, Leiden, Netherlands, were cultured in DMEM with 5% FBS at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Sunitinib-resistant sub-lines (786-O SUN and HT-29 SUN) were previously established by continuous exposure to 5 µM or 10 µM sunitinib, respectively. For sorafenib, pazopanib, and erlotinib resistance induction, parental 786-O and HT-29 cells were exposed to gradually increasing drug concentrations over 3–4 months. Initial concentrations were between the IC10 and IC50 values. TKI concentrations were doubled at most when cells reached confluence, a process repeated until a stable, well-tolerated concentration was achieved, after which TKI sensitivity was assessed.
To assess drug resistance, cells were first plated in 96-well plates and allowed to attach for 24 hours. A baseline proliferation measurement (t=0) was then taken using the MTT assay. Drugs were subsequently introduced at varying concentrations. After 96 hours, cell proliferation was re-evaluated using the MTT assay. This procedure was independently repeated three times.
To prepare cells for TKI accumulation analysis, parental and TKI-exposed cells (sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib, or erlotinib) were first seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 48 hours. Following this, cells were washed once with PBS and then incubated with their respective TKI-containing media for 24 hours. Subsequently, cells underwent three washes with ice-cold PBS before being trypsinized at 37 °C for 5 minutes. Once detached, ice-cold PBS was added, and the cell suspensions were collected and counted. Finally, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm/4 °C for 10 minutes, the supernatant was removed, and the resulting cell pellets were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until further analysis.
To analyze TKI accumulation, cell pellets were first prepared by reconstituting them in 100 μl of Milli-Q water and homogenizing them through gentle aspiration. A 20 μl aliquot of this homogenized solution was then combined with 80 μl of ice-cold acetonitrile in a round-bottomed 96-well plate, which was subsequently sealed to prevent evaporation. Following a 30-second ultrasonication, the plate underwent centrifugation at 4 °C and 1,000 × g for 10 minutes. Finally, 50 μl of the resulting solution was transferred to a conical 200 μl 96-well plate for analysis using an optimized liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system. Previously established chromatographic and mass spectroscopic parameters were used for sunitinib, sorafenib, and erlotinib. For pazopanib, optimized parameters were determined by diluting a standard reference compound to 1 μg/ml in mobile phase and assessing linearity, accuracy, and precision across a concentration range of 5–5000 ng/ml.
To analyze protein expression, parental cells and those exposed to sorafenib, pazopanib, or erlotinib were cultured for 48 hours in TKI-containing medium. Prior to lysis, the cells underwent two washes with ice-cold PBS. Lysates were then prepared using M-PER mammalian protein extraction reagent, fortified with protease and phosphatase inhibitors from Thermo Scientific. The cells were incubated with this lysis mixture for 20 minutes on ice, then scraped, collected, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was stored at -80 °C until further analysis. This entire lysis procedure was performed in triplicate. Protein concentrations were quantified using a micro BCA protein kit. Subsequently, 20–50 μg of protein from each sample was separated by 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. LAMP-1, LAMP-2, and β-actin proteins were detected using specific antibodies (Santa Cruz biotechnology for LAMP-1 and LAMP-2, Sigma-Aldrich for β-actin). After incubation with IRDye-labeled secondary antibodies, membranes were scanned with an Odyssey Infrared Imaging System. Protein expression was quantified using the accompanying software, normalized to β-actin levels, and then further normalized against untreated samples.
Statistical significance was determined by a p-value less than 0.05, using Student's t-test for analysis. Resistance was quantified as a resistance factor exceeding 2.5, derived from dividing the IC50 of the exposed cell line by that of the parental cell line. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with normalized results provided where suitable.
The initial phase of the study involved assessing the sensitivity of parental 786-O and HT-29 cell lines to various inhibitors through 96-hour proliferation assays. These inhibitors included the tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) sunitinib, sorafenib, and pazopanib, as well as the EGFR TKIs erlotinib and lapatinib, and the mTOR inhibitor everolimus. The proliferation assays revealed that the two cell lines exhibited comparable sensitivities to the TKIs, with IC50 values falling within a low micromolar range, specifically between 0.8 and 6.5 μM (Table 1, Fig. 1a). In contrast, the mTOR inhibitor everolimus displayed a distinct sensitivity profile. Its effect on cell proliferation reached a plateau between approximately 1 nM and 10 μM, where proliferation was only marginally reduced (Fig. 1b). Specifically, for 786-O cells, this plateau corresponded to an approximate IC60, while for HT-29 cells, it was around an IC30. These findings established the baseline sensitivities of the parental cell lines to the tested therapeutic agents.
The study initially re-confirmed sunitinib resistance in the 786-O SUN and HT-29 SUN cell lines, observing a resistance factor (RF) of 3–4 fold (as detailed in Fig. 2a and Table 1). Following this, the researchers assessed the sensitivity of these sunitinib-resistant cell lines to a panel of other drugs: sorafenib, pazopanib, erlotinib, lapatinib, and everolimus. A significant finding was the pronounced cross-resistance to erlotinib in both 786-O SUN and HT-29 SUN cells, with resistance factors of at least 4.5 and greater than 3.2, respectively. For erlotinib, the IC50 could not be determined within the tested concentration range. Pazopanib also demonstrated cross-resistance, specifically in HT-29 SUN cells (RF = 22), but not in 786-O SUN cells (RF = 1.6). In contrast, sorafenib and lapatinib did not induce cross-resistance in the sunitinib-resistant cell lines. The mTOR inhibitor everolimus exhibited only a modest effect on cell proliferation across a broad concentration spectrum in the parental cells, and no cross-resistance to everolimus was detected in the sunitinib-resistant cell lines. These cross-resistance patterns were determined using MTT proliferation assays, comparing the sunitinib-resistant lines to their parental counterparts (Fig. 2a and Table 1).
Our earlier research indicated a link between sunitinib resistance and elevated intracellular drug levels. Building on this, we quantified the intracellular concentrations of sorafenib, pazopanib, and erlotinib in sunitinib-resistant (SUN) cells versus parental (PAR) cells (Fig. 2b). We first re-confirmed the increased total intracellular accumulation of sunitinib in resistant cells. Subsequently, we observed a 2- to 8-fold increase in erlotinib accumulation within the sunitinib-resistant cell lines. Pazopanib accumulation was twofold higher in HT-29 SUN cells compared to HT-29 PAR cells, though it remained consistent between 786-O SUN and PAR cells. Sorafenib accumulated threefold more in HT-29 SUN cells than in their parental counterparts, but its accumulation was lower in 786-O SUN cells.
To determine if the resistance phenotype observed with sunitinib was a general resistance mechanism, researchers continuously exposed parental 786-O and HT-29 cells to escalating concentrations of sorafenib, pazopanib (multi-targeted TKIs), and erlotinib (a single-targeted EGFR family TKI) for 3–4 months. This allowed for a comparison of resistance induction to an EGFR family TKI. The resistance patterns induced by these three TKIs mirrored the cross-resistance observed in sunitinib-selected cells. Specifically, significant resistance developed against pazopanib and erlotinib, with resistance factors (RF) ranging from ≥ 3.1 to > 25 (as detailed in Fig. 3a and Table 2). In contrast, sorafenib exposure did not lead to resistance development, showing an RF of 1.2. The maximum achievable concentrations after 3–4 months of continuous exposure were 3 μM for sorafenib and 20 μM for both pazopanib and erlotinib. Higher concentrations of pazopanib and erlotinib could not be used due to their limited solubility in the culture medium. Interestingly, cells that developed resistance to pazopanib or erlotinib, as well as those exposed to sorafenib, did not exhibit cross-resistance to any of the other tested drugs, which included sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib, erlotinib, lapatinib, and everolimus (data not shown). Further analysis involved measuring the intracellular accumulation of sorafenib, pazopanib, and erlotinib in these continuously exposed cells. These measurements were then compared to parental cells that had been exposed to the drugs for a short period. The results indicated a substantial increase in intracellular pazopanib and erlotinib content (approximately 5–50 fold) in the continuously exposed cells compared to parental cells. However, no such increase in sorafenib content was observed in the continuously exposed cells (Fig. 3b).
Western blotting was employed to assess the lysosomal compartment by measuring the expression of lysosome-associated membrane proteins LAMP-1 and LAMP-2, which are the primary components of the lysosomal membrane. Previous research indicated that these proteins were elevated in sunitinib-resistant cells and returned to normal levels upon drug sensitivity restoration. Our Western blot analysis confirmed increased LAMP-1 and LAMP-2 expression in most cell lines continuously exposed to TKIs. Specifically, quantification showed a 1.5 to 2-fold increase in LAMP-1 and LAMP-2 levels across the three TKI-exposed 786-O and HT-29 cell lines, with the exception of unaltered LAMP-1 expression in the 786-O SOR cell line.
Given pazopanib's growing clinical relevance, the significant resistance observed after 3-4 months of continuous exposure warranted further investigation. To understand the kinetics of resistance development and its reversal, additional experiments were conducted. 786-O and HT-29 cells were treated with pazopanib for durations of 1, 2, and 4 weeks. Resistance to pazopanib emerged quickly in 786-O cells, reaching full development after only two weeks of exposure. In contrast, HT-29 cells exhibited a slower progression to resistance. Furthermore, the reversibility of this resistance was examined. After 4 months of continuous pazopanib exposure, the drug was withdrawn from the resistant cells. Sensitivity was then reassessed at 1, 2, and 4 weeks post-withdrawal. Both cell lines demonstrated a rapid recovery of sensitivity, with nearly complete restoration observed within just one week.
Our prior research demonstrated that continuous, long-term exposure of 786-O and HT-29 cancer cell lines to sunitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), led to the development of resistance in vitro. This resistance was characterized by increased intracellular accumulation of sunitinib and an expansion of the lysosomal compartment, evidenced by elevated expression of lysosome-associated membrane proteins LAMP-1 and LAMP-2. Notably, this sunitinib resistance was transient, reversing within 8–12 weeks upon drug removal. The current study sought to expand on these findings by investigating whether sunitinib-resistant cancer cells exhibit cross-resistance to other targeted therapies and if this resistance mechanism is a general characteristic of TKIs. We evaluated pazopanib, sorafenib, and everolimus, which are TKIs used in renal cell cancer, along with lapatinib and erlotinib, two TKIs with different mechanisms of action. We utilized the 786-O renal cell cancer (RCC) cell line, where sunitinib, sorafenib, and pazopanib are effective as single agents, and the HT-29 colorectal cancer (CRC) cell line, where these drugs show limited activity. The limited efficacy in HT-29 CRC highlights the importance of understanding the effects of prolonged drug administration in this context. Our results showed that sunitinib-resistant cell lines developed cross-resistance to pazopanib and erlotinib, but not to sorafenib, lapatinib, or everolimus. A similar resistance pattern emerged when parental cell lines were continuously exposed to sorafenib, pazopanib, or erlotinib for several months: resistance was induced to pazopanib and erlotinib, but not to sorafenib. Both the development of resistance and cross-resistance correlated with increased intracellular drug accumulation and higher levels of LAMP-1 and LAMP-2 in resistant cells, suggesting the lysosomal compartment plays a role in these cellular adaptations. Furthermore, pazopanib resistance could be rapidly induced in 786-O cells and quickly reversed within weeks after drug withdrawal.
To understand how cancer cells develop resistance to TKIs, our study utilized tumor cell lines and clinically approved drugs, mirroring the extended treatment regimens and resistance profiles seen in patients. Continuous drug administration, common in clinical practice (e.g., for RCC), often leads to acquired resistance. While mutations driven by selective pressure are frequently implicated in resistance, this isn't always the case for TKIs. For instance, unlike chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) or gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), where oncogene addiction is a clear factor, no definitive resistance mutations have been identified in primary RCCs or their derived cell lines that unequivocally link a mutated kinase to tumor growth or survival. Furthermore, even though sunitinib effectively treats c-KIT-driven GISTs, inhibiting HT-29 colorectal cancer cells or xenografts requires blocking additional kinases like EGFR or c-MET, alongside BRAFV600E. This multi-kinase inhibition aligns with the low response rates (around 5%) observed in BRAF-mutant colorectal cancers. The mechanisms of resistance to VEGF inhibitors are still under active investigation, but it's increasingly evident that cellular adaptations to treatment, such as the upregulation of various angiogenic mediators and reversible epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), likely play a significant role. Our findings support this adaptive model, as resistance proved temporary and only persisted with ongoing drug exposure, with cells quickly regaining sensitivity once the drug was withdrawn.
At sunitinib concentrations that inhibit cell growth (1–2 μM), several key downstream targets of tyrosine kinases, including p-Akt(Ser473), p-ERK1/2(Thr202/Tyr204), and p-STAT3(Tyr705), are known to be suppressed. Since no single kinase target appears sufficient for effective growth inhibition in the cell lines studied, it is likely that the downstream effects of multi-targeted TKIs like sunitinib and sorafenib result from a broader, low-level inhibition of multiple upstream receptor and non-receptor kinases. For example, kinases such as Axl and RSK4, which are highly phosphorylated in HT-29 and 786-O cells, are moderately inhibited by sunitinib in in vitro assays. Consequently, the simultaneous inhibition of these, alongside other prevalent kinases like AMPK or even low-level, high-affinity kinases such as CSFR1, could disrupt essential downstream survival pathways. These observations also extend to sorafenib and pazopanib, which share some kinase inhibition profiles with sunitinib. Further phospho-proteomic research is needed to understand how kinase activity profiles adapt and whether kinome reprogramming contributes to TKI resistance. This understanding could inform the development of more effective combination therapies, potentially involving direct modulation of the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway.
Our investigation revealed that resistance to pazopanib and erlotinib, when induced in parental 786-O and HT-29 cells, shared key characteristics with sunitinib resistance. Specifically, these resistant cell lines exhibited increased intracellular drug accumulation and elevated levels of lysosome-associated membrane proteins LAMP-1 and LAMP-2. Sunitinib, pazopanib, and erlotinib are all hydrophobic, membrane-permeable weak bases. This shared property likely facilitates their accumulation in the acidic environment of lysosomes in their protonated form, leading to increased intracellular drug levels and an expansion of the lysosomal compartment. Previous research has confirmed sunitinib's lysosomal accumulation and demonstrated that it disrupts intra-lysosomal pH, potentially causing lysosomal proteases to leak into the cytosol. This mechanism could contribute to the cell death induced by this class of TKIs. The observed increase in LAMP-1 and LAMP-2 expression, which enhances lysosomal stabilization, may also play a role in the development of resistance. However, closer examination revealed some distinctions between sunitinib resistance and resistance to pazopanib or erlotinib. For instance, cells resistant to pazopanib or erlotinib did not exhibit cross-resistance to other tested drugs, unlike sunitinib-resistant cells, which showed cross-resistance to both pazopanib and erlotinib. Furthermore, pazopanib-resistant cells recovered their sensitivity much more quickly (within approximately one week of drug withdrawal) compared to sunitinib-resistant cells. The time required for resistance induction also differed, with pazopanib or erlotinib resistance developing within 4 months, while sunitinib resistance took over a year. Other factors, such as drug characteristics and metabolism, may also influence resistance patterns. The efficiency of drug efflux by transporters like P-glycoprotein and the drugs' mutation-inducing properties could play a role. For example, while sunitinib and doxorubicin share similar physicochemical properties that promote lysosomal accumulation, doxorubicin causes DNA mutations, whereas sunitinib, as a TKI, primarily affects survival signaling pathways. This leads to both shared and distinct cellular adaptations and resistance mechanisms. Our findings also highlighted that resistance to sorafenib did not develop under similar experimental conditions. This suggests that even subtle differences in a drug's physicochemical properties and kinase inhibition profile can lead to varied resistance patterns. In the case of sorafenib, the lysosomal compartment, as indicated by LAMP1/2 expression, was either not induced or only moderately induced, and no intracellular accumulation of sorafenib was observed after continuous exposure. These findings point to a different resistance mechanism for sorafenib compared to the other TKIs. Research indicates that sorafenib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma cells might involve p38α (MAPK14)-dependent MEK-ERK activation, with other downstream proteins like STAT3 also being important. Given that sunitinib-resistant and pazopanib-resistant cells retain sensitivity to both sorafenib and everolimus, switching to these drugs presents a viable therapeutic strategy for patients experiencing progression on sunitinib or pazopanib. The lack of cross-resistance to sorafenib in sunitinib-resistant cells aligns with clinical trial data showing a survival benefit for sorafenib as a second-line treatment after sunitinib failure. The mTOR inhibitor everolimus, due to its distinct mechanism of action compared to multi-targeted TKIs, may be an even more promising candidate for sequential therapy. Everolimus has been shown to extend progression-free survival after VEGF-targeted therapy failure and is currently being evaluated in a rotating schedule with pazopanib for RCC patients.
Tumor cells can acquire resistance, including cross-resistance, to TKIs like sunitinib, pazopanib, and erlotinib. This resistance is linked to higher intracellular drug accumulation. Resistant cells exhibit an enlarged lysosomal compartment, which likely sequesters the drug, leading to this increased accumulation and potentially hindering its activity within the cell. The observation that extended TKI treatment can induce these cellular adaptations and resistance to certain, but not all, agents is important, given their common use in combination or sequential therapies. Furthermore, lysosomal protein expression may serve as a biomarker for these resistance mechanisms.
