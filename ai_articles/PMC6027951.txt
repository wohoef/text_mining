Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients often receive interferon-α (IFN-α) as an adjuvant therapy alongside chemotherapy and radiotherapy, yet some individuals exhibit resistance. To understand this variability, we investigated the role of ISG15 in IFN-α sensitivity using both loss-of-function and gain-of-function approaches in LH86, HLCZ01, SMMC7721, and Huh7 cell lines, as well as tumor samples. Our experiments revealed that increased ISG15 expression diminished apoptosis in Huh7 and LH86 cells when IFN-α was present. Conversely, reducing ISG15 expression via shRNA enhanced apoptosis in these same cell lines. We discovered a potential binding site for miR-370 within the 3’-UTR of ISG15 mRNA. Notably, the level of miR-370 in HCC cell lines correlated with their susceptibility to IFN-α-induced apoptosis. Both HCC cell lines and tumor samples displayed significantly lower miR-370 levels compared to their respective controls (P< 0.05). Furthermore, introducing additional miR-370 into IFN-α-treated LH86 and Huh7 cells boosted apoptosis and decreased the size of xenograft tumors derived from these cells in IFN-α-treated mice, relative to control tumors. These results indicate that miR-370 acts as a tumor suppressor in HCC and modulates IFN-α sensitivity, potentially serving as a valuable prognostic marker for patients.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a significant global health concern, being the second leading cause of cancer-related death in men and the sixth in women. A substantial majority, 80% to 90%, of HCC patients present with advanced disease upon diagnosis, rendering surgical removal impossible. This highlights the urgent need for improved chemotherapy approaches. Interferon-alpha (IFN-α) has demonstrated therapeutic benefits as an adjuvant to chemotherapy and radiation in various cancers, including melanoma, breast, stomach, pancreas, and bladder. Furthermore, research across cell cultures, animal models, and clinical trials has confirmed IFN-α's ability to hinder HCC progression and recurrence.
Chronic infections with hepatitis B (HBV) or hepatitis C (HCV) viruses are prevalent in most hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients. While type I interferons (IFNs) induce microRNA (miRNA) expression to regulate cellular antiviral defenses, changes in miRNA expression are also linked to various human cancers. MiR-370 is one such miRNA, known for its ability to suppress HBV replication. Its expression is often abnormal in several cancers, including esophageal and laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, and prostate cancer, as well as in cholangiocarcinoma cell lines. Interestingly, miR-370 acts as a tumor suppressor in some contexts, like malignant cholangiocytes and laryngeal carcinoma cells, but its overexpression can promote the progression of gastric carcinoma, prostate cancer, and acute myeloid leukemia. The precise biological mechanisms by which miR-370 influences tumorigenesis across such diverse tissue types are not yet fully understood.
ISG15, a ubiquitin-like protein, is induced by IFN-α in HCV-infected hepatoma cells. Its expression and the process of ISG15-mediated conjugation have been linked to various cancers, including those of the breast, prostate, bladder, oral cavity, esophagus, stomach, pancreas, colon, and liver. However, the precise role of ISG15 in tumor development remains poorly understood. While ISG15 forms conjugates with cellular proteins in a manner similar to ubiquitin, this conjugation does not lead to proteasome-dependent degradation, and its biological implications are still unknown. Interestingly, proper regulation of ISG15 expression is linked to apoptosis, whereas dysregulation of ISG15 is associated with increased cell proliferation and migration.
This study builds upon prior research identifying ISG15 as a prognostic biomarker for HCC in patients with chronic HBV infection. Here, we explored ISG15's impact on IFN-α sensitivity in HCC cell lines through experiments that either reduced or increased its function. A key focus was to determine if microRNA (miRNA) could regulate ISG15 by binding to its 3' untranslated region (UTR). We also investigated how a potential miRNA candidate might influence ISG15's role in IFN-induced apoptosis within HCC cells. Our findings indicate that miR-370 plays a crucial role in mediating ISG15's contribution to IFN-α's anti-tumor effects in HCC.
The study's protocol for analyzing HCC tumor and noncancerous liver tissue was approved by the Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital Review Board. These tissue samples, including both cancerous and adjacent healthy liver tissue, were acquired from patients at the Hunan Provincial Tumor Hospital in Changsha, China, after obtaining their informed written consent. For cell-based experiments, human HCC cell lines—HLCZ01, LH86, LO2, Huh7, and SMMC7721—were sourced from the Translational Medicine Research Center at Hunan University. These cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment. Key reagents included recombinant human IFN-α from Kexing Biotech (Beijing, China) and various antibodies: rabbit anti-PARP polyclonal antibodies from Cell Signaling Technologies (Danvers, MA, USA), rabbit anti-ISG12a polyclonal antibodies from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), rabbit anti-ISG15 polyclonal antibodies from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA), and mouse anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).
To achieve ISG15 knockdown, the siPORT NeoFx reagent from ThermoFisher (Waltham, MA, USA) was employed for transfections. Anti-ISG15 siRNA and a randomized negative control RNA oligonucleotide were procured from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. For manipulating miR-370 levels, Ambion (ThermoFisher) supplied the pre-miR miRNA precursor (miR-370), the anti-miR miRNA inhibitor (anti-miR-370), and their respective negative control RNAs. Overexpression of FLAG-tagged ISG15 protein was facilitated by constructing the pFLAG-ISG15 plasmid, which involved inserting ISG15 cDNA into the pFLAG-CMV-2 plasmid's multiple cloning site (Sigma-Aldrich). For siRNA-mediated ISG15 knockdown, the pSilencer system (ThermoFisher) was used to create the pSilencer-shISG15 plasmid, designed to express a 21-nucleotide short hairpin RNA with a 9-nucleotide loop. The specific ISG15 shRNA-coding sequences for pSilencer-ISG15 were provided as 5’-GATCCCCGCACCTACGAGGTACGGCTTTCAAGA GAAGCCGTACCTCGTAGGTGCTTTTTA-3’ (sense) and 5’-AGCTTAAAAAGCACCTACGAGGTACGGCTTCTCTTGAAAGCCGTACCTCGTAGGTGCGGG-3’ (antisense).
To assess the sensitivity of LH86, HLCZ01, SMMC7721, and Huh7 cells to IFN-α, a cell viability assay was performed. Cells were initially cultured in 96-well plates at a density of approximately 8 x 10^3 cells per well in 100 μL of medium for 24 hours at 37°C. Following this, the existing medium was removed and replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing varying concentrations of IFN-α (125, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, or 4,000 IU/mL). After further incubation for 24, 48, or 72 hours, 20 μL of a 5 mg/mL MTT solution was introduced to each well. After a 4-hour incubation period, the MTT-containing medium was aspirated. Subsequently, 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a plate reader.
For protein analysis, cells underwent a specific preparation and detection protocol. Initially, cells were rinsed twice with PBS, then lysed for 30 minutes on ice using a buffer comprising 137 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10% glycerin, 1% Triton X-100, and a protein inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics). The resulting lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C to collect the supernatant. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on a 10% acrylamide gel, followed by electrophoretic transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked for 1.5 hours at room temperature with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 5% nonfat dry milk and 0.1% Tween-20. Primary antibodies were then applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing with TBS, membranes were exposed to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) in TBS. Detection of bound primary antibody was achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (GE Healthcare), and the membranes were imaged with an LAS-4000 luminescence imager (Fujifilm). Finally, membranes were stripped using 20 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.3), and protein loading was normalized by probing with an anti-β-actin antibody, followed by HRP-anti-mouse antibody incubation and ECL visualization.
To evaluate apoptosis in LH86 and Huh7 cells, flow cytometry was employed following transfection. A consistent volume of transfectant was ensured across all experiments by adding vehicle controls. All IFN-α treatments utilized a concentration of 2,000 IU/mL. After 48 hours post-transfection, apoptosis was quantified using the Dead Cell Apoptosis kit from ThermoFisher. This kit allowed for the detection of early-stage apoptosis via annexin V (AV) binding to extracellular phosphatidylserine, and late-stage apoptosis through intracellular propidium iodide (PI) staining. A FACS-Canto flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) was used for cell analysis, and FITC and PI fluorescence levels were determined with Cell Quest software (BD Biosciences).
We utilized the PicTar computational tool (http://pictar.mdc-berlin.de) to explore potential miRNA binding sites within the human ISG15 mRNA (Genbank accession no. NM_005101.3). This in silico analysis aimed to uncover mechanisms behind ISG15 repression in IFN-α resistant cells, leveraging PicTar's ability to align 3' UTR sequences and predict miRNA targets.
To quantify miR-370 levels, qRT-PCR was employed across various samples: human tumor tissues, LH86- and Huh7-derived xenograft tumors, and the cell lines LH86, HLCZ01, L02, SMMC-7721, and Huh7. Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the MagMAX mirVana kit, while miRNA from cultured cells was isolated with the TaqMan MicroRNA Cells-to-CT Kit. The Taqman Advanced miRNA Assay (cat. no. A25576) was then used to measure miR-370, followed by real-time PCR with the TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix.
To evaluate apoptosis in LH86 and Huh7 cells, researchers employed fluorescence microscopy following various transfection conditions. These conditions included treatment with IFN-α alone, miR-370 (with or without IFN-α), anti-miR-370 siRNA (with or without IFN-α), ISG15 alongside IFN-α, and ISG15 combined with both IFN-α and miR-370. A consistent concentration of 2,000 IU/mL IFN-α was used for all IFN-α treatments, and vehicle controls ensured uniform transfectant volumes. After a 48-hour transfection period, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 5 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, they were stained for 30 minutes with 0.5 μg/mL DAPI in PBS, washed, and then mounted in 90% glycerol in PBS. A Nikon Eclipse TE2000-U inverted fluorescence microscope was used for observation. Apoptosis was quantified by identifying condensed chromatin and nuclear fragmentation, with results expressed as a percentage relative to control cells.
Animal experiments were conducted following strict ethical guidelines, with approval from the Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital Review Board and the Hunan Province Animal Care and Use Committee. All procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were kept in a controlled environment, free from pathogens, with regulated temperature and light. To establish xenograft tumors, 6-week-old male NOD/SCID mice received subcutaneous injections of 5 × 10^6 LH86 or Huh7 cells, suspended in 200 μL PBS, into their lower right flank. These cells had previously been infected with either Ad-miR-370 (expressing miR-370) or Ad-GFP (control adenovirus) and selected with antibiotics for 10 days. Three days post-implantation, IFN-α treatment commenced, administered every three days via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 5 × 10^6 U/kg. Tumor volume was monitored and calculated using the formula: TV = 0.5 × width^2 × length. The study concluded with the sacrifice of the mice 42 days after the initial HCC cell implantation.
Statistical significance was established at P< 0.05 for all data comparisons. Various statistical tests, including Student t-tests, log-rank sum, Wilcoxon’s matched-pairs, Mann-Whitney U, and chi-squared analyses, were conducted using SPSS software version 17.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).
Our investigation into the role of ISG15 expression and apoptosis in HCC cells, specifically LH86, HLCZ01, SMMC-7721, and Huh7 lines, was prompted by the known link between ISG15 expression, tumor grade, metastasis, and patient survival in HCC. We observed that IFN-α treatment induced ISG15 protein expression across all HCC cell lines when administered at 2,000 IU/mL for 48 hours (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, our MTT assays revealed a differential sensitivity to IFN-α among these cell lines; LH86 cells consistently exhibited the highest sensitivity across all tested concentrations and time points, while Huh7 cells demonstrated the lowest sensitivity. Optimal sensitivity for all cell lines was generally achieved with approximately 2,000 IU/mL IFN-α after 48 or 72 hours of treatment (Fig. 1A–C). These findings collectively confirm that IFN-α stimulates ISG15 expression in HCC cells and highlight the varying degrees of sensitivity to IFN-α-induced apoptosis among different HCC cell lines.
To evaluate IFN-α's impact on human HCC cell lines, Huh7, HLCZ01, SMMC7721, and LH86 cells were exposed to varying concentrations (125-4,000 IU/mL) of IFN-α for 24, 48, or 72 hours. Cell viability was then determined using an MTT assay. Additionally, western blotting, with β-actin as a control, was employed to analyze the expression levels of ISG15 and ISG12a proteins. These experiments were conducted three times, and the presented data are representative.
To understand how ISG15 expression impacts IFN-α-induced apoptosis, we analyzed its effect on PARP cleavage and apoptosis in LH86 and Huh7 cells. Our Western blot analysis revealed that, without IFN-α, neither overexpressing ISG15 nor knocking down its mRNA significantly altered PARP cleavage or apoptosis in either cell line when compared to the vector control (Fig. 2A-D). However, when LH86 cells were treated with 2,000 IU/mL IFN-α, without ISG15 overexpression, both PARP cleavage and apoptosis increased significantly compared to untreated LH86 controls (Fig. 2A and C). Interestingly, in IFN-α-treated LH86 cells, ISG15 overexpression led to a decrease in both apoptosis (Fig. 2C) and PARP cleavage (Fig. 2A) relative to the IFN-α-treated vector control. For Huh7 cells, IFN-α treatment alone (pSilencer control) did not noticeably change cleaved PARP levels compared to untreated Huh7 cells (Fig. 2B). While ISG15 overexpression in IFN-α-treated Huh7 cells didn't affect PARP cleavage (Fig. 2B), it did reduce apoptosis (Fig. 2D) compared to the IFN-α-treated Huh7 vector control. Conversely, reducing ISG15 mRNA expression via shRNA increased both PARP cleavage and apoptosis in both LH86 and Huh7 cells treated with IFN-α, relative to pSilencer control cells also treated with IFN-α (Fig. 2A-D). These findings indicate that ISG15 expression regulation plays a role in the sensitivity of HCC cells to IFN-α-induced apoptosis. Notably, the apoptotic changes due to ISG15 manipulation were more pronounced in LH86 cells than in Huh7 cells, suggesting that other factors, beyond just ISG15, contribute to the varying IFN-α sensitivities among HCC cell lines.

To understand what controls ISG15 expression in HCC cells, we looked for potential miRNA binding sites within the ISG15 mRNA sequence. Our analysis indicated a strong match between human miR-370 and a 7-base pair sequence located in the 3'-untranslated region (UTR) of human ISG15 mRNA (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, our investigation into miR-370 levels revealed a significant decrease in its expression in four out of five HCC tumor samples when compared to surrounding healthy tissues (Fig. 3B). Similarly, the HCC cell lines HLCZ01, LH86, Huh7, and SMMC7721 all exhibited lower miR-370 expression than the normal liver cell line LO2 (Fig. 3C). These findings imply that a reduction in miR-370 expression may play a role in the advancement of HCC.

Transfecting Huh7 and LH86 cells with miR-370 led to a decrease in ISG15 protein levels, both with and without IFN-α treatment (Fig. 4A, B, C). Conversely, introducing anti-miR-370 into LH86 cells elevated ISG15 expression, irrespective of IFN-α presence (Fig. 4A, D). Using DAPI staining and fluorescence microscopy, researchers explored how miR-370's regulation of ISG15 expression influences HCC cells' responsiveness to IFN-α-induced apoptosis. When miR-370 was introduced, both LH86 and Huh7 cells became more susceptible to IFN-α-induced apoptosis. Conversely, introducing exogenous FLAG-ISG15 decreased this sensitivity in both cell lines, leading to reduced apoptosis compared to IFN-α-treated controls. Notably, Huh7 cells displayed less sensitivity to IFN-α-induced apoptosis than LH86 cells. The enhanced apoptosis caused by miR-370 transfection was counteracted by the overexpression of exogenous FLAG-ISG15. Furthermore, reducing endogenous miR-370 levels through anti-miR-370 transfection made LH86 cells less sensitive to IFN-α-induced apoptosis, mirroring the resistance observed in Huh7 cells. These findings indicate that miR-370 influences IFN-α-induced apoptosis in HCC cells by inhibiting ISG15 expression.
To validate the impact of miR-370 on IFN-α sensitivity, mediated by ISG15, within a living system, we conducted xenograft studies in NOD/SCID mice. LH86 and Huh7 cells, engineered to express miR-370 via adenovirus, were implanted into the mice's right flank. Following implantation, these mice received IFN-α treatment. By day 17, tumors with elevated miR-370 expression were roughly half the size of those in control mice. This outcome indicates that miR-370 plays a significant role in the in vivo immune response of HCC tumors to IFN-α.
To assess miR-370's impact on IFN-α sensitivity in a living organism, xenograft tumors were established in NOD/SCID mice. This involved implanting Huh7 or LH86 cells, previously engineered to express miR-370 via an adenovirus. Following implantation, these mice received IFN-α treatments every three days. After 42 days, the mice were euthanized, and the resulting tumor volumes from both LH86 and Huh7 cell lines were measured and compared against control virus-infected tumors.
Interferon-alpha (IFN-α) is a cytokine with broad effects, known to directly suppress the growth and proliferation of malignant hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells. It achieves this by inducing numerous proapoptotic genes and by inhibiting angiogenesis within HCC, thereby hindering tumor progression. IFN-α also enhances the effectiveness and prognosis for HCC patients undergoing chemotherapy with agents like 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, methotrexate, and doxorubicin. Furthermore, it is a primary treatment component, combined with antiviral drugs, for HBV and HCV infections, which are major contributors to HCC. Despite these benefits, many HCC patients do not respond favorably to IFN-α adjuvant therapy, highlighting the critical need to understand the biological mechanisms governing hepatocyte sensitivity to IFN-α.
Our investigation revealed that IFN-α stimulated ISG15 expression across several HCC cell lines, specifically LH86, HLCZ01, SMMC7721, and Huh7 (Fig. 1). This observation aligns with previous research by Zhu et al., who similarly noted IFN-α-induced ISG15 expression in Huh7 and FCA1 cells [32]. When ISG15 was overexpressed in the presence of IFN-α, we observed a decrease in apoptosis in both Huh7 and LH86 cells. Conversely, knocking down ISG15 expression using shRNA led to an increase in apoptosis in these same cell lines when treated with IFN-α (Fig. 2). However, ISG15 overexpression alone, without IFN-α treatment, did not significantly alter apoptosis in Huh7 and LH86 cells. These findings present a contrast to those reported by Wan et al. [41]. Their study indicated that while IFN-α induced ISG15 expression in HepG2 cells, IFN-α treatment alone did not trigger apoptosis in these cells. This suggests that HepG2 cells might be less responsive to IFN-α compared to Huh7 cells, and indeed, HepG2 cells demonstrated the lowest IFN-α sensitivity among the cell lines we examined. Furthermore, Wan et al. found that ISG15 overexpression enhanced apoptosis in HepG2 cells even without IFN-α [41]. Our experiments, however, showed that ISG15 overexpression did not increase apoptosis in Huh7 and LH86 cells in the absence of IFN-α (Fig. 2C and D). This difference implies that the apoptotic mechanism linked to ISG15 expression in HepG2 cells might operate independently of other IFN-α-stimulated genes.
Our study, using bioinformatic analysis, indicated that miR-370 reduces ISG15 expression in Huh7 and LH86 cells by potentially binding to the 3’-UTR of ISG15 mRNA (Figures 3 and 4). This aligns with previous research by Xu et al., who found a progressive decline in miR-370 expression during HCC development in rats. Their work also linked lower miR-370 levels in tumor tissues to advanced tumor progression, increased angiogenesis, the presence of microsatellite tumors, capsular invasion, and a poorer prognosis for HCC patients. Consistent with these findings, we observed markedly lower miR-370 levels in all tested HCC cell lines and the majority of HCC tumor samples when compared to normal LO2 hepatocytes and adjacent noncancerous tissues, respectively (Figure 3). Furthermore, our experiments demonstrated that introducing miR-370 into Huh7 and LH86 cells treated with IFN-α led to decreased ISG15 expression and enhanced apoptosis (Figure 5). In an in vivo model, mice treated with IFN-α and implanted with Huh7 or LH86 cells overexpressing miR-370 developed smaller xenograft tumors compared to controls lacking miR-370 overexpression (Figure 6). Separately, animal studies by Li et al. revealed that suppressing ISG15 expression hindered the growth of HepG2 and 97L xenograft tumors even without IFN-α treatment, suggesting that ISG15-mediated apoptosis might involve mechanisms beyond IFN-α-induced genes.
Our study's cell culture experiments revealed a correlation between miR-370 expression levels in HCC cell lines and the extent of IFN-α-induced apoptosis (Fig. 3). Furthermore, we observed that overexpressing miR-370 consistently decreased ISG15 expression, regardless of whether IFN-α was present (Fig. 4). While we did not investigate miR-370's impact on xenograft tumors without IFN-α treatment, previous work by Li et al. demonstrated a link between elevated ISG15 expression and tumor progression, metastasis, and poorer survival in HCC patients, even without IFN-α intervention [29]. Integrating these findings suggests that additional mechanisms, beyond those directly involving IFN-α, contribute to miR-370's regulation of ISG15 in HCC cells. This aligns with existing research highlighting the potential role of reduced miR-370 expression in HCC development. Our data also indicated that HCC cells with lower miR-370 expression developed larger xenograft tumors (Figs 3 and 6), implying a possible association between miR-370 expression and diminished sensitivity to IFN-α.
IFN-α triggers apoptosis and upregulates ISG15, which negatively regulates this apoptotic process. MiR-370 silences ISG15 by binding to its 3'-UTR, thereby counteracting the downregulation of apoptosis.
Our study concluded that miR-370 acts by targeting a specific sequence in the 3'-UTR of ISG15 mRNA, thereby decreasing ISG15 expression in Huh7 and LH86 cells. This downregulation of ISG15 subsequently enhances IFN-α's apoptotic activity. We observed significantly reduced miR-370 levels in all tested HCC cell lines and most HCC tumor samples compared to controls. Furthermore, both reducing ISG15 expression and increasing miR-370 levels promoted apoptosis in IFN-α-treated HCC cell lines. In animal models, elevated miR-370 expression led to a reduction in the size of LH86 and Huh7 xenograft tumors when treated with IFN-α.
MiR-370 appears to act as a tumor suppressor in HCC cells and could serve as a valuable prognostic marker, particularly for patients whose tumors resist IFN-α treatment. Further large-scale studies are needed to validate these findings, especially by examining miR-370 expression in patient HCC tumor samples.
