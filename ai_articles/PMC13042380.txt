Implanting intraocular lenses (IOLs) in eyes lacking adequate capsular support has historically presented a surgical challenge. Intrascleral fixation of three-piece posterior chamber IOL haptics has emerged as a widely adopted solution. However, a critical and demanding phase of this procedure is the externalization of the leading haptic during IOL injection. To address this, we developed a modified technique designed to enhance both the ease and safety of this step. Our approach utilizes a motorized injector, incorporating specific modifications detailed within the paper. This system enables surgeons to maintain precise IOL orientation with superior control throughout the injection process. A retrospective review of 13 patients who underwent this modified technique demonstrated a significant improvement in corrected-distance visual acuity post-surgery (p < 0.05). Importantly, no instances of retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, IOL decentration, or vitreous hemorrhage were observed during the follow-up period. Ultimately, this motorized injector-assisted intrascleral IOL fixation technique offers a safe and effective alternative, simplifying and improving control over the crucial leading haptic externalization.

Modern cataract surgery aims for a single-procedure lens removal and IOL implantation, ideally resulting in a centered IOL within the capsular bag. When capsular support is insufficient, alternative IOL placements include the anterior chamber (AC), iris fixation, or posterior chamber fixation with trans-scleral sutures, each with drawbacks. AC IOLs can cause corneal endothelium issues, uveitis, and glaucoma. Trans-scleral fixation carries risks of IOL decentration, tilt, and dislocation due to suture degradation.
Intrascleral fixation of three-piece posterior chamber IOL haptics, first described by Scharioth and Pavlidis in 2007, has gained popularity for managing eyes lacking adequate capsular support. This technique offers several advantages over alternatives, including the elimination of suture degradation issues, posterior chamber placement, and superior IOL stability, resulting in reduced postoperative decentration and tilting. Numerous modifications have since been introduced to refine specific procedural aspects. These innovations include the use of fibrin glue for wound closure, known as the glued IOL technique, specialized methods for intraocular haptic manipulation like the handshake technique, and approaches such as the no-assistant technique to facilitate trailing haptic externalization.
Externalizing the leading haptic during IOL injection presents a significant challenge. To address this, we developed a modified technique employing a motorized injector instead of a manual one. This approach enhances procedural stability, offering improved control and ease for this crucial step.
The study, conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki, received Institutional Review Board approval from National Cheng Kung University Hospital. All patients provided written informed consent before the procedure.
Patients undergoing motorized injector-assisted intrascleral IOL fixation from January 2015 to May 2016 were included. Indications were aphakia, subluxated crystalline lens, or IOL. Those with preoperative retinal detachment were excluded. Follow-up extended to July 2016.
For statistical analysis, decimal corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) was converted to logMAR. Preoperative and postoperative CDVA data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with a p-value less than 0.05 considered significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 3.3.1.
The surgical procedure was segmented into five key stages. Initially, scleral flaps and an infusion setup were created. Subsequently, the motorized injector was prepared. The third stage involved IOL injection, focusing on externalizing the leading haptic. Following this, the trailing haptic was externalized. The final step included tucking the haptics and closing the wound with fibrin glue. A supplementary video detailing the entire process is available online.
To begin, a localized peritomy was made at the haptic exit sites, with careful cauterization of the sclera. Two partial-thickness limbus-based scleral flaps, each measuring 2.5 mm by 2.5 mm, were then created diametrically opposite each other. A pars plana infusion cannula or an anterior chamber maintainer was inserted, typically in the inferonasal quadrant for ease of access. Subsequently, two pars plicata sclerotomies were made 1 mm from the limbus, situated beneath the scleral flaps, using either a 20-gauge blade or a 22-gauge needle. Finally, anterior vitrectomy was performed through these sclerotomies or corneal incisions to alleviate vitreous traction.
A motorized Autosert IOL Injector system was used. It was fitted with a Monarch II C cartridge containing a foldable three-piece IOL. The cartridge was then attached to the injector handpiece.
To prevent damage, the trailing haptic was positioned away from the plunger tip using a hook or forceps before the preload step. Following this, the preload was initiated, and the surgeon confirmed the plunger smoothly advanced the optic. As the IOL moved through the cartridge tunnel, it folded. Once the IOL reached the nozzle, it was prepared for injection. At this point, the leading haptic's configuration was checked. Any folded leading haptic could be straightened with an IOL dialer. Should the trailing haptic be damaged or detached, the plunger would be retracted, and the compromised IOL replaced.
A 2.8-mm corneal incision was made using a keratome, and a side port was created. The cartridge tip was inserted into the anterior chamber through the corneal incision. Subsequently, a 23-gauge glued IOL forceps was introduced from the left sclerotomy. By supinating the right wrist and rotating the cartridge clockwise, the leading haptic was positioned to the surgeon's left, forming the "Lucky 7" sign.
The motorized injector was activated using a footswitch. Once the leading haptic emerged from the nozzle, the injection was temporarily halted. Throughout this phase, the leading haptic was kept in the "Lucky 7" orientation. Forceps secured the leading haptic at its tip while the IOL optic remained within the cartridge. Injection then resumed, with pronation movements of the right wrist potentially required to ensure proper IOL alignment. The forceps maintained their grip on the leading haptic until the optic had fully exited the nozzle. Subsequently, the leading haptic was guided out through the left sclerotomy, and the injector was removed. The trailing haptic was held outside the eye to prevent accidental IOL dislodgement.
Following injection, the trailing haptic should ideally present as an "Upright C" sign, pointing to the surgeon's right. If the IOL's orientation was incorrect, careful manipulation of the haptics, as shown in the video, could typically correct it with an IOL flip.
The surgeon stabilized the externalized leading haptic with left-hand forceps. The trailing haptic was then flexed into the globe through the incision with the right hand, crossing the midline before being externalized through the right sclerotomy via the handshake technique. This combined method is known as the no-assistant technique. Alternatively, an assistant could stabilize the leading haptic to prevent slippage.
Intrascleral tunnels, parallel to the limbus and created with a 26-gauge bent needle at the scleral flap's edge, received the haptics. These tunnels corresponded to the sclerotomy sites. IOL centering was achieved by adjusting the length of the tucked haptics. Following this, infusion was halted, and air was introduced into the anterior chamber to constrict the pupil. Fibrin glue (Tisseel VH; Baxter, Vienna, Austria) was then used to close the scleral flaps and conjunctival peritomy. Post-surgery, patients received topical antibiotics and steroids four times daily for four weeks, with ointment at bedtime. They were also advised to use an eye protector and refrain from any eye trauma, including massage or rubbing.
The modified technique was applied to 15 eyes across 15 patients. Two patients with preoperative retinal detachment were excluded, leaving a cohort of 13 individuals (seven male, six female). The median age of participants was 63 years, with an interquartile range of 60–75 years. The study included five cases of aphakia, five with subluxated crystalline lenses, and three with subluxated intraocular lenses. Patients were followed for a median of 5 months, ranging from 1 to 17 months. Table 1 details the clinical characteristics and intermediate postoperative results.
Postoperative corrected-distance visual acuity significantly improved from a mean of 1.01 to 0.63 logMAR units (p < 0.05), with all intraocular lenses centered and without tilt.
No intraoperative IOL drops occurred. Two patients (15.4%) experienced persistent corneal edema unresponsive to treatment, linked to pre-existing decompensated corneal endothelium from prior complex cataract surgery. Additionally, one eye (7.7%) developed iris capture of the IOL with pigment dispersion.
Three eyes showed suboptimal visual outcomes, possibly due to high corneal astigmatism, in addition to prolonged corneal edema. One patient's poor vision was unexplainable, and they were lost to follow-up. No endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, IOL dislocation, or vitreous hemorrhage occurred.
Our technique combines a motorized injector with glued IOL implantation to simplify and enhance the challenging step of leading haptic externalization during IOL injection.
Conventional glued IOL procedures typically employ either screw-type or plunger-type manual injectors. Screw-type injectors are preferred for their consistent injection velocity, which prevents abrupt IOL entry. However, this method necessitates the surgeon's full attention on both the injector and forceps, along with an assistant to operate the screw. This reliance on an experienced assistant can be problematic, as trained staff may not always be available. Moreover, the presence of an assistant's hand in the anterior chamber carries a risk of damaging ocular structures if their hand is unsteady, and generally contributes to a more crowded surgical field.
Plunger-type injectors allow the surgeon to perform this step independently, but they carry a higher risk of sudden IOL movement and potential IOL drop. This method also demands simultaneous bimanual manipulation within a short timeframe, necessitating a steeper learning curve.
A motorized injector offers advantages over manual ones by providing steady injection velocity. Activated by the surgeon's foot, it eliminates the need for an assistant's hand.
Dr. Amar Agarwal noted in May 2016 that Alcon cartridges may inject three-piece IOLs inverted. This issue can be prevented by carefully observing the correct IOL configurations, specifically the "Lucky 7" and "Upright C" signs, as previously described.
A key benefit of our technique is the controlled IOL injection, allowing surgeons to easily maintain correct IOL orientation throughout the procedure. The adjustable injection rate provides surgeons with more time and space for precise hand movements. Consequently, this stressful step becomes significantly easier and safer.
This study's limitations include a small sample size, short follow-up, and no control group. Further controlled studies are needed to assess long-term outcomes.
Long-term outcomes are currently unavailable. A potential risk of scleral wound leakage exists. To aid healing and prevent infection, patients should avoid eye trauma and use topical antibiotics for one month.
Following scleral tuck-in, the haptics are effectively supported and stabilized by the mechanical force of the scleral tunnel. Fibrin glue promotes immediate adhesion between the haptics and the sclera, with this effect lasting approximately eight days. Within about 48 hours post-surgery, fibroblasts initiate scar formation as part of the physiological healing process. This wound healing mechanism then continues to enhance the haptic fixation, providing a theoretically permanent effect as the fibrin glue degrades.
This motorized injector-assisted glued IOL technique is easy, safe, and effective. It simplifies leading haptic externalization and prevents complications from insufficient assistance.
