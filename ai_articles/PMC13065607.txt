Existing and planned UK climate policies are projected to fall short of meeting national targets, creating a "mitigation gap." This shortfall is driven by industrial greenhouse gas emissions, which are increasing faster than the economy's carbon intensity is improving, largely due to material and product demand. Research indicates that both reducing the carbon intensity of product manufacturing and decreasing demand for new products are viable strategies. However, the economy-wide impact of material productivity and lifestyle adjustments on closing this gap remains underexplored. This study employs an input-output framework combined with econometric analysis and case study data to assess the potential of material productivity in addressing the UK's projected emissions deficits. It also examines the additional efforts needed for transformative change to align with 2°C and 1.5°C temperature targets. Our findings suggest that emissions savings from material productivity measures are comparable to those from the government's current climate policy package. Implementing these additional measures could potentially reduce the UK's anticipated emissions deficit by as much as 73%. Consequently, the results highlight that material productivity warrants greater attention in climate policy development.

Climate change is a critical sustainability challenge, significantly driven by material use. Greenhouse gas emissions are generated throughout the entire lifecycle of materials, from resource extraction and processing to manufacturing, product use, and disposal. Globally, material consumption continues to rise alongside economic growth, with little evidence of decoupling. This material and product demand is the primary driver of industrial emissions. However, current climate change mitigation strategies predominantly focus on low-carbon energy technologies and energy efficiency improvements, largely overlooking the impact of material and product demand. This narrow focus means that a crucial area for emissions reduction is currently understudied.
Current approaches to assessing climate mitigation primarily rely on integrated assessment models (IAMs) and cost-optimization models of energy systems, such as computable general equilibrium (CGE) models. These IAMs typically concentrate on direct energy-consuming industries and technologies like power generation, transportation, and building heating. While some recent studies have begun to incorporate supply chain analysis into these conventional models, a significant limitation persists. These models often overlook the crucial link between energy use within supply chains and the everyday consumption patterns driving demand for mobility, comfort, food, and leisure. A key consequence of this oversight is that emissions embedded within materials and products, which are essential for meeting societal needs, are not adequately accounted for in decision-making processes. For instance, in the EU, direct emissions from service and manufacturing sectors might appear minor (only 9%), but these sectors are substantial consumers of greenhouse gas-intensive materials and products throughout their supply chains. Consequently, they embody a much larger proportion of emissions – 22% and 26% respectively – to satisfy EU consumption. By adopting a supply chain or embodied perspective, we can uncover additional opportunities for intervention. These opportunities lie in how products are designed, marketed, utilized, and ultimately discarded, which the authors define as material productivity measures.
Traditional economic analyses simplify behavioral changes by assuming agents operate under a utility-maximizing framework. This approach posits that individuals exhaustively rank their preferences across all available products and markets, disregarding intrinsic motivations, habits, or broader societal and institutional influences. Both business and individual consumers are often treated as rational actors. For instance, firms are presumed to consistently minimize costs by selecting optimal intermediate inputs, implying they always make fully informed, rational decisions to maximize efficiency or minimize supply chain impacts. Similarly, households are modeled as a single, homogenous unit aiming to maximize utility, essentially purchasing the most goods at the lowest cost, with complete market information. However, real-world decision-making frequently diverges from these rational choice principles. These oversimplified assumptions have led to policies that fail to reach their full potential due to unrealistic expectations of human behavior. Consequently, prevailing models struggle to accurately assess the mitigation potential of material productivity measures, largely because they overlook how emissions become embedded in materials and products and rely on flawed representations of consumer behavior.
To address limitations in climate assessment methods, researchers use qualitative and case study data to model consumer behavior. This is often done in conjunction with quantitative consumption-based techniques like footprinting. A GHG footprint quantifies the direct and indirect greenhouse gas emissions associated with fulfilling specific consumption needs, whether for a single product, an activity, or a collection of them.
Greenhouse gas (GHG) footprinting serves as a valuable tool for tracking progress toward climate goals and evaluating the potential of demand-side mitigation strategies. Research employing footprinting and life-cycle assessment methods, including material flow, life-cycle, and input-output analyses, consistently highlights the importance of enhancing material productivity. This improvement is considered essential, if not a prerequisite, for achieving global climate mitigation objectives. Despite this evidence, measures focused on material productivity have not yet been widely adopted as effective policy responses. Current discussions in the literature explore various avenues for improvement, such as maximizing the utilization of infrastructure through shared assets, extending product lifespans, designing products with reduced material requirements, and decreasing overall consumption and waste. These approaches necessitate both industrial redesign and shifts in lifestyle.
To assess the economy-wide impacts of shifts in technologies and consumption, input-output (IO) models provide a macro-scale framework for calculating greenhouse gas (GHG) footprints. These models track emissions through supply chains, from their origin in production to their ultimate destination in final product demand. This is achieved by following the monetary transactions between economic sectors. IO analysis reveals the interconnectedness of sectors within an economy, illustrating "pull effects" where sectors consume significant intermediate inputs, and "push effects" where sectors face demand for their outputs from both other industries and end consumers. A key strength of IO analysis is its ability to attribute each unit of emissions from production activities to a specific region of final demand. This is done by tracing emissions through intricate supply chains, thereby preventing double-counting. The methodology also captures the real emissions intensity of industries across different countries, allowing for the allocation of production activities in one nation to final demand in another through intersectoral and trade monetary flows. Although a direct tracing of physical material flows would offer greater precision, data constraints necessitate the use of aggregate monetary transactions, sourced from national accounting systems, as a proxy for material and product flows between economic sectors and regions.
To project material use and emissions until 2050, some researchers have employed dynamic Input-Output (IO) models. These models simulate economic structure changes in response to price shifts. For instance, a decrease in material inputs for a specific sector can alter prices and profits, leading to redistribution throughout the economy. Alternatively, taxing material extraction industries can raise their costs and subsequently reduce demand. This paper, however, prioritizes non-price simulations. As suggested by Dietz et al. (2009), this approach offers a more realistic portrayal of behavior, a quality often absent in purely technological or economic assessments.
Several studies have explored consumption-based interventions within input-output (IO) frameworks. Wood et al. (2018b), for instance, outlined three primary approaches: altering consumption habits, including overall reduction; adjusting industrial production inputs, akin to modifying production recipes; and decreasing direct emissions via measures like pollution control or efficiency enhancements. Their work utilized case studies to estimate potential reductions in material flows for the EU's clothing and food sectors. Further research by Lekve Bjelle, Steen-Olsen, and Wood (2018) examined how Norwegian households could shrink their greenhouse gas (GHG) footprint through various behavioral changes identified in existing literature. This involved comparing current behaviors with more effective alternatives and scaling the resulting yearly savings per household. Their analysis also addressed the rebound effect, where financial savings from reduced demand might be reinvested, thereby offsetting emission reductions. Cooper et al. (2017) focused on industrial energy demand reductions across supply chains, stemming from circular economy initiatives applied to a 2007 baseline. Their investigation explored strategies aimed at either minimizing the requirement for high-impact production inputs ("putting less in") or decreasing the overall demand for products ("getting more out"). A common limitation across these studies is their focus on a single point in time, failing to capture the dynamic interplay of economic strategies and climate policy-driven decarbonization over time.
Barrett and Scott (2012) took a different approach, integrating projections of crucial input-output (IO) variables like demand, production methods, and carbon efficiencies into their assessment of resource efficiency's role in achieving UK climate targets for 2050. Their modeling specifically addressed shifts in material demands within both production systems and consumption patterns, alongside efforts to lower the electricity sector's emissions intensity. Other researchers, such as De Koning et al. (2018), have extrapolated IO variables to forecast future material demands, considering technical and socio-economic factors like economic growth and efficiency gains. Wilting, Faber, and Idenburg (2008) also projected economic production structures by employing trend analysis and expert insights to anticipate changes in sectoral production inputs. A significant limitation across these studies, however, is the absence of a comprehensive analysis that combines the full spectrum of climate mitigation policies with resource efficiency measures, hindering effective policy comparisons.
To assess the economy-wide potential of material productivity and lifestyle changes in helping the UK meet its climate objectives, this study employs an Input-Output (IO) framework. The UK has established five-year carbon budgets, aiming for an 80% reduction in domestic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 2050, supported by policies targeting energy use in power, industry, buildings, and transport. At the time of this research, five carbon budgets were legislated, covering the period from 2008 to 2032. Our analysis focuses on two key areas: first, the capacity of material productivity measures to address the anticipated shortfall in the UK's fourth and fifth carbon budgets (2022-2032), which current and planned policies are projected to miss. Second, we quantify how much of the UK's carbon budget, necessary for transformative change aligned with 2°C and 1.5°C temperature targets, will be consumed by 2032. A unique aspect of our methodology is its consideration of the temporal evolution of emissions savings. By integrating emission reductions from existing and planned climate policies as they are implemented, we can identify genuine additional savings and their contribution to long-term climate goals. Ultimately, we compare the emissions reductions achieved through material productivity strategies with those from the UK's existing energy-focused climate policies.
To analyze emissions, we created a time series of material and product flows in the UK using an input-output framework. This model integrates UK government economic and emissions forecasts, which are derived from macro-econometric models and policy interventions. These forecasts account for shifts in the carbon intensity of UK production, household income, government and capital spending, and export demand. Furthermore, we incorporated changes in product design, or "production recipes," and consumer behavior, informed by case study evidence. By adjusting the ambition and adoption rates of material productivity strategies, we explored the range of potential emissions savings.
This study examines the temporal dimension of material productivity strategies, assessing their potential to contribute to UK greenhouse gas emission reductions alongside existing and planned climate policies. Our focus is on the 4th (2023-2027) and 5th (2028-2032) carbon budget periods, which are projected to have a significant emissions shortfall requiring additional policy interventions. The analysis spans from 2013, the beginning of the second carbon budget, through to 2032. At the time of this research, only five carbon budgets had been legislated. Furthermore, we quantify the UK's remaining carbon budget from 2032 to 2050, aligning with international climate objectives, and project how quickly this budget could be depleted without further policy action. While accounting for the planned implementation timelines of technological advancements and policies affecting carbon intensity, we assume a linear deployment of material productivity measures from 2013 to their peak in 2032, due to limited data on their deployment speed. Consequently, we explore various levels of ambition and adoption rates for these measures, drawing insights from case studies. These case studies highlight the practical applicability of material productivity measures in specific, often localized, contexts. Their implementation is constrained not only by technological readiness but also by institutional and societal hurdles, such as insufficient policy incentives and public acceptance.
Our analysis focuses on the geographical scope of emissions savings relevant to UK carbon budgets, specifically considering reductions within the UK. While acknowledging the UK's broader responsibility for emissions linked to its consumption, even those occurring abroad, our model adheres to the current political framework where UK carbon budgets are met through domestic emission reductions. Therefore, we exclude emissions embedded in imports from our calculations. Our methodology accounts for emissions generated within the UK that are embodied in products consumed domestically, as well as those embodied in products exported from the UK. This means we are modeling alterations in both the production methods of UK goods and the demand for these products. By setting imported emissions to zero, our scope is limited to emissions within the purview of domestic targets. It is important to note that implementing these changes would also lead to emission reductions in supply chains outside the UK, which, if factored in, would significantly enhance the calculated domestic emission savings.
Our material productivity strategies concentrate on the design and demand for high-embodied greenhouse gas (GHG) materials and products, specifically textiles, food and drink, vehicles, construction, electronics, and packaging. In 2013, these sectors accounted for 14% of industrial emissions within the UK's territory, but represented nearly a quarter (23%) of total UK emissions when considering embodied emissions. These material productivity approaches are underpinned by efforts to improve the carbon intensity of production through existing and planned UK climate policies, which also address residential heating and travel demands. While service sectors are not directly targeted, their product usage within supply chains is incorporated into our modeling.
We employ the UK multiregional input-output model (MRIO) to attribute UK emissions from 2013 to the final products they are embodied in, rather than by the source sector of production. These products are then consumed domestically and internationally by households and governments, or contribute to significant capital expenditure.
To analyze embodied emissions, the global economy was simplified into a two-region model: the UK and the Rest of the World (RoW). This structure allows for the tracking of emissions from goods and services, categorized into 106 sectors based on the UK Standard Industrial Classification system. Retaining this two-region approach is crucial for capturing emissions associated with products that are exported and subsequently reimported into the UK through international supply chains. The calculation of embodied emissions utilizes the established Leontief demand-pull model. This method reallocates greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions originating from UK sectors to the final products they are incorporated into. This process follows products through various stages of trade and transformation. The core of this calculation is represented by Equation 1: **q = e(I - A)⁻¹Ŷ**. In this equation, 'q' denotes a vector of embodied emissions per sector. 'e' represents the GHG intensity of UK production sectors, with RoW intensities set to zero. 'I' is an identity matrix, and 'A' is the technical coefficients matrix, which details the intermediate inputs, both domestic and foreign, a sector needs for a unit of output – essentially, its production recipe. The term **(I - A)⁻¹**, known as the Leontief inverse (L), quantifies the increase in output across all sectors resulting from a unit increase in final demand. Finally, 'Ŷ' is a diagonalized vector representing the total final demand from households, government, and capital investment in both the UK and the RoW, including UK goods destined for export.
Macroeconometric modeling projects UK production's carbon intensity, household demand, government/capital spending, and export demand. Additionally, case study evidence informs changes in production recipes and demand patterns. These methods align with approaches used by Wilting et al. (2008) and Wood et al. (2018b). Since the late 1970s, the UK Government has been projecting energy demand and supply. By the 1990s, these projections expanded to encompass anticipated carbon dioxide (CO2) and other greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, with the Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy (BEIS) now publishing these annually. BEIS's model forecasts energy demand through econometric equations that link it to factors like economic growth, fossil fuel prices, carbon prices, population, disposable income, and household numbers. Electricity producers aim to maximize investment returns when meeting this demand. Emissions factors are then used to translate energy demand into emissions. These projections are adjusted to reflect the impact of policies that reduce energy demand. The energy and emissions forecasts assess cumulative emissions savings from climate policies and are crucial for tracking progress towards carbon budgets. Policies are classified based on their implementation status: expired, implemented, adopted, or planned.
To project UK emissions from 2013 to 2032, two climate policy scenarios were modeled using BEIS energy and emissions projections. These scenarios considered the implementation of (1) existing policies and (2) both existing and planned policies, with detailed emissions scenarios available in the supporting information. The analysis involved aligning different emissions classification systems. UK CO2 emissions were projected across 109 IPCC source sectors, while non-CO2 GHGs were covered by nine high-level sectors. This data was then mapped to the 106 sectors of the Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) used in economic accounting to create IO-compatible production emissions data. The specific mappings between the IPCC and BEIS classification systems and the SIC sectors are provided in the supporting information.

To explore material productivity strategies, we analyzed 43 case studies focusing on six manufactured product categories. These studies investigated two main approaches: redesigning products with less carbon-intensive inputs ("putting less in") and reducing demand for new products to maximize their use ("getting more out"), drawing on methods from Cooper et al. (2017). For each case, we identified the specific consumer and supplier within the UK MRIO's 106 sector classifications and determined the impact on transaction flows within the input-output model. The emissions linked to these transaction flows were projected to decrease over time as climate policies are implemented.
The extent of change in transaction flows is dictated by two key factors: the degree to which material or product use is reduced, denoted by 'm' and representing the ambition of a strategy, and 'c', the rate at which consumers adopt these changes. To account for the inherent uncertainties in ambition and adoption, we developed low, medium, and high scenarios for each case study. The high-end scenario assumes maximum technical feasibility for product redesign or demand reduction, often exceeding observed levels in current case studies and reaching 100% adoption. Conversely, the low-end scenario reflects proven potential with adoption rates around 33%. Our medium estimate, representing best-case projections, assumes a 66% adoption rate. This approach, akin to the technical penetration and implementation variables used by Wood et al. (2018b), provides a framework for understanding the potential range of mitigation outcomes given uncertainties in strategy ambition and deployment. It also allows us to explore potential benefits beyond those documented in existing case studies, acknowledging that a low-carbon transition necessitates substantial transformations in material and product production and consumption.

This approach assumes incremental implementation of material productivity strategies, reaching full adoption by 2032. In contrast, climate policies were implemented at five-year intervals, aligning with the temporal detail available in official government emissions projections.
To calculate the combined emissions savings from "putting less in" and "getting more out" material productivity strategies, all identified measures were integrated into a single calculation. This approach, utilizing the standard Input-Output (IO) calculation, accounted for varying levels of material/product use and implementation rates while preventing double-counting, as detailed in the supporting information. However, the study acknowledges limitations due to a lack of transferable case study evidence for certain material productivity options. Strategies such as extending the lifespan of buildings and packaging could not be modeled. Therefore, this research offers an estimate based on available case study data within the established modeling framework, rather than a complete inventory or an upper limit of potential reductions.
The rebound effect, where cost savings from reduced demand are reinvested in further consumption, was excluded from this analysis. This decision was made due to the anticipated changes in pricing structures resulting from demand reduction strategies, which would introduce further uncertainty. By reallocating saved expenditure on intermediate inputs to value added, the emissions intensities of industries were kept constant. This approach isolates the emissions impact of altered production recipes, thereby excluding the consideration of additional rebounds.

This existing target, however, carries a 50% chance of exceeding 2°C and is not reconciled with a global 2°C cumulative budget. This comparison allows us to assess the UK's progress towards these critical climate goals.

The results of our scenario modelling are presented in five distinct sections. Firstly, we detail the economy-wide emissions savings projected across the four carbon budget periods from 2013 to 2032. This analysis clarifies whether integrating material productivity strategies with current and planned climate policies can help the UK achieve its overall carbon budgets. Secondly, we compare the cumulative emissions savings derived from economy-wide material productivity strategies against those from existing and planned climate policies over the same four budget periods. This comparison aims to ascertain if the magnitude of reductions from material productivity is comparable to that of climate policies. Thirdly, we examine the emissions savings generated by combined "using less" and "getting more" material productivity strategies specifically for the 4th and 5th carbon budgets (2023-2032). This is contrasted with the savings from planned climate policies (excluding those from existing measures) to assess if these combined material productivity approaches can yield greater carbon reductions than planned climate policies alone. Fourthly, we break down emissions savings by sector for the year 2032, presenting both absolute savings and savings as a proportion of each sector's total emissions. This highlights sectors with the greatest absolute mitigation potential and also identifies those with relatively low emissions but significant proportional savings through material productivity. Finally, we quantify the percentage of carbon budgets associated with different 2050 climate targets that will be expended by 2032. By assuming emissions remain constant at 2032 levels, we project the remaining time until these budgets are exhausted, indicating the future emissions reductions needed based on the ambition of longer-term targets.

Bridging the mitigation gap necessitates changes in both product design and consumption patterns, influenced by consumer preferences, business practices, and policy frameworks. For decades, planned obsolescence has been a strategy for businesses to reduce production costs and boost sales. Currently, the low cost of products hinders the development and adoption of longer-lasting goods. Although consumers often prefer durable items, production systems that incorporate planned and perceived obsolescence must be addressed. Designers may lack the skills for creating lighter or more durable products. Furthermore, a societal shift towards product sharing models requires public acceptance of access-based costs, despite consumer concerns regarding the risks and responsibilities associated with contractual agreements.

This study aimed to quantify how material productivity strategies could help close the UK's territorial emissions gap and contribute to international climate goals. Utilizing an input-output (IO) framework allowed for economy-wide savings calculations, offering a broader perspective than product-specific analyses. Econometric analysis projected economic and emissions data within the IO model, enabling an assessment of how material productivity measures interact with future climate policies and their cumulative targets. Furthermore, case studies, incorporating varying levels of ambition and adoption, simulated material productivity improvements. This approach introduced behavioral realism, explored uncertainties, and addressed limitations in rational choice theory assumptions.
This study assessed the capacity of material productivity strategies to contribute to the UK's climate objectives. Specifically, we evaluated their potential to address the anticipated deficits in the UK's fourth and fifth carbon budgets (2022-2032), which current policies are projected to miss. Furthermore, we examined the extent to which these strategies would deplete the carbon budgets required for achieving transformative change aligned with 2°C and 1.5°C temperature targets by 2032. Our findings indicate that the emissions reductions achieved through material productivity measures are comparable to those from the UK government's existing and planned climate policies. These measures could significantly reduce the deficits in the fourth and fifth carbon budgets, by up to 73% and 47% respectively. The analysis also highlights that more ambitious future climate goals will lead to earlier exhaustion of remaining carbon budgets. Without additional interventions beyond those modeled, the UK's budget for an 80% reduction target could be depleted in just 10 years, a timeframe that shortens to 6 years under a 1.5°C scenario. Consequently, this research underscores the importance of integrating material productivity into climate policy considerations.
