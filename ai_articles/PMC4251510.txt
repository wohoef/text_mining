Industrial ecology has largely overlooked national material stock (MS) accounts, likely due to the challenges in their creation. This study introduces a new approach to model national MS, leveraging historical material flow data. This method bypasses the extensive data collection typically required for bottom-up stock accounts, yielding credible estimates of national stock accumulation. To demonstrate its efficacy, the method is applied to the United States and Japan, representing distinct industrial development trajectories. Analyzing the period from 1930 to 2005, the research reveals that the United States consistently maintained a higher per capita MS. However, Japan, reflecting its later industrialization, exhibited significantly faster growth rates in MS throughout this time. By 2005, despite these differing paths, both nations achieved remarkably similar levels of national MS, with Japan at 310 tonnes per capita and the United States at 375 tonnes per capita. This investigation offers fresh perspectives on the interplay between MS and material flows within national economies, broadening the discussion on material efficiency beyond mere throughput to encompass the crucial role of stocks.

For the past twenty years, industrial ecology (IE) research has primarily concentrated on understanding the "metabolic performance" of nations through the lens of material flows. These flows, representing the "throughput" of materials, are crucial for sustaining production and consumption processes, and this focus has led to a comprehensive body of knowledge. However, this emphasis has inadvertently sidelined another critical aspect of societal materiality: material stocks (MS). Despite this, the significance of material stocks is undeniable. The quantity of buildings, transportation networks, industrial facilities, and household goods within a society directly correlates with its productivity and overall material standard of living. Material stocks and flows are intrinsically linked; stocks deliver essential services and are continuously built, supplied, and maintained by material flows. The volume, age, and expansion rate of these stocks dictate the demand for both material inputs and outputs. Within IE studies, defining the system and its boundaries is fundamental, and material stocks are key to this definition, simplifying the identification of relevant inflows and outflows. Yet, material flows have remained the primary research focus for several reasons. They offer insights into natural resource extraction rates and resource depletion concerns, and national-level flow data is readily available due to existing economic statistics. Conversely, the policy community's understanding of the importance of measuring material stocks is less developed. Furthermore, quantifying stocks is inherently more challenging, often necessitating time-consuming and data-intensive bottom-up methodologies.
National material stock accounts, when they exist, are generally found within broader statistical publications not primarily concerned with social metabolism. Examples include housing statistics (United Nations Economic Commission for Europe 2004), social capital reports (Cabinet Office of Japan 2007), and road and rail data (OECD 2012). While often thorough, these studies suffer from limited scope and inconsistent measurement units (e.g., dwelling units, monetary value, kilometers), making them difficult to use in industrial ecology without extensive data manipulation. Research has also explored building stock lifespans, such as housing in New Zealand (Johnstone 1994) and buildings in Japan (Komatsu 2008).
National material stock accounts are uncommon in industrial ecology research, which is understandable given their complexity. A notable exception is the World Resources Institute's 2000 study by Matthews et al., which used a thorough material balance method to quantify net additions to material stocks by comparing inputs and outputs. This research offered data for five nations between 1975 and 1997, though it did not specify total stock levels. Similar methodologies have been applied elsewhere, such as in a 2009 study by Japan's Ministry of the Environment, which observed a decline in net additions to stock in Japan from 2001 to 2006.
Instead of comprehensive national accounts, material stock (MS) research has typically concentrated on more confined areas. Studies have either focused on individual materials, such as steel, iron, copper, or metals broadly, and more recently carbon. Alternatively, the scope has been geographically restricted to urban areas. This narrow focus is largely due to the demanding analytical requirements of the bottom-up methods employed in these studies. Furthermore, investigations have explored the interplay between material flows and stocks. For instance, Hashimoto and colleagues (2007) documented Japanese stocks from 1975 to 2030, though their analysis was limited to construction minerals. Similarly, Tanikawa and Imura (2000) detailed material stocks in specific Japanese infrastructure types during the latter half of the 20th century, a study later updated to 2005 by Nagaoka and colleagues (2009). Lichtensteiger and Baccini (2008) also examined Switzerland's building MS throughout the 20th century.
This research addresses a gap in existing literature by introducing a top-down method for measuring national material stocks (MS), applied to Japan and the United States. The study's objectives include developing national MS accounts from historical material flow data, examining economic efficiency differences between material throughput and MS, and modeling future MS accumulation using hypothetical scenarios. A key aim is to determine if national MS reaches a saturation point as economies mature.
The international discussion on sustainable resource management significantly benefits from understanding if national material stocks (MS) reach a saturation point. Should MS growth stabilize with economic maturity, material flows would then sustain existing stock levels, curtailing excessive resource consumption. This concept is particularly pertinent for nations like China, Brazil, and India, which currently account for a major share of global material usage.
This study selected Japan and the United States for analysis. While both are highly developed nations with powerful economies, their distinct historical trajectories, societal structures, economic systems, and geographical features provide a compelling basis for comparing material consumption and buildup.
For this study, the geographical scope aligns with national economies, following Eurostat (2001) standards. Materials entering the system are categorized as inputs, originating either from domestic natural resources or through imports from other countries. Conversely, outputs from the system include materials discharged back into the environment as waste or emissions, or those exported internationally. The research uses a yearly timeframe, consistent with its data sources. Within this temporal framework, materials that enter and exit the economy within a single year are classified as "throughput." Any material remaining within the system for more than one year is designated as "stocked material."
For this study, material inflow data spanning extended periods were utilized: Krausmann and colleagues (2011) provided data for Japan from 1878 to 2005, while Gierlinger and Krausmann (2012) supplied data for the United States from 1870 to 2005. These datasets adhere to the established material flow accounting standards and definitions outlined by Eurostat (2009). Each dataset comprises annual material flow statistics, categorizing approximately 60 material groups into imports, exports, and domestic extraction. Crucially, indirect flows linked to imports and exports, as well as unused domestic extraction, are excluded from these statistics. This exclusion is deliberate, as these materials do not enter the core economy and therefore cannot contribute to its material stock (MS). The direct material consumption (DMC) for a given material in a specific year within an economy is calculated using Equation 1, as defined by Eurostat (2001). This equation states that DMC for material *i* in year *t* is the sum of domestic extraction of material *i* in year *t* and imports of material *i* in year *t*, minus the exports of material *i* in year *t*. This methodology ensures a consistent and standardized approach to quantifying material consumption within each national economy.
The primary material inflows for this research were construction materials, which constitute the bulk of a nation's material stock (MS). These materials, unlike fossil fuels or biomass that are quickly consumed, persist in societal infrastructure for extended periods. Their long-term presence distinguishes them from other material categories and highlights their significance in national MS.
For the stock model, construction-related materials were categorized into four groups: timber, iron, other metals, and minerals. The "other metals" category encompasses construction metals beyond iron, such as copper, aluminum, and tin. Nonmetallic substances like stone, sand, limestone, gravel, and clay constitute the "minerals" category. The proportion of these materials from Direct Material Consumption (DMC) that actually becomes part of the stock, known as the stocking rate, varies for each material group. This variability arises because DMC values represent a mix of raw, processed, semi-manufactured, and final products. Additionally, the percentage of material allocated to construction versus other consumption types differs across material categories. It's important to note that certain materials used in construction, like scaffolding, are either reused or become demolition waste on-site and are therefore not incorporated into completed structures or counted towards the overall stock. The quantity of input material that ultimately contributes to the stock, termed Gross Addition to Stock (GAS), is determined using specific formulas. For each material 'i' in a given year 't', the GAS is calculated by multiplying its DMC by its stocking rate (ri), which is the aggregated percentage of that material stocked as constructed structures in the anthroposphere (Equation 2a). The total GAS for year 't' is then the sum of the GAS for all individual materials (Equation 2b).
Data on the duration construction materials remain within the built environment are scarce, with no existing lifespan data specifically for these materials as stocks. Therefore, this study adapted and generalized lifespan probabilities for buildings and infrastructure in Japan (Komatsu 2008; Cabinet Office of Japan 2007). This adaptation assumes that the lifespan of construction materials mirrors that of the structures they form. The rate at which materials stocked in a particular year depreciate over time is modeled using the s-shaped cumulative distribution function of a normal distribution.
In this study, certain generalizations and simplifications were necessary. Due to the lack of dependable long-term data, recycling processes were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, the rates at which materials are stocked and depreciate in construction were assumed to be constant throughout the study period. It's also presumed that buildings in the United States generally have a longer lifespan than those in Japan, largely because Japanese housing, particularly single-family homes, has historically been of lower quality and more susceptible to rapid material degradation. To ensure the credibility of our findings, we cross-referenced our results with existing research. A sensitivity analysis further confirmed that variations in these assumptions have only a minor impact on the overall patterns of material stock accumulation over time, and do not alter the scale of the results. This study develops a top-down method for calculating national material stock (MS) accounts, utilizing existing long-term material flow data. The model, visually represented in Figure 1, tracks four distinct material categories throughout their lifecycle of input, accumulation, and eventual output. Each year's material input is treated as a new "layer" added to the accumulated stock from previous years. As structures age, they are eventually demolished, and the material quantity within each layer diminishes. This aging process is quantified using previously defined survival functions, applied in yearly calculations. To determine the total material stock in any given year (t), the model sums the material that remains (i.e., has not yet been demolished) from all Gross Additions to Stock (GAS) layers from prior years. This calculation is performed using the following formulas: Equation 3a: `MS_i(t) = Σ_{τ=t_0}^{t} [GAS_i(τ) × (1 - Φ((t-τ)-μ_i)/σ_i))]` Equation 3b: `MS(t) = Σ_i MS_i(t)` In these equations, `MS_i(t)` represents the stock of material `i` in year `t`. The variable `t0` denotes the initial year for which Direct Material Consumption (DMC) data is available (1878 for Japan and 1870 for the United States). The variable `τ` spans the years from `t0` up to the current year `t`, also serving as an index for the yearly layers of previously stocked material. `Φ` is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, which indicates the percentage of material `i` still remaining in stock in year `t` after `(t-τ)` years have elapsed. `μ_i` signifies the mean lifespan for material `i`, and `σ_i` is its standard deviation (values for `μ_i` and `σ_i` are provided in Table 1). Finally, `MS(t)` represents the total material stock in year `t`. Since historical MS data are unavailable for periods predating the material flow statistics (before 1870 for the United States and 1878 for Japan), the initial years of the data series up to 1929 are used as a buffer for stock accumulation. Our calculations indicate that material stocked before this buffer period contributes a negligible 0.1% to the total stock in 1930, making 1930 a suitable base year for this study.
Net additions to stock (NAS) are defined by Eurostat (2001) as the difference between gross additions to stock (GAS) and removals from stock (RS). Essentially, NAS represents the year-over-year change in total material stock (MS). By rearranging the Eurostat formula, the amount of material removed in any given year can be determined. This relationship is expressed in equations 6 and 7. Specifically, RSi(t) denotes the removal from stock for material 'i' in year 't', NASi(t) is the net addition to stock for material 'i' in year 't', and RS(t) signifies the total material removed from stock in year 't'.
To complement the historical data, which concludes in 2005 for both Japan and the United States, the model was extended to 2050 using material flow projections. These projections serve to investigate the model's behavior and illustrate various future material use and accumulation scenarios, particularly regarding potential material saturation. It's important to view these as "what if" scenarios for proactive exploration, rather than definitive forecasts or statistical predictions.
Future material stock accumulation was explored through two scenarios. These scenarios involve varying Gross Additions to Stock (GAS) rates for 2006-2050, while the core model and historical rates remain consistent. Essentially, these scenarios modify the GAS calculation (equation 2), but equations 3 and 4 for stock calculation are unchanged.
Scenario 1 projects future material stock accumulation by maintaining a constant material input. For the 45-year period from 2006 to 2050, the gross addition to stock (GAS) for each material is set to the average GAS observed during the last decade of the historical data (1996-2005). This steady rate is calculated using Equation 8, where GAS1i represents the amount of GAS for material 'i' in a given year within this projection period.
The second scenario links changes in Gross Additions to Stock (GAS) to projected population growth. This is achieved using Equation 9, where the GAS for a specific material in a given year (between 2006 and 2050) is calculated by multiplying the GAS of that material in 2005 by the ratio of the estimated population in the current year to the population in 2005. This approach allows for modeling future material accumulation based on demographic shifts.
To calculate per capita material stock (MS) and material efficiencies, population and GDP data were utilized. Maddison (2010) provided these statistics for both Japan and the United States up to 2005. For future projections, population data for Japan from 2006 to 2050 came from the United Nations (2010), while the US Census Bureau (2008) supplied similar data for the United States.
Our model's findings reveal a significant increase in Japan's total construction material stock (MS) over a 75-year span, from 1930 to 2005. Starting at roughly 920 million tonnes in 1930, it expanded forty-fold to reach 38.7 billion tonnes by 2005 (Figure 2a). Initially, growth was gradual, with MS doubling during the prewar period extending into the 1950s. It's important to note that the model's design prevents the observation of specific MS losses, such as the extensive destruction of Japanese infrastructure during World War II. The post-war era, however, saw a dramatic surge in MS, fueled by Japan's rapid economic expansion, modernization, and urbanization. By the early 1970s, Gross Additions to Stock (GAS) had stabilized at around 1 billion tonnes annually, a rate maintained until the close of the 20th century. More recently, a combination of fluctuating inflows and rising outflows of previously stocked materials (Figure 2b) has led to a noticeable deceleration in MS growth. Looking ahead, both future scenarios predict a continued slowdown in MS growth. In Scenario 1, which assumes a consistent inflow of construction materials similar to the average of the last decade, total stocks are projected to keep rising, albeit at progressively slower rates, eventually reaching 50 billion tonnes by 2050. This suggests an S-shaped growth curve for MS, potentially indicating a future saturation point for total stocks. Scenario 2, however, presents a different trajectory, linking GAS to Japan's anticipated significant population decline (United Nations 2010). This reduction in population is expected to lower GAS to such an extent that it will fall below Removals from Stock (RS) by 2031, leading not just to MS saturation but also to dematerialization. Consequently, MS is predicted to peak at 45.9 billion tonnes in 2031 before subsequently decreasing to 43 billion tonnes by 2050, which is approximately 15% less than the MS projected in Scenario 1 for the same year. Notably, the trend for RS consistently increases, approaching 1 billion tonnes annually by 2050, and remains largely unaffected by the assumptions of either scenario, as most removed material originates from historical stock additions.
Japan's material stock (MS) categories have shown varied developmental paths, reflecting shifts in construction materials and techniques throughout the 20th century. Since the 1960s, coinciding with Japan's economic expansion, stocks of iron, other metals, and minerals have all experienced rapid growth. Iron stocks, for instance, demonstrated the most significant relative increase among the four categories, reaching 791 million tonnes in 2005, a 109-fold rise from their 1930 levels. Construction minerals also saw substantial growth, expanding 66 times from 703 million tonnes in 1930 to 36 billion tonnes by 2005. In contrast, other metal stocks grew at a more modest rate, increasing approximately 12-fold from nearly 8 million tonnes in 1930 to over 92 million tonnes in 2005.
While construction minerals, iron, and steel stocks continue to expand, their growth has slowed. Timber stocks peaked around 1995 at 1.3 billion tonnes and are now declining, while other metal stocks are projected to saturate within the next two decades.
Timber stocks are projected to decline further in both scenarios. Other metals are expected to peak by the 2020s. Minerals and iron stocks would stabilize in scenario one, but peak and then decrease in scenario two.
Minerals consistently formed the largest portion of Japan's material stock (MS) throughout the study, increasing their contribution from 76% in 1930 to 95% by 2005, with a projected rise to 96% by 2030. Conversely, timber, which constituted 22% of Japan's MS in 1930, saw a significant decrease to about 3% by 2005. Notably, timber's share temporarily rebounded to 18% immediately after the war, but has steadily fallen since, anticipated to reach 2% by 2050. Other metals, including copper and aluminum, always represented a minor fraction of the total stock. Iron's share grew from 1% to 2% by 1964 and has stayed at that level. These proportions are expected to largely hold constant across both future scenarios until 2050.
Throughout the study period, the United States maintained a higher level of construction material stock compared to Japan, a reflection of its larger economy, population, and greater wealth. Starting at 11 billion tonnes in 1930, total stocks in the U.S. expanded ninefold to reach 107.5 billion tonnes by 2005. This growth was marked by consistent annual additions to stock, even with some variability during the 1970s. While removals from stock also saw a gradual increase, they remained lower than the inflows, ensuring a continuous rise in U.S. material stock up to 2005. Looking ahead, our model's first scenario predicts a deceleration in material stock accumulation, with total stocks reaching 155 billion tonnes. Conversely, the second scenario indicates an uninterrupted increase in stocks, driven by projected population growth in the U.S. until 2050—a trend that contrasts with the Japanese projections. In both future scenarios for the U.S., removals from stock are estimated to approach 3 billion tonnes annually by 2050.

In the United States, construction mineral stock experienced a 15-fold increase, rising from 6.5 billion tonnes in 1930 to 96 billion tonnes in 2005. This growth was moderate until the early 1950s, followed by a period of sustained rapid expansion. Timber stock also grew, but at a much slower pace, nearly doubling from 4.5 billion tonnes in 1930 to 8.2 billion tonnes in 2005. Iron stock saw an increase from 366 million tonnes in 1930 to 950 million tonnes by 1979, after which its growth largely stagnated, reaching approximately 970 million tonnes in 2005. In contrast, other metals did not exhibit a similar slowdown, with their stock expanding from 346 million tonnes to 1.6 billion tonnes by 2005. Looking ahead, both timber and mineral stocks are projected to continue their growth in both future scenarios. However, the growth of iron and other metals stocks appears to be nearing its conclusion in these projections. Consistent with the situation in Japan, nonmetallic minerals constitute the primary component of the US material stock. Their proportion of the total stock grew from 55% in 1930 to 90% in 2005. The shift from timber to concrete as a dominant construction material led to timber's share declining from 39% in 1930 to under 10% by the 1980s. By the late 1960s, construction minerals had fully superseded timber, and the overall composition of the stock has remained relatively stable since then. Despite their absolute growth, both iron and other metal stocks in the United States, similar to Japan, maintained a small and diminishing share of the total stock, decreasing from 3% to 1% each.
By 2005, material stocks (MS) in both Japan and the United States were still growing, primarily due to the accumulation of minerals in buildings and infrastructure, indicating no saturation. While future projections for Japan suggest potential saturation and even dematerialization of total stocks, the United States, though not showing full dematerialization in any scenario, does exhibit a slowdown in MS growth under scenario 1, hinting at a possible future stabilization of its stocks.
Earlier studies have investigated Japan's material stock (MS) growth, primarily focusing on construction-related materials. Hashimoto et al. (2007) utilized a bottom-up approach to estimate MS from 1976 to 2005, projecting trends until 2030. Their method involved calculating material stocking rates per unit of floor space for various building types and material intensities for different infrastructure. This research specifically considered materials like asphalt, cement, sand, gravel, and crushed stone, which align with the "construction minerals" category in the current study. Separately, Nagaoka and colleagues (2009) examined material intensities per unit of construction, concentrating on materials within buildings, roads, and sewerage systems between 1945 and 2005.
Given the narrower focus of prior research—Hashimoto et al. (2007) examining fewer materials and Nagaoka et al. (2009) fewer construction types—our findings were anticipated to show higher material stock estimates.
When comparing our material stock (MS) estimates for Japan with prior research, a year-by-year analysis (Figure 6) reveals notable differences. Nagaoka et al. (2009) reported MS figures that were, on average, only 40% of our estimates. This discrepancy is attributed to their exclusion of highly material-intensive infrastructure projects, including harbors, airports, agricultural structures, and landslide/flood control systems. Conversely, the aggregate data from Hashimoto et al. (2007) closely align with our findings, though their estimates were about 20% lower, primarily due to a narrower range of material categories considered. Despite these variations in absolute values, the consistent year-to-year differences across all three studies indicate highly similar underlying growth trends in Japan's MS.
While direct comparisons for U.S. material stock (MS) studies are unavailable, Figure 7 illustrates a strong alignment between our research and the findings of Matthews et al. (2000) regarding net additions to stock (NAS) for both the United States and Japan. This close correspondence holds despite the differing methodologies employed. Matthews et al.'s study included non-construction materials, which, though minor contributors to overall stock additions, account for their slightly higher results compared to our present study. Our analytical method's validity is reinforced by its strong agreement with prior national material stock studies, both in overall levels and historical trends.
To assess the model's robustness, a sensitivity analysis was performed, examining the impact of varying stocking rates and lifespan assumptions. Given the model-based nature of the stock accounts, this analysis was relatively simple. Changes to stocking rates directly and proportionally alter the calculated stock accounts. Conversely, lifespan variables exert only a minor influence on the total stock. For instance, a 10% alteration in the average lifespan typically leads to just a 2% change in the stock account. This limited impact stems from the cumulative effect of numerous stock layers, which tends to mitigate the influence of individual lifespan variations. Unlike bottom-up methods, this modeling approach facilitates the examination of hypothetical future stock trends, material input needs, and waste generation. Such insights could be crucial for policy decisions, particularly if economic strategies prioritize enhancing the longevity of buildings and infrastructure to transition from a material throughput-centric economy to one focused on existing stocks and their derived services.
An examination of per capita material stock (MS) in Japan and the United States reveals distinct historical trajectories and future projections. In 1930, the United States exhibited a significantly higher per capita MS, approximately 100 tonnes, a reflection of its advanced industrialization and economic prosperity. Japan, in contrast, possessed only about one-fifth of the US's per capita MS at that time. Over the subsequent decades, Japan experienced a far more rapid expansion in its MS, achieving a 20-fold increase, while the United States saw a fourfold growth. Despite Japan's accelerated development, the US maintained a lead in per capita MS by 2005, with 375 tonnes per person compared to Japan's 310 tonnes. This suggests that, irrespective of differing historical and socioeconomic contexts, developed economies tend to converge towards a comparable level of per capita MS. This observation is supported by Rubli and Jungbluth's (2005) finding of 311 tonnes per capita for Switzerland. Looking ahead, future scenarios indicate divergent trends, largely influenced by population dynamics—Japan's population is projected to decline, while the US population is expected to grow. Under Scenario 1, Japan's per capita MS is projected to reach 467 tonnes by 2050, surpassing the United States' per capita MS by 2023. The US, in this scenario, would see its per person stocks peak at 392 tonnes per capita before declining to 368 tonnes per capita.
Scenario 2 presents an alternative outcome where the U.S. maintains a higher per capita material stock than Japan. Under this scenario, U.S. stocks are projected to reach 476 tonnes per person by 2050, while Japan's per person stocks are expected to stabilize around 395 tonnes by 2042.
When the amount of material stock per person stabilizes or declines, it indicates per capita material saturation. For the United States, policies aligning with scenario 1 could lead to a reduction in per capita material use. However, the same scenario in Japan would see per capita material levels continue to rise, even as overall stock approaches saturation. This highlights that identical policy approaches may yield different outcomes in various countries.
Effective resource management at a national level is often gauged by material efficiency, defined as the economic output generated per unit of material consumed. Prior research, such as that by Schandl and West (2010) and Steinberger and Krausmann (2011), indicates that as economies develop, their material efficiency typically improves. This improvement is often attributed to a greater reliance on minerals and fossil fuels, a reduced use of biomass, and varying income elasticities across these material types. However, these earlier studies primarily focused on material throughput. The current model, in contrast, enables a long-term assessment that includes stock efficiency. Given that material stocks deliver societal services, optimizing the use of existing stocks offers advantages for both the economy and the environment. It's crucial to recognize that material flow efficiency reflects annual flows and economic activity, while material stock (MS) efficiency assesses the cumulative material efficiency over time. Consequently, comparing these two metrics is best done by observing their long-term trends rather than year-to-year fluctuations.
In the United States, direct material consumption (DMC), material stock (MS), and gross domestic product (GDP) all exhibited synchronized growth until approximately 1975, as illustrated in figure 9a. A decoupling of these trends began around 1974, with GDP growth rates then exceeding the growth of both MS and material flows. This enhancement in efficiency is further detailed in figure 9b, which presents the two material efficiency trends for the United States. From the end of World War II until 1974, material efficiency indicators moved in unison. However, from 1974 onward, material flow efficiency (GDP/DMC) has shown an increase, with improvements in MS efficiency (GDP/MS) following at a slower pace.
In Japan, the growth trajectories of Direct Material Consumption (DMC), Gross Domestic Product (GDP), and Material Stock (MS) largely mirrored each other from the 1930s until 1975, as depicted in Figure 10a. However, a divergence emerged after 1975, with DMC stabilizing while MS experienced significantly faster growth than GDP. This shift led to an inverse relationship between the two measures of material efficiency for the Japanese economy (GDP/DMC and GDP/MS), as illustrated in Figure 10b. It's important to acknowledge that this model omits certain historical events, such as the impact of World War II on Japanese MS, which warrant more in-depth investigation. The 1940s, in particular, show starkly different trends for the two efficiency metrics. From the early 1950s until approximately 1972, material efficiency of stock (GDP/MS) improved, while material efficiency of throughput (GDP/DMC) declined. This period was characterized by substantial material input, fueling Japan's economic expansion. A turning point in material efficiency occurred after 1972, likely influenced by rising oil prices and a general economic slowdown in Japan. Since then, flow efficiency has improved, establishing Japan as a global leader in material efficiency (Schandl and West 2012). Conversely, because MS continued to outpace GDP growth, material efficiency based on MS has either declined or stagnated. Despite a prolonged period of deinvestment in Japan since the 1990s (Randers 2012), the material stock required for economic activity has not decreased. Several factors could explain the persistent increase in MS. Firstly, Japan's susceptibility to natural disasters, such as earthquakes, necessitates periodic updates to building codes, leading to more material-intensive construction for new or replacement structures (Tanikawa and Hashimoto 2009). Secondly, the increasing affluence of Japanese society has driven demand for larger and higher-quality construction. Thirdly, government policies have consistently promoted nationwide infrastructure projects, including high-speed rail, dams, and national highways. Japan's challenging mountainous terrain requires extensive bridges, tunnels, and other material-intensive civil engineering to support these projects, contributing to the growth in stocks. A fourth possibility is the inclusion of "dissipated stock" in calculations, such as disused buildings and infrastructure that remain within the anthroposphere but no longer contribute meaningfully to the economy (Brunner 2004; Hashimoto et al. 2009). However, a lack of data on abandoned infrastructure in Japan or other countries currently prevents a definitive answer to this question.
This study introduces a new top-down methodology for calculating national material stocks (MS), drawing on historical material flow data. The method was successfully applied to the United States and Japan, demonstrating its reliability and advantages over labor-intensive, costly bottom-up accounting methods. Our analysis of the two countries' MS evolution revealed that by 2005, both had attained similar per capita MS levels: Japan at roughly 310 tonnes per person and the United States at 375 tonnes per person. This indicates that neither nation had reached material saturation by that year, as both absolute and per capita MS continued to grow. Looking ahead to 2050, two future scenarios were developed. These scenarios suggest that material saturation is a future possibility, with Japan potentially experiencing a dematerialization of its total stocks, and the United States seeing a dematerialization of per capita stocks. Even with simplified assumptions, these scenarios offer valuable insights for policymakers regarding future material demands for stock upkeep and anticipated demolition waste. However, a limitation of this input-driven approach is its inability to quantify the mass output from significant MS destruction events, such as those caused by World War II, Japan's 2011 Tsunami, or the United States' 2005 Hurricane Katrina.
Our analysis also contrasted the material efficiency of throughput with that of material stock. In the United States, both measures of efficiency have shown improvement since the 1970s, though the gains in stock efficiency were less pronounced. Conversely, Japan experienced enhanced throughput material efficiency, but this coincided with a decrease in material stock efficiency, largely due to rapid growth. This pattern in Japan is intensified because a greater proportion of material throughput contributes to stock compared to the United States, which operates on a throughput-centric metabolic model. These insights into national material stocks could be invaluable for an economic paradigm that prioritizes the utility of well-built infrastructure and aims to reduce material throughput to achieve a stable material state.
The current analysis overlooks the "geosocial" dimension of material stocks (MS). Buildings and infrastructure are inherently static, occupying physical space and reshaping landscapes. Consequently, factors like the total land area consumed by MS, its spatial arrangement, and the availability of land for new construction all influence MS accumulation patterns and trends. Each nation possesses distinct geographical characteristics, including land area, land use practices, and building densities, which contribute to unique geosocial profiles. While this study explored material saturation in absolute and per capita terms, future research could benefit from integrating a geographical perspective alongside the economic and demographic viewpoints already presented. Furthermore, a more in-depth examination of material efficiency trends in Japan and the United States is warranted. Observing the combined behavior of flow efficiency and stock efficiency in both countries reveals intriguing correlations. For instance, during economic downturns like the U.S. Great Depression (1930s) and Japan's "lost decade" (1990s), flow efficiency rose while stock efficiency declined. Further investigation is needed to determine if these and similar patterns are mere coincidences or indicative of broader material-economic dynamics.
