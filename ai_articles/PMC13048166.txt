Hepatic dysfunction is a significant long-term complication for patients who have undergone Fontan surgery. This study aimed to assess hepatic computed tomography (CT) findings in these patients and determine their correlation with clinical factors. A total of 43 patients, comprising 23 males and 20 females with an average age of 15.3 years, who had received Fontan surgery, were included. Their medical records were reviewed to gather data on age, sex, type of congenital heart disease, date of surgery, laboratory results, and hepatic CT findings. The analysis focused on the relationship between observed hepatic findings and these clinical parameters. The average follow-up period after Fontan surgery was 6.8 years. The study found that 77% of patients exhibited abnormal hepatic parenchymal enhancement on CT scans. This abnormality was categorized as mild in 18 patients, moderate in 10, and severe in 5. Univariate analysis identified several risk factors associated with abnormal hepatic parenchymal enhancement. These included the duration of follow-up (odds ratio [OR]: 1.354), hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) (OR: 3.262), mean pulmonary artery pressure (OR: 1.598), pulmonary vascular resistance index (OR: 1.263), and brain natriuretic peptide levels (OR: 1.956). However, multivariate analysis revealed that only HLHS (OR: 3.856), mean pulmonary artery pressure (OR: 1.846), and pulmonary vascular resistance index (OR: 1.185) remained significant risk factors for abnormal parenchymal enhancement. In conclusion, abnormal hepatic parenchymal enhancement, as detected by CT, is prevalent in Fontan patients. The findings suggest that regular liver function tests, combined with imaging studies, should be considered as part of the follow-up care for these individuals.

For over 40 years, Fontan surgery has improved survival for individuals with congenital single functional ventricles, including tricuspid atresia. Studies show 20-year survival rates ranging from 60% to 80%. At our institution, event-free survival rates at 1, 5, and 10 years were 90.6%, 89.3%, and 77.2%, respectively.
Despite the success of Fontan surgery, later failures remain a concern. These can stem from ventricular dysfunction, high pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), and elevated central venous pressure (CVP) with protein-losing enteropathy. The Fontan physiology, lacking a pumping ventricle and transmitting PVR systemically, causes chronic high systemic venous pressure. This can lead to serious complications like liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).
In Fontan patients, hepatic vascular congestion can result in parenchymal damage and altered blood flow. This manifests as abnormal parenchymal contrast enhancement, hypervascular nodules, fibrosis, and cirrhosis, observable through sonography, CT, or MRI. Prior research by Wallian et al. on 42 Fontan patients using CT or MRI indicated abnormal parenchymal enhancement in 38, hypervascular nodules in 13, cirrhosis in 8, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in one. Agnoletti et al.'s ultrasound study of Fontan patients revealed hepatomegaly in 36%, coarse or lobulated liver contours in 16%, and inhomogeneous parenchymal echogenicity in 50%. Bae et al. found hepatic parenchymal changes, such as heterogeneous echotexture or surface nodularity, in 67% of 55 Fontan patients via ultrasound, noting a positive correlation with the time elapsed since Fontan surgery. Additionally, Pundi et al. reported that among 195 Fontan patients assessed with liver biopsy or imaging, the rates of freedom from cirrhosis at 10, 20, and 30 years were 99%, 94%, and 57%, respectively. Consequently, liver imaging surveys are vital for Fontan patients.
This study aimed to assess hepatic CT findings post-Fontan surgery and their clinical parameter associations.
This institutional review board-approved study examined 124 patients who underwent Fontan surgery at our institution between 1995 and 2018. We collected data on age, sex, congenital heart disease type, Fontan surgery date, laboratory results, and hepatic CT scans. Seventy-nine patients were excluded due to missing hepatic CT and laboratory data, and two due to hepatitis C infection. Ultimately, 43 patients with complete liver data, and no pre-existing liver disease or viral hepatitis, were included in the study.
Two radiologists specializing in hepatic imaging reviewed the hepatic CT scans. A grading system from 0 to 3 was used to assess hepatic enhancement, with grade 0 indicating normal enhancement and grades 1 through 3 signifying abnormal enhancement. Specifically, grade 1 (mild) was defined as slight patchy enhancement appearing in a few sections. Grade 2 (moderate) involved coalescent or diffuse patchy enhancement across most sections, while grade 3 (severe) was characterized by diffuse patchy enhancement throughout all liver sections, consistent with prior definitions. The study then analyzed the correlation between these hepatic CT findings and various clinical parameters.
Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviation, while categorical variables were shown as absolute numbers and percentages. Cox regression analysis was employed to assess risk factors for abnormal parenchymal enhancement, identifying both univariate and multivariate predictors. For the multivariate analysis, significant univariate factors (p < 0.05) were selected using a stepwise method. Odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated via the Cox regression model. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the 43 Fontan circulation patients. The average age at Fontan surgery was 7.1 ± 6.3 years, with a mean follow-up period of 6.8 ± 4.1 years until CT imaging. Patients had an average oxygen saturation of 93% ± 2.5%. The majority of Fontan operations, 41 out of 43, utilized an extracardiac conduit, while two involved an atriopulmonary connection. Tricuspid atresia was the most prevalent congenital heart disease (CHD) type, affecting 15 patients (34%), followed by double-inlet single ventricle in 10 patients (23%). Regarding functional status, 21 patients were in NYHA class I, 15 in class II, and 7 in class III/IV. Over the follow-up, six patients developed protein-losing enteropathy, three were diagnosed with cirrhosis, and three succumbed to severe heart failure.
In this study of 43 Fontan patients, hepatic CT revealed abnormal parenchymal enhancement in 33 individuals (77%). Mild enhancement was seen in 18, moderate in 10, and severe in 5. Ten patients presented with normal liver images. Additionally, hepatic nodules were identified in nine patients. One 24-year-old female patient with a single ventricle and multiple liver nodules underwent a biopsy, which showed focal nodular hyperplasia and other characteristic histological findings.
Table 2 presents the hemodynamics, exercise test results, and serum laboratory data for the study participants. Key hemodynamic measurements included a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 16 ± 4.9 mmHg and an end-diastolic ventricular pressure of 3 ± 9 mmHg. The pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) index was recorded as 1.4 ± 2.9 WU × m². Exercise capacity was assessed by peak oxygen consumption (VO2), which averaged 24.9 ± 5.6 mL/kg/min. Among the serum laboratory markers, the brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level was 36.2 ± 47.2 pg/mL. Albumin levels were 4.37 ± 0.38 g/dL. Liver function tests showed aspartate aminotransferase at 33.9 ± 9.8 U/L, alanine transaminase at 28.9 ± 16.6 U/L, and total bilirubin at 0.93 ± 0.55 mg/dL. Gamma-glutamyltransferase was 51.2 ± 28.7 U/L, and platelet count was 222.1 ± 92.0 × 1000/mm³.
Table 3 presents the findings of the risk factor analysis for abnormal parenchymal enhancement. In the univariate analysis, several factors were significantly associated with abnormal hepatic parenchymal enhancement. These included a longer follow-up duration (OR 1.354, p=0.042), hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) (OR 3.262, p=0.002), elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure (OR 1.598, p=0.026), a higher pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) index (OR 1.263, p=0.032), and increased brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels (OR 1.956, p=0.045). Conversely, no significant associations were found with variables such as age at operation, specific congenital heart disease types (tricuspid atresia, pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum, double-inlet single ventricle, asplenia/heterotaxy, double-outlet right ventricle), end-diastolic ventricular pressure, peak oxygen consumption (VO2), albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine transaminase, total bilirubin, gamma-glutamyl transferase, and platelet count. The multivariate analysis refined these findings, identifying HLHS (OR 3.856, p=0.001), mean pulmonary artery pressure (OR 1.846, p=0.015), and PVR index (OR 1.185, p=0.047) as the only significant independent risk factors for abnormal parenchymal enhancement.
In this study, computed tomography (CT) revealed abnormal hepatic parenchymal enhancement in a significant proportion (77%) of Fontan patients, observed at an average follow-up of 6.8 years post-surgery. This finding aligns with previous research, though Wallihan et al. reported a higher prevalence (90%) and a greater proportion of severe cases (40%) in their CT or MRI examination. The shorter interval between Fontan surgery and imaging in our cohort (mean 6.8 years compared to Wallihan et al.'s 15.1 years) may explain the comparatively lower rate and severity of abnormal enhancement observed here. Prior ultrasound studies by Agnoletti et al. and Bae et al. also noted abnormal hepatic parenchymal echogenicity in 50% and 67% of Fontan patients, respectively. These collective findings, including our own, are consistent with the well-established risk of hepatic dysfunction and cirrhosis in long-term Fontan survivors, as highlighted by Agnoletti et al. and Pundi et al.
Fontan-associated liver disease can manifest in various ways, ranging from asymptomatic presentations to focal nodular hyperplasia-like nodules, liver cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The underlying mechanisms are thought to involve chronic passive venous congestion stemming from elevated systemic venous pressure and hypoxic damage resulting from low cardiac output. Several factors contribute to the severity of hepatic disease in Fontan patients. These include a failing Fontan circulation, elevated hepatic venous pressure, advanced age, pre-existing hepatitis, alcohol consumption, and the use of hepatotoxic drugs, in addition to the duration of the Fontan circulation. The overall quality of the Fontan circulation itself can be compromised by numerous factors. These include fenestration secondary to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), protein-losing enteropathy, high central venous pressure (CVP) exceeding 15 mm Hg, low oxygen saturation below 90%, significant collateral blood flow, markedly reduced exercise capacity with peak oxygen uptake less than 50% of normal, substantial atrioventricular valve insufficiency, ejection fraction below 50%, dilatation of the Fontan tunnel, diaphragm paralysis, a cardiac index of 2.5 L/min/m2 or less, and a morphologically right systemic ventricle. Research by Burchill et al. has identified elevated brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) as an independent predictor of Fontan failure and mortality in adults. Similarly, Pundi et al. found a strong association between hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) and an increased risk of cirrhosis in Fontan patients. Our univariate analysis corroborated previous findings, identifying follow-up duration, HLHS, elevated pulmonary artery pressure, high PVR, and elevated BNP as risk factors for abnormal parenchymal enhancement. However, multivariate analysis revealed that only HLHS, high pulmonary artery pressure, and high PVR remained significant predictors. Further prospective research into these risk factors is crucial for the early identification of Fontan patients at higher risk.
While liver biopsy remains the definitive method for diagnosing liver fibrosis and cirrhosis in Fontan patients, the development of reliable noninvasive techniques for early detection of hepatic dysfunction is essential to avoid invasive procedures. Existing noninvasive methods like CT, MRI, ultrasound, and liver elastography have proven effective in assessing liver disease in this population. In the present study, mean hepatic biochemical parameters were within normal limits, highlighting the insensitivity of common biomarkers such as aspartate aminotransferase and alanine transaminase for monitoring hepatic status in Fontan patients. Therefore, liver imaging is paramount. Given the radiation exposure risks associated with CT in pediatric patients, ultrasound is recommended as an initial screening tool. MRI offers a superior alternative to CT for further evaluation of hepatic abnormalities, mitigating radiation concerns. For individuals with a high suspicion of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), α-fetoprotein levels can supplement imaging findings. Further research is needed to establish more specialized serum tests and scoring systems for liver disease in Fontan patients.
Hepatic nodules were identified in 21% of Fontan patients in this study, aligning with previous reports of 20%-30% prevalence. Ibarrola et al. categorized these nodules as benign regenerative nodules or focal nodular hyperplasia, particularly in cases of hepatic venous outflow obstruction. One patient in this series underwent a liver biopsy confirming focal nodular hyperplasia. The precise cause of nodule formation is not fully understood, though some research suggests that elevated central venous pressure (CVP) could lead to reduced portal flow and arterilization. The potential for malignancy in these nodules remains a subject of debate. Fontan patients with hepatic nodules should be monitored for signs suggestive of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), such as increasing nodule size, portal venous phase washout, mosaic architecture, and elevated alpha-fetoprotein. Therefore, routine evaluation of these nodules for possible HCC is advised.
Given the frequent occurrence of liver complications following Fontan surgery, establishing a screening protocol and personalized strategy for these patients is crucial. While controlling PVR medically may benefit Fontan-associated liver disease and exercise capacity, its efficacy in managing pulmonary hypertension in this population remains debated. Antifibrotic therapies, targeting the aldosterone or renin-angiotensin systems, have shown potential utility. Current recommendations suggest annual or biennial serologic liver tests and hepatic ultrasounds post-Fontan surgery to facilitate early detection and management of liver dysfunction. Beyond ten years after the operation, more in-depth evaluations, including CT/MRI, catheterization, and liver biopsy, should be considered.
This study has several limitations. The relatively short follow-up duration may lead to an underestimation of liver disease prevalence and severity post-Fontan surgery. Additionally, this retrospective, cross-sectional study lacks evidence to confirm if the degree of CT abnormality independently predicts future outcomes. Furthermore, the potential radiation exposure risk from hepatic CT in pediatric patients might outweigh the benefits of detecting liver abnormalities. Finally, the study's single-center nature and limited patient cohort restrict generalizability.
Abnormal hepatic parenchymal enhancement on CT is frequent in Fontan patients. Therefore, routine liver function tests combined with imaging are recommended for follow-up.
