The European Food Safety Authority's (EFSA) Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA) was tasked with evaluating a health claim application submitted by Tate & Lyle PLC. This application, made under Article 13(5) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 and routed through the UK's Competent Authority, sought authorization for a claim linking polydextrose to the maintenance of normal defecation. The claim was based on newly developed scientific evidence. Polydextrose, the food constituent in question, was deemed sufficiently characterized by the Panel. The applicant proposed the claimed effect as "improved bowel function by increasing stool bulk," targeting the general population. The Panel acknowledged that maintaining normal defecation is a beneficial physiological outcome. However, upon reviewing the evidence, the Panel noted that out of three human intervention studies that could be used to support the claim, only one demonstrated an effect. This positive finding occurred at a dosage of 20g per day over a 10-day period. In contrast, two other studies, which administered polydextrose at doses of 21g and 18g per day for extended durations (3-4 weeks), did not show any beneficial effect on maintaining normal defecation. Furthermore, the Panel found the evidence supporting the proposed mechanisms by which polydextrose might contribute to normal defecation in humans to be weak. Consequently, the Panel concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship between polydextrose consumption and the maintenance of normal defecation could not be established.

The EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA) was requested to assess a health claim regarding polydextrose and the maintenance of normal defecation. This request followed an application from Tate & Lyle PLC for authorization under Article 13(5) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, submitted through the UK's Competent Authority. The Panel's task was to determine the scientific substantiation for this claim.
The application for a health claim, based on new scientific evidence and seeking proprietary data protection, was submitted.
The NDA Panel evaluates health claims based on EFSA's guidance for stakeholders on Article 13.5 and 14 claims. Specific guidance also exists for claims concerning the immune system, gastrointestinal tract, and defense against pathogens. This framework informs the Panel's assessment process.
Polydextrose, the food constituent for the health claim, is deemed sufficiently characterized by the Panel.
The applicant proposed a claim of "improved bowel function by increasing stool bulk" for the general population. The Panel recognized maintenance of normal defecation as a beneficial physiological effect.
Previously, the Panel issued an unfavorable opinion on a claim concerning polydextrose and bowel function under Article 13(1) due to the poor methodological quality of the submitted human intervention studies. For the current application, the applicant has supplemented this with three additional human intervention studies investigating various outcomes related to the claimed effect. Furthermore, three in vitro studies pertinent to the claim were also provided.
The Panel considered the available evidence when forming its opinion. Three human intervention studies were deemed suitable for substantiating the claim. Of these, one study indicated a positive effect of polydextrose at a daily dose of 20g over 10 days. However, two other studies, which administered polydextrose at daily doses of 21g and 18g for longer durations (3-4 weeks), did not demonstrate any effect on maintaining normal defecation. Furthermore, the Panel found the evidence supporting the proposed mechanisms for how polydextrose aids normal defecation in humans to be weak.
Based on the presented data, the Panel concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship between consuming polydextrose and maintaining normal defecation has not been established.
Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 harmonizes rules for nutrition and health claims on food products within the EU. Generally, health claims are forbidden unless they meet the Regulation's requirements, receive authorization, and are listed under Articles 13 or 14. Specifically, Article 13(5) outlines the process for adding claims to the Community list of permitted claims (Article 13(3)). This applies to claims based on new scientific evidence or those requesting proprietary data protection. These additions exclude claims related to disease risk reduction or children's development and health. The Regulation ensures that only authorized and scientifically substantiated claims are communicated to consumers.
To be listed among permitted claims under Article 13(3) of the Regulation, an application must first be submitted to a Member State's competent authority. This authority then forwards the application, along with any additional information from the applicant, to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) for evaluation.
EFSA will evaluate the submitted scientific data as per Article 16(3) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. Based on this evaluation, EFSA will provide an opinion on whether the health claim concerning polydextrose and the maintenance of normal defecation is scientifically substantiated.
This opinion does not authorize the marketing of polydextrose, nor does it assess its safety or classify it as a foodstuff. Such assessments are outside the scope of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
The scope, proposed claim wording, and usage conditions may change based on the authorization process outlined in Article 18(4) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
The Panel previously issued an unfavorable opinion on a claim concerning polydextrose and changes in bowel function.
The applicant identifies polydextrose as the food constituent central to the health claim. This substance is a randomly bonded, water-soluble, low-calorie polysaccharide that functions mainly as a bulking agent and texturizer in food products. As a highly branched dextrose polymer, it exhibits a wide molecular weight range, with most molecules falling between 504 and 5,000 mw. Polydextrose, which is not sweet, has been utilized globally for over 25 years as a low-calorie bulking agent and soluble fiber ingredient. In numerous countries, it is commonly classified as dietary fiber.
The applicant claims polydextrose improves bowel function by increasing stool bulk.
The health claim proposed by the applicant is: 'Polydextrose contributes to an improved bowel function by increasing stool bulk.'
The applicant proposed that a daily intake of 18g of polydextrose, consumed in multiple portions, would achieve the claimed effect. They suggested that foods qualifying for a 'high in fibre' nutrition claim, or similar wording, would be suitable conditions for this claim, aligning with existing approved claims. The applicant identified the general population as the target group.
Tate & Lyle PLC submitted a health claim application for polydextrose and the maintenance of normal defecation under Regulation 1924/2006, Article 13(5). The application followed the structured format detailed in the EFSA Scientific and technical guidance for the preparation and presentation of applications for authorisation of health claims.
Applicants must provide all available evidence, as detailed in the EFSA General guidance for stakeholders on evaluating health claims (EFSA NDA Panel, 2016a). This guidance covers Article 13.1, 13.5, and 14 health claims.
The NDA Panel's approach to evaluating health claim applications is detailed in the EFSA stakeholder guidance on health claims.
EFSA guidance (2016b) details scientific requirements for health claims concerning the immune system and gastrointestinal tract. The food constituent under consideration for this claim is polydextrose.
Polydextrose is manufactured through the polycondensation of glucose, utilizing sorbitol and citric acid under vacuum and high temperatures. This process results in a highly branched polymer with a degree of polymerization ranging from 2 to 110, averaging around 12 glucose units. Its average molecular weight is approximately 2,000 Da. The structure of polydextrose incorporates all possible linkages with the glycosidic carbon of glucose, with the 1,6 linkage being the most prevalent. It exhibits high solubility in water, dissolving up to 80 g per 100 g at 25°C, which contributes to its low viscosity in solution. In addition to the polymer itself, polydextrose contains minor quantities of its initial components, glucose, sorbitol, and citric acid. Caramelization during its production also yields levoglucosan and hydroxymethylfurfural. Primarily, polydextrose serves various functions in the food industry, including acting as a stabiliser, thickening agent, humectant, and carrier, and is designated as E 1200.
Polydextrose comes in granular and liquid forms. The applicant clarified that 100g contains 3.2g of sugars (mono- and disaccharides). Details on its production, stability, and batch variability were supplied. Established analytical methods exist for measuring polydextrose in food. Consequently, the Panel deems polydextrose adequately characterized.
The applicant proposed a health claim for polydextrose, stating it improves bowel function by increasing stool bulk for the general population.
EFSA clarified to the applicant that, based on the submitted application and human studies, the claimed effect pertains to maintaining normal defecation, particularly in cases of constipation. This interpretation aligns with the updated guidance concerning scientific requirements for health claims related to gut and immune function.
Assessing the maintenance of normal defecation involves various interrelated outcome variables that shed light on its function and potential mechanisms. These include factors like defecation frequency, stool consistency, the feeling of complete evacuation, faecal bulk, and transit time. While reduced stool frequency and consistency are indicative of functional constipation, the role of transit time and faecal bulk in maintaining normal defecation within this context is not always clear. Nevertheless, the Panel acknowledges that maintaining normal defecation is a beneficial physiological effect.
To support their health claim application, the applicant conducted a literature search. They used databases including PubMed, CAB abstracts, Sci Finder, and Science Direct. Their search terms focused on "polydextrose" combined with terms related to the gut, bowel, and feces, specifically for adult humans. Only papers written in English or French were considered.
Previously, the Panel issued an unfavorable opinion on a health claim regarding polydextrose and changes in bowel function under Article 13(1). This decision was based on the methodological weaknesses identified in the human intervention studies presented at that time, including those by Tomlin and Read (1988), Endo et al. (1991), Achour et al. (1994), and Jie et al. (2000). For the current application, the applicant has submitted three additional human intervention studies that examined various outcomes related to the claimed effect. These are complemented by three in vitro studies that are considered relevant to the claim.
In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study, Vester Boler et al. (2011) investigated the gastrointestinal effects of polydextrose. Twenty-five healthy young men, with an average age of 27.5 years, participated in the study. Participants consumed snacks containing either 21g of polydextrose, 21g of soluble corn fibre, or no added fibre (control) daily for three consecutive 21-day periods, with no wash-out between them. These snacks, made from rice crisps, each provided approximately 7g of the respective fibre source. The study measured various gastrointestinal outcomes, including stool weight (both wet and dry), frequency of defecation, stool consistency, and the perceived ease of passing stool. Other measured variables included faecal composition and chemistry, as well as symptoms of gastrointestinal discomfort. Faeces were collected during the final five days of each intervention period. While 21 subjects completed the study and were included in the data analysis, three did not begin the study, and one was removed by the researchers according to predefined criteria. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS mixed models, accounting for treatment as a fixed effect and period and subject as random effects, with total dietary fibre as a covariate. Treatment differences were assessed using a Fisher-protected least significant difference with a Tukey adjustment.
During the study, the background diet's macronutrient composition remained consistent across all periods. Participants' average daily fiber intake, excluding supplemental fibers, ranged from 14.4 to 14.8 grams and showed no significant variation between periods. While polydextrose consumption led to a statistically significant increase in faecal dry weight compared to the placebo (184.8 g vs. 155.9 g, SEM = 11.81, p = 0.02), no significant differences were observed for wet stool weight, defecation frequency, stool consistency, or the perceived ease of stool passage. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this study did not demonstrate an effect of 21 g/day of polydextrose over three weeks on maintaining normal defecation.
Timm and colleagues conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study with a design similar to previous research to examine the impact of polydextrose (20 g/day), soluble corn fiber (20 g/day), and a low-fiber control (no added fiber) on various gastrointestinal outcomes. This study involved three 10-day intervention periods, separated by 2-week wash-out intervals. A total of 36 participants, comprising 18 females with a mean age of 25.8 ± 9.1 years, were selected based on their adherence to low-fiber diets (less than 15 g/day) which they were instructed to maintain throughout the study. During each intervention period, participants consumed a daily portion consisting of a cereal packet and a muffin, each containing either polydextrose, soluble corn fiber, or no added fiber. The order of these three crossover intervention periods was randomized. Throughout each period, participants maintained food records for three days. They meticulously documented the time and date of each bowel movement and assessed stool consistency using the Bristol stool scale. On the sixth day of each period, participants ingested 20 radio-opaque marker (ROM) pellets and collected their feces for the subsequent five days. A 5-day bowel movement diary was also maintained, recording the time and date of each defecation, and all fecal samples were collected. Whole gut transit time (WGTT) was determined by X-raying fecal samples to track the passage of the ROM pellets, with the 80% transit time method employed for calculation. Gastrointestinal comfort was evaluated through self-reported questionnaires. All participants successfully completed the study and their data were included in the analysis. Statistical comparisons between treatments were performed using mixed-effects linear models, incorporating treatment and visit as fixed effects and a random intercept for each participant to account for the correlation between repeated measurements. The models also checked for equal carry-over and treatment-visit interactions for each outcome, with differences in least square means used to identify treatment disparities.
In the polydextrose intervention period, participants experienced a significantly higher frequency of defecations and a greater wet stool weight when compared to the low-fibre control group. Specifically, defecation frequency was 5.5 ± 2.3 per 5 days versus 4.4 ± 2.1 (p=0.0005), and wet stool weight averaged 830 ± 443 g compared to 623 ± 342 g (p=0.0007). Stool consistency was also observed to be softer during the polydextrose phase (4.67 vs 3.86, p=0.002). Whole gut transit time, however, showed no significant difference between the periods. The Panel acknowledged the study's short 10-day intervention duration. Despite this, the products were well-tolerated, with no reported differences in bowel habit quality of life. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this short-term study indicated an effect of 20 g/day of polydextrose on maintaining normal defecation over a 10-day period.
A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study, conducted by Sarkkinen et al. (2014) and claimed as proprietary by the applicant, examined the impact of 18 grams of polydextrose daily on individuals experiencing mild constipation, characterized by 3 to 5 bowel movements per week. The study design incorporated four distinct phases: an initial 2-week run-in period, followed by two 4-week intervention periods, separated by a substantial 6- to 8-week wash-out period. During the intervention phases, participants consumed a daily portion consisting of two sachets of a drink mixture and three biscuits. These products either contained 18 grams of polydextrose or were identical but without polydextrose, serving as the placebo. The order of these two crossover intervention periods was randomized. Participant adherence to the study protocol was monitored, with a compliance rate of over 80% in product consumption set as the benchmark. Fecal samples were collected by participants at home over four consecutive days within each intervention period, specifically between the 21st and 24th day. This collection preceded the measurement of total colonic transit time (CTT). CTT was determined using a radio-opaque marker (ROM) technique, where participants ingested two capsules, each containing 10 pellets, at 24-hour intervals for three consecutive days. A single abdominal radiograph, taken in a supine position, was used to track the passage of these markers 24 hours after the final pellet ingestion. In addition to these objective measures, participants self-assessed stool consistency using the Bristol Stool Form scale throughout the study, including the run-in period, both intervention periods, and the wash-out period, over seven consecutive days. They also reported on the ease of stool passage at the conclusion of each intervention period. The primary objective of the study was to measure faecal wet weight, which was instrumental in calculating the required sample size. The study initially aimed for a sample size of 50 participants to detect a 20-gram difference in mean daily stool bulk between treatment groups with 80% power at a 0.05 significance level. To ensure at least 50 participants completed the study, a total of 73 individuals were randomized. Secondary outcomes assessed included the frequency of bowel movements, stool consistency, CTT, and subjective evaluation of gastrointestinal (GI) tolerance. The study employed both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses. The ITT analysis encompassed all randomized subjects who completed the study, while the PP analysis included only those who adhered strictly to the study protocol without significant deviations that could impact efficacy. The Panel noted that the analysis focused on completers rather than a strict ITT approach. For the primary outcome, crossover analyses were conducted using a general linear model for repeated measures. However, a significant and unexplained carry-over effect was observed during the analysis of the primary outcome, leading to the exclusion of data from the second treatment period. Consequently, data from the first period was analyzed as a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled parallel study. Statistical comparisons between the study groups were performed using the Student's t-test and the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Post-hoc calculations indicated that with a sample size of 26 completers per group and an estimated standard deviation of 50 grams for stool bulk, the study would have 80% power to detect a 38.5-gram difference in mean daily stool bulk between groups at a 0.05 significance level.
The study saw 10 participants drop out and another 10 withdrawn. One subject was removed from the completers analysis (n=52) due to suspected gastrointestinal illness. Additionally, one participant who did not adhere to the study product consumption plan was excluded from the per-protocol analyses (n=51).
In the Sarkkinen et al. (2014) study, participants consuming 18g of polydextrose daily for 28 days showed a significant increase in mean faecal wet weight over a 4-day period compared to the placebo group (406.7 ± 210.1 g vs. 286.0 ± 167.0 g, p=0.047, Wilcoxon). Similarly, mean faecal dry weight was significantly higher in the polydextrose group (110.4 ± 47.1 g vs. 84.7 ± 46.4 g, p=0.045, Wilcoxon). However, when analyzing only the completers, the increased wet faecal weight in the polydextrose group (406.7 ± 210.1 g vs. 296.7 ± 172.5 g) was only statistically significant when using a t-test (p=0.044), not the Wilcoxon test (p=0.075). Crucially, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups for defecation frequency, stool consistency, ease of stool passage, or colonic transit time (CTT) in either the per protocol (PP) or completers analyses. Therefore, the Panel concluded that this study, despite the observed changes in faecal weight, did not demonstrate an effect of 18g/day of polydextrose over 28 days on the maintenance of normal defecation.
Three human intervention studies, involving both healthy and constipated individuals, were examined. These studies utilized comparable daily polydextrose doses, ranging from 18 to 21 grams, and included between 21 and 36 participants with a mean age of 25 to 48 years. Notably, two of these studies, which involved intervention periods of 21 and 28 days, failed to demonstrate a positive effect of polydextrose on maintaining normal defecation. In contrast, a third, shorter study, lasting only 10 days, did indicate that polydextrose could support the maintenance of normal defecation.
Previously, the Panel assessed four human studies on polydextrose and bowel function (EFSA NDA Panel, 2011a). These studies, individually, were insufficient for substantiating the claim due to methodological weaknesses. However, the Panel would re-evaluate their potential to support the maintenance of normal defecation, considering the new evidence submitted by the applicant.
A single-arm, non-randomized study by Endo et al. (1991) examined faecal weight in eight healthy volunteers. Participants followed a low-cholesterol diet, then a high-cholesterol diet, and finally a high-cholesterol diet with 15 g/day of polydextrose for two weeks each. Crucially, other relevant outcomes for normal defecation were not measured. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this study could not support the claim.
Achour et al. (1994) conducted a single-arm, sequential study with seven male volunteers on a controlled diet. Participants initially consumed only the controlled diet for 8 days. Subsequently, they received 30g of polydextrose daily alongside the controlled diet for days 9-16, followed by 30g of polydextrose with ad libitum eating from days 17-38. The Panel concluded that this study provided insufficient evidence to support the health claim.
In a randomized, single-blind, three-arm crossover study, Tomlin and Read (1988) examined how polydextrose, when added to the usual diet, affected whole gut transit time, stool mass, stool frequency, and stool consistency in 12 healthy male volunteers. Following a 10-day run-in period, participants were given either 30 g/day of polydextrose, 7 g/day of psyllium, or a combination of polydextrose and psyllium (30 g and 2 g/day, respectively) for 10-day periods, separated by one-week wash-out intervals. The study's statistical analysis, which used the Wilcoxon's matched-pairs signed ranks test to assess differences between intervention periods and the run-in, was deemed inappropriate for the study's design by the Panel. Consequently, the Panel concluded that no valid conclusions could be drawn from this study to support the health claim.
A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel study involving 120 healthy volunteers (30 per group) examined the impact of polydextrose (0, 4, 8, or 12 g/day for 28 days) on defecation frequency and fecal weight. Statistical analysis, including ANOVA and Dunnett's test, was employed to compare polydextrose groups with the placebo. However, the Panel noted several limitations in the study's reporting and analysis. Specifically, the overall effect determined by ANOVA was not presented, and comparisons between the different polydextrose dosage groups were absent. Furthermore, despite the study's design, no formal dose-response analysis was conducted. Consequently, the Panel concluded that this study could not be used to scientifically substantiate the claim.
Of the three human intervention studies that could be used to support the claim, only one demonstrated an effect. This study found that 20 g/day of polydextrose, administered for 10 days, led to a positive outcome. In contrast, two other studies, which used doses of 21 g/day and 18 g/day respectively, did not show any beneficial effect on maintaining normal defecation, even when the intervention periods were extended to 3-4 weeks.
The applicant suggests polydextrose works by reaching the colon undigested, where it's slowly and partially fermented by bacteria. Roughly half the ingested polydextrose is excreted intact, increasing faecal mass and causing a mild osmotic effect. Additionally, colonic fermentation boosts bacterial mass and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, potentially aiding peristalsis and increasing faecal bulk.
Applicant-provided in vitro studies indicate polydextrose undergoes partial fermentation in artificial colons, leading to increased short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production. Human metabolic studies using labeled polydextrose also demonstrated approximately 50% fermentation in the colon.
The Panel observed that the human intervention studies submitted by the applicant did not demonstrate any impact of polydextrose on gut microbiota composition, apart from a reduction in *Clostridium perfringens* (Endo et al., 1991). Furthermore, no changes in bacterial profile with increased short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production were reported (Jie et al., 2000). One study assessing bacterial mass (as a percentage of faecal dry matter) found no significant effect of 30 g/day polydextrose consumption, nor did it show any impact on wet or dry faecal weight (Achour et al., 1994). The Panel also noted that polydextrose did not appear to significantly affect transit time in the human studies. The effects on dry and wet faecal weight were inconsistent across the studies. For instance, one study reported an increase in dry but not wet faecal weight at 21 g/day (Vester Boler et al., 2011). Another study documented an increase in wet faecal weight at 20 g/day but did not report on dry faecal weight (Timm et al., 2013). A third study found no consistent effect on either dry or wet faecal weight at 18 g/day (Sarkkinen et al., 2014). In contrast, two other studies indicated significant effects on faecal mass at 30 g/day (Tomlin and Read, 1988) and on both dry and wet faecal weight at doses as low as 4 g/day (Jie et al., 2000).
While studies suggest polydextrose undergoes partial fermentation in the colon, leading to increased short-chain fatty acid production, human trials have not demonstrated an impact on bacterial mass. Furthermore, the evidence supporting polydextrose's bulking capacity via osmotic effects is inconsistent. Consequently, the Panel finds the evidence for the mechanisms by which polydextrose could aid normal defecation in humans, under the proposed usage conditions, to be weak.
The Panel considered the available evidence when forming its opinion. Three human intervention studies were deemed suitable for substantiating the claim. Of these, one study indicated a positive effect when 20 g of polydextrose was consumed daily for 10 days. However, two other studies, which administered 21 g and 18 g of polydextrose daily for longer durations (3-4 weeks), did not demonstrate any effect on maintaining normal defecation. Furthermore, the Panel found the evidence supporting the mechanisms through which polydextrose might contribute to normal defecation in humans to be weak.
The Panel concluded that a cause and effect relationship between polydextrose consumption and maintaining normal defecation has not been established, based on the presented data.
Polydextrose, the food constituent in question, is well-defined. The applicant proposed a claim of "improved bowel function by increasing stool bulk" for the general population, recognizing that maintaining normal defecation is beneficial. However, the Panel concluded that a causal link between consuming polydextrose and maintaining normal defecation has not been demonstrated.
