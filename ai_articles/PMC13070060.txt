Accurate estimations of the average electricity intensity for transmitting data over the Internet, measured in kilowatt-hours per gigabyte (kWh/GB), are crucial for understanding the energy consumption of online services. This research establishes representative estimates for the electricity intensity of fixed-line Internet transmission networks across different time periods and proposes criteria for future accurate assessments. Significant variations in these estimates arise from differing system boundaries, applied assumptions, and the reference year of the data. Contrary to previous suggestions, the methodology employed does not appear to be a primary driver of error. This paper outlines criteria for identifying reliable historical estimates and presents a new figure of 0.06 kWh/GB for 2015. Applying these criteria retrospectively to existing studies reveals that the electricity intensity of data transmission, encompassing core and fixed-line access networks, has halved roughly every two years since 2000 in developed countries. This rate of improvement is comparable to the efficiency gains observed in computing more broadly. Supplementary information is available online.

Internet data traffic has surged over fivefold since 2010 and is projected to triple in the next five years, according to Cisco (2015). This expansion is fueled by a growing number of connected devices, anticipated to reach 28 billion by 2020 (Ericsson 2016), and the rising adoption of digital and cloud services. For instance, by 2012, online movie consumption surpassed DVD and Blu-ray sales in the US on a per-unit basis (Cryan 2012).
The escalating use of the Internet has prompted concerns regarding the electricity demands of Information and Communication Technology (ICT). In 2007, ICT products and services consumed 3.9% of global electricity, a figure that rose to 4.6% by 2012. This trend has led policymakers to prioritize enhancing the energy efficiency of Internet networks. Notably, a report from the International Energy Agency (IEA) identified the creation of energy efficiency metrics as a crucial element for effective policy aimed at reducing network energy consumption.
Estimates for the electricity intensity of Internet data transmission, defined as energy consumed per data transmitted, have been developed. This metric, measured in kWh/GB, assesses the efficiency of data transmission over time. The study prioritizes average electricity intensity over specific or marginal values due to its broader applicability as a historical measure of energy use for data transmission.
In life cycle assessment (LCA) research, the electricity intensity of Internet data transmission is frequently employed to quantify the carbon-equivalent emissions associated with Internet usage. For instance, a 2014 study by Mayers and colleagues utilized these estimates within an LCA framework to compare game distribution methods. Their findings indicated that in 2010, an average 8.8-gigabyte game downloaded online generated higher carbon-equivalent emissions than distribution via Blu-ray Disc. Generally, within LCA studies, the electricity intensity of Internet data transmission is determined by dividing the total electricity consumed by the total data transmitted. This calculation mirrors the methodology used for allocating carbon emissions in transport networks, as well as in electricity generation and transmission.
Estimates for the electricity intensity of Internet data transmission between 2000 and 2015 show a vast disparity, spanning up to five orders of magnitude. These figures range from 136 kWh/GB in 2000 (Koomey et al. 2004) to as low as 0.004 kWh/GB in 2008 (Baliga et al. 2009). While technological advancements have contributed to a two-order-of-magnitude improvement in efficiency, this alone does not account for the wide spread of results. Significant variability also stems from differing system boundaries employed by studies and the assumptions made (Schien and Preist 2014; Coroama and Hilty 2014). Furthermore, the methodology used, categorized as either top-down or bottom-up, has been identified as a potential source of uncertainty. Top-down approaches involve dividing total electricity consumption of a network or subsystem by the data transferred, while bottom-up methods sum individual equipment electricity consumption relative to the data it handles, often necessitating the use of utilization factors.
Top-down methods are criticized for overestimating electricity intensity, while bottom-up methods are thought to underestimate it. However, it remains unclear which estimates best represent real-world data transmission.
Reliable electricity intensity estimates for Internet data transmission are crucial for both research and informing policy and industry efforts to enhance network energy efficiency. This study focuses on developed countries' Internet networks, as their characteristics and electricity intensity are more consistent and better understood than those in developing nations.
This study conducts a meta-analysis to pinpoint the most accurate average electricity intensity estimates for Internet data transmission. The research aims to understand current estimation methods, establish criteria for robust approaches and representative data, and highlight trends like rapid improvements in electricity efficiency over time.
The electricity intensity of data transmission is quantified in kilowatt-hours per gigabyte (kWh/GB) or joules per bit. To facilitate a comprehensive analysis, we examined 14 studies that offered electricity intensity estimates. These estimates were converted into a standardized kWh/GB unit and subsequently organized chronologically. The average electricity intensity of transmission networks is a crucial metric for life cycle assessments (LCAs) that aim to evaluate the carbon emissions associated with Internet services. LCAs typically rely on average energy intensity to quantify the impact of background systems, such as transportation networks and electricity generation and transmission, which fall under attributional allocation approaches. However, Coroama and colleagues (2015) propose that the electricity consumption of access networks and home networking equipment should be allocated based on usage time rather than data volume, as their energy use is not directly proportional to data throughput. Despite this, Internet usage exhibits daily fluctuations, and access networks and home equipment are provisioned to meet peak demand consistently. Consequently, the electricity consumption of these components is influenced by both data volume and time, presenting a challenge in accurately allocating electricity use across different levels of Internet activity. To align with existing research and provide a complete picture of Internet data transmission energy use over time, our data is presented in kWh/GB.
The Internet, a vast and intricate system, is frequently simplified into subsystems for analysis, as depicted in Figure 1 and Table 1. This study utilizes a common system boundary for data transmission, indicated by the dotted box in Figure 1, which illustrates the scale of key processes.
To understand how different system boundaries affected electricity intensity estimates, we categorized the findings by Internet subsystem, as defined in Table 1. The 14 studies examined used eight distinct combinations of these subsystems. Consequently, we recalculated the estimates to align with a unified system boundary, encompassing solely the Internet Protocol (IP) core network and access networks. This combined entity is termed the "transmission network." This specific boundary was selected because it accurately reflects the national-level infrastructure responsible for data transmission and access. The electricity intensity of this transmission network remains consistent regardless of the data type, whether it's media streaming, financial transactions, or email. In contrast, the electricity intensity associated with user devices and data centers exhibits significant variability, largely contingent on the specific service being delivered (Coroama et al. 2015). These components, along with home or on-site networking equipment, typically operate with low utilization and substantial baseline electricity consumption. This characteristic makes their electricity intensity estimates particularly susceptible to variations in usage assumptions and allocation methodologies. Our approach aligns with the perspective of Coroama and colleagues (2015), who advocate for evaluating user devices and data centers independently from the transmission network, to be combined later as needed, for instance, when calculating the energy requirements of a particular service.

The study analyzed various methods, data application years, access network types, and technical assumptions to understand their impact on derived estimates. This analysis led to the establishment of criteria for identifying representative electricity intensity estimates for transmission networks. These criteria were subsequently used to review and evaluate existing estimates from each study.

Four primary methods—modeling, annual electricity consumption, direct measurement, and extrapolation—were identified across the 14 studies examined.
While all studies implicitly model the Internet due to its complexity, a distinct "modeling" approach involves deriving equations based on equipment energy consumption, usage, and data flow to describe specific Internet subsystems. This method necessitates detailed input data for the equipment involved. For instance, Baliga and colleagues (2009) employed a sophisticated mathematical framework to estimate Internet data transmission electricity intensity, developing equations for each subsystem across various bandwidths. Their approach relied on a limited set of power consumption data for particular equipment types and incorporated numerous assumptions regarding network characteristics and data traffic patterns.
Modeling offers the advantage of forecasting future electricity intensity or assessing the impact of changes in variables like bandwidth. However, these models are highly susceptible to the assumptions made regarding input variables and the chosen boundaries. For instance, Baliga and colleagues' (2009) data relied on power ratings for specific equipment, which might not represent actual usage, and numerous assumptions about energy efficiency and utilization introduced uncertainty. Similarly, Costenaro and Duer (2012) employed a top-down modeling approach for the global Internet, drawing from Raghavan and Ma (2011), which also heavily depended on such assumptions.
Schien and Preist (2014) developed a meta-model for various Internet subsystems by integrating the modeling approaches of multiple researchers, referencing works by Baliga et al. (2009) and Van Heddeghem et al. (2012). Their model incorporated input data and assumptions from prior studies, including those by Baliga et al. (2009), Coroama et al. (2013), and Kilper et al. (2011). To project to a 2014 baseline, they applied a 12.5% annual improvement rate derived from Tamm et al. (2010). Schien and colleagues (2014) later employed a pure modeling approach specifically for core networks. Despite utilizing more comprehensive equipment energy use data than Baliga et al. (2009), these modeling methods remain highly reliant on the accuracy of their underlying assumptions.
The Annual Electricity Consumption (AEC) method estimates a network's total energy usage over a period by utilizing data on equipment power consumption, usage patterns, and the existing equipment stock. This approach generally involves taking estimated annual electricity consumption figures for equipment and dividing them by the corresponding estimated annual data traffic. Notably, Koomey and colleagues (2004) employed this AEC method, drawing on network equipment data from Roth and colleagues (2002) and dividing it by annual data flow estimates. This work represents the earliest known attempt in peer-reviewed literature to estimate transmission network electricity intensity. A key advantage of AEC over modeling approaches is its reduced reliance on assumptions, potentially leading to a more accurate representation if the AEC data itself is precise. Specifically, assumptions regarding utilization factors are unnecessary as they are inherently captured within the AEC data.
Direct measurement of equipment power consumption and data traffic offers another method for estimating electricity intensity. Coroama and colleagues (2013) employed this approach, measuring the electricity used by equipment along the data path of a single teleconference. They calculated the electricity intensity for this specific event by dividing the measured electricity use by the teleconference's data transfer rate (40 megabits per second) and multiplying by its duration. The authors presented their case study estimate of 0.2 kWh/GB as a "pessimistic" figure, suggesting the global average for transmission electricity intensity must be lower. However, the representativeness of this estimate for a broader average is questioned due to its focus on a single teleconference event and a specific network path.
A case study focusing on a specific teleconference network path between Japan and Sweden is unlikely to yield a representative average for transmission network electricity intensity. While the study examined data transmission equipment, the diversity of equipment found in a national network far exceeds what was measured. Direct measurement offers greater accuracy than modeling. However, comprehensively measuring all network equipment is often impractical due to the Internet's dynamic scale and complexity.
Extrapolation is another method employed by some researchers to estimate electricity intensity for different years. This involves applying factors that account for changes in equipment energy use or data traffic to existing estimates. For instance, Shehabi and colleagues (2014) projected their 2011 electricity intensity estimate by applying a 20% energy efficiency improvement factor, sourced from Malmodin and colleagues (2014), to prior estimates from 2009 and 2010 by Coroama and colleagues (2013) and Malmodin and colleagues (2014) respectively. A significant risk with this extrapolation approach is that its accuracy is heavily reliant on the precision of the initial estimates and the assumed rates of change for future projections. The intricate nature of these methods is further detailed in subsequent discussions.
Malmodin and colleagues (2014) employed a combined methodology for their estimate. This involved utilizing empirical data, including organizational information from the Swedish ISP TeliaSonera, alongside energy measurements from thousands of network sites. Furthermore, they developed energy consumption models. These models were built upon supplier energy use data, encompassing a database of hundreds of thousands of network equipment entities. The results from these models were then aggregated and compared with findings from their site-level analysis. Malmodin and Lundén (2016) utilized this same approach to update their 2012 estimate for 2015.
Krug and colleagues (2014) developed an organizational model for BT's network electricity consumption in the UK, utilizing power measurements from sample equipment. Unlike Baliga and colleagues (2009), this combined approach, along with studies by Malmodin and colleagues (2014) and Malmodin and Lundén (2016), benefits from models grounded in inventories of actual network equipment. These researchers also incorporated organizational site-level data to validate their estimates and utilized measurements of overall network data flows.
The notion that top-down and bottom-up methodologies inherently lead to over- or underestimations, respectively, was found to be an oversimplification. These classifications do not adequately capture the nuances of the actual methods employed. Crucially, the specific method utilized was not identified as a significant contributor to the variation in estimates. Instead, a combined methodological approach can serve to validate findings, as demonstrated by Krug and colleagues (2014), who utilized a top-down analysis to confirm a bottom-up analysis based on actual equipment. Moreover, modeling and extrapolation techniques, when lacking data validation, are heavily reliant on assumptions, introducing greater uncertainty and potentially limiting data availability.
The scale of the network studied is more critical than the method employed. Studies have examined specific network paths, national-level networks, or global system representations. Estimates relying on equipment specific to a particular service, like that by Coroama et al. (2013), are restricted and unlikely to yield representative figures for average transmission network electricity intensity.
Estimates for electricity intensity must account for all network equipment, including older, less efficient legacy devices. Studies focusing solely on state-of-the-art technology, like Baliga et al. (2009), overlook this less efficient equipment. Consequently, they significantly underestimate the actual electricity intensity, as seen in their 2008 estimate of 0.004 kWh/GB, failing to represent the full range of equipment in use.
To ensure representative estimates, approaches must reflect national transmission networks. Data should encompass the full spectrum of deployed equipment, including older, less efficient legacy devices.
The year to which data applies is a crucial consideration for existing estimates. Ideally, the underlying data should correspond to the reference year or be adjusted with sound assumptions. For instance, Williams and Tang (2012) calculated data transmission's carbon intensity for 2010 using 2005 equipment data. This approach likely introduced inaccuracies, as it seemingly disregarded changes in equipment energy use between 2005 and 2010, a factor that can significantly impact results for several reasons discussed later.
Several estimates rely on extrapolating older figures, incorporating assumptions about evolving energy consumption, data volumes, and Internet efficiency. For instance, an early 2000 estimate from Koomey and colleagues utilized Annual Electricity Consumption (AEC) data for network equipment, adjusted for auxiliary systems. Later, Taylor and Koomey revised this estimate and produced 2006 figures by applying equipment energy use growth factors. Subsequently, Weber and colleagues extended the trend observed between 2000 and 2006 to project the electricity intensity of data transmission for 2008. These methods highlight the challenges of accurately forecasting future electricity intensity due to inherent uncertainties in such projections.
Shehabi et al. (2014) estimated electricity intensity for 2011 by applying energy efficiency improvements to prior estimates. Extrapolating results over time presents challenges due to factors like technological advancements, equipment replacement, increased usage, and significant shifts in technology.
Determining the pace of change in Internet technology power consumption is challenging. Historically, advancements in equipment processing power, akin to Moore's Law, led to a doubling of chip density every two years due to increased transistor counts. This enhanced processing capability translates to greater energy efficiency, allowing tasks to be completed with less power. Even though Moore's Law has decelerated, technological progress in circuit design, component integration, software, and power management is anticipated to continue improving energy efficiency. While networking equipment faces unique constraints compared to general-purpose computing, the general trends observed in computing technology, as identified by Koomey and colleagues, offer insights into the expected efficiency improvements in networking devices built with similar silicon-based components.
The rate at which new technology improves network electricity efficiency is tied to equipment replacement cycles, largely influenced by capital costs. Historically, computing equipment's energy efficiency at peak performance doubled every 1.6 years until 2000, and subsequently every 2.6 years. Relying solely on data from the latest equipment for country-wide network electricity intensity calculations is problematic. This approach overlooks the energy consumption of older, less efficient legacy equipment still in use, leading to an underestimation, as seen in the work by Baliga and colleagues.
Internet data traffic is experiencing rapid and continuous growth, driven by an expanding user base, increasing per-person data consumption, and the proliferation of connected devices, a trend anticipated to accelerate with the "Internet of Things." Cisco's 2015 white paper forecasts a 42% annual increase in Internet traffic through 2020. This surge necessitates that Internet Service Providers (ISPs) enhance their networking infrastructure capacity, consequently increasing power consumption. Due to the multifaceted nature of this growth, precise modeling and extrapolation are challenging, underscoring the importance of grounding such calculations in empirical evidence.
Predicting energy efficiency improvements is challenging due to unpredictable technological shifts. Historically, the internet has seen significant technological leaps, such as the transition from dial-up to ADSL and then to fiber optic broadband, all driven by the demand for faster speeds. Consequently, when updating estimates with factors for changes in energy use, data traffic, or efficiency, it is crucial to proceed with caution. This requires a thorough understanding of the most recent data concerning these trends.
Extrapolating future trends requires accurate predictions of technological advancements, equipment deployment, usage patterns, and shifts in technology. Therefore, any extrapolation must account for these factors, incorporating industry roadmaps alongside historical data. This leads to the third criterion: when using extrapolation, it should be grounded in an analysis of planned future technological development and short-term improvements, utilizing industry roadmaps rather than solely relying on past trends.
The composition of access networks includes a variety of equipment, as detailed in Table 1. A customer's received bandwidth is significantly influenced by their specific access network. For instance, Fiber to the Node (FTTN) offers considerably higher average speeds than ADSL (Baliga et al. 2009). Studies reviewed in Table 2 have varied in their consideration of access networks, with some focusing narrowly on FTTN (Coroama et al. 2013) while others encompass all access networks within national borders (Malmodin et al. 2014; Krug et al. 2014). Modern fiber optic technologies like FTTN enable more efficient data transmission, consuming less electricity per bit than older copper-based systems such as ADSL. Consequently, any estimate of average electricity intensity must account for all types of access networks present within the studied network. Therefore, the fourth criterion established is that estimates must incorporate data from all access network types within the network under examination, reflecting the data flows through each in a given year.
The variability of estimates is tested by comparing common technical assumptions used across studies.
The utilization factor, representing the ratio of a network's actual usage to its total capacity, varied significantly in studies, from 15% to 100%. This choice is tied to the estimation method. However, comprehensive Annual Electricity Consumption (AEC) studies and direct organizational measurements bypass the need for explicit utilization assumptions, as actual equipment usage is inherently included in their results.
National-scale Internet networks show distinct daily usage cycles, with peak activity typically occurring in the evening. This pattern, illustrated by data on internet traffic variations over 15-minute intervals, highlights that networks are not consistently used at maximum capacity throughout the day.
Internet Service Providers (ISPs) build network infrastructure to meet peak demand, meaning most of the day networks operate below maximum capacity. Certain equipment, like access network and home routers, doesn't efficiently adjust energy consumption based on data traffic, using similar amounts of power regardless of usage levels. Consequently, assuming 100% utilization for average transmission networks is inaccurate due to daily usage fluctuations, potentially leading to underestimations of electricity intensity. Furthermore, electricity consumed during periods of low utilization may be overlooked if estimates rely solely on transmission time. For instance, Williams and Tang (2012) based their estimate on equipment power consumption multiplied by transmission time, omitting the energy required to maintain service availability around the clock. This omission might explain why their estimate was significantly lower than that of Malmodin and colleagues (2014) for the same year. Therefore, lower utilization factor values, as employed by Schien and colleagues (2014), are more likely to accurately represent national-scale networks. This observation leads to a key criterion: utilization estimates should reflect typical diurnal network usage, not assume 100%.
Power-Use Effectiveness (PUE) quantifies the energy efficiency of network facilities. It is calculated by dividing a facility's total energy consumption by the energy consumed by its Information Technology (IT) equipment, such as servers and routers. This metric accounts for the energy used by all necessary components within the system, including those not directly involved in computation, like power supply and cooling systems. In the reviewed studies, PUE values typically ranged from 1.25 to 2.0 across 10 out of 14 investigations. For instance, Shehabi and colleagues (2014) reported a PUE of 1.3, reflecting an efficient setup. While the European Union's data center code of conduct suggests best practice PUEs of 1.2 or lower, such low figures are unlikely to represent the average PUE found within a national network.
Krug and colleagues (2014), along with Malmodin and colleagues (2014), validated their average Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) estimates by comparing modeling results with empirical data from UK and Swedish networks, respectively. For studies where PUE is an assumed factor, a range of 1.8 to 2.0 is recommended, reflecting typical current Internet networks as presented in these studies. It's important to note that these figures are representative of data centers, and further research is needed to accurately determine PUE for core and access network equipment. While lower PUE values are achievable for specific services, future improvements in Internet network equipment are anticipated. Therefore, when PUE is a necessary assumption, recent average values should fall between 1.8 and 2.0, potentially higher for early 2000s estimates and lower for more advanced facilities.
The number of hops, representing the data's journey through various network nodes, is an assumption used in six of the fourteen studies examined. These assumptions varied, with estimates ranging from 12 hops (Schien and Preist 2014) to 24 hops (Coroama et al. 2013). Unlike utilization and PUE, which act as multipliers with a clearer impact, the precise relationship between the number of hops and the final electricity intensity estimate is less defined and dependent on the specific model employed. While assumptions regarding the number of hops can influence electricity intensity results, the extent of this influence remains uncertain.
Determining the average number of hops data traverses during Internet use presents a challenge. While Coroama et al. (2013) provided hop estimates for a particular service, Krug et al. (2014) validated their assumptions with UK-wide organizational data from BT. When making assumptions about hop count, it is crucial to support them with empirical data that reflects the entire system. Therefore, estimates for the number of hops must be corroborated by empirical data and accurately represent data flows across the entire network.
Based on the established criteria, the most representative electricity intensity estimates for transmission networks, excluding data centers and edge devices, are presented. For the year 2000, these estimates range from 6.5 to 7.1 kWh/GB, and for 2006, they are between 0.65 and 0.71 kWh/GB, both from Taylor and Koomey (2008). Subsequent estimates include 0.16 kWh/GB for 2010 (Malmodin et al. 2014), 0.14 kWh/GB for 2012 (Krug et al. 2014), and 0.023 kWh/GB for 2015 (Malmodin and Lundén 2016).
The paper discusses trends in transmission network electricity intensity and methodological findings.
Between 2000 and 2015, the electricity intensity of transmission networks saw a substantial decline, decreasing by a factor of approximately 170 among the five studies that met the established criteria. This significant improvement is further illustrated by specific network data. For instance, Krug (2015) reported that BT's access network electricity intensity halved, while its core network intensity decreased tenfold between 2012 and 2015. Incorporating these updated figures into the 2012 estimate from Krug and colleagues (2014) yields a data transmission electricity intensity of 0.06 kWh/GB for 2015, specifically for the UK's BT network. This figure aligns closely with the updated 2015 estimate provided by Malmodin and Lundén (2016). The trend of decreasing electricity intensity over this period is visually represented in Figure 3. The graph, which utilizes a logarithmic scale for electricity intensity (kWh/GB) against the year, demonstrates that the electricity intensity of data transmission has been observed to halve roughly every two years, as indicated by a high coefficient of determination (R² = 0.98). This rate of efficiency improvement is notably faster than the historical trends in electrical efficiency for computing at peak output observed after the year 2000 by Koomey and Naffziger (2015, 2016). Figure 3 specifically displays electricity intensity estimates for the transmission network system boundary, derived from studies that adhere to the study's criteria. The data points plotted include: the median estimate of 6.5 to 7.1 kWh/GB from Taylor and Koomey (2008) for the year 2000; their median estimate of 0.65 to 0.71 kWh/GB for 2006; Malmodin and colleagues' (2014) estimate of 0.16 kWh/GB for 2010; Krug and colleagues' (2014) estimate of 0.14 kWh/GB for 2012; Malmodin and Lundén's (2016) estimate of 0.023 kWh/GB for 2015; and the newly proposed estimate of 0.06 kWh/GB for 2015, based on Krug and colleagues (2014) with updated 2015 data from Krug (2016).
An extrapolation of the observed trend beyond 2015 suggests that transmission network electricity intensity could continue to decrease if the current trajectory persists. However, future research should focus on generating new estimates that adhere to the study's criteria, as the extrapolation relies on a limited number of data points and is susceptible to various influencing factors. Despite these limitations, the derived regression model can provide estimates for transmission network electricity intensity for any year between 2000 and 2015 where published data is scarce.
Four distinct methods—modeling, annual electricity consumption (AEC), direct measurement, and extrapolation—or combinations thereof, were employed in existing studies to estimate the electricity intensity of transmission networks, rather than relying solely on top-down or bottom-up approaches. Contrary to prior suggestions, the specific method utilized did not significantly contribute to the variability observed in these estimates. Instead, discrepancies in system boundaries across studies and methodological errors were identified as the primary drivers of this variation. These errors included using study networks that were not representative of the entire Internet in scale or technical assumptions, extrapolating based solely on past trends without justified future predictions, assuming 100% utilization when national networks typically operate below this level, and failing to include data for all fixed-line access network types.
When the Internet network is a primary focus in research, a deeper understanding of what drives increased electricity consumption is needed. In such cases, a consequential allocation method, like those detailed in Table 5, might be suitable. This approach could involve using weighted averages or considering marginal changes in electricity use and data flow.
If networks operated at full capacity, both consequential and attributional allocation methods would rely on average electricity intensity. The electricity consumed for specific data transmissions should be determined by the time and data capacity utilized. However, electricity used to power unutilized network capacity needs to be allocated. This allocation should be proportional to services demanding peak data capacity, as these services compel Internet Service Providers to expand infrastructure and bandwidth.
Future networking equipment might adjust power consumption based on utilization and enter efficient idle modes when inactive. Therefore, allocation methods need constant updates to reflect evolving networking technology and energy performance. Further research should investigate consequential versus attributional allocation for calculating transmission network electricity intensity in greater detail.
Since 2000, estimates for the electricity intensity of Internet data transmission have shown considerable variation. As noted by Coroama and Hilty (2014), differences in system boundaries and applied assumptions are major contributors to this variability. However, our analysis suggests that the methodologies employed are not a primary source of discrepancy, unlike prior research. Instead, the timing of data, methodological inaccuracies, and the definition of system boundaries emerge as the principal factors influencing uncertainty. To mitigate such errors moving forward, future estimations of average transmission network electricity intensity must adhere to the established criteria.
Studies meeting our criteria indicate that average transmission network electricity intensity halves every two years. Our regression analysis allows for the estimation of Internet core and access network electricity use from 2000 to 2015, addressing prior uncertainties. Further research is needed to update current and future estimates and enhance the certainty of observed trends.
Future research should refine consequential allocation methods for specific cases of transmission network electricity intensity. Attributional allocation will likely remain the most practical approach for Life Cycle Assessments, thus, estimating average electricity intensity will continue to be a research priority.
