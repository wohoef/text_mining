This study addresses a gap in the existing research concerning child welfare decision-making processes for young mothers, despite their known vulnerability to child welfare involvement. It investigates the factors influencing child protection workers' assessments that young children of adolescent and young adult mothers face a risk of future maltreatment. Analyzing a provincial dataset, the research revealed that children with young mothers were disproportionately identified as being at risk of future maltreatment compared to those with adult mothers, leading to a higher degree of child welfare intervention. Specifically, for young mothers, a determination of risk was closely linked to their mental health and socioeconomic conditions. The findings suggest a need for changes in practice and policy to ensure prompt and adequate material and emotional support for these families.

Young mothers, encompassing adolescents and young adults, confront numerous documented difficulties. These include diminished physical and mental health, alongside reduced educational and vocational achievements. Their children also face elevated risks of maltreatment and engagement with child welfare services, experiencing long-term physical, emotional, social, and educational hurdles. The causes of maltreatment by young mothers are intricate and multifaceted, mirroring factors that predict early parenthood. These include poverty, homelessness, family instability, trauma, and substance abuse. When investigated for maltreatment, young mothers exhibit a greater prevalence of risk factors and behaviors. These encompass poverty, unstable housing, limited social support, mental health issues, substance abuse, intimate partner violence, and children with developmental or behavioral concerns. Furthermore, young mothers are more prone to having a personal history of child welfare involvement compared to adult mothers.
Following child welfare investigations, younger maternal age correlates with a higher degree of intervention. For instance, one Canadian study revealed that even after accounting for various risk factors, families led by young mothers were more frequently moved to ongoing services, indicating that caseworkers perceived a greater need for support and oversight in these situations. Another study specifically noted that 81% of cases involving infants with a young primary caregiver experiencing mental health concerns were subsequently transferred. Conversely, in California, adolescent mothers facing maltreatment allegations were less likely to have those allegations substantiated or to receive services compared to older mothers. However, when maltreatment was investigated, children of younger mothers faced a greater likelihood of being placed in out-of-home care than those with older mothers.
Young mothers are consistently identified in existing research as being more susceptible to child protection reports and, once reported, tend to experience prolonged engagement with the child welfare system. However, the specific ways in which their demographic, social, emotional, and socioeconomic circumstances influence their child welfare service pathways remain poorly understood. A critical element in how child welfare workers make decisions regarding young mothers and their children during an investigation is the determination of a child's risk for future maltreatment. In Ontario, the Child Protection Standards explicitly recognize the risk of future harm, alongside actual harm events, as a legitimate basis for initiating a child protection investigation and providing further child welfare services. This means that a finding of future maltreatment risk can justify intervention. An Ontario study highlighted the significance of this assessment, revealing that a worker's determination of future risk was a strong predictor of current maltreatment substantiation, increasing the odds by over six times. This finding raises questions about the practice of using potential future harm to validate past maltreatment. Consequently, researchers have emphasized the need for further investigation into the factors that contribute to these risk determinations.
This research aims to fill a critical knowledge void concerning young mothers, specifically those aged 21 and under, a demographic already recognized as high-risk. The study's objectives are twofold: first, to investigate how child welfare workers' risk assessments impact subsequent decisions during investigations involving adolescent and young adult caregivers; and second, to identify which factors—related to caregivers, households, and maltreatment—lead to a determination that their children face future maltreatment risks. This inquiry is particularly pertinent given that young mothers often perceive child welfare interventions as disruptive and intrusive (Haight et al., 2009; Maxwell et al., 2011). Many fear losing their children, a concern frequently rooted in their own experiences within the foster care system (Aparicio, 2017; Haight et al., 2009). Paradoxically, this apprehension about child welfare involvement can inadvertently heighten risks for their children, as support from these services could otherwise mitigate challenges faced by young parents (Pryce & Samuels, 2010).
This analysis utilized data from the Ontario Incidence Study of Reported Child Abuse and Neglect (OIS-2013), specifically its most recent cycle (Fallon et al., 2015). The OIS aims to provide a provincial estimate of child maltreatment occurrences in Ontario and to record decisions made during investigations. The methodology involved a cluster sampling approach: first, 17 out of 46 child welfare agencies across Ontario were chosen to form a representative sample. Subsequently, cases within these selected agencies were sampled. Eligibility for inclusion extended to cases initiated for investigation between October 1 and December 31, 2013, at the randomly chosen sites. Larger agencies had their open cases further subsampled, with a maximum of 250 cases. The ultimate stage of sample selection focused on identifying children who had been subject to child protection investigations. Child welfare workers overseeing these investigations completed a data collection form. This form comprised over 40 questions covering demographic details and risk factors for the child and family, as well as information pertinent to maltreatment concerns and decisions made throughout the investigation. To ensure consistency, participating workers received training and an OIS-2013 Guidebook detailing definitions for each question (Fallon et al., 2015). The University of Toronto Research Ethics Board reviewed and approved all OIS-2013 protocols for data collection, handling, and subsequent analyses.
For this analysis, an initial unweighted sample of 1,107 young children, aged 0-3 years, was selected. These children were all under the primary care of their biological mothers and were chosen due to their heightened vulnerability, a characteristic particularly pronounced in infants and very young children. This age restriction also reflected the demographic reality that younger mothers tend to have very young children; specifically, 93% of children under the care of mothers aged 21 and below in the OIS-2013 dataset were less than 4 years old. To generate provincial annual estimates, two weighting procedures were subsequently applied. First, the data were annualized to project the total number of cases investigated by each study location over an entire year. Following this, regional weights were incorporated to adjust for nonproportional sampling, ensuring that each site's contribution accurately reflected its size relative to the provincial child population. After these adjustments, the final weighted estimate for child maltreatment investigations involving young children, based on the OIS-2013 data, totaled 26,904 investigations.
The data for this study was gathered from the investigating workers and encompassed various details about each case. These details included demographic information about the family, characteristics related to maltreatment, caregiver profiles, and socioeconomic factors. The short-term service dispositions were also recorded. A key focus of the analysis was the workers' assessment of whether a child faced a significant risk of future maltreatment, recorded as a "yes" or "no/unknown" response. This specific determination, based on the worker's "clinical judgment" at the investigation's conclusion, is a direct question within the OIS-2013 instrument. Other decisions made during the investigation that were analyzed included referrals to services outside of standard child welfare, such as family counseling or social assistance, transfers to ongoing child welfare services, and decisions regarding out-of-home placement. The study's explanatory variables were categorized into caregiver and family characteristics, investigation specifics, and socioeconomic risk factors.
For this study, maternal age was categorized into two distinct groups: "adolescent and transition-age mothers," encompassing those 21 years old and younger, and "adult mothers," defined as those 22 years and older. Racial and ethnic background of mothers was classified as either "White" or "Indigenous/visible minority." The latter category was a composite, including Indigenous (6.7%), Black (8.2%), Latin American (3.1%), Arab/West Asian (3.1%), East Asian (3.3%), South Asian (2.3%), Southeast Asian (1.7%), and other (0.7%) groups. Investigators also documented specific risk factors concerning the biological mother, the primary caregiver. These factors, identified as either confirmed or suspected by the worker (versus unknown or not present), were transformed into dichotomous variables. They included: substance abuse (alcohol, drugs, or solvents), cognitive impairment, mental health issues, limited social support networks, and a personal history of foster care or group home placement. Additionally, household structure was examined. This involved determining if a second caregiver resided within the household, creating a binary variable for single-parent status, and separately, whether a second caregiver was present outside the household.
Child welfare investigations in Ontario can stem from two main types of concerns: either a specific alleged incident of child maltreatment or the perceived risk of future harm, even without a current incident. The latter focuses solely on potential future maltreatment based on the family's situation. The primary categories for these investigations included cases based purely on risk, physical or sexual abuse (combined due to the low incidence of sexual abuse allegations at 1.1%), neglect, emotional maltreatment, and exposure to intimate partner violence (IPV). When an allegation of maltreatment was present, workers had the option to identify up to three distinct types. Furthermore, investigations involving co-occurring maltreatment were noted if both a primary and secondary type of maltreatment were alleged. A separate variable also tracked whether the family had been subject to a previous investigation.
The study considered several socioeconomic risk factors, especially pertinent for young parents. These included economic hardship, housing stability, and income source. Economic hardship was defined as a dichotomous variable, indicating if a family lacked funds for essential needs like food, shelter, or utilities within six months before the investigation. Furthermore, three other binary indicators were used: if the household's main income came from social assistance or other benefits, if the mother resided with friends or family, and if the family had relocated at least twice in the preceding year.
Our analysis began with descriptive bivariate comparisons, employing chi-square testing to identify statistical differences. We first investigated how various factors—including maternal age, investigative decisions, demographics, maltreatment-related issues, family characteristics, and socioeconomic status—differed among the young children under investigation. Subsequently, we focused on the determination of significant future maltreatment risk in young children, examining its variation across the same set of covariates, specifically comparing children of mothers aged 21 and younger with those of mothers aged 22 and older. To understand which demographic traits and risk factors were most influential in determining risk for children of younger mothers, we constructed a logistic regression model. This model exclusively incorporated variables that had shown statistical significance in the initial descriptive analysis. The Nagelkerke pseudo R2 statistic was then utilized to quantify the explanatory power of these variables. While we calculated estimated frequencies for investigations involving very young children using the full sample weight, all significance testing, both bivariate and multivariable, relied on unweighted data. This decision was made to avoid inflating statistical significance and overstating marginal findings, a potential issue given the smaller sample size for younger mothers, thus ensuring a more conservative and parsimonious assessment of significance.
In 2013, Ontario saw an estimated 26,904 child protection investigations involving very young children residing with their biological mothers. A notable 17% of these investigations concerned mothers aged 21 or younger. A comparison of young mothers and adult mothers, detailed in Table 1, revealed significant disparities in investigative outcomes and characteristics. Children of younger mothers faced a higher likelihood of being identified as at risk for future maltreatment. Consequently, these families were more frequently referred to services outside of child welfare and transitioned to ongoing child welfare support. Younger mothers were predominantly White and exhibited a greater incidence of cognitive impairment, mental health issues, and limited social support. They also had a higher prevalence of past involvement with foster care or group homes. Investigations involving young mothers more often focused on risk alone or neglect, rather than physical or sexual abuse, emotional maltreatment, or exposure to intimate partner violence. Furthermore, young mothers consistently showed worse socioeconomic conditions than older mothers, experiencing higher rates of economic hardship, reliance on social assistance, cohabitation with friends or family, and residential instability.
The connection between a determination of significant future maltreatment risk in young children's investigations and subsequent decisions varied based on the mother's age. For younger mothers, approximately 30% of investigated children were deemed at risk. This percentage rose notably with increased intervention: nearly 40% for those referred to additional services, 55% for those transferred to ongoing services, and over 80% for children placed in out-of-home care during the investigation. A similar trend was observed for older mothers, as illustrated in Figure 1, which shows the proportion of cases where workers identified significant risk at each decision stage. However, the percentages for older mothers were lower across all stages: 21% for investigations, 31% for referrals, 51% for transfers, and 63% for placements.
An analysis of investigations involving young children, detailed in Table 2, revealed distinct patterns in the determination of future maltreatment risk. For children of younger mothers, this risk assessment was notably influenced by both caregiver and socioeconomic factors. Specifically, young mothers facing challenges such as substance abuse, cognitive impairment, mental health issues, or social isolation showed a greater likelihood of having a risk determination made against them. Similarly, young mothers experiencing severe economic hardship, relying on social assistance, residing with friends or family, or exhibiting residential instability (moving twice or more within a year) were more frequently identified as posing a future risk. While older mothers exhibited some comparable trends, their risk determinations also correlated with specific investigative characteristics. For instance, investigations involving concerns of neglect or exposure to intimate partner violence (IPV) were more often linked to a risk determination compared to other reasons for investigation. Furthermore, cases with multiple allegations of maltreatment were more prone to a finding of future risk. Among mothers aged 22 and older, a prior history of investigations also increased the probability of a risk determination.
Table 3 details the findings from the multivariable logistic regression model, which evaluated how various risk factors influenced the determination of future maltreatment risk for children of younger mothers under investigation. The logistic regression exclusively incorporated variables that demonstrated statistical significance at the bivariate level. Collectively, these selected variables accounted for approximately 45% of the variability observed in the decision to transition cases to ongoing services. After considering all the included characteristics, a strong correlation emerged between this decision and maternal mental health issues (Odds Ratio [OR] = 5.09, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] = [2.31, 11.21], p < .001), as well as households receiving social assistance and other benefits (OR = 4.51, 95% CI = [1.81, 6.24], p = .001). Furthermore, younger mothers who lacked sufficient social support or resided with family or friends were also found to have an elevated likelihood of their children being deemed at risk of future maltreatment.
This study's findings corroborate earlier research, highlighting that young mothers under child protective services investigation exhibit a greater concentration of established risk factors for both maltreatment and child welfare system involvement. These factors encompass social, economic, and emotional difficulties. Notably, children of young mothers in Ontario faced a higher likelihood of being deemed at risk for future maltreatment, even though investigations into physical and sexual abuse allegations were less common for them. Consequently, these families frequently experienced transfers to ongoing child welfare services and referrals to supplementary, non-child welfare-related services. The prevalence of mental health issues, social isolation, prior foster care experiences, socioeconomic precariousness, and housing instability among the investigated young mothers in this study aligns with the reasons for their investigations, primarily neglect and general risk. These very conditions were strongly linked to the determination that young children of young mothers faced a risk of future maltreatment, influencing their path through the investigative process. While the proportion of mothers considered at risk of future maltreatment rose at each decision point for all mothers in the study, this trend was more pronounced among younger mothers. For instance, 80% of young mothers whose children were placed out-of-home within the initial 30 days of investigation were assessed as posing a persistent risk to their children, compared to 63% of older mothers.
Investigated young mothers who were identified as posing a risk of future maltreatment exhibited a similar high concentration of risk factors. Both the bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses highlighted caregiver risks and socioeconomic conditions as the most influential factors in determining this risk. Specifically, young mothers facing personal mental health challenges and lacking sufficient social support were more likely to be deemed at risk compared to those without these issues. It's crucial to note that young motherhood itself is linked to a greater chance of postpartum depression, a risk further amplified by limited social support (Reid & Meadows-Oliver, 2007). Prior research has established the impact of adolescent and caregiver behavioral health on investigative decisions (Filippelli et al., 2017; King et al., 2018). This suggests that the child welfare system often steps in to address critical service gaps when mental and behavioral health resources are scarce. In Ontario, wait times for community-based mental health services and publicly funded psychiatric care are notably extended (Kowalewski et al., 2011; Kurdyak et al., 2014), with reports indicating average waits of six months or more for youth requiring intensive treatment, counseling, or therapy (Office of the Auditor General of Ontario, 2016). While this study points to mental health concerns as a potential driver for risk determinations, it remains uncertain if the child welfare system is the most suitable avenue for addressing these issues. Nevertheless, it's clear that to adequately support investigated young mothers, the child welfare system must enhance its ability to provide accessible services and foster collaboration with mental health professionals, particularly given the heightened risk of postpartum depression in this demographic. The study's findings indicate that workers are indeed facilitating some external service connections, as nearly 40% of young mothers with a risk determination received referrals. However, considering how significantly mental health needs and social isolation contribute to more intensive child welfare involvement, this referral rate should ideally be higher.
Socioeconomic hardship is a significant factor in child welfare decisions for young mothers, reflecting both their societal position and the enduring influence of poverty on system involvement (Lee & George, 1999; Lefebvre et al., 2017). A striking majority, over 85%, of young mothers identified as posing a future maltreatment risk were reliant on social assistance or other income support. Predictably, even when other variables were accounted for, young mothers receiving such benefits faced considerably higher odds of a risk determination. Similarly, precarious housing, often indicated by living with friends or family, was also linked to a finding of future risk. This heightened scrutiny of impoverished and precariously housed young mothers can be partly understood through historical events. For instance, the 1997 starvation death of a 5-week-old infant, whose young mother was under the supervision of a Toronto child welfare agency and residing in a homeless shelter, led to a coroner's inquest (Robson, 2005). This inquest prompted recommendations for greater oversight and higher expectations for child welfare agencies assisting high-risk pregnant and parenting young mothers. While addressing fundamental material needs like income and housing typically falls outside the direct mandate of child welfare, families struggling with these issues are disproportionately represented within the system. If poverty is indeed a predictor of future maltreatment risk, then policies and funding should prioritize improving the living conditions for young mothers. This aligns with a contemporary shift towards a social model of child welfare, which recognizes that many reasons for families' engagement with child protective services are rooted in social determinants (Featherstone et al., 2018).
Conflicting evidence exists regarding the appropriateness of assessing future maltreatment risk in young mothers. While some research indicates that factors like psychological distress, limited social support, and a personal history of childhood maltreatment contribute to a higher potential for child abuse in impoverished young mothers, justifying risk assessment, other population-based studies suggest that younger maternal age alone does not predict subsequent reports of maltreatment for infants initially investigated. Therefore, further investigation is crucial to understand the rates of out-of-home placement and repeated maltreatment allegations among young mothers involved in child protection, especially those identified as high-risk and receiving ongoing services.
The study's limitations primarily stem from the characteristics of the OIS-2013 dataset. This dataset is cross-sectional, meaning it captures a snapshot of decisions made during investigations, along with various case, caregiver, and household details. The data collection instrument was filled out by the workers who led the investigations, at the investigation's conclusion. There was no independent verification of this information, raising the possibility that workers might have completed the instrument in a way that supported their prior assessments. Furthermore, the data typically reflects a 30-day period for investigations, leaving the long-term service pathways for these families and children unclear. Information regarding caregiver risk factors and other crucial variables might have been unknown to the investigating worker when they completed the data collection, potentially leading to underestimations. The study also lacks measures for the severity of issues like child or caregiver functioning, and it did not utilize validated tools to identify clinically significant conditions such as depression or substance abuse. Finally, individual worker characteristics were excluded from the analysis, consistent with findings from similar studies suggesting these factors don't consistently explain decision-making variations. The cross-sectional nature of the data also prevents drawing conclusions about cause-and-effect relationships or the sequence of events.
This study provides crucial data for child welfare administrators, policymakers, and practitioners to enhance their strategies when engaging with families led by young mothers. The findings highlight that investigating workers frequently identify young mothers with mental health issues and limited social support as posing a risk to their children. Consequently, offering these caregivers accessible and suitable resources to address these difficulties could help reduce the likelihood of maltreatment. Furthermore, the prevalence of social assistance among young mothers deemed at risk underscores the necessity for child protection agencies to better address the material needs of families facing poverty, including housing and income assistance. Implementing referrals to evidence-based programs, such as home visiting initiatives, which tackle this range of challenges, could alleviate the strain on both the child welfare system and the affected families. Additionally, child welfare organizations should strive to cultivate stronger relationships with external service providers to guarantee that these families receive adequate and appropriate support. Future research should build on these findings by examining how the provision of timely and suitable services influences risk determination and ongoing child welfare involvement.
Decision-making in child welfare investigations involving adolescent and young adult mothers is significantly influenced by their increased exposure to high-risk situations compared to older mothers. While young mothers don't exhibit a higher prevalence of mental health issues than adult mothers, the existence of such concerns was a key determinant in classifying their children as at risk. This suggests that the child welfare system might be acting as an essential support for young mothers who struggle to access timely mental health services in a province with limited public funding for them. Furthermore, the well-documented socioeconomic vulnerability associated with early motherhood also plays a crucial role in how decisions are made for these young families. Since investigations into younger mothers are less frequently initiated due to allegations of physical or sexual abuse, the pressing nature of these cases seems to stem from maternal circumstances that fall outside the typical scope of child welfare mandates. Addressing the needs of investigated young mothers necessitates both internal and external changes within the child welfare system, impacting both its practices and policies.
