Doxorubicin (DOX), an anthracycline antibiotic, is a potent cytotoxic agent effective against various cancers, including hematological and solid tumors. Its efficacy stems from its ability to be efficiently delivered to cancer cells, often via nanocarriers. However, its widespread distribution can lead to significant side effects and the development of drug resistance. This study investigated a novel doxorubicin-transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) to assess its potential to induce apoptosis in human normal and leukemia cells. The hypothesis was that DOX released from the conjugate would undergo a redox cycle, generating free radicals that trigger programmed cell death. To evaluate this, researchers exposed human leukemia cell lines (CCRF-CEM, K562, and its resistant counterpart K562/DOX) and normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to DOX-TRF. They meticulously tracked apoptotic and necrotic events over time, alongside the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential, and changes in cytochrome c and intracellular calcium levels. The findings revealed that DOX-TRF effectively induced apoptosis in all tested leukemia cell lines, accompanied by characteristic morphological changes and a decline in mitochondrial membrane potential. Notably, compared to free DOX, DOX-TRF demonstrated a stronger correlation between increased ROS generation and a greater drop in mitochondrial membrane potential, particularly in the doxorubicin-resistant K562/DOX cells. Furthermore, apoptosis induced by DOX-TRF was directly linked to the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria and an increase in intracellular calcium concentrations across all leukemia cell lines. In conclusion, the study indicates that DOX-TRF exhibits significantly higher cytotoxicity towards human leukemia cells than free DOX. It also demonstrates that DOX-TRF effectively generates free radicals, which play a direct role in inducing apoptosis.

Over the past 15 years, peptides and proteins have been increasingly utilized to enhance the delivery of cytotoxic agents. These chemical modifications of existing chemotherapeutics offer new avenues to improve their limited clinical efficacy, often hampered by tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance. Strategies explored include encapsulating drugs within carriers such as liposomes, polymeric micelles, and nanoparticles. More recent research has concentrated on identifying novel vehicles for intracellular drug delivery, with examples like lactosaminated human albumin and transferrin.
Doxorubicin (DOX), an anthracycline antibiotic, is a potent cytotoxic agent effective against various cancers. Its transfer to cancer cells is enhanced by nanocarriers. DOX functions by damaging DNA, inhibiting topoisomerase, and sequestering iron, which leads to free radical generation. Despite its efficacy, DOX's widespread distribution causes severe side effects and contributes to drug resistance, limiting its clinical application.
To enhance the therapeutic success of doxorubicin (DOX), which is limited by factors like tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance, various strategies have been employed. Polymer-based delivery systems have been developed to redirect DOX from toxic sites, such as the heart, to tumor locations, aiming to improve its therapeutic efficacy, though with modest success, particularly in DOX-sensitive cancers. Seeking to alter the overall DOX dosage and reduce peak serum concentrations, we created a doxorubicin-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate. This approach, utilizing transferrin (TRF) as a drug carrier, is recognized for improving the delivery and effectiveness of anthracycline antibiotics. TRF offers several advantages, including its non-immunogenic nature, its role in iron metabolism and cell growth regulation, and the elevated expression of its receptors on many malignancies. Consequently, conjugating anticancer drugs like DOX with TRF can facilitate targeted delivery of these cytotoxic agents to cancer cells, thereby minimizing harm to healthy tissues.
Our prior research demonstrated that DOX-TRF can effectively bypass the resistance mechanisms cancer cells develop against anthracycline-based chemotherapy. However, the extent to which DOX-TRF induces programmed cell death, or apoptosis, in various cancer cell types remained largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the role of free radicals in DOX-TRF-mediated apoptosis. Employing a range of analytical techniques, including spectrometric, spectrofluorimetric, immunoenzymatic, and microscopic methods, we examined the temporal progression of apoptotic and necrotic events. Our investigation specifically focused on the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), intracellular calcium levels, cytochrome c release into the cytosol, and morphological changes in both leukemic and normal cells. These assessments were conducted both with and without the presence of the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC). We observed that DOX-TRF exhibits greater cytotoxicity towards leukemic cells compared to normal blood cells. Furthermore, our findings suggest that DOX-TRF's induction of apoptosis in human leukemia cells is intrinsically linked to the generation of free radicals and a disruption of cellular redox balance.
For this study, essential cell culture components like RPMI-1640 medium, FBS, and PBS were sourced from Lonza. Doxorubicin (DOX) was obtained from Sequoia Research Products. Key reagents for assays, including the XTT kit, H2DCF-DA, and JC-1, were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. The doxorubicin-transferrin (DOX-TRF) conjugate was synthesized using a modified procedure by Berczi et al. and subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry. Assay kits for cytochrome c, YO-PRO-1, and Fluo-4 AM were supplied by Invitrogen. Additional chemicals were acquired from POCH S.A., with tissue culture dishes coming from Corning.
For this study, three human leukemia cell lines were utilized: CCRF-CEM, an acute lymphoblastic leukemia line; K562, a chronic erythroleukemia line; and its doxorubicin-resistant counterpart, K562/DOX. Normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were also included in all experimental setups. These PBMCs were obtained from healthy, non-smoking men aged 23-35, isolated via Histopaque density gradient centrifugation. Both the leukemic and normal cells were cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) in RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with L-glutamine, penicillin, streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells in their logarithmic growth phase were employed throughout the experiments. The K562/DOX cell line was maintained with 0.02 μM doxorubicin as a selection agent, and all cell lines were regularly checked for mycoplasma contamination. In specific experiments, cells were pre-incubated for 1 hour with 3 mM N-acetylcysteine (NAC), an antioxidant, before the addition of either DOX or DOX-TRF. Incubation then continued for the necessary duration under identical conditions. Control groups received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) instead of the tested agents.
To assess cell viability, the XTT assay was employed. This method relies on the reduction of the tetrazolium salt XTT by metabolically active cells, yielding a water-soluble orange formazan product. For the assay, 10^4 leukemia cells (CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX) or 10^5 peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were plated in 96-well microplates. Following seeding in 0.1 ml of culture medium, cells were incubated with varying concentrations of either doxorubicin (DOX) or the doxorubicin-transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) for 72 hours. After this incubation, cells were centrifuged and the supernatant removed. Subsequently, 0.05 ml of XTT solution (0.3 mg/ml in medium) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 hours. The resulting formazan product was quantified by measuring absorbance at 492 nm using a microtitre plate reader. Cell viability was determined by comparing XTT reduction in treated cells to that of untreated control cells. Cytotoxicity was quantified as the IC50 value, representing the drug concentration that reduced cell viability by 50% relative to controls. These IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 4.03 software.
Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were quantified using the fluorescent probe H2DCF-DA. Cells, including PBMC, CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX, were incubated with DOX or DOX-TRF for varying durations (3-72 hours). Subsequently, cells were exposed to H2DCF-DA for 30 minutes at 37°C. ROS fluorescence, detected as DCF, was then measured using a Fluoroskan Ascent FL microplate reader.
To assess apoptosis and necrosis, cells were treated with DOX or DOX-TRF and subsequently stained with a combination of YO-PRO-1 iodide and propidium iodide (PI). This dual staining method leverages the differential membrane permeability of these dyes. YO-PRO-1 iodide, a green fluorescent dye, penetrates the compromised membranes of apoptotic cells, while PI, a red fluorescent dye, enters cells with severely damaged membranes, indicative of necrosis. Following treatment, cells were incubated with a mixture of YO-PRO-1 iodide and PI (0.1 μM each) on ice for 20 minutes. Analysis was then performed using flow cytometry, specifically the LSR II system from Becton Dickinson. The excitation wavelength of 488 nm allowed for the visualization of both green (YO-PRO-1) and red (PI) fluorescence. In this assay, viable cells displayed minimal green fluorescence. Apoptotic cells showed progressively increasing levels of green fluorescence, while necrotic cells exhibited both green and red fluorescence. Untreated cells served as negative controls, and campthotecin (10 μM) was used as a positive control for apoptosis. The percentage of apoptotic cells was quantified as the proportion of YO-PRO-1 stained cells within the total analyzed cell population, with 10,000 events recorded and analyzed per sample.
Cells were treated with DOX or DOX-TRF in 96-well plates. Following incubation with 7 μM Fluo-4 AM in DMSO for 60 min at 37 °C, fluorescence intensity was measured. Analysis was performed using a Fluoroskan Ascent FL microplate reader with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 525 nm.
Mitochondrial membrane potential changes were evaluated using the JC-1 fluorescence intensity ratio. Cells were initially incubated with 5 μM JC-1 in HBSS for 30 minutes. Following this, cells were centrifuged at 300 g for 30 minutes at 22 °C and washed twice with HBSS. Fluorescence measurements were then performed on a Fluoroskan Ascent plate reader, utilizing filter pairs at 530 nm/590 nm and 485 nm/538 nm. The data was presented as the ratio of fluorescence at 530 nm/590 nm (dimer) to that at 485 nm/538 nm (monomer), normalized against the control untreated cell fluorescence ratio, which was established as 100%.
To assess cytochrome c release from mitochondria, cellular fractions were separated following the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were then treated with DOX-TRF or DOX for 6, 12, or 24 hours. After centrifugation, cell pellets were homogenized with a cytosol extraction buffer. The resulting supernatant, containing the cytosolic protein fraction, was stored at -70 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis.
Cytosolic cytochrome c levels were quantified using a Cytochrome c Assay kit from R&D Systems. This colorimetric assay involved pre-coated 96-well plates and an anti-cytochrome c antibody, following the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm with a Cary 50 microplate reader.
Statistical analysis involved expressing data as means ± S.D. Multiple comparisons were performed using ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. All statistical calculations utilized the STATISTICA program. Significance was determined by a p-value < 0.05. Viability curves were fitted using a four-parameter logistic equation in GraphPad Prism 4.03.
To evaluate the cytotoxic effects of doxorubicin (DOX) and its transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF), normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and leukemia-derived cell lines (CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX) were treated with varying concentrations of each compound. A dose-dependent reduction in cell viability was observed for both DOX and DOX-TRF across all tested cell types, as determined by the XTT assay. Notably, DOX-TRF exhibited significantly lower cytotoxicity towards normal PBMCs, being approximately two times less potent than free DOX. Conversely, DOX-TRF demonstrated enhanced efficacy against leukemia-derived cells, with considerably lower IC50 values compared to DOX. Specifically, the mean IC50 ratios favored DOX-TRF by 2.4-fold in CCRF-CEM cells and 3.7-fold in K562 cells. This difference was even more pronounced in the doxorubicin-resistant K562/DOX cell line, where DOX-TRF was 9.9 times more cytotoxic than DOX. Furthermore, pre-incubation with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) provided partial protection against the cytotoxicity of both DOX and DOX-TRF in leukemia cells. This protection was more substantial for DOX-TRF, reducing its cytotoxicity by approximately 12–30%, compared to a 5–15% reduction for DOX. These findings suggest a differential impact of DOX-TRF on normal versus cancerous cells, with a marked increase in potency against leukemia cells, particularly those exhibiting resistance to conventional doxorubicin treatment.
The generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was examined over time in normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and the leukemia-derived cell lines CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX following exposure to either free doxorubicin (DOX) or the doxorubicin-transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF). Using the fluorescent probe H2DCF-DA, which detects DCF fluorescence, we observed a significant increase in ROS levels in PBMCs after 3 to 24 hours of DOX treatment. In contrast, a similar increase in ROS in PBMCs was noted only after 24 hours of DOX-TRF exposure. Within the leukemia-derived cell lines, ROS production peaked at 12 hours after treatment with DOX-TRF. Specifically, CCRF-CEM cells showed a roughly 1.3-fold increase in ROS, while K562 cells exhibited a 1.5-fold increase. Interestingly, no significant differences in ROS levels were found between cells treated with DOX and those treated with DOX-TRF in these leukemia cell lines. However, a notable difference emerged in the doxorubicin-resistant K562/DOX cell line, where DOX-TRF induced a 1.3-fold higher ROS production compared to free DOX. To ascertain the role of ROS in the observed cytotoxicity of both DOX and DOX-TRF, cells were pre-treated with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC). This pretreatment effectively attenuated the drug-induced elevations in ROS levels across all cell types tested. The data presented in Table 1 detail these findings, showing the percentage of DCF fluorescence relative to control cells at various time points and treatment conditions. The table highlights significant increases in ROS compared to control cells (marked with *) and significant reductions in ROS when NAC was included (marked with +). Furthermore, specific comparisons between DOX and DOX-TRF treatments in the K562/DOX cell line revealed significant differences in ROS production (marked with #).
To assess the induction of apoptosis and necrosis, normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and leukemia-derived cell lines (CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX) were treated with either DOX or DOX-TRF at their respective IC50 concentrations for up to 72 hours. Following treatment, flow cytometry using YO-PRO-1/PI double staining was employed to differentiate between three distinct cell populations: viable cells exhibiting green fluorescence, apoptotic cells showing yellow fluorescence, and necrotic cells displaying red-brown fluorescence. This method allowed for the quantification of early and late apoptotic and necrotic cells. Concurrently, cellular morphology was examined via fluorescence microscopy. The findings revealed that DOX-TRF was more effective than free DOX in inducing characteristic apoptotic features, such as chromatin condensation and nuclear fragmentation, in the tested cell lines. This was visually confirmed through fluorescence microscopy, which depicted these morphological changes more prominently in cells treated with DOX-TRF. The inclusion of the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) in parallel experiments demonstrated a reduction in both apoptotic and necrotic cell numbers, suggesting the involvement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the drug-induced cell death pathways. The presence of NAC also mitigated the observed morphological changes indicative of apoptosis. The data presented in Figure 2 and Figure 3 further illustrate these findings, detailing the quantitative effects of DOX and DOX-TRF on cell death populations and the impact of NAC pre-treatment on these outcomes.
Apoptosis and necrosis levels remained low, below 5%, in both normal and leukemia-derived cells after 3-6 hours of exposure to DOX or DOX-TRF. However, prolonged incubation (12-72 hours) with either drug led to an increase in these cell death markers. Specifically, DOX treatment of PBMCs resulted in up to 30% necrotic cells after 72 hours. Leukemia-derived cell lines treated with DOX-TRF showed peak apoptosis rates at 48 hours, reaching 21.6% in CCRF-CEM and 17.5% in K562 cells. Notably, the DOX-resistant K562/DOX cells exhibited higher apoptosis percentages with DOX-TRF (19.2%) compared to DOX (10.1%) at 48 hours, indicating that the conjugate induced apoptosis even in resistant cells. The inclusion of the antioxidant NAC reduced the number of apoptotic and necrotic cells in both normal and leukemia-derived cell types, suggesting a role for reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the drug-induced apoptosis.
To determine the role of intracellular calcium ion fluctuations in apoptosis induced by DOX-TRF and DOX, we employed the fluorescence probe Fluo-4-AM to measure free Ca2+ levels. Our findings indicated that in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), both DOX and DOX-TRF treatments resulted in comparable increases in free cytoplasmic Ca2+ after 24 hours of incubation, reaching approximately 26% (Table 2). In the leukemia-derived cell lines, DOX-TRF treatment led to an elevation in intracellular free Ca2+ within the initial 24 hours. At this time point, leukemia cells treated with the IC50 concentration of DOX-TRF exhibited an approximate increase in Ca2+ levels of 30% for CCRF-CEM cells, 33% for K562 cells, and 25% for K562/DOX cells (p < 0.05; Table 2). Furthermore, pre-treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a free radical scavenger, significantly attenuated the DOX-TRF and DOX-induced alterations in intracellular calcium. This observation suggests a connection between free radical generation and the observed changes in calcium levels. Table 2 details the effect of DOX and DOX-TRF, with and without NAC, on intracellular Ca2+ levels across different cell types and incubation times. The data presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments highlight significant differences compared to control cells (* p < 0.05), protective effects of NAC (+ p < 0.05), and differences between DOX and DOX-TRF treatments (# p < 0.05). The intensity of Fluo-4 AM fluorescence in control cells was normalized to 100%.
Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) were evaluated in normal PBMCs and leukemia-derived cell lines (CCRF-CEM, K562, K562/DOX) following DOX or DOX-TRF treatment. This assessment utilized the fluorescence probe JC-1. A profound decrease in membrane potential, indicated by a reduced JC-1 dimer to monomer fluorescence ratio, was observed when cells were pre-incubated with CCCP, a known uncoupler of oxidative phosphorylation, serving as a positive control.
The study investigated the impact of Doxorubicin-Transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) and free Doxorubicin (DOX) on mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) across various cell types, including normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and leukemia-derived cell lines (CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX). Time-dependent alterations in Δψm were observed following treatment with both DOX and DOX-TRF, as detailed in Table 3. In normal PBMCs, a significant collapse of Δψm occurred after 6 hours of DOX exposure, reaching 78% of the control value. DOX-TRF induced a similar, though slightly delayed, decline in Δψm in normal cells, with a maximal reduction to 86% observed at 24 hours. Leukemia-derived cell lines exhibited varying responses. In CCRF-CEM cells, JC-1 fluorescence intensity, indicative of Δψm, decreased by 21% at 6 hours post-DOX treatment. K562 cells showed a 26% reduction in fluorescence intensity at 12 hours after DOX exposure. The doxorubicin-resistant K562/DOX cell line displayed the most pronounced drop in Δψm, with a maximal decrease detected after 24 hours of DOX-TRF treatment. Following prolonged incubation periods (48–72 hours), the fluorescence intensity of JC-1 in all cell lines tested showed a recovery, returning to approximately 95% of the control value. This suggests that the initial collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential is a transient event. To elucidate the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in these mitochondrial changes, cells were pre-incubated with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC). The observation that NAC pre-treatment conferred a protective effect on Δψm strongly indicates that the observed drop in mitochondrial membrane potential is ROS-dependent. Further confirmation of these Δψm changes was obtained through fluorescence microscopy. Figure 4 illustrates that DOX-TRF treatment led to a substantial increase in green fluorescence, corresponding to JC-1 monomers, within the leukemia-derived cells. This phenomenon is characteristic of a reduced mitochondrial membrane potential. Conversely, untreated control cells, which maintained a high mitochondrial potential, predominantly exhibited red fluorescence, indicative of JC-1 dimers. This microscopic evidence visually supports the quantitative data obtained from JC-1 fluorescence measurements. The findings collectively demonstrate that both DOX and DOX-TRF impact mitochondrial membrane potential, with DOX-TRF showing a more pronounced effect in malignant cells and this effect being mediated by ROS.
The release of cytochrome c from mitochondria into the cytosol was investigated using an ELISA assay kit following 6, 12, and 24-hour treatments with DOX-TRF or DOX in normal PBMCs and the leukemia-derived cell lines CCRF-CEM, K562, and K562/DOX. In normal cells, DOX treatment resulted in a progressive rise in cytochrome c levels, reaching 38.9 ng/mg protein at 12 hours and 50.8 ng/mg protein at 24 hours. DOX-TRF exposure in PBMCs also led to an increase in cytochrome c, measuring 40.2 ng/mg protein after 24 hours. Significantly higher increases in cytosolic cytochrome c were observed in the leukemia-derived cell lines CCRF-CEM and K562 following 24-hour treatment with DOX-TRF, with levels reaching 590.1 and 679.0 ng/mg protein, respectively. In contrast, the maximal release of cytochrome c from mitochondria in these same cell lines after DOX treatment occurred at 12 hours, with levels of 415.5 ng/mg protein for CCRF-CEM and 451.6 ng/mg protein for K562.
DOX-TRF induced cytochrome c release from mitochondria in K562/DOX cells throughout the study period. In contrast, free DOX resulted in a peak cytosolic cytochrome c increase at 12 hours, reaching 560.5 ng/mg protein. This suggests that cytosolic cytochrome c levels do not directly correlate with cellular sensitivity to these agents. However, our findings do establish a time-dependent link between reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and cytochrome c release when cells are treated with DOX-TRF.
The emergence of drug-resistant cell populations is a significant challenge in cancer treatment, often linked to the overexpression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) proteins. Consequently, developing novel strategies to overcome this resistance is crucial. Utilizing specific drug carriers, including liposomes, dendrimers, nanoparticles, or plasma proteins, represents a promising avenue for achieving this goal.
Our prior research established a link between the enhanced cytotoxicity of the DOX-TRF conjugate and its impact on P-glycoprotein function, a key ABC transporter. This suggests that the conjugate's distinct mechanism of action might bypass issues like cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and drug resistance. However, the specific type of cell death induced by DOX-TRF remained largely uncharacterized. Consequently, we investigated DOX-TRF's induction of cell death, focusing on its relationship with reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Our findings indicate that DOX-TRF can trigger both apoptosis and necrosis in the tested cells. Extended exposure to DOX-TRF led to a greater apoptotic response compared to similar durations of DOX treatment. ROS generation was observed to be partly responsible for the cytotoxicity of both DOX-TRF and DOX. In leukemia cells treated with either compound, ROS arose from both normal metabolic processes and the activation of ROS-producing enzymes. DOX-TRF demonstrated its highest toxicity against K562/DOX cells, which are inherently resistant to DOX. Specifically, K562 and CCRF-CEM cells exhibited 2.6 and 4 times less sensitivity to DOX than to DOX-TRF, respectively. In the case of K562/DOX cells, DOX-TRF was ten times more cytotoxic than DOX. Furthermore, with an IC50 exceeding 1000 nM in normal blood cells, DOX-TRF appears to exert a more significant detrimental effect on leukemic cells compared to peripheral blood mononuclear cells.
The involvement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and subsequent oxidative stress in the initiation and execution of apoptosis by anticancer drugs is well-established. Our findings demonstrate that incorporating the free radical scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC) into DOX formulations significantly reduced the proportion of apoptotic and necrotic cells, as well as the observed apoptotic characteristics. These results align with prior research by Rogalska et al. [18], which indicated that aclarubicin's ability to generate free radicals impacts the apoptotic process in human solid tumors. The presence of para-quinone and para-hydroquinone groups within anthracyclines suggests that ROS generation can be triggered by either the reduction or oxidation of the drug itself.
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was quantified using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF) fluorescence. DOX-TRF induced ROS production in leukemic cells after extended incubation periods. In contrast, free DOX elevated DCF fluorescence in leukemia-derived cell lines within 6 to 12 hours and in PBMCs after 24 hours. Previous research has demonstrated reduced free radical toxicity to normal cells, such as cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, when treated with an epirubicin conjugate of polyethyleneglycol and nitric oxide. This conjugate selectively targeted Caco2 cancer cells, leaving normal cells unharmed.
Our investigation delved into the biochemical alterations accompanying DOX-TRF-induced apoptosis in both normal and leukemia cells. Specifically, we examined changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), intracellular calcium levels, and the cytosolic fraction of cytochrome c. A decrease in Δψm is recognized as a key indicator of mitochondrial involvement in apoptosis. Our findings revealed that a reduction in Δψm occurred within the initial 6 hours of DOX or DOX-TRF exposure in the tested leukemia cell lines. For peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and the doxorubicin-resistant K562/DOX cell line, Δψm declined after 24 and 12 hours of DOX-TRF treatment, respectively. Notably, Δψm gradually recovered across all cell types after extended incubation periods (48–72 hours). These results underscore Δψm loss as an early event in the apoptotic process, triggered by both DOX and DOX-TRF. Furthermore, DOX-TRF demonstrated superior efficacy in depolarizing mitochondria within malignant cells compared to DOX. Prior research has also documented a decrease in Δψm following DOX-induced apoptosis in various human tumor cells and specific cell lines like MTLn3 and LLC-PK1.
Intracellular calcium (Ca2+) is widely recognized as a critical signaling molecule in human cancer cells. An increase in intracellular Ca2+ can initiate events leading to cell damage or apoptosis. Previous research has demonstrated that certain chemotherapy agents, such as aclarubicin and ara-cytidine-59-alkylphosphono-phosphates, can elevate intracellular Ca2+ levels in various cell types. In this study, we observed a rapid increase in cellular Ca2+ levels following treatment with both DOX and DOX-TRF. This rise in Ca2+ was concurrently associated with a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) across all tested cell types.
The release of cytochrome c from mitochondria is a critical step in the apoptotic pathway. Normally residing in the mitochondrial intermembranous space, cytochrome c is loosely associated with the inner membrane. Previous studies have documented its release into the cytosol following doxorubicin (DOX) treatment. While apoptosis can proceed independently of cytochrome c, its translocation to the cytosol typically initiates a cascade involving APAF1 and procaspase-9, ultimately activating caspase-3. This mechanism, whereby anthracyclines elevate cytosolic cytochrome c levels, is a known inducer of apoptosis in cancer cells. Our findings demonstrate that both the DOX-transferrin conjugate (DOX-TRF) and free DOX facilitate the efflux of cytochrome c from mitochondria.
Childs et al. [41] have demonstrated doxorubicin's capacity to release cytochrome c into the cytosol. Their findings suggest doxorubicin induces oxidative stress and mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. Furthermore, they observed that mitochondria can mount adaptive responses to protect cardiac myocytes in vivo.
DOX-TRF induces apoptosis in leukemia cells via ROS-dependent mechanisms. It demonstrates reduced toxicity to normal cells while exhibiting enhanced cytotoxicity against leukemia cells compared to free DOX. Transferrin effectively facilitates targeted doxorubicin delivery.
