The EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources (ANS) has re-evaluated the safety of sucrose acetate isobutyrate (SAIB, E 444) as a food additive, issuing a new scientific opinion. Previously, the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) set an Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 10 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day in 1994, while the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) established a higher ADI of 20 mg/kg bw per day in 1997. Both earlier bodies, and the current Panel, agreed that liver effects observed in dogs were not significant for human safety assessments. The Panel's re-evaluation found that SAIB is absorbed orally at approximately 70% in rats, 50% in dogs, and at least 88% in humans. No genotoxicity concerns were raised by SAIB. Human studies involving 14-day exposure to 20 mg/kg bw per day showed no adverse effects on clinical signs, hematology, or clinical chemistry. Based on comprehensive chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, and reproductive and developmental studies in rats, the Panel identified a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg/kg bw per day, which was the highest dose tested. Applying an uncertainty factor of 100 to this NOAEL, an ADI of 20 mg/kg bw per day for SAIB was established. This updated ADI represents a revision from the 10 mg/kg bw per day allocated by the SCF in 1994, supported by current data on absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, genotoxicity, subchronic, developmental, and long-term toxicity, and carcinogenicity. The Panel concluded that SAIB's use as a food additive at permitted or reported levels does not pose a safety concern, as the ADI is not exceeded in any population group.

Sucrose acetate isobutyrate (SAIB, E 444) is approved as a food additive within the European Union. Its authorization aligns with Annex II of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, and Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012 outlines its specific purity criteria.
In 1997, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) set an Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) for SAIB at 0–20 mg/kg body weight per day. This decision, following their 1993 evaluation, was based on a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day from chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity studies in rats, using an uncertainty factor of 100. JECFA also determined that liver effects observed in dogs were not pertinent to human safety.
In 1994, the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) assessed SAIB, utilizing the same data as JECFA. They determined that liver effects observed in dogs were largely irrelevant for human safety evaluations. Based on a chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity rat study, the SCF established a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 1,000 mg/kg body weight per day. This led to an Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 10 mg/kg body weight, incorporating an uncertainty factor of 100.
TemaNord's 2002 assessment of SAIB as a food additive concluded that, excluding dogs—deemed unsuitable for human risk evaluation—no treatment-related effects were observed in the available studies.
Research using 14C-SAIB, labeled at the sucrose part, showed that SAIB is quickly absorbed after oral intake in rats, dogs, and humans. Humans exhibited the highest absorption, at 88% or more, while rats absorbed about 70%, and dogs approximately 50%. Once absorbed, SAIB undergoes de-esterification in the gastrointestinal tract, primarily in the small intestine, by non-specific esterases. This process breaks SAIB down into sucrose and partially acylated sucrose, both of which are then readily absorbed. A notable amount of unabsorbed SAIB and its partially de-esterified forms were expelled through feces, especially in dogs. Absorbed radioactivity was primarily eliminated in human exhaled air as carbon dioxide (44–66% of the dose), with lesser amounts appearing in urine (15–21%) and feces (10%). In dogs, a small fraction of absorbed radioactivity was also excreted in bile. No toxicokinetic data were available for SAIB labeled at the isobutyrate moiety.
High doses of SAIB did not cause death in rats or mice, indicating its low acute toxicity.
When evaluating the safety of SAIB for human consumption, the primary concern has been its impact on canine hepatobiliary function. However, these effects are specific to dogs and have not been observed in other species, including mice, rats, monkeys, or humans. This species-specific response likely stems from metabolic differences between dogs and other animals. Given these discrepancies in animal studies and the absence of adverse effects in human clinical trials, it is improbable that SAIB, when used as a food additive, would pose a risk to human health.
The Panel concluded that SAIB is not genotoxic, based on both in vitro and in vivo studies.
High doses of SAIB were well-tolerated by rats, mice, and monkeys over extended periods, showing no significant toxicity. The NOAELs were 2,000 mg/kg bw/day for rats, 2,500 mg/kg bw/day for mice, and 2,400 mg/kg bw/day for monkeys. While liver function effects were noted in dogs at single oral exposures of 20 mg/kg bw or more, these were not observed in monkeys. The Panel deemed these dog-specific hepatic effects largely irrelevant to human safety.
In a 52-week rat study, the Panel determined that a minor, non-adverse reduction in body weight and feed intake was likely due to palatability. Consequently, 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day was established as the No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) for this study.
In human studies, a daily dose of 20 mg/kg body weight of SAIB for 14 days showed no adverse effects on clinical signs, blood parameters, or liver function.
Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies showed no adverse effects on fertility, reproduction, maternal toxicity, or development in rats at doses up to 2,000 mg/kg bw/day, or in rabbits at 1,200 mg/kg bw/day.
An Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 20 mg/kg body weight per day for SAIB was determined. This was derived by applying a 100-fold uncertainty factor to the No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day, which was the highest dose tested in chronic, carcinogenicity, reproductive, and developmental rat studies.
The Panel determined that existing data on SAIB's absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, genotoxicity, and various toxicities (subchronic, reproductive, developmental, long-term, and carcinogenicity) warrant increasing the ADI from 10 mg/kg bw/day (set by SCF in 1994) to 20 mg/kg bw/day.
The safety of SAIB (E 444) as a food additive was evaluated by estimating dietary exposure through two primary methods. One approach involved calculating exposure based on the maximum levels permitted by EU legislation, termed the regulatory maximum level exposure assessment scenario. The second method, the refined exposure assessment scenario, utilized actual usage data provided by the industry. It was noted that EFSA lacked information on analyzed SAIB levels for the refined scenario. Crucially, the exposure estimates derived from both scenarios consistently remained below the established Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 20 mg/kg body weight per day. Consequently, the Panel determined that SAIB's use as a food additive, at its authorized or reported levels, poses no safety concerns, as the ADI is not surpassed in any demographic.

SAIB (E 444) is a distilled blend of reaction products. It's created by esterifying food-grade sucrose with acetic and isobutyric anhydrides, as per EU regulation.
Reynolds and Chappel (1998) state that SAIB, a mixture of up to 256 esters, primarily consists of sucrose (acetate)2 (isobutyrate)6, with sucrose (acetate)6 (isobutyrate)2 present in smaller quantities.
Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012 specifies SAIB's molecular formula as C40H62O19, with an approximate molecular weight of 832–856 g/mol. This formula, corresponding to 846.9 g/mol, indicates a 2:6 acetate to isobutyrate ratio. Its EINECS number is 204-771-6.
E 444, also known as SAIB, is assigned CAS Registry Number 126-13-6. Its most representative structural formula, with an acetate-to-isobutyrate ratio of 2:6, is shown in Figure 1.
JECFA specifications (2006) list three CAS Registry Numbers for SAIB: 126-13-6, 27216-37-1, and 137204-24-1. CAS Registry Number 27216-37-1 denotes an incompletely defined substance sharing the molecular formula and EINECS 204-771-6 with CAS 126-13-6. Meanwhile, CAS Registry Number 137204-24-1 identifies a sucrose ester of acetic and isobutyric acids, where the molecular ratios of these acids are not specified.
Sucrose acetate isobutyrate (SAIB) has several alternative names. These include saccharose acetate isobutyrate, sucrose diacetate hexaisobutyrate, and Sustane SAIB MCT, among others.
SAIB is a clear, pale straw-colored liquid with a bland odor, described as very viscous at room temperature. It is water-insoluble but dissolves in most organic solvents. Industry data indicates a log Pow range of 3.4 to 7 for this substance.
Purity criteria for SAIB (E 444) are outlined in Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012 and JECFA (2006). These specifications detail the acid value, saponification value, and triacetin content, in addition to assay methods and identification parameters.
The Panel highlighted that EU specifications for SAIB (E 444) permit arsenic, lead, cadmium, and mercury impurities at concentrations up to 3, 2, 1, and 1 mg/kg, respectively. Such contamination levels are concerning because exposure to these metals, even at these permitted amounts, could significantly impact overall intake, which already approaches EFSA's health-based guidance values.
Sucrose acetate isobutyrate (SAIB) is produced through the controlled esterification of food-grade sucrose with acetic and isobutyric anhydrides, followed by distillation. The molar ratio of acetate to isobutyrate in this process is approximately 2:6.
SAIB production involves reacting sucrose with acetic and isobutyric anhydrides, using a catalyst. Following this, the resulting acetic and isobutyric acid co-products are eliminated. The mixture then undergoes activated carbon treatment before final distillation. Industry sources corroborate this manufacturing process.
Methods exist to analyze SAIB in soft drinks and food additive premixes.
Conacher and Chadha (1972) developed a quick method to quantify SAIB in soft drinks. This involved extracting SAIB with diethyl ether, then analyzing it via gas chromatography with trimyristin as a standard. Despite SAIB producing multiple peaks, its total chromatographic area showed a consistent response relative to the standard. The method achieved SAIB recoveries between 94% and 98%, with a calculated LOQ of 1.6 mg/L.
A subsequent study by Conacher et al. (1973) detailed SAIB analysis in soft drinks using GC. This involved transesterification of sucrose esters with decanol, followed by quantification of dodecyl acetate and dodecyl isobutyrate. Recoveries were high (95.5–98%) for SAIB concentrations near maximum permitted levels, and commercial samples showed levels below 24 mg/L.
Uematsu et al. (2001) developed a method for detecting SAIB in food and additive premixes. This involved solid-phase extraction followed by GC with a flame ionization detector or GC-mass spectrometry, without derivatization. The reported detection limit was 100 mg/L. However, the Panel found this method insufficient for current EU legislation compliance, as its quantification limit matches the 300 mg/L maximum permitted level.
While data on SAIB's reactions in food are absent, studies show it remains stable when heated to 180-200°C in vegetable oil. This specific application, however, is not currently permitted under EU regulations.

To determine dietary exposure to SAIB (E 444), the maximum permitted levels (MPLs) for each food category were multiplied by the consumption amount per kilogram of body weight for each person in the Comprehensive Database. The daily total exposure for each individual was then calculated by summing the exposures across all food categories. These daily exposure estimates were subsequently averaged over the duration of the survey to yield an individual's average daily exposure. Surveys with only one day of data per subject were omitted, as they were deemed insufficient for evaluating repeated exposure.
Individual exposure to SAIB was evaluated for each survey and population group, generating comprehensive distributions. From these distributions, both mean and 95th percentile exposure levels were determined for the overall population and specific groups. However, the 95th percentile was only computed for population groups with adequate sample sizes. Consequently, this metric was omitted for Italian infants and for toddlers in Belgium, Italy, and Spain within this assessment.

The metabolic fate and elimination of SAIB were investigated in studies by Reynolds et al. (1974) and Phillips et al. (1976). These investigations involved oral dosing with 14C-SAIB, where the sucrose component was radiolabeled. An overview in Table 8 details the radioactivity elimination across various species and at different dose levels, providing insights into absorption.
Research by Reynolds et al. (1974) and Phillips et al. (1976) investigated the gastrointestinal absorption of 14C-SAIB in rats, dogs, and humans following oral administration. These studies consistently showed partial absorption of the radioactive compound across all species. In rats, absorption rates varied with dosage: approximately 71% at 11.2 mg/kg bw and 76% at 5.8 mg/kg bw were observed, calculated from the sum of radioactivity in exhaled air, urine, and carcass. Similar absorption, around 70%, was noted at 26-28 mg/kg bw, though carcass data were not available for these doses. Higher doses of 14C-SAIB (89-98 mg/kg bw) administered in corn oil resulted in a reduced absorption of about 40%. Phillips et al. (1976) further supported these findings in rats, reporting 67% absorption of radioactivity after a 50 mg/kg bw dose over one day. However, earlier measurements at 6 hours indicated incomplete absorption or elimination, with most radioactivity still present in the carcass and gastrointestinal tract.
Dogs eliminated approximately 50% of the administered dose in faeces. However, bile excretion accounted for an additional 6% of the dose at similar levels (3-5 mg/kg bw). This indicates that absorption rates in dogs were slightly above 50%.
Human studies by Reynolds et al. (1974) indicated high absorption (≥88%) of SAIB at doses of 0.2–1.2 mg/kg bw. However, the available documentation for these findings was limited to the data in Table 8, lacking additional details or explanations.
In vitro investigations by Phillips et al. (1976) explored SAIB's fate using rat tissues and gut microflora. Within six hours, about 75% of SAIB underwent significant hydrolysis when exposed to small intestine contents under anaerobic conditions. Intestinal mucosa homogenates, under aerobic conditions, yielded comparable results. This hydrolysis was attributed to non-specific esterases within the intestinal mucosa, not gut microflora. Human faecal homogenates (1 mg SAIB/mL) hydrolyzed 40% of 14C-SAIB over 16 hours. Conversely, liver homogenates exhibited minimal hydrolysis.
Studies using 14C-labeled SAIB (at the sucrose part) demonstrated varying absorption rates across species. In rats, approximately 70% of the radioactivity was absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, with absorption efficiency decreasing at higher doses. Humans exhibited a higher absorption rate of at least 88%, while dogs showed a lower rate of about 50%. SAIB undergoes metabolism primarily in the small intestine, starting in the duodenum and proximal jejunum, though the main absorption site is in the lower digestive tract. Its breakdown products in the gut, such as partially de-esterified SAIB, acetate, isobutyrate, sucrose, fructose, and glucose, are readily absorbed.
Following single oral doses of 14C-SAIB, a small amount of radioactivity was detected in rat carcasses after three days. This residual radioactivity, distributed evenly throughout the body, accounted for 6% of the administered dose and approximately 10% of the absorbed dose that was not eliminated. This observation aligns with findings from studies using 14C-sucrose, where rats incorporated about 11% of the pure 14C-sucrose dose. Therefore, the radioactivity remaining in SAIB-exposed rats is likely due to the integration of the radiolabel from sucrose into cellular components. Interestingly, the peak catabolism of 14C-SAIB to 14CO2 occurred later than that of 14C-sucrose, suggesting that SAIB requires hydrolysis before absorption.
Oral administration of 14C-SAIB to rats resulted in significant hydrolysis within 24 hours. A substantial portion (45%) of the administered radioactivity was metabolized into 14CO2, suggesting complete hydrolysis and physiological utilization. While 33% of the radioactivity was found in faeces, two-thirds of this retained the chromatographic characteristics of SAIB. The caecum exhibited the highest degree of hydrolysis into sucrose and other less mobile metabolites.
In a study by Reynolds (1961), rats were given a single dose of SAIB, either unlabeled or labeled with 14C on the sucrose part, to track how it was eliminated. While the unlabeled SAIB studies were not definitive, the 14C-labeled SAIB revealed a rapid elimination process in rats, primarily through excretion and metabolism. At lower doses, fecal excretion was the main route for SAIB disposal. When absorbed, the sucrose component of SAIB was quickly broken down, with 26% of the dose becoming carbon dioxide and 33% appearing in the urine as sucrose or sucrose esters. This suggests that SAIB undergoes intestinal degradation before absorption. Overall elimination was nearly complete within four days, with 90-93% of the dose removed. Approximately half of the administered dose was excreted unchanged in the feces, alongside various metabolites. The majority of the radioactivity found in the urine was in the form of sucrose, with minimal or no glucose present.
Reynolds and Zeigler (1977) administered a single dose of 14C-SAIB to dogs, rats, and humans. Urinary chromatography revealed no 14C-labeled molecules larger than sucrose, indicating their absence as metabolites in these species.
A male volunteer ingested a single dose of 1.18 mg 14C-SAIB/kg body weight. Urine samples, collected before and 6.2 hours after dosing, were analyzed using chromatography. The analysis revealed two primary, yet unidentified, SAIB metabolites. Notably, glucose, fructose, and their esters were absent. This study also highlighted a stronger similarity in the disposition and urinary excretion products between humans and rats compared to dogs.
In human studies, neither SAIB nor its metabolites were found in the feces of a volunteer consuming 0.1 g/day for seven days. Similarly, unlabelled SAIB, administered as a single dose or repeatedly for seven days at 0.2 or 1 g, resulted in no detectable sucrose or sucrose esters in the urine of volunteers.
Research by Reynolds et al. (1974) investigated SAIB excretion patterns across humans, dogs, and rats. A consistent finding was the swift absorption of SAIB in all three species. However, notable differences emerged in the elimination routes. Dogs excreted a significantly larger fraction of radioactivity through faeces compared to humans and rats, particularly at doses up to 28 mg/kg body weight. Across all species, over half of the absorbed SAIB was eliminated as 14CO2 in exhaled breath. While rats and humans showed comparable proportions of radioactivity eliminated through breath and urine, dogs exhibited lower urinary excretion. Specifically, rats and humans excreted 11–21% of the administered dose in urine, whereas dogs only eliminated 6–7%. The excretion of radioactivity in urine was rapid across all species, peaking immediately after exposure and largely completing within 15 hours. Similarly, 14CO2 elimination began quickly, reaching its peak between 7–8 hours in rats and 8–16 hours in humans and dogs. After 30 hours, only trace amounts (less than 0.5%) of the applied radioactivity remained. In humans, neither additional exposure to unlabelled SAIB for seven days nor food intake affected radioactivity elimination. In rats, higher doses led to a decrease in exhaled 14CO2 and a slight increase in urinary elimination.
Studies using 14C-SAIB, labeled at the sucrose part, showed quick oral absorption across species. Humans exhibited the highest absorption, at 88% or more, while rats absorbed about 70%, and dogs approximately 50%. SAIB undergoes de-esterification primarily in the small intestine by non-specific esterases, yielding sucrose and partially acylated sucrose, both of which are easily absorbed. A notable amount of SAIB, both ingested and partially de-esterified, was expelled in faeces, especially in dogs. Absorbed radioactivity was mostly excreted in urine, with a small amount appearing in bile in dogs. In humans given 0.1–2 mg/kg body weight of 14C-SAIB, 44–66% of the absorbed radioactivity was exhaled as carbon dioxide, with 15–21% eliminated in urine and 10% in faeces. Data on SAIB labeled at the isobutyrate moiety were unavailable.
SAIB exhibited low acute toxicity in rats, with an LD50 exceeding 5,000 mg/kg body weight, as documented in various studies.
Cynomolgus monkeys (two males and two females) were administered single, escalating doses of SAIB in orange juice, ranging from 1.25 to 20 g SAIB/kg body weight, with 72-hour intervals between doses. Although vomiting and diarrhea were observed at lower doses, these effects were attributed to the orange juice vehicle. No fatalities occurred, and post-mortem examination revealed no SAIB-related adverse effects. The animals were euthanized 48 hours following the final dose.
Rats and mice, given single gavage doses of SAIB (400–25,600 mg/kg bw in corn oil), showed no mortality during a two-week observation.
High doses of SAIB caused only minor clinical effects in monkeys, and no mortality in rats or mice. This indicates low acute oral toxicity.

Dogs exhibit a distinct reaction to oral SAIB exposure compared to other species. Subchronic feeding studies showed that SAIB, at dietary levels of 0.5% or more (equivalent to 225 mg/kg bw per day), led to increased liver weight and impaired liver function, evidenced by elevated BSP retention and higher serum alkaline phosphatase activity. Histopathological examination of the liver also revealed no alterations, even at doses as high as 1,800 mg/kg bw per day.
Studies in rats revealed no adverse effects from SAIB. For instance, a Procter and Chappel (1998) study found that even the highest dietary SAIB concentration of 10% (equivalent to 9,000 mg/kg bw per day) did not cause toxicologically significant issues. While a decrease in male body weight gain was observed, it was not dose-dependent and likely linked to the feed's palatability, not SAIB. Food consumption effects were inconsistent. Furthermore, measurements of urinary ascorbic acid excretion and zoxazolamine hypnotic effects, indicators of liver enzyme induction, yielded negative results, confirming no such induction occurred. This 10% dietary level was thus deemed the No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) by the authors.
Monkeys, rats, and mice exhibited no toxicological effects even at high doses of SAIB, with monkeys tolerating up to 10,000 mg/kg bw per day in subacute gavage studies. In contrast, dogs displayed hepatobiliary dysfunction at a much lower dose of 225 mg/kg bw per day. These species-specific responses highlight the differing sensitivities to SAIB. Genotoxicity studies conducted in vitro are detailed in Table 10.
In multiple in vitro tests, SAIB demonstrated no genotoxic effects. It did not cause mutations in Salmonella Typhimurium strains (TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537, TA1538), even with metabolic activation. Furthermore, SAIB did not induce forward mutations at the HPRT locus in CHO cells, nor did it lead to chromosomal aberrations in these cells. Studies on rat hepatocytes also indicated no DNA damage, as evidenced by the absence of unscheduled DNA synthesis. The lack of testing in TA102 or E. coli WP2 was noted, but SAIB is not anticipated to have oxidizing or cross-linking properties.
The Panel observed that the genotoxicity assessment for numerical chromosomal aberrations (aneugenicity) was incomplete, primarily due to the absence of an in vitro micronucleus assay. While chromosomal aberration assays primarily identify structural changes, the existing in vitro assay showed no mitotic disturbances. Consequently, the Panel deemed the omission of an in vitro micronucleus assay to be of minor concern.
To evaluate SAIB's genotoxic properties, a rat dominant lethal assay was conducted. Male Sprague-Dawley rats, 20 per group, received single oral gavage doses of SAIB (20, 200, or 2,000 mg/kg bw) in corn oil, alongside a solvent-treated control group.
After SAIB administration, male rats were sequentially bred with two virgin females for seven weeks, five days a week. Following each five-day mating period, females were separated from males and housed individually until sacrifice. Fourteen days post-separation, the females were sacrificed, and their uteri were examined for early and late fetal deaths, as well as total implantations. The SAIB-treated groups exhibited no significant differences in pregnancy rate, early deaths per pregnancy, or mean total implantations compared to the solvent control. The positive control group, however, demonstrated clear positive results. The study concluded that SAIB did not induce dominant lethal mutations in rats under the experimental conditions, a conclusion the Panel concurred with.
The study also investigated chromosomal aberrations in peripheral blood lymphocytes, sampled 72 hours post-treatment. Despite the limited number of metaphases analyzed (10 per animal, 50 per group), the findings suggested SAIB did not induce clastogenicity. Consequently, the Panel concluded that SAIB poses no genotoxicity concern. Chronic toxicity data is summarized in Table 11.
Dogs exhibited hepatobiliary dysfunction in a subchronic study at 225 mg/kg bw per day, a dose significantly lower than the NOAEL for other species. In contrast, rodents (mice and rats) and monkeys showed no such effects on hepatobiliary function, even when exposed to the highest tested doses.
Monkeys, rats, and mice exhibited high tolerance to SAIB, even at substantial doses over extended durations, showing no significant signs of toxicity. Chronic and carcinogenicity studies established NOAELs of 2,000 mg/kg bw/day for rats, 2,500 mg/kg bw/day for mice, and 2,400 mg/kg bw/day for monkeys, representing the maximum doses evaluated for rats and monkeys.
A comprehensive three-generation reproductive toxicity study, conducted by MacKenzie in 1990, investigated the effects of SAIB on F344 rats. The study involved groups of 30 male and 30 female rats, which were fed diets containing SAIB at concentrations of 0, 500, 1,000, or 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day, with weekly adjustments to the diets. The exposure regimen began with F0 males receiving SAIB for 10 weeks and F0 females for 2 weeks before mating. This exposure was maintained across the F0, F1, and F2a generations until their sacrifice. F0 rats were euthanized following the weaning of the F1 generation. The F1 rats underwent two mating cycles to produce the F2a and F2b generations. F1 males were sacrificed after their second mating, while F1 females were sacrificed on gestation day (GD) 20 during caesarean sections to deliver the F2b litters. These F2b litters were subsequently utilized for a teratology study. F2a rats were mated to assess fertility for the F3 pregnancy, with F2a males sacrificed post-mating and F2a females on GD 14. Reproduction indices were evaluated for each generation, involving mating groups of 22–30 females and 19–30 males. Throughout the study, rats from all generations were monitored daily for clinical signs, and females were specifically observed for abortion, premature delivery, or difficult parturition. Body weights and food consumption were recorded weekly. Post-birth, data on litter size and the number of live and dead pups were collected. Dead pups and those from culled litters were examined for visceral abnormalities. On postnatal days 4 and 28, pups were weighed, sexed, and checked for abnormalities. Litters exceeding 10 pups were culled on postnatal day 4, and pups not designated as future parents were removed on day 28. Necropsy was performed on all F0 and F1 rats. The study revealed no clinical signs in F0, F1, or F2 rats, and no consistent impacts on food consumption or body weight were observed. Furthermore, there were no differences in reproduction parameters between the control and SAIB-treated groups. The only notable adverse effect was a minor reduction in the fertility index of high-dose F1 female rats during the mating for F2a litters. However, this effect was not observed in subsequent matings for F2b litters or in any other F0 or F2 dams. The author concluded that the reduced female fertility in F1 rats at 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day was not treatment-related, establishing a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day. The Panel concurred with this conclusion.
A 104-week study by Harper et al. (1966), referenced by JECFA (1993), examined the effects of SAIB on Sprague-Dawley rats. During breeding cycles (weeks 10-36), three groups of 10 male and 10 female rats received SAIB in their diet at concentrations of 0%, 0.4% (200 mg/kg bw/day), and 9.4% (4,200 mg/kg bw/day). While no differences were observed in the average number of pups per litter, pup growth rates, or offspring abnormalities across the groups, the high-dose group exhibited a reduced conception rate and a lower percentage of pups born or successfully weaned. JECFA (1993) suggested these effects were likely due to the diminished nutritional quality of the high-dose feed, a conclusion supported by the Panel.
Developmental toxicity was evaluated using the F2b litters from a three-generation reproductive study involving SAIB. F1 dams, in groups of 26-27, received daily doses of 0, 500, 1,000, or 2,000 mg SAIB/kg body weight from gestation day 0 to 20. Researchers monitored dams daily for clinical signs, abortion, and premature delivery, and weekly for body weight and food consumption. On gestation day 20, dams underwent caesarean section and necropsy, and their fertility index was assessed. Fetal parameters recorded included number and distribution within the uterus, implantations, resorptions, corpora lutea, and viability. Fetuses were weighed, sexed, and checked for external abnormalities. Half were examined for soft tissue abnormalities, while the rest were stained with Alizarin Red for skeletal assessment. No maternal toxicity or developmental effects were observed. Based on these fetopathological evaluations, the Panel established a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg SAIB/kg body weight per day, which was the highest dose tested.
A developmental toxicity study, conducted on New Zealand White rabbits, investigated the effects of SAIB administration. Artificially inseminated rabbits, divided into groups of 16, received SAIB via gavage in corn oil at daily doses of 0, 500, 850, or 1,200 mg/kg body weight from gestation day 7 to 19. Throughout the study, clinical signs were monitored twice daily, and body weight and food consumption were documented on gestation days 0, 7, 13, 20, and 29. On gestation day 29, a Caesarean section was performed. Parameters such as corpora lutea count, total implantations, post-implantation loss, viable fetuses, mean fetal weights, and fetal sex distribution were recorded. Fetuses underwent dissection for visceral malformation assessment, and after evisceration and skinning, were fixed and stained for skeletal examination. No maternal toxicity or developmental abnormalities linked to the treatment were observed. The study authors determined a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 1,200 mg SAIB/kg body weight per day, which was the highest dose tested, a conclusion supported by the Panel.
Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies revealed no adverse effects on fertility, reproduction, maternal health, or development. In a three-generation rat study, SAIB administered orally via diet at doses up to 2,000 mg/kg bw per day showed no impact. Similarly, developmental studies in rats and rabbits, even at the highest tested doses (2,000 mg/kg bw per day for rats and 1,200 mg/kg bw per day for rabbits), reported no treatment-related issues.
In a study by Procter and Chappel (1971), Beagle dogs (two male, two female) exhibited a significant reduction in bromosulfophthalein (BSP) elimination rates when treated with SAIB doses of 1,350 mg or more per animal. This effect, measured before and at 24 and 48 hours after dietary administration, was found to be reversible within 48 hours.
Dickie et al. (1980a) investigated SAIB's impact on BSP elimination in dogs. A single 2,000 mg/kg bw dose showed peak effects 4-6 hours post-treatment. When tested at varying doses, a reduction in BSP elimination rate was observed at doses of 20 mg/kg bw and higher, 5 hours after administration. However, no such reduction occurred in two out of three dogs given 5 mg/kg bw. The study also noted no changes in SAP levels.
Researchers investigated SAIB's impact on liver function in squirrel monkeys. Using a crossover design, three male monkeys, acting as controls, received a single gavage dose of 1 gram of SAIB in cottonseed oil. Bromosulfophthalein (BSP) elimination rates, measured 24 hours post-treatment, showed no alteration, indicating no effect on liver function.
In a study by Dickie et al. (1980b), cynomolgus monkeys (four males, two controls) were given a single oral dose of 5,000 mg SAIB/kg body weight in corn oil. Researchers assessed liver function by measuring serum alkaline phosphatase (SAP) levels and bromosulfophthalein (BSP) elimination rates. These measurements, taken 24 hours before and 5 hours after treatment, showed no impact on SAP or BSP retention.
Single oral doses of SAIB at or above 20 mg/kg bw affected liver function in dogs, but not in monkeys.
A 14-day clinical study involving 20 healthy volunteers (10 male, 10 female, aged 18-22) investigated the effects of daily SAIB intake. Participants consumed 10 mg SAIB/kg body weight per day. Blood samples, taken before treatment and on days 7 and 18, revealed no significant changes in various clinical parameters, including liver enzymes (ASAT, ALAT, SAP), bilirubin, proteins, uric acid, BUN, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, electrolytes (sodium, potassium, phosphorus), carbon dioxide, cholesterol, or glucose.
A clinical investigation involving male and female volunteers, aged 21-42, assessed the impact of SAIB on hepatic function. Participants were given SAIB in soft drinks for 14 days, at daily doses of 0, 7, or 20 mg/kg body weight. Researchers collected blood samples before treatment and on days 7 and 14 to analyze various hematological and clinical chemistry markers, including platelet counts, white blood cell differentials, and liver enzymes like ASAT and ALAT. Standard urinalysis was also performed. Additionally, a 45-minute BSP retention test was administered both before and after the treatment period. The study found no adverse effects on any of the measured parameters in any individual following oral SAIB exposure.
A single-blind clinical study investigated the effects of SAIB on healthy volunteers. Twenty-seven participants (13 men, 14 women) consumed SAIB daily for 14 days at a dose of 20 mg/kg body weight, delivered in an orange juice-diluted gum arabic/water emulsion (total volume 1.16 mL/kg body weight). Before the SAIB treatment, for seven days (days -6 to 0), each participant served as their own control by ingesting a placebo emulsion in orange juice. Blood samples were taken on days -6, 0, 7, and the day following the last dose. These samples were analyzed for a comprehensive panel of hematological and clinical chemistry parameters, including specific markers for hepatobiliary function. No adverse symptoms were reported by the subjects, and no changes related to SAIB ingestion were observed in any of the measured hematological or clinical chemistry values.
In human studies, a daily dose of 20 mg SAIB/kg body weight for 14 days showed no adverse effects on clinical signs, blood parameters, or liver function.
Recent research indicates that certain emulsifiers can influence gut microbiota, contributing to inflammation, obesity, and compromised glycemic regulation. While these effects are not routinely assessed in standard toxicity studies, the Panel acknowledges their potential significance. Such observations would prompt further investigation on an individual basis if suggested by general toxicity test outcomes, as outlined in the Guidance for food additive submissions. The Panel emphasized the necessity of further studies to determine the human health relevance of these findings, particularly those observed in mice.
Oral absorption of SAIB, labeled with 14C at its sucrose component, was observed to be rapid across rats, dogs, and humans. Humans exhibited the highest absorption, at 88% or more, while rats absorbed approximately 70%, and dogs around 50%. SAIB undergoes de-esterification primarily in the small intestine by non-specific esterases, yielding sucrose and partially acylated sucrose, both of which are readily absorbed. A notable amount of ingested SAIB, including its partially de-esterified forms, was eliminated through faeces, especially in dogs. Absorbed radioactivity was primarily excreted in urine, with a smaller fraction appearing in bile in dogs. In humans given 0.1–2 mg/kg body weight of 14C-SAIB, 44–66% of the absorbed dose was exhaled as carbon dioxide, with 15–21% and 10% excreted in urine and faeces, respectively. Information regarding SAIB labeled at the isobutyrate moiety was not available.
While SAIB exhibits low acute toxicity, the Panel observed that no specific studies have investigated its potential impact on microbiota, despite recent findings linking other emulsifiers to such effects.
Subchronic toxicity studies across multiple species revealed varied responses to SAIB. Dogs exhibited hepatobiliary dysfunction at 225 mg/kg bw per day. In contrast, mice, rats, and monkeys showed no such effects, even at significantly higher doses of 5,000, 9,000, and 10,000 mg/kg bw per day, respectively.
SAIB poses no genotoxicity concern, according to the Panel's review of available studies.
In chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity studies, the Panel determined a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,500 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day for mice. For rats, the NOAEL was established at 2,000 mg/kg bw per day, which was the highest dose tested. A minor body weight reduction (less than 10%) and reduced feed intake in a 52-week rat study were deemed non-adverse, likely due to palatability issues rather than toxicity.
The SCF previously set a NOAEL of 1,000 mg/kg bw per day for SAIB, citing a body weight decrease of less than 10% at the 2,000 mg/kg bw per day dose in a chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity study. However, the Panel observed this body weight effect only in a 52-week rat study, not in the chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity study itself.
In subchronic feeding studies, dogs exhibited impaired hepatobiliary function at low SAIB doses, with a single oral dose of 20 mg/kg body weight causing BSP retention, though 5 mg/kg body weight had no effect. Conversely, human clinical trials demonstrated no impact on hepatobiliary function after consuming 20 mg/kg body weight per day for 14 days. Furthermore, chronic studies in rodents (rats and mice) and monkeys revealed no significant toxicity or hepatobiliary effects, even at high SAIB doses.
The observed effects of SAIB on hepatobiliary function in dogs are considered species-specific and not relevant to human safety. This conclusion is supported by the absence of such effects in mice, rats, monkeys, and humans, even at higher doses, highlighting significant interspecies differences in response to SAIB exposure.
Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies showed no adverse effects on fertility, reproduction, maternal toxicity, or development in rats (up to 2,000 mg/kg bw/day) or rabbits (up to 1,200 mg/kg bw/day).
Based on chronic, carcinogenicity, reproductive, and developmental studies in rats, a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day was determined. This highest tested dose, with an uncertainty factor of 100, allows for an Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 20 mg/kg body weight per day.
The Panel concluded that the existing data on absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, genotoxicity, and various toxicity studies (subchronic, reproductive, developmental, long-term, and carcinogenicity) justify increasing the ADI for SAIB from 10 mg/kg bw/day (set by SCF in 1994) to 20 mg/kg bw/day.
The Panel evaluated dietary exposure to SAIB (E 444) as a food additive using two methods. The first, a regulatory maximum level scenario, assumed lifetime exposure at EU-mandated maximum permitted levels (MPLs). The second, a refined assessment, utilized actual usage data. A potential underestimation in the refined scenario arose because usage data for SAIB were only available for flavored drinks (FCS 14.1.4), not for alcoholic beverages (FCS 14.2.8). Despite this, the Panel concluded that overall SAIB exposure is unlikely to exceed the more conservative regulatory maximum level scenario. Crucially, all calculated exposure levels for SAIB (E 444) were found to be below the established acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 20 mg/kg body weight per day.
An Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of 20 mg/kg body weight per day for SAIB (E 444) was established. This was derived from a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 2,000 mg/kg body weight per day, identified in rat studies covering chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, and reproductive and developmental effects, applying a 100-fold uncertainty factor.
The Panel determined that SAIB (E 444) as a food additive, when used at permitted or reported levels, poses no safety concerns. This conclusion was reached because the acceptable daily intake (ADI) is not surpassed in any population group.
To prevent SAIB (E 444) from becoming a notable source of toxic element exposure in food, the Panel advised revising the EU's maximum impurity limits for arsenic, cadmium, lead, and mercury. This ensures the food additive's safety regarding these elements.
