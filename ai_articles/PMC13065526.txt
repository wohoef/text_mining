China has implemented various emission reduction policies for high-emitting industries to combat climate change, with a particular focus on the cement sector due to its significant contribution to process-related emissions. This research quantifies emissions from China's cement industry, categorizing them as process-related, direct fossil fuel-related, and indirect electricity-related. A key finding is that China's cement-related emissions reached their highest point in 2014. Uniquely, this study disaggregates these emissions into seven categories based on the types of new buildings utilizing the cement. The analysis also reveals a trend where more developed provinces have shifted their cement production capabilities to less developed areas. To understand the factors influencing these emission changes, the study utilizes index decomposition analysis. The findings indicate that economic expansion was the main catalyst for emission increases. Conversely, improvements in emission intensity and efficiency acted as mitigating forces. Since 2014, the decline in emissions has primarily been driven by shifts in the construction industry's structure and enhanced efficiency.

China, recognized as the world's leading CO2 emitter and energy consumer, is increasingly committed to global climate initiatives. To enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of greenhouse gas (GHG) emission reductions, China has implemented various strategies and policies specifically targeting major emitting sectors. These sectors include power generation, coal mining, and the iron and steel industry. Globally and within China, the cement industry stands out as a significant contributor to process-related emissions. Official GHG emission inventories from China's National Communications on Climate Change highlight the substantial and growing emissions from the cement lime industry. For instance, process-related GHG emissions from this sector escalated from 157.78 million tons (representing 57% of total process-related emissions) in 1994, to 411.57 Mt (72%) in 2005, and further to 834.03 Mt (70%) by 2012. This rapid increase in emissions underscores the critical role of the cement industry in GHG mitigation efforts. Consequently, China has introduced multiple policies aimed at curbing energy consumption and emission intensity within its cement sector. The 13th five-year plan for the cement industry, for example, mandates a 30% reduction in air pollutant emissions by 2020, relative to 2015 levels. Furthermore, the plan targets a reduction in energy consumption per tonne of clinker production to below 105 kg coal equivalent by 2020, a decrease from 112 kg in 2015. These governmental efforts have yielded results, with process-related CO2 emissions from China's cement industry reaching their peak in 2014, mirroring the trend observed in China's overall emissions.
Understanding the factors that drive changes in cement-related emissions and accurately quantifying these emissions are crucial for developing effective emission reduction policies. Previous research has explored various aspects of cement emissions. For instance, Oda, Maksyutov, and Andres (2018) created a global, high-resolution inventory of CO2 emissions that incorporated cement production as a nonpoint source. Şanal (2018) examined the CO2 emissions from different cement types and assessed how replacing cement in concrete could reduce these emissions. Andrew (2018) offered a new global analysis of process emissions from cement, reporting figures 30% lower than those from the Global Carbon Project (Le Quéré et al., 2015). Additionally, Xi et al. (2016) highlighted that the carbonation of cement materials mitigated 43% of CO2 emissions from cement production between 1930 and 2013, demonstrating cement's role beyond just a carbon source.
The logarithmic mean Divisia index (LMDI) model, a type of index decomposition analysis (IDA), has been widely used in prior research to examine changes in carbon emissions. For instance, Branger and Quirion (2015) studied the European cement industry's CO2 emissions between 1990 and 2012, concluding that the activity effect (including factors like clinker trade and energy efficiency) was the primary cause of emission changes. Similarly, Xu et al. (2012) observed that in China, the activity effect (specifically calcining and electricity consumption) drove increases in cement-related emissions, while clinker share, structural changes, and kiln efficiency had a mitigating impact. Other researchers, such as Wang et al. (2013), have also pinpointed key drivers of China's cement-related greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, Lin and Zhang (2016) identified labor productivity as a significant factor contributing to the rise in China's cement-related CO2 emissions from 1991 to 2010.
Most prior research on emission inventories, while including cement processes, typically bases emission calculations on overall cement production. However, this approach can be imprecise, as process-related CO2 emissions primarily stem from clinker production. Relying solely on cement production figures risks overlooking regional variations in manufacturing techniques and the crucial cement-to-clinker ratio. Consequently, more recent investigations have adopted clinker production as the basis for emission calculations to enhance accuracy.
This research begins by analyzing CO2 emissions from China's cement industry, including regional variations. It quantifies process-related emissions (derived from clinker production), direct emissions from burning fossil fuels, and indirect emissions from electricity use. Uniquely, the study categorizes these cement-related CO2 emissions into seven segments based on the demand from various new building types. Additionally, it details the provincial differences in cement production and the movement of production capacity between regions.
Our research utilizes the LMDI method to dissect shifts in China's cement-related CO2 emissions, identifying four key drivers: construction industry structure, emission intensity, efficiency, and economic growth. Notably, this study is pioneering in its inclusion of "construction industry structure," defined by cement usage across various new building types, as a driver in this analysis. A specific focus was placed on comparing how these drivers evolved before and after the emissions reached their peak in 2014.
This study offers transparent data, crucial for environmental assessments and sustainable policymaking in China's cement industry.
Cement production typically involves three stages. Initially, limestone (mainly CaCO3) undergoes crushing and grinding to create a raw meal. This raw meal is then calcined in kilns, producing clinker. Finally, the clinker is combined with various additives, such as fly ash and gypsum, and ground to yield the finished cement.
CO2 emissions stemming from cement production primarily originate from a chemical reaction known as calcination. This process involves heating limestone (CaCO3) to produce lime (CaO) and CO2, as detailed in Equation 1. These emissions are categorized as process-related, distinguishing them from CO2 generated by industrial energy combustion. Conversely, the initial stages of cement manufacturing, specifically crushing and grinding, are physical processes that do not release any CO2. Therefore, the chemical transformation during calcination is the key source of process-related CO2 in cement production.
While crushing and grinding in cement production don't generate process-related CO2, they require significant energy. This energy consumption leads to direct emissions from coal combustion and indirect emissions from electricity generation. Notably, indirect emissions typically originate from power plants, not the cement facilities themselves.
This study quantifies China's cement industry CO2 emissions, encompassing those from the production process, direct coal combustion, and indirect electricity use. These emissions are calculated using the mass balance method, as suggested by the IPCC (2006).
To ensure greater accuracy in estimating process-related CO2 emissions from the cement industry, this study utilizes clinker production rather than cement production. Previous research often relied on cement production for these calculations, but this approach can neglect variations in regional manufacturing techniques and clinker-to-cement ratios. By basing the estimation on clinker production, as detailed in Equation 2 (IPCC, 2006), a more precise accounting of emissions is achieved.
The emission factor for clinker production, denoted as EFclinker, represents the CO2 released per unit of clinker produced. This factor is multiplied by the clinker production, ADclinker, to estimate process-related CO2 emissions.
Liu et al. (2015) provided the cement emission factor, stating that 0.4964 tonnes of CO2 are emitted per tonne of clinker produced.
Direct CO2 emissions from coal combustion are calculated using Equation 3, as per IPCC (2006) guidelines. This involves multiplying coal consumption by its emission factor, which comprises net caloric value, carbon content, and oxygenation efficiency.
Direct CO2 emissions from coal combustion in cement production, denoted as CEcoal, are determined by coal consumption (ADcoal) and the coal emission factor (EFcoal). This emission factor is a composite of three elements: net caloric value (NCV), carbon content (CC), and oxygenation efficiency (O). Data for these parameters were sourced from a prior study on Chinese coal quality, which involved a comprehensive analysis of 4,243 coal mines. Specifically, the NCV for coal is established at 20.60 PJ/mt, while the CC is 26.32 tC/TJ. The oxygenation efficiency (O) is 92%. When combined, these values result in an overall coal emission factor (EFcoal) of 0.499 tonnes of CO2 emissions for every tonne of coal consumed.
Equation 2 (IPCC, 2006) is used to determine indirect CO2 emissions from purchased electricity consumption. This calculation involves multiplying the activity data (electricity consumption) by the emission factor.
The calculation of indirect CO2 emissions from electricity consumption in cement production involves two key variables: the amount of electricity consumed (activity data, AD_ele) and the corresponding emission factor (EF_ele). For each province, this study utilizes regional average electricity emission factors, as documented by NDRC in 2009 and presented in Table 1, to determine the electricity-related CO2 emissions (CE_ele).
To facilitate a more comprehensive analysis of CO2 emissions, this study disaggregates total cement-related emissions into seven categories. This division is based on the cement demand from various new domestic building types and export activities, recognizing the strong link between cement use in new construction and associated CO2 emissions.
To analyze CO2 emissions, export-related emissions are initially separated from the total, determined by cement clinker export data from CCA (2009–2014). The remaining emissions are attributed to domestic new building construction, which is classified into six categories: residential, manufacturing, infrastructural, commercial, science–education–culture–health, and other buildings. Proportions for each building type are calculated using construction output data from CCA (2009–2014), allowing for the division of domestic emissions accordingly.
Analyzing the factors contributing to the peak in cement-related CO2 emissions is crucial for developing effective future emission reduction policies. Two primary analytical methods, Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) and Index Decomposition Analysis (IDA), are widely used for this purpose. Both techniques have been extensively applied to quantify socioeconomic drivers behind changes in energy consumption and CO2 emissions. SDA, for instance, can identify direct and indirect impacts across an entire supply chain by utilizing input-output tables. However, this study, which examines emissions from 1996 to 2016, opts for IDA. This choice is based on two key considerations: firstly, cement-related CO2 emissions originate exclusively from the nonmetallic products sector, meaning only a limited number of economic sectors are involved in the supply chain; and secondly, comprehensive time-series input-output tables for China are not readily available. Therefore, IDA is employed to break down the cement-related CO2 emissions in this research.
This study utilizes the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) method to quantify the factors influencing CO2 emission changes within China's cement industry. The LMDI approach, introduced by Ang (2004) and rooted in the Divisia index, is widely favored among various Index Decomposition Analysis (IDA) techniques, which often include Laspeyres index variations. Its preference stems from its ability to satisfy key index number tests, ensure perfect decomposition without residual terms, and effectively handle zero values, a common challenge for other methods (Ang & Liu, 2007a, 2007b). Furthermore, the LMDI method offers benefits such as "path independence, consistency in aggregation, and easily interpreted results" (Liu, Geng, Lindner, & Guan, 2012; Meng et al., 2016). Prior research has leveraged LMDI to investigate drivers of regional emission growth (Chong, Guan, & Guthrie, 2012; Guan et al., 2018; Liu, 2016a; Liu, Fan, Wu, & Wei, 2007; Wang et al., 2014; Zhang, Li, Zhou, Zhang, & Gao, 2016). These factors allow for the quantification of CO2 emission changes by considering cement usage, environmental impacts, technological progress, and economic expansion.
The total CO2 emissions linked to cement, denoted as CE, are broken down into components. CE_i represents emissions originating from various new building types, offering insight into China's construction industry structure. For a clearer decomposition analysis, the initial seven building types are consolidated into three main categories: residential and commercial structures; infrastructural buildings (including manufacturing, science-education-culture-health facilities); and exports/other uses. These merged categories group building types with shared characteristics. Additionally, P signifies the volume of cement produced, while F indicates the fixed capital formation for each year.
Economic growth is represented by F, which signifies fixed capital formation in Chinese yuan. The environmental impact of cement production is captured by emission intensity (I), defined as CO2 emissions per unit of cement produced (tonne/tonne). Technological progress in cement production is reflected by input efficiency (E), which quantifies cement production per unit of fixed capital formation (tonne/Chinese yuan). Lastly, the construction industry's structural effect is measured by the proportion of CO2 emissions (Si) from a specific cement usage (i), expressed as a percentage of total CO2 emissions.
To ensure precise CO2 emission calculations for China's cement industry, this study combined data from two primary sources: the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) and the China Cement Association (CCA). The NBS provided national and provincial cement production figures spanning 1996 to 2016. Complementing this, the CCA published national cement production data from 1996 to 2013, national clinker production from 2002 to 2013, and provincial cement-clinker ratios for 2005 to 2012. A comparison of aggregated cement production from both sources revealed a minimal discrepancy of less than 1%, affirming the high reliability of China's cement statistics. This integrated approach allowed for the acquisition of comprehensive cement and clinker production data.
For national-level data, cement production figures from 1996 to 2013 were sourced from the CCA, while the NBS provided data for 2014 to 2016. Clinker production data for the period 2002 to 2013 was also obtained from the CCA. The clinker-to-cement ratio was determined by dividing clinker production by cement production. This ratio was calculated for China from 2002 to 2013. To estimate China's clinker-to-cement ratios for 2014–2016, a linear regression of historical ratios (2002–2013) was employed. For the years 1996–2001, these ratios were assumed to match the 2002 value. Finally, clinker production for 1996–2001 and 2014–2016 was derived by multiplying the estimated clinker-to-cement ratios by the respective cement production volumes.
For provincial-level analysis, cement and clinker production data were sourced from the CCA for 2005-2012, while the NBS provided cement production figures for 1996-2004 and 2013-2016. The clinker-to-cement ratios for each province between 2005 and 2012 were directly computed using their respective cement and clinker production volumes. For other periods, these ratios required estimation. Provincial clinker-to-cement ratios for 2002-2004 and 2013 were estimated by applying the national-level change rates to the provincial ratios. For instance, the 2004 provincial ratio was derived by scaling the 2005 provincial ratio by the national ratio change from 2005 to 2004. Similarly, 2013 provincial ratios were estimated using 2012 provincial and national ratios. For the periods 1996-2001 and 2014-2016, provincial clinker-to-cement ratios were assumed to be constant, matching their 2002 and 2013 values, respectively. Finally, provincial clinker production for 1996-2004 and 2013-2016 was calculated by multiplying the estimated clinker-to-cement ratios by the corresponding cement production volumes.

China's cement industry generated 1,019 Mt of CO2 emissions in 2016, representing 11.06% of the nation's total CO2 output (9,217 Mt). These emissions reached their highest point in 2014, at 1,093 Mt.
China's cement-related CO2 emissions exhibited three distinct growth phases before reaching their 2014 peak. From 1996 to 2002, emissions saw stable growth, rising from 264 to 389 Mt at an average annual rate of 6.6%. Following China's WTO entry in 2002, a period of rapid growth ensued (2002-2011), with emissions surging at an average of 10.9% annually, though a temporary halt occurred in 2008 due to the global financial crisis. Subsequently, from 2011 to 2014, emission growth decelerated to an average of 3.4% per year, attributed to an economic slowdown.
China's cement-related CO2 emissions closely mirrored the nation's GDP growth trends. This strong link stems from several factors within the cement industry. Firstly, CO2 emissions are directly tied to clinker and cement production volumes. Secondly, cement, being a perishable building material, is typically manufactured in response to immediate market demand or existing orders. Finally, the demand for cement is heavily influenced by the construction of new buildings, which itself is primarily spurred by the country's fixed capital formation. Consequently, it's evident that economic expansion was a significant driving force behind the levels of cement-related CO2 emissions.
China's cement-related emissions first declined in 2015, reaching a peak of 1,093 Mt in 2014. This 7.5% reduction, or 76 Mt, was primarily due to a decrease in overall cement production. Total production fell from 2,492 Mt in 2014 to 2,359 Mt in 2015.
An examination of the emission composition reveals that process-related CO2 emissions were the dominant contributor to China's cement industry emissions, making up 63.1% in 2016. The remaining emissions were split between coal-related (12.3%) and electricity-related (21.6%) sources. Over the two-decade study period, the proportions of these emission sources saw only minor shifts. Specifically, process-related emissions saw a slight decline from 67.0% in 1996 to 63.1% in 2016, averaging 66.1%. Conversely, emissions from purchased electricity rose from 20.2% to 24.4% over the same period, with an average of 21.6%. Coal-related emissions, however, maintained a consistent share of roughly 12.3%. The stability in the overall emission mix suggests that energy intensity and the underlying energy efficiency and utilization technologies in China's cement production have remained largely unchanged throughout these 20 years, given the consistent emission factors used in the analysis.
An analysis of CO2 emissions linked to cement use in various new building types, as shown in Figure 3, reveals a consistent pattern over time, suggesting a stable structure within China's construction sector. Residential buildings are the primary source of these emissions, contributing roughly 40%. Infrastructure projects follow, accounting for about 25%, with manufacturing plants responsible for approximately 10%. Commercial buildings and the Science-Education-Culture-Health-Government (SECHG) sector contribute smaller shares, at around 4% and 7% respectively. Cement exports generate a minimal 1% of total emissions, largely due to the high cost of long-distance transportation, the product's short shelf life, and its high density. This makes local production more economical. Consequently, China's annual export of cement and clinker to regions like Africa, the USA, and the EU is less than 10 Mt, a tiny fraction of its total production of 2,410 Mt of cement and 1,295 Mt of clinker.
Over the last two decades, the cement industry's emission intensity, measured as CO2 emissions per unit of cement produced, showed a modest decline. It decreased from 0.54 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of cement in 1996 to 0.42 tonnes in 2016.

While China's overall cement-related emissions have peaked, significant regional disparities exist. Not all provinces have reached their emission peak, with some still experiencing rapid growth. To illustrate these differences, 31 provinces (excluding Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macao due to data limitations) are categorized into four groups based on their emission peak stages. These groups are visually represented in Figure 4, with early-peaked, mid-peaked, late-peaked, and fast-growing provinces distinguished by green, blue, red, and yellow, respectively. The emission intensity for each province in 2016 is also indicated next to its name.
Provinces in China are categorized into four groups based on when their cement-related CO2 emissions peaked. The "early-peaked" group, comprising Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Zhejiang, reached their emission peak around 2006 and exhibit the lowest emission intensity at 0.35 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of cement. Following them, the "mid-peaked" group, consisting of 11 provinces primarily in northern and northeastern China, saw their emissions peak around 2012, with an average intensity of 0.43 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of cement. A "late-peaked" group of 10 provinces peaked around 2014. Finally, the "fast-growing" provinces, including Yunnan, Guangxi, Hainan, Guizhou, Chongqing, and Tibet, are still experiencing increasing emissions, making them the group with the highest average emission intensity of 0.47 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of cement.
Variations in provincial emission peak stages and intensities are partly attributable to differing technical levels, with early-peaked provinces employing advanced, less energy-intensive cement production. However, inter-provincial clinker trade, evident in clinker-to-cement ratios, offers a more comprehensive explanation for these discrepancies.
Figure 5 illustrates provincial clinker-to-cement ratios. A higher ratio signifies greater clinker production relative to cement, while a lower ratio indicates the opposite. Nationally, this ratio consistently declined from 72.4% in 2002 to 53.7% in 2016, averaging 65.0% over the period.
Since 2006, provinces that experienced early emission peaks, particularly Shanghai, Tianjin, and Zhejiang, exhibited a notable reduction in their clinker-to-cement ratios. This downward trend suggests a strategic shift where these provinces decreased their clinker manufacturing and instead sourced clinker from other regions for cement production. Given that the majority of CO2 emissions originate from clinker production, this outsourcing approach effectively lowered the total CO2 emissions for these provinces. For instance, Shanghai's clinker-to-cement ratio saw a significant drop from 33.9% in 2005 to 16.6% in 2006, further declining to 8.5% by 2010. This was accompanied by substantial reductions in clinker production in 2006 (1.7 Mt or 46.9%) and 2010 (0.8 Mt or 57.5%), even as its cement production remained relatively consistent. Consequently, Shanghai achieved the lowest emission intensity among all provinces, at 0.09 tonne CO2 per tonne of cement produced.
In contrast to the mid-peaked provinces, which generally saw a decline in their clinker-to-cement ratios between 2007 and 2010 (e.g., Heilongjiang and Jilin), the late-peaked provinces did not experience a sharp reduction in this ratio. For instance, while Anhui's ratio steadily decreased from 2003, this was primarily due to an increase in its cement output, not a reduction in clinker production. Anhui's clinker production continued to rise until 2014, leading to a peak in its cement-related emissions at 9,145 Mt that year. Anhui stands as China's leading clinker producer, contributing 121 Mt in 2016, or 9.3% of the national total. Despite this, its cement production was only 136 Mt (5.6% nationally), indicating significant clinker exports. Over two decades, Anhui's average clinker-to-cement ratio was 134.5%, more than double the national average of 61.7%.
Analysis of the six fast-growing provinces (Figure 5d) reveals generally stable clinker-to-cement ratios over two decades. However, Tibet experienced a notable surge in its ratio, climbing from 75.7% in 2008 to 91.0% in 2009. This increase primarily stemmed from expanded clinker production, as Tibet had been boosting its cement production capacity since 2000, culminating in the establishment of "Huaxin Cement (Tibet)" in 2009.
Over the last decade, developed provinces have opted to close or relocate their clinker production facilities to less developed regions. While this strategy may offer short-term emission reductions for the developed areas, it poses a challenge to the nation's overall emission reduction goals. This is because developed provinces typically possess superior technologies, leading to lower emission intensities. Consequently, shifting production capabilities from technologically advanced regions to less developed ones can inadvertently escalate the country's total emissions. Therefore, it is crucial to focus on key cement-producing provinces like Tibet, which plans future expansion, and Anhui, China's largest cement production hub.
China's cement-related CO2 emissions saw a significant increase starting in 2002, following its entry into the WTO, and reached their highest point in 2014. Analyzing the factors that influenced these emissions, particularly before and after this 2014 peak, offers crucial insights for developing effective emission control policies within the cement industry. This research utilized the LMDI method to break down the changes in China's cement-related CO2 emissions between 2002 and 2016 into four primary drivers: the construction industry's structural composition, emission intensity, efficiency, and overall economic growth. The "construction industry's structure" factor was further quantified using three specific indicators: the share of emissions linked to cement used in new residential buildings, new infrastructural buildings, and a combined category for exports and other uses. These findings are detailed in Table S7 of the Supporting Information and visually represented in Figure 6.
Economic growth, specifically fixed capital formation, consistently drove increases in cement-related emissions. Its impact was particularly pronounced between 2008 and 2010, accounting for 26.4% of emission growth, assuming other factors were constant. This surge was largely a consequence of China's "Economic Stimulus Plans," initiated in response to the 2007 global financial crisis. The Chinese government injected four trillion Chinese yuan (around 586 billion U.S. dollars) to boost domestic demand, leading local authorities to construct numerous manufacturing facilities and infrastructure projects like power plants, cement plants, subways, and high-speed rail. Although economic growth's contribution to emissions gradually declined after 2010 (to 21.3% in 2010–2012, 17.0% in 2012–2014, and 11.2% in 2014–2016) as the stimulus waned, it remained the dominant force behind rising cement-related emissions.
Two key factors mitigating the rise in cement-related CO2 emissions were efficiency and emission intensity. Efficiency improvements, specifically a 14.3% decrease from 1.19 to 1.02 tonnes of cement production per 10,000 yuan of fixed capital formation, led to a 14.5% offset in emission growth between 2014 and 2016. This indicates that cement plants achieved greater output with fewer economic inputs. Concurrently, the emission intensity of cement production saw a slight but consistent reduction over two decades, falling from 0.54 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of cement in 1996 to 0.42 tonnes in 2016. This decrease in intensity has consistently counteracted 3.5–4.5% of emission growth since 2008.
Since 2010, shifts within the construction sector's structure have notably impacted cement-related emissions. Initially, the contribution from new residential buildings led to an increase in these emissions, rising by 6.9% between 2010 and 2012, and then by 2.6% from 2012 to 2014. However, this trend reversed in the 2014-2016 period, with residential construction offsetting emission growth by 0.4%. This fluctuation appears to be linked to housing price dynamics. Data from the China Real Estate Index System (CREIS) shows a 19.0% increase in housing prices between 2010 and 2014, followed by a 1.1% decline in 2015. Given the inherent delays in the construction industry, where planning often reflects the previous year's market conditions, the 2015 housing price drop likely contributed to the subsequent negative impact of residential building investments on cement-related emissions.
Since 2010, the share of newly invested infrastructural buildings has consistently reduced cement-related emissions. This factor mitigated emissions by 4.7% between 2010 and 2012, and by 1.8% from 2012 to 2014. Following the "Economic Stimulus Plans," China experienced overcapacity from 2010, leading to decreased investment in infrastructure construction.
Cement exports generally had minimal impact on emission fluctuations, except between 2010 and 2012. During this specific period, a decline in exports was primarily attributed to the contraction of USA/EU markets, a consequence of the global financial crisis.
Since 2014, the decline in cement-related CO2 emissions was primarily driven by two factors: enhanced efficiency and a decrease in residential building construction, as revealed by comparing factor contributions across two periods (2012–2014 and 2014–2016).

Given China's ongoing infrastructure expansion, particularly in less developed cities, driven by initiatives like the "Economic Stimulus Plans," central and local governments must coordinate cement production management across all regions. This coordination is essential to prevent overcapacity and avoid inefficient outsourcing or relocation of cement production. Furthermore, less developed regions should adopt practices from their more advanced counterparts to enhance cement production efficiency and reduce emissions per unit of output.
An analysis of cement-related CO2 emission changes reveals the underlying factors influencing these fluctuations. Economic growth consistently drove emission increases over an extended period, particularly between 2008 and 2010. Conversely, both emission intensity and efficiency acted as significant counteracting forces, mitigating the rise in CO2 emissions. Post-2014, efficiency became increasingly crucial in emission reduction, contributing to a decoupling between economic growth and cement-related CO2 emissions. This trend aligns with broader observations that China is moving towards decoupling economic expansion from carbon emissions, suggesting that future efficiency improvements can further decrease emissions. To achieve this, cement plants could explore adopting a cleaner energy portfolio to lower emission intensity or utilize inexpensive alternative fuels like non-recycled plastics and paper to boost efficiency. It is imperative that these low-cost energy sources are implemented cleanly. Furthermore, the development and deployment of advanced cleaner production technologies, such as "calcium looping CO2 capture," are essential for China's cement industry.
Since 2010, shifts in the construction industry's structure have notably impacted emissions, alongside economic factors. Specifically, the contribution of new residential buildings to emissions growth turned negative after 2014, largely due to fluctuating housing prices in China. Given the high sensitivity of housing prices, governmental planning for the construction sector and new building projects is crucial. This strategic planning aims to mitigate the unpredictable influence of housing prices on cement production, thereby preventing overcapacity and waste.
Future research will delve deeper into the cement industry of individual provinces to assess the impact of CO2 emissions. Additionally, employing life cycle assessment (LCA) and carbon footprint analysis could offer a more thorough evaluation of emission performance for both cement production and consumption. These methods would enable the allocation of cement-related environmental performance to its ultimate use in construction, thereby facilitating emission control from both supply and demand sides.
