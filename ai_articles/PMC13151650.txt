Lancer, a lanthanide citrate, is a feed additive composed primarily of the rare earth elements lanthanum (La) and cerium (Ce). This additive has not received prior authorization within the European Union. In 2013, the EFSA FEEDAP Panel evaluated Lancer's safety and efficacy as a zootechnical additive for weaned piglets. Their initial assessment indicated an inability to confirm its safety for the target animals, consumers, or the environment. However, subsequent information submitted by the applicant led the FEEDAP Panel to revise its stance regarding weaned piglets, concluding that Lancer is safe for this species when administered at the maximum recommended dose of 250 mg/kg of complete feed. Despite this, significant concerns persist regarding consumer safety. These include potential developmental neurotoxicity linked to La, numerous deficiencies in the toxicological data for both Ce and La, and a complete lack of studies on Lancer's long-term toxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity, developmental toxicity, and residue levels in edible tissues. Consequently, the FEEDAP Panel remains unable to determine Lancer's safety for consumers. Similarly, due to insufficient data, the Panel cannot ascertain the environmental safety of Lancer.

The FEEDAP Panel evaluated Lancer's safety using methods consistent with Regulation (EC) No 429/2008. This assessment also adhered to several specific guidance documents. These included guidelines for zootechnical additives (2012a), tolerance and efficacy studies in target animals (2011), environmental safety of feed additives (2008), and consumer safety of additives (2012b).
Lancer, a feed additive, comprises lanthanum (La) and cerium (Ce) as citrates. This zootechnical additive, intended for weaned piglets, has not yet received authorization for use within the European Union.
Lancer, a feed additive, is designed for weaned piglets up to 120 days old. It can be added directly to feed or through a premixture, at a maximum dose of 250 mg/kg of complete feed.
In 2013, the European Food Safety Authority's (EFSA) FEEDAP Panel issued an opinion regarding the safety and effectiveness of Lancer as a zootechnical additive for weaned piglets. This initial assessment was inconclusive on several fronts. The Panel could not confirm the additive's safety for the target animals due to significant deficiencies in the tolerance studies. Furthermore, consumer safety remained unaddressed because a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) could not be established, and the potential for consumer exposure to residues from the additive could not be ruled out. Lastly, a comprehensive environmental assessment was not possible due to a lack of data concerning the additive's toxicity to terrestrial organisms.
This opinion addresses new information submitted by the applicant concerning Lancer's safety for target animals, consumers, and the environment. A tolerance study was conducted to assess how weaned piglets tolerate the additive.
Fifty-six healthy, 4-week-old weaned piglets (equal numbers of males and females from a Piétrain × Large White cross) were used. Upon arrival, they underwent physical examinations, blood sampling, and weighing.
Following a 7-day acclimation, piglets were divided into four treatment groups, ensuring an even distribution by body weight and sex. The study employed a randomized block design, with each of the four dietary treatments assigned to seven replicate pens. Each pen housed two piglets, one male and one female. The treatments included a control group receiving a non-supplemented diet, and three Lancer-supplemented groups at 250 mg/kg (1x the maximum recommended dose), 1,250 mg/kg (5x), and 2,500 mg/kg (10x) of complete feed. Analytical confirmation verified the additive doses. For six weeks, experimental diets were provided ad libitum. Daily observations encompassed animal behavior, clinical status, illness signs, and fecal characteristics, with mortality recorded. Weekly, piglets were weighed, and feed intake was monitored twice a week to calculate feed-to-gain ratios. Clinical examinations were conducted weekly. From each treatment group, four pens (comprising four males and four females) were chosen for blood sampling and necropsy. Blood samples were collected from these 32 piglets (eight per treatment) on days 1 and 42 to assess hematological and serum biochemistry parameters. On days 42–43, these same 32 piglets were euthanized for a comprehensive necropsy. Histological examinations were performed on various organs, including kidneys, liver, spleen, heart, lung, pancreas, gall bladder, stomach, jejunum, ileum, colon, caecum, spinal cord, brain, ovaries (if applicable), testes (if applicable), bone and marrow, urinary bladder, lymph nodes, and thymus. The heart, kidneys, liver, and spleen were also weighed.
Statistical analysis of the data involved comparing supplemented animals to a control group. For most measurements, individual animals served as the experimental unit, except for feed intake and feed conversion, where the pen was the unit. Clinical examination and gross pathology frequencies were analyzed using the chi-squared test. An ANOVA with repeated measures over time was applied to data including body weight, feed intake, and blood parameters. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
Throughout the 42-day study, no mortalities occurred among the piglets. Treatment with Lancer had no significant impact on key performance indicators, as average final body weight (48–50 kg), daily weight gain (0.94–0.98 kg), and daily feed intake (1.32–1.39 kg) remained comparable between treated and control groups. Furthermore, clinical examinations, hematological and biochemical parameters, gross pathology, and microscopic findings showed no treatment-related abnormalities. Organ weights for the heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys were also similar across all groups.
The tolerance study demonstrated that Lancer, even at ten times the recommended dose, had no detrimental impact on piglet performance, blood parameters (haematology and chemistry), or any observed pathological changes, whether gross or microscopic.
Lancer, at its maximum recommended dose of 250 mg/kg in complete feed, is deemed safe for weaned piglets by the FEEDAP Panel.
The FEEDAP Panel previously expressed reservations about consumer exposure to Lancer. A single study indicated no lanthanum or cerium deposition in piglet tissues, but this contradicted findings in cattle and rats. Consequently, the Panel couldn't rule out consumer exposure. Furthermore, without an identified safe exposure level, they couldn't establish a correlation between potential exposure and a harmless dose.
Previous assessments by the FEEDAP Panel (2013) examined Lancer's toxicology. It was determined to have low acute oral toxicity in rats. A 28-day rat study indicated alterations in certain serum biochemistry parameters, specifically elevated ALP and ALT activities, at doses of 250 mg/kg body weight per day and above, without establishing a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL). Furthermore, a 90-day oral toxicity study in rats reported an increased occurrence of forestomach epithelial hyperplasia across all tested doses, including 100, 300, and 600 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day (which corresponds to 8.5 mg La/kg body weight per day and 16.3 mg Ce/kg body weight per day at the lowest dose). While mutagenicity studies, including bacterial reverse mutation, in vitro mammalian chromosome aberration, and in vitro mammalian micronucleus tests, confirmed Lancer is not genotoxic, there were no available studies on its chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, or reproductive effects.
Following the initial FEEDAP Panel opinion, the applicant provided a peer review of the 90-day rat study's histopathology findings for Lancer. This study involved dose levels of 0, 100, 300, and 600 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day. A second pathologist re-examined the lungs, mesenteric lymph nodes, and stomach. Their consensus reclassified certain lesions, and this assessment was subsequently reviewed by the FEEDAP Panel.
Lung alterations were observed in certain male subjects within the 100 mg Lancer/kg bw/day group, and in both male and female subjects at 600 mg Lancer/kg bw/day. These changes were deemed localized responses, likely caused by accidental inhalation of the test substance, and thus held no significance for evaluating Lancer's systemic toxicity.
At the highest Lancer dose (600 mg/kg bw per day), an apparent increase in mesenteric lymph node microgranulomas was observed, but only in male subjects. However, this effect's link to Lancer treatment was unclear due to its high prevalence across all groups, including controls, and the lack of a similar response in males at lower doses or in females at any dose.
In both male and female subjects, a slight to moderate epithelial hyperplasia of the forestomach's limiting ridge was noted at a dosage of 600 mg/kg body weight per day. This condition was absent in control animals and appeared in only one male within the 100 mg/kg and 300 mg/kg dose groups. Consequently, a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 300 mg/kg body weight per day was established for this particular effect. It is important to recognize that the forestomach is highly responsive to local irritants, a characteristic not shared by human anatomy, rendering these findings irrelevant for human risk assessment.
The FEEDAP Panel concluded that the 90-day rat toxicity study, even at repeated doses up to 600 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day, showed no effects relevant to consumer risk assessment. The study observed lung lesions, but these were attributed to accidental aspiration. An increase in lymph node microgranulomas was noted in top-dose males, though this finding was considered equivocal. Additionally, mild hyperplasia occurred in the non-glandular forestomach of top-dose animals, an area not relevant to human physiology. The Panel established a no observed effect level (NOEL) of 300 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day, with the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) being the highest dose tested, 600 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day.
Studies on lanthanum compounds, distinct from citrate, have revealed various toxicological findings. For instance, a three-generation rat study using a mixture of oxides containing La oxide (up to 36 mg La/kg body weight per day) indicated no adverse effects, apart from a minor decrease in body weight gain among young rats across all tested dosages. Another developmental toxicity study in rats, though lacking detailed description and specific La dosage information, examined "rare earth nitrates." This study reported reduced offspring survival at doses between 80 and 400 mg/kg body weight, with no such effect observed at 16 mg/kg body weight. Furthermore, a mouse developmental toxicity study investigated lanthanum chloride (LaCl3). This research identified several neurobehavioral developmental issues, including alterations in swimming and walking behavior, eye and ear opening, and touch and visual placing responses. These effects were noted at drinking water concentrations of 125 mg/L or higher (equivalent to 25 mg LaCl3/kg body weight per day or 9.3 mg La/kg body weight per day), and a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) could not be established. In a separate evaluation, neurotoxicological effects of LaCl3 were assessed in rats. These rats were exposed to LaCl3 throughout gestation, lactation, and up to six months of age, with mothers receiving doses of 0, 0.1, 2, or 40 mg/kg body weight per day via gavage. Adverse impacts on learning, specifically in the Morris water maze, were observed at doses of 2 and 40 mg LaCl3/kg body weight per day (0.73 mg La/kg body weight per day or more), with the severity increasing proportionally with the dose. For these neurotoxicological effects, the NOAEL was determined to be 0.1 mg LaCl3/kg body weight per day, which corresponds to 0.037 mg La/kg body weight per day.
Lancer's toxicology studies revealed no consumer safety concerns at oral doses up to 600 mg/kg body weight/day. While a 28-day rat study indicated altered serum enzyme activities at 250 mg/kg body weight/day or higher, these changes weren't linked to liver or tissue toxicity. Furthermore, a 90-day rat study, using doses up to 600 mg/kg body weight/day, did not corroborate these enzyme activity changes. Lancer has been confirmed as non-genotoxic, but comprehensive assessments for reproduction/developmental toxicity, chronic toxicity, or carcinogenicity are still lacking.
Concerns regarding Lancer's potential neurodevelopmental and reproductive toxicity are raised by studies on another lanthanum compound. Specifically, LaCl3 induced adverse neurobehavioral developmental effects in mice at doses of 25 mg/kg body weight per day (9.3 mg La/kg bw per day) and above. Furthermore, neurotoxicological effects were observed in rats exposed to LaCl3 at 2 mg/kg body weight per day (0.73 mg La/kg bw per day) or more, throughout gestation, lactation, and for the initial six months of life. Consequently, an investigation into Lancer's potential reproduction and developmental toxicity, including neurodevelopmental effects, is deemed necessary.
The FEEDAP Panel observed that while the US EPA evaluated cerium (Ce) toxicology, the available data were insufficient to establish an acceptable daily intake (ADI). This was due to a lack of crucial information regarding genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity, and target organ toxicity for Ce citrate.
The extent of consumer exposure to Lancer remains unknown. This is because no new data clarifies how much of the ingested additive or its components (La and Ce) are absorbed from the digestive system. Furthermore, there's no information on whether these elements accumulate in the animals' bodies or leave residues in edible tissues.
A re-evaluation of the 90-day rat toxicity study found no consumer safety concerns up to 600 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day. While forestomach hyperplasia was observed at 300 mg Lancer/kg body weight per day, this lesion was deemed irrelevant for consumer safety.
The FEEDAP Panel cannot confirm Lancer's safety for consumers. This is due to concerns regarding potential developmental neurotoxicity of lanthanum, significant gaps in toxicological data for both cerium and lanthanum, the lack of long-term, carcinogenicity, reproductive, and developmental toxicity studies for Lancer, and missing residue data in edible tissues.
The FEEDAP Panel's prior assessment noted lanthanum's potential environmental toxicity, which varies significantly with its chemical form. Due to the limited solubility of both lanthanum and cerium in most conditions, the additive was not expected to pose a risk to aquatic ecosystems. However, a comprehensive environmental evaluation remained incomplete because no data existed regarding the additive's toxicity to terrestrial organisms. The applicant has since submitted further information to substantiate Lancer's environmental safety.
The applicant submitted data on La and Ce levels in European soil and surface water, aiming to demonstrate that Lancer's use wouldn't elevate environmental concentrations. However, specific measured concentrations of La and Ce in groundwater within EU Member States were not clearly presented.
Evaluating the environmental risk of lanthanum (La) and cerium (Ce) requires considering the additional concentrations introduced, as species can adapt to naturally occurring levels of these elements. While background soil concentrations in EU Member States range from 1–109 mg/kg for La and 1.6–266 mg/kg for Ce, the applicant failed to provide calculations for the predicted environmental concentrations (PEC) of these elements.
The applicant failed to provide predicted environmental concentrations of La and Ce in groundwater from Lancer's use.
The applicant failed to supply sufficient data regarding the ecotoxicity of Lancer to plants and earthworms, specifically lacking studies compliant with OECD 208, 207, and 222 guidelines. This deficiency prevented the determination of safe soil concentrations (PNEC values).
Without predicted environmental concentrations (PECs) or predicted no-effect concentrations (PNECs), a risk quotient cannot be calculated. Therefore, due to insufficient data, the FEEDAP Panel cannot determine Lancer's environmental safety.
Beyond existing Feed Hygiene Regulation and Good Manufacturing Practice, the FEEDAP Panel deems no additional post-market monitoring necessary.
