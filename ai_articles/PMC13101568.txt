Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) results from mutations in the Janus family kinase JAK3 gene. This deficiency in humans presents as a lack of circulating T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, alongside poorly functioning but normally numerous B cells (T−B+NK−). Our research, utilizing SCID patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and an in vitro T cell differentiation system, reveals a complete arrest in the early development of T cells in JAK3-deficient cells. However, correcting the JAK3 mutation through CRISPR/Cas9-enhanced gene targeting effectively reinstates normal T cell development. This restoration includes the generation of mature T cell populations exhibiting a diverse T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire. Furthermore, whole-genome sequencing of these corrected cells confirmed the absence of any unintended CRISPR/Cas9 off-target modifications. These findings offer a valuable method for studying human lymphopoiesis and lay the groundwork for gene correction therapies for human immunodeficiencies.

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) is characterized by significant T-cell deficiencies, sometimes accompanied by B-cell defects. The most frequent T−B+ immunodeficiencies stem from natural mutations in the autosomal recessive JAK3 gene or the X-linked common gamma chain (γC) gene (IL-2RG). Studies in mice with ablated Jak3 genes indicate its crucial role in early T-cell differentiation; these Jak3 knockout mice also exhibit significantly reduced mature B-cell populations in both bone marrow and peripheral circulation. The similar phenotypes observed in γC and JAK3 SCID suggest that JAK3's main function involves transmitting signals from γC-dependent cytokine receptors (e.g., IL-2R, IL-7R) to STAT transcription factors, which then activate downstream genes. While Jak3-deficient mice show greatly reduced thymus cellularity, their remaining thymocytes can still mature and replenish the peripheral T-cell population. This contrasts sharply with human JAK3-deficient patients, who possess very few, if any, peripheral T cells. The precise impact of JAK3 mutations on human lymphocyte progenitor development remains largely unexplored.
Current SCID treatment primarily relies on allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. However, this method carries substantial risks, including delayed immune system recovery and graft-versus-host disease. Gene therapy, particularly retroviral-based approaches, has shown promise for X-linked SCID and ADA-SCID. Yet, a significant drawback of retroviral gene therapy is the potential for severe adverse effects due to insertional mutagenesis. While self-inactivating lentiviral vectors have demonstrated efficacy in recent clinical trials, their long-term safety requires further investigation. An emerging alternative therapeutic approach involves patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). This strategy entails generating iPSCs from a patient, correcting disease-causing mutations through gene targeting, and then differentiating these corrected iPSCs into hematopoietic progenitors. These progenitors could subsequently be transplanted back into the patient to treat the disease. The recent advancement of CRISPR/Cas9-enhanced gene targeting has significantly improved the feasibility of this iPSC-based therapeutic strategy.
The OP9-DL1/4 system offers a robust in vitro platform for studying T cell development, effectively mimicking in vivo conditions. This system utilizes OP9 stromal cells engineered to express Notch ligands Delta-like-1 or Delta-like-4, creating an environment that promotes T cell induction. Notably, it efficiently drives T lymphopoiesis from induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs), which can subsequently mature into functional CD8 T cells. Although OP9 cells lack proper major histocompatibility complex (MHC) expression, this method remains invaluable for investigating early T cell commitment and thymocyte maturation processes in a controlled laboratory setting. This approach has been widely adopted by various research groups to advance our understanding of these critical developmental stages.
This paper investigates the developmental block in human T cells lacking JAK3. We show that JAK3-deficient early T cell progenitors exhibit high rates of apoptosis, mirroring observations in mouse models, which is attributed to reduced BCL2 expression. Our findings further demonstrate that correcting the JAK3 mutation using CRISPR/Cas9-enhanced gene targeting effectively restores the differentiation capacity of these progenitors. The corrected cells can then generate both NK cells and mature T cell populations, characterized by a diverse T cell receptor repertoire. This research establishes a robust platform for understanding the mechanisms of immunodeficiency in human SCID patients and for evaluating potential pharmacological and genetic treatments.
To investigate the in vitro manifestation of severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) caused by a specific Janus family kinase 3 (JAK3) gene mutation, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were created. These iPSCs originated from the skin keratinocytes of a four-month-old SCID patient. The patient exhibited a T−B+NK− clinical phenotype, and their JAK3 gene contained a homozygous C > T nucleotide substitution in exon 14. This mutation resulted in a premature stop codon (TGA) at position 613, replacing the normal arginine codon (CGA). When these JAK3-deficient iPSCs were subjected to a two-step differentiation process using OP9 and OP9-DL4 stromal monolayers—a system previously established for T cell development—a significant reduction in natural killer (NK) and T cell differentiation was observed compared to healthy controls. While the patient-derived iPSCs proliferated similarly to control iPSCs and successfully differentiated into CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors on OP9 monolayers, their subsequent development into NK and T cells on OP9-DL4 monolayers was severely impaired. Specifically, on day 14 of T cell induction, only a small population of CD7+CD16−CD56− T cells or CD7+CD16+CD56+ NK cells was detected.
T-cell development normally progresses through distinct stages, beginning with early T-cell progenitors (ETPs) transitioning from CD4−CD8− double-negative (DN) to CD4+CD8+ double-positive (DP), and finally to CD4+ or CD8+ single-positive (SP) T cells. This process is precisely regulated by the activation and repression of specific transcription factors. In healthy cells, the silencing of PU.1 gene expression and the induction of GATA3 and BCL11B genes are crucial for guiding early hematopoietic progenitors towards T-lineage commitment. However, in JAK3-deficient cells, this regulatory mechanism is disrupted. PU.1 gene expression is not fully silenced, and the induction of GATA3 and BCL11B genes is significantly reduced compared to controls, severely limiting T-cell specification. Despite this, a low level of expression of T-cell-specific genes like RAG1, RAG2, and PTCRA indicates that JAK3-deficient cells can inefficiently reach an early T-cell progenitor stage. To investigate the developmental defects caused by human JAK3 deficiency, we compared T-cell lineage commitment and maturation in JAK3-deficient cells to normal JAK3 wild-type controls. We cultured iPSC-derived CD34+ cells on OP9-DL4 monolayers and analyzed lymphocyte markers after 28 days of T-cell induction. Our findings revealed a complete absence of CD4+CD8+ DP, CD4+ SP, and CD8+ SP T cells in JAK3-deficient cells. Furthermore, CD3 and TCR αβ were not significantly detected. This complete lack of CD4+CD8+ cells suggests that human JAK3-deficient cells arrest at the CD4−CD8− DN stage. The low expression of DN3-associated genes such as PTCRA, RAG1, and RAG2 further implies an arrest at or before the DN2 stage. This observation contrasts with studies in Jak3 knockout (KO) mice, where residual thymocytes can develop past the CD4−CD8− DN stage into mature T cells, partially reconstituting the peripheral blood system, despite having a small thymus due to a block in thymocyte differentiation at the DN2 stage. In human JAK3-deficient cells, however, our data demonstrate a complete block in T-cell development. This developmental arrest is consistent with the observed absence of mature T cells in the peripheral blood of human SCID patients with JAK3 deficiency.
JAK3-deficient cells exhibit significant defects in lymphocyte development, primarily due to the absence of IL-7 signaling, which is crucial for the survival and differentiation of lymphoid progenitors. The IL-7/JAK3 signaling pathway is responsible for maintaining thymocyte homeostasis by regulating the BCL2 family of apoptotic factors. Studies in Jak3 knockout mice have shown that thymocytes and peripheral T cells display increased apoptosis, characterized by elevated levels of the pro-apoptotic factor Bax and reduced expression of the anti-apoptotic factor Bcl2. Our in vitro experiments with human JAK3-deficient cells corroborate these findings, demonstrating a similar increase in apoptosis compared to control cells. Specifically, at T cell induction day 10, apoptosis in deficient cells was 9% versus 2.2% in controls, and at day 17, it was 7% versus 1.9%. This apoptotic phenotype was further supported by increased BAX levels and decreased BCL2 levels in JAK3-deficient cells, consistent with the critical role of IL-7/JAK3 signaling in preventing programmed cell death during lymphocyte development.
To investigate whether BCL2 overexpression could mitigate T cell developmental defects in human JAK3-deficient cells, we introduced a lentivirus carrying a BCL2–2A-GFP polycistron into JAK3-deficient CD34+ cells grown in vitro. These transduced cells were then cultured on OP9-DL4 monolayers and assessed for NK and T cell markers on day 28 of T cell induction. Our results showed no presence of CD3−CD16+CD56+ NK cells in either GFP− (low BCL2, JAK3−) or GFP+ (high BCL2, JAK3−) cells. This observation aligns with existing research indicating that the absence of NK cells in both γc-deficient and Jak3-deficient mice stems from a lack of functional IL-15 signaling, independent of BCL2’s anti-apoptotic effects. However, CD3+ cells were exclusively detected in the GFP+ (high BCL2, JAK3−) population, suggesting that BCL2 successfully overcame the developmental arrest at the DN stage in JAK3-deficient cells. Despite this, a subsequent developmental block was observed at the DP stage, as no further differentiation of CD8+CD4+ DP cells occurred in the GFP+ cells. This finding supports recent evidence highlighting the necessity of intrathymic IL-7 signaling for CD8 lineage specification in DP thymocytes. These experimental outcomes parallel previous studies in γc-deficient mice and Jak3 knockout mice, where BCL2 expression was shown to rescue T cell development, but not B or NK cell development, and to improve peripheral T cell numbers.
Patient-derived iPSCs effectively model human SCID phenotypes in vitro. JAK3 deficiency impairs DN thymocyte proliferation. While BCL2 overexpression improves DN cell survival and differentiation into DP thymocytes, these DP cells fail to mature into SP T cells. This maturation defect likely stems from absent IL-7/JAK3 signaling.
To investigate if normal T cell development could be re-established in SCID patient cells lacking JAK3, we employed CRISPR/Cas9-enhanced gene targeting to correct the JAK3 mutation within induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Our strategy involved designing six guide RNAs (gRNAs) located in the introns flanking exon 14. These gRNAs were intended to direct either wild-type Cas9 or the D10A Cas9 nickase to the region of the C1837T mutation. Homology-directed repair (HDR) was facilitated by a correction template. iPSCs were transfected with plasmids: either two plasmids encoding the D10A Cas9 nickase and its corresponding paired gRNAs, or a single plasmid expressing wild-type Cas9 and a single gRNA. Following transfection, cells were cultured for two weeks in G418-containing medium. Individual colonies were then isolated, expanded, and genotyped using PCR to confirm successful gene correction. Sanger sequencing was used to further validate 12 selected clones: three from wild-type Cas9 with gRNA #1, three from wild-type Cas9 with gRNA #2, and six from D10A Cas9 nickase with paired gRNAs #1 and #2. This analysis identified two homozygously corrected clones (one from each Cas9 system) and ten heterozygously corrected clones. Successful restoration of JAK3 gene expression was subsequently confirmed at both the mRNA level via RT-PCR and the protein level through Western blot analysis.
Concerns exist regarding potential off-target genomic modifications from CRISPR/Cas9 in human therapeutic applications, particularly given reports of high off-target mutagenesis in cancer cell lines. However, recent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) studies indicate that such modifications are infrequent in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and embryonic stem cells. To assess the specificity of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated JAK3 correction in human severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) iPSCs, we conducted WGS both before and after gene correction. This involved sequencing the genomes of three corrected clones: two heterozygous clones, which were corrected using gRNA #2 and wild-type Cas9, and one homozygous clone, corrected with gRNA #1, gRNA #2, and D10A nickase Cas9.
To assess CRISPR/Cas9 specificity, potential off-target sites were identified by mapping 20-base guide sequences to the human genome, permitting up to three mismatches. Subsequently, these sites were examined for variations in iPSC samples after gene correction. Whole-genome sequencing of one homozygous and two heterozygous corrected iPSC lines revealed no mutations (SNVs or indels) within the 1,450 potential off-target sites. This confirms the high specificity of CRISPR/Cas9-directed gene correction.
To assess whether JAK3 gene correction restored T cell development, we examined NK and T cell generation, along with T cell lineage commitment and maturation. Our established in vitro OP9-DL4 system, consistent with previous research, models T cell differentiation through sequential stages observed in vivo: CD34+CD7+ (T/NK committed), CD7+CD4+CD8− (immature, single-positive), CD4+CD8+ (double-positive), and finally, CD3+CD8+ TCRαβ (mature). Mature T cells are known to be polyclonal, proliferative, and capable of cytokine secretion in response to mitogens. For this investigation, control, JAK3-deficient, and JAK3-corrected human iPSCs were first differentiated into hematopoietic progenitors on OP9 monolayers. Subsequently, CD34+ cells were isolated using anti-CD34 magnetic beads and then cultured on OP9-DL4 monolayers. Non-adherent cells were periodically analyzed for lymphocyte markers on T cell induction days 14, 21, 28, and 35. In normal control cells, 1.2 × 10^7 CD7+ cells, representing 84% of the lymphoid gate, were generated by day 14 from an initial 1–2 × 10^6 CD34+ cells. At this 14-day mark, approximately 20% of these CD7+ cells (2.4 × 10^6) were identified as CD7+CD16+CD56+ NK cells. As T cell maturation progressed, markers such as CD4, CD8, CD3, and TCR αβ became detectable. By day 35, the NK cell population had decreased to 6.9 × 10^4, and over 50% of the remaining cells were CD8 single-positive cells (1.2 × 10^6). In stark contrast, JAK3-deficient cells produced only 4.5 × 10^4 CD7+ cells (38.9% of the lymphoid gate) from the same initial number of CD34+ cells by day 14. Over extended culture periods, the total number of CD7+ cells in JAK3-deficient samples declined, and the expression of T cell markers like CD3, CD4, CD8, and TCR αβ remained negligible.
JAK3-corrected iPSCs efficiently generated NK and T cells, mirroring control cell differentiation. From 1–2 x 10^6 CD34+ cells, 4.7 x 10^6 CD7+ cells (91% of lymphoid cells) were produced by day 14, including 4.3 x 10^5 NK cells. Subsequent differentiation to days 21, 28, and 35 showed abundant expression of T cell maturation markers (CD3, CD4, CD8, and TCR αβ), indicating restored T cell development.
To assess the restoration of T cell function in JAK3-corrected cells, we investigated two key aspects: T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire and signaling pathway integrity. Flow cytometry-based TCR Vβ typing revealed that JAK3-corrected T cells expressed all 21 tested Vβ segments, indicating a broad and restored TCR repertoire (Figure 5A). This suggests successful re-establishment of diverse T cell populations. Furthermore, we evaluated the TCR signaling pathway, a crucial indicator of T cell functionality, by measuring cell surface activation markers after anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation. Three days post-stimulation, the percentage of activated CD3+CD25+CD69+ T cells in JAK3-corrected cells dramatically increased from 0.68% to 59.7%. This significant increase mirrored the activation observed in control cells (0.01% to 37.6%) (Figure 5B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CRISPR/Cas9-enhanced gene targeting of the JAK3 C1837T (p.R613X) mutation in an in vitro iPSC model system successfully restores normal T cell development, producing functional, mature T cell populations with a broad TCR repertoire.
While mouse models, including knockout and knock-in strains, have significantly advanced our understanding of lymphopoiesis and lymphocyte development over the last two decades, studying these processes in human severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) patients presents considerable challenges. Obtaining bone marrow and thymocyte samples from untreated infants with SCID is difficult due to the rarity of the condition and the urgent need for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation to address life-threatening infections. To overcome these limitations, our research utilizes a novel approach: generating large quantities of hematopoietic progenitors from patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in vitro. This strategy allows for precise investigation into the mechanisms underlying human immunodeficiency. Our findings indicate that T cell development in human JAK3-deficient SCID is entirely arrested at or before the CD4−CD8− (DN2) stage. Interestingly, while forced BCL2 expression promotes the survival and subsequent differentiation of these DN cells into DP thymocytes, these DP thymocytes fail to mature into SP T cells, a defect potentially attributable to the absence of IL-7/JAK3 signaling.
Our findings demonstrate that CRISPR/Cas9-enhanced gene targeting effectively corrects the human JAK3 mutation, restoring the developmental capacity of early T cell progenitors. These corrected progenitors successfully generate natural killer (NK) cells and mature T cell populations exhibiting a diverse T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire. Whole-genome sequencing of corrected induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines (one homozygous, two heterozygous) revealed no unintended mutations (single nucleotide variants or insertions/deletions) across 1,450 potential off-target sites, confirming the high specificity of this gene correction method. This research offers a valuable approach for studying human lymphopoiesis and lays the groundwork for gene correction therapies targeting immunodeficiencies and other single-gene disorders. For therapeutic application, we propose transplanting CD34+ cells, produced during the initial in vitro culture phase. These cells encompass early multipotent hematopoietic progenitors, capable of forming all myeloid and erythroid lineages in colony-forming assays, alongside the lymphoid cells detailed here. While our data indicate no inherent defects in the lineage specification of these in vitro-produced early hematopoietic progenitors, we have not yet achieved complete lineage generation following transplantation into immunodeficient (NSG) mice. This suggests that human hematopoietic progenitors generated in vitro may not engraft effectively in mouse bone marrow niches that support self-renewal. However, these early progenitors might integrate successfully into human bone marrow niches, which are naturally conducive to endogenous progenitor homing. Following comprehensive safety evaluations in NSG mice, phase 1 clinical trials will be essential to ascertain the engraftment potential and sustained multilineage hematopoietic reconstitution of these early progenitors in human patients.
A patient, whose family had no history of immune deficiencies, was enrolled in an IRB-approved study after parental consent, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. For the initial eight months of life, the patient experienced poor weight gain, diarrhea, and frequent hospitalizations due to recurrent bronchiolitis. At eight months, the patient was admitted with severe respiratory distress and oral thrush. Bronchoscopy revealed bacterial infections (pseudomonas, H flu, S. pneumonia) and viral organisms (respiratory syncytial virus). Immunological assessments showed severe hypogammaglobulinemia, with IgE <3 μl/ml, IgA <4 mg/dl, IgG = 29 mg/dl, and IgM = 26 mg/dl. Peripheral blood immune phenotyping indicated a complete absence of CD3+ T cells and NK cells, though B cells were present (875 μl absolute count). Mitogen studies confirmed a complete lack of response to concanavalin A, pokeweed mitogen, and phytohemagglutinin A. Genetic testing confirmed a SCID diagnosis, identifying a homozygous C > T nucleotide substitution in exon 14 of the JAK3 gene. This mutation at amino acid position 613 replaced an arginine codon (CGA) with a stop codon (TGA). This case represents the first documented link between this specific JAK3 variant (rs149316157) and clinical SCID. The patient subsequently underwent a reduced-intensity conditioning matched unrelated bone marrow transplant and, two years post-therapy, exhibits complete immune reconstitution.
To generate induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), primary keratinocytes were initially seeded in a 6-well plate. The following day, these cells underwent two consecutive rounds of transduction: they were exposed to a viral supernatant and human keratinocyte medium containing polybrene, followed by spinfection for 45 minutes each day. After the second transduction, the medium was replaced with fresh human keratinocyte medium, and cells were cultured for an additional two days. On day 5, the keratinocytes were detached using trypsin and transferred to a 10-cm dish. This dish had been prepared with mitomycin C-treated murine embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) and contained human keratinocyte medium. Two days later, on day 7, the medium was switched to human embryonic stem (ES) medium, which was refreshed daily. Cells were then continuously cultured for 3–4 weeks. Within 2–3 weeks, iPSC colonies became visible. These colonies were individually isolated and expanded on MEFs for further study. To eliminate integrated lentiviral and polycistronic sequences, the iPSCs were subsequently infected with a Cre-expressing adenovirus. The successful Cre-mediated removal of these sequences was confirmed by PCR analysis of individual colonies.
Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) were prepared for further differentiation by first forming small cell clumps. This involved treating hiPSC cultures, grown in a single well of a 6-well plate, with a collagenase-trypsin-knockout serum replacement (CTK-KSR) solution, following a previously established protocol (Ohnuki and Yamanaka, 2009). These cell clumps were then transferred to a 10-cm plate, which had been pre-seeded with 2-day-old OP9 cells. The cells were maintained in an α-MEM-based medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin/streptomycin, and 100 mM mono-thioglycerol. The culture medium was refreshed every other day, and the cells were allowed to co-culture for 18 days without subculturing. Following the 18-day co-culture period, the cells were harvested. This involved a two-step dissociation process: first, treatment with a solution containing 0.15% collagenase IV and 0.015% hyaluronidase in α-MEM medium for approximately 30 minutes, followed by a 30-minute incubation with 0.25% trypsin. Subsequently, CD34+ cells were isolated using anti-CD34+ magnetic beads (MicroBead Kit; Miltenyi Biotec). For T cell differentiation, these purified CD34+ cells were seeded onto OP9-DL4 cells and cultured in α-MEM medium containing 20% FBS, 5 ng/ml hFlt3-L, 5 ng/ml hIL-7, and 10 ng/ml hSCF. The medium was changed every other day, and the cells were transferred to fresh OP9-DL4 plates every four days to support continuous differentiation.
Flow cytometry analysis of hiPSC-derived T cells occurred after three days of stimulation with CD3/28 beads, following the manufacturer's protocol. This method was previously established.
An LSRFortessa cell analyzer (BD Bioscience) was used for cell analysis. Prior to analysis, cells were harvested and washed. Propidium iodide (PI, Sigma-Aldrich) was utilized to identify and exclude non-viable cells during surface staining. For apoptosis assays, harvested cells underwent a two-step staining process. Initially, cells were stained with surface antibodies for 30 minutes. Following a single wash with 1x PBS, cells were resuspended in 100 µl of Annexin Binding Buffer (Invitrogen), which included Annexin V-647 (Invitrogen) and PI. This mixture incubated for 15 minutes before an additional 400 ml of Annexin Binding Buffer with PI was added. To prepare iPSCs for gene correction, cells were first dissociated into single-cell suspensions using trypsin, then washed. Subsequently, 1-2 million cells were combined with 5 μg of a JAK3 repair plasmid and 5 μg of either pX330-JAK3 or pX335-JAK3 plasmid. This mixture was then used for nucleofection, following the Human Stem Cell Nucleofector Kit (program A-023, Lonza) protocol, before being plated onto MEFs. After 2 to 4 days, hES medium supplemented with 30 μg/ml G418 was introduced to select for drug-resistant colonies. These colonies were isolated after 3-4 weeks of selection and expanded for genomic DNA extraction. Genotyping was performed using specific 5' and 3' primer sets. To eliminate the Neo selection marker, iPSCs were infected with a Cre-expressing adenovirus (rAd-Cre-IE), and the loss of the marker was confirmed via PCR using designated primers (Jak3-Creout-F and Jak3-Creout-R), as detailed in Figure S2.

For whole-genome sequencing, iPSC DNA was first fragmented to an average size of 400 bp using a Covaris S2 Focused-ultrasonicator, with 130-µl samples processed in microTUBEs for two 60-second cycles at 10% duty cycle, intensity 4, and 200 cycles per burst in frequency sweeping mode. Fragment size was verified via DNA Chip analysis (DNA 1000 Kit; Agilent Technologies) on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Subsequently, libraries were prepared using an NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #E7370). The final library concentration was then quantified by qPCR, employing a KAPA Illumina Library Quantification Kit (KK4835; KAPA Biosystems) and an Applied Biosystems ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies).
An Illumina cBot and TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v.3-cBot-HS generated sequencing clusters on the flow cell. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was then conducted using an Illumina HiSeq 2500, upgraded with a TruSeq SBS Kit v.3-HS-200 cycles, to produce 2x100 single-index paired-end reads for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.
Using EMBOSS fuzznuc software, CRISPR/Cas9 guide sequences were aligned to the hg19 reference genome. This alignment, allowing up to three mismatches, identified 1,193 potential off-target sites for the first guide sequence (GTGAGATACAGATACAGACA) and 257 for the second (AATGATTTGCCTGGAATGCC).
Whole-genome sequencing reads for each sample were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using the BWA mem algorithm (v.0.7.5a). Duplicate reads were subsequently eliminated with Picard tools (v.1.100). Further processing involved local realignment and base quality recalibration, executed via GATK (v.2.7–2). Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were identified using GATK HaplotypeCaller. These SNVs and indels underwent separate recalibration, adhering to GATK Best Practices, and were subjected to quality filtering. Large deletions were detected using Genome STRiP v.2.0, referencing a 100-sample background population from the 1000 Genomes Project. Inversions, duplications, and translocations were identified with Delly v.0.6.3, while Pindel v.0.2.5a8 was employed for calling insertions.
Variants shared across all four iPSC samples were excluded from CRISPR/Cas9 off-target analysis. Remaining variants were then screened using BEDTools to identify any located within 100 base pairs of potential off-target sites. This analysis revealed no such variants, indicating that the observed variations were randomly acquired, not CRISPR/Cas9-induced off-target modifications.
Functional genetic variants were identified and analyzed. These variants exhibited low allele frequencies (under 1% in dbSNP 138, 1000 Genomes, and NHLBI-ESP 6500), were conserved across phastCons 46-way elements (Siepel et al., 2005), and possessed high CADD scores (CADD R10) (Kircher et al., 2014). Using the ANNOVAR software (Wang et al., 2010), these variants were annotated. Subsequently, they were screened for disease associations in ClinVar (v.20140902) (Landrum et al., 2014), GWAS Catalog (Welter et al., 2014), and COSMIC (v.70) (Forbes et al., 2008). Variants with CADD scores exceeding 20 were also manually checked in HGMD (Stenson et al., 2003). No disease associations were found for any of these variants, including the JAK3 C1837T variant, in the databases examined. However, the JAK3 C1837T (p.R613X) variant (rs149316157) stands apart, predicted to be significantly deleterious with a GERP score of 3.85 (Davydov et al., 2010) and a CADD score of 36. This study establishes a link between this specific JAK3 variant and a clinical case of SCID.
