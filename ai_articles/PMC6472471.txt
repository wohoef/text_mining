A framework for a comprehensive, economy-wide biophysical assessment of a circular economy (CE) is introduced, addressing the growing interest from policymakers, industry, and academia. This framework systematically links official statistics on resource extraction, use, and waste flows through a mass-balanced approach, building upon established economy-wide material flow accounting. It expands on previous methods by incorporating waste flows, recycling, and downcycled materials. The study proposes a robust set of indicators to quantify the scale and circularity of material and waste flows, including their socioeconomic and ecological loop closing. When applied to the European Union (EU28) in 2014 for CE monitoring, the framework revealed that 7.4 gigatons (Gt) of materials were processed, with only 0.71 Gt being secondary materials. This resulted in an input socioeconomic cycling rate of 9.6%. Additionally, of the 4.8 Gt of interim output flows, 14.8% underwent recycling or downcycling. These initial findings and sensitivity assessments highlight several areas for improvement: better waste reporting, explicit modeling of societal in-use stocks, defining criteria for ecological cycling, and developing disaggregated mass-based indicators to evaluate the environmental impacts of various materials and circularity initiatives. The concept of a circular economy (CE) has garnered significant interest across policy, industry, and academia (Geisendorf and Pietrulla 2017; Bocken et al. 2017a). This increased attention stems from the CE's core principles: extending product value and utility, and transforming production and consumption waste into secondary resources. These practices offer potential solutions and co-benefits for various economic and environmental challenges (Kirchherr et al. 2017; Winans et al. 2017). However, a universally agreed-upon definition of CE remains elusive, with stakeholders interpreting the concept based on their individual interests (Murray et al. 2017; Bocken et al. 2017b; Lieder and Rashid 2016; MacArthur 2013). For instance, European CE applications often prioritize business opportunities and resource efficiency, whereas China's CE development has focused on pollution control amidst rapid economic expansion (Bocken et al. 2017a). Furthermore, the CE is frequently cited for its positive impact on employment (Stahel 2016; Wijkman and Skanberg 2015; MacArthur 2013), though its broader environmental implications, including benefits and trade-offs, are still subject to extensive discussion (Pauliuk 2018; Geissdoerfer et al. 2017; Geisendorf and Pietrulla 2017).
The European Union has adopted the Circular Economy (CE) concept as a central approach to achieve more sustainable resource utilization. In 2015, the European Commission launched an enhanced CE package, emphasizing the importance of maximizing the lifespan of products, materials, and resources within the economy while simultaneously reducing waste generation. This initiative is crucial for the EU28's goal of fostering a sustainable, low-carbon, resource-efficient, and competitive economy, shifting away from a "take-make-consume and dispose" model to one focused on "recycle-and-reuse." Furthering these efforts in 2018, the European Commission introduced various CE-related policies, including measures concerning plastics and improved regulations for waste and critical raw materials. Concurrently, monitoring systems have been implemented across the EU28 to track progress.
Despite the intuitive appeal and widespread policy adoption of the circular economy (CE) concept, evaluating its progress remains a contentious topic (EASAC 2016; Haupt et al. 2017; Kovanda 2014; Hashimoto et al. 2009; Yuan et al. 2006). Much of the existing CE research has concentrated on specific substances or individual products, either at regional, national, or global levels (Huysman et al. 2017). Notably, understanding of anthropogenic cycles, particularly for metals, has advanced significantly in recent years (BIO by Deloitte 2015; Cullen and Allwood 2013; Graedel et al. 2013; Reck et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2007). Furthermore, a substantial amount of work has been dedicated to the company or industry scale (Pauliuk 2018; BSI 2017; Lieder and Rashid 2016). Nevertheless, broad, macro-level assessments of circularity at the national scale, complete with associated policy indicators, are still uncommon (Nuss et al. 2017; Haas et al. 2015; Hashimoto et al. 2004).
Circular Economy (CE) strategies typically involve two primary methods for "loop closing" (Haas et al. 2015; MacArthur 2013; UNEP 2012; Braungart et al. 2007; McDonough and Braungart 2003). One approach is socioeconomic loop closing, which reintroduces waste materials as secondary inputs through recycling. The other is ecological loop closing, achieved by utilizing renewable biomass. When these strategies successfully decrease the need for primary resources, a more circular economy can alleviate the strain associated with resource extraction. Furthermore, by diminishing the volume of waste and emissions released into the environment, it can lessen the impact on the output aspect of societal metabolism (Geissdoerfer et al. 2017). However, simply boosting recycling or making partial improvements in circularity is not enough to foster a truly sustainable economy (Cullen 2017; Zink and Geyer 2017). It is crucial to also achieve an absolute reduction in resource extraction and consumption, effectively scaling down the socioeconomic metabolism (Akenji et al. 2016). This necessity influences how CE strategies are evaluated and the types of monitoring tools required to assess both the extent of loop closing and the overall material flows within society (Krausmann et al. 2017a).
Effective monitoring tools are essential for evaluating Circular Economy (CE) strategies, particularly given several critical considerations. Firstly, not all recycling efforts genuinely decrease overall resource demand; some might merely shift environmental burdens. In fact, certain recycling processes can even demand more materials or energy than directly utilizing virgin resources. Secondly, the CE is frequently presented as a pathway to green economic growth, promising to decouple resource consumption and environmental impact from economic expansion. However, the feasibility of these claims requires rigorous evaluation. Thirdly, the expansion of manufactured capital in-use stocks across most nations significantly constrains the potential for material loop closing, as these stocks demand an ever-larger proportion of total resource use. Substantial loop closure only becomes viable when in-use stocks stabilize, allowing end-of-life materials from demolition and discards to match the resources needed for stock maintenance and replacement. Despite these complexities, comprehensive macro-level assessments of the CE, considering all material flows, remain scarce.
This article seeks to advance the development of macro-level monitoring tools for material flows within a circular economy (CE). Our foundational premise is that an effective CE should alleviate environmental pressures stemming from resource consumption. Rather than focusing on individual products, substances, or industrial symbiosis, our approach advocates for an economy-wide perspective, applicable at national or broader scales. This comprehensive scale is crucial for identifying system-wide phenomena like displacement or rebound effects (Geyer et al. 2016) and for evaluating whether overall reductions in resource use and waste flows have been achieved. Our contribution to the discourse on CE's potential and constraints involves creating a mass-based monitoring framework specifically for the European Union (European Commission 2018; Eurostat 2018). This framework builds upon earlier system-wide CE assessments at global and European levels, some of which were conducted by the authors (Haas et al. 2015). However, previous work by Haas and colleagues (2015) relied exclusively on economy-wide material flow (ew-MFA) data for material consumption and usage. Similarly, Nuss and colleagues (2017) concentrated their circularity research on metals, nonmetallic minerals, and non-energy, non-food biomass. Our current effort expands upon these predecessors in two key ways: first, by adopting a comprehensive approach that encompasses all biomass, metals, nonmetallic minerals, and fossil energy carriers; and second, by integrating and reconciling official datasets from the Statistical Office of European Communities (EUROSTAT) concerning material use, waste, and recycling (Eurostat 2017b, 2017c). We innovatively linked these datasets in a consistent and mass-balanced manner to systematically monitor resource use, waste, and recycling across the socioeconomic system. From this integrated framework, we then derived a suite of indicators designed to quantify the magnitude of input and output flows, alongside socioeconomic and ecological loop closing.
This article first details the general accounting framework, methods, indicators for CE monitoring, and an initial sensitivity assessment. Subsequently, we present and discuss the 2014 material flow results for the EU28 economy, visualized as Sankey diagrams. The final section addresses the framework's relevance, limitations, and future directions.
Our macro-level framework for monitoring the Circular Economy (CE) expands upon prior research, notably Haas et al. (2015), which suggested integrating secondary material flows into economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA) to track socioeconomic loop closing in national economies. This framework adopts a systems and material-centric view of the economy. A key innovation is its reliance on official statistical data from the EU's environmental reporting system (Eurostat 2017c, 2017b), ensuring a systematic mass-balance between material inputs and waste flows reported across various statistical sources. Secondary materials, encompassing all forms of recycling, reuse, remanufacturing, downcycling (e.g., backfilling), and cascadic use, are explicitly included. While waste statistics allowed for direct quantification of these recovered materials, other CE strategies, such as extending product lifetimes, direct reuse, remanufacturing, or sharing, proved challenging to measure directly. However, our framework can still observe the impact of these strategies indirectly. They are expected to increase the service life of in-use stocks and potentially stabilize their growth, reflected in changes to Net Additions to Stocks (NAS), as illustrated in Figure 1. Consequently, even without direct measurement, the substantial effects of these strategies on inflows, stock additions, and outflows are discernible through this CE monitoring framework. Figure 1 itself outlines the framework and throughput indicators for an economy-wide CE assessment. It is versatile, applicable from individual materials (e.g., Domestic Extraction of corn or iron) to aggregated material categories (e.g., Processed Materials of biomass, fossil energy carriers), and even to the total material level (e.g., total Domestic Extraction). The figure uses color-coding to denote data sources: orange for official ew-MFA data (Eurostat 2017c), blue for official waste and emissions statistics (Eurostat 2017b), and green for mass-balanced modeling. A transition from green to blue signifies a blend of statistical data and modeling. Further details, definitions, and results for all flows and variables in Figure 1 are provided in Table S1 and Figure S1 in the supporting information on the Journal’s website.
Figure 1 illustrates an accounting framework that maps material movement through the socioeconomic system. This framework identifies key physical stages of material flow using "throughput indicators" depicted as boxes. These stages encompass material input sources, such as domestic extraction and imports, as well as significant processing steps like material transformation, energetic use, and the accumulation of materials in in-use stocks. The framework also tracks waste treatment and end-of-life waste. Outflows, including exports and domestic processed output to the environment, represent the final destinations. Arrows connecting these boxes signify material flows, with their colors indicating the data source (e.g., orange for ew-MFA, blue for waste statistics).
To determine processed materials (PMs), domestic material consumption (DMC) and secondary material (SM) inputs are summed. DMC is calculated by subtracting exports from direct material input (DMI). DMI itself consists of materials domestically extracted (DE) and imported raw materials and manufactured goods.
Processed materials (PMs) are categorized into two primary uses: energetic (eUse) and material (mUse). Energetic use encompasses not only technical energy sources like fuelwood and biofuels but also food and feed for human and animal consumption. Most fossil energy carriers fall under eUse, with exceptions such as petrochemical feedstock. Conversely, material use includes all metal ores, metals, nonmetallic minerals, and portions of fossil and biomass materials not used for energy. Specifically, biomass allocated to mUse comprises industrial roundwood, non-food crops, and feed components like seeds and most harvested crop residues. (See Table S2 in Supporting Information S1 for comprehensive allocation details).
Material use (mUse) is categorized into three main types: extractive waste, stock-building materials, and throughput materials. Extractive waste, generated early in the processing of domestically extracted ores, moves directly from processed materials (PM) to interim output (IntOut). Stock-building materials, which include items like concrete, asphalt, or steel, are those that accumulate in long-lasting structures such as buildings, infrastructure, or durable goods, with an estimated share in mUse determined by industry data, production statistics, material flow studies, and various assumptions. Throughput materials, unlike stock-building materials, do not accumulate in in-use stocks. This category includes materials intentionally dissipated, such as salt or fertilizer minerals, along with processing losses not captured in waste statistics. It also encompasses short-lived products like packaging, newspapers, manufacturing waste, and food waste, which are reported in waste statistics.
Materials not integrated into stocks or recycled are transformed into gaseous, solid, or liquid outputs within the same year they are extracted. These outputs, along with materials from in-use stocks reaching the end of their service life through demolition and discard, are collectively termed "interim outputs" (IntOut) in Figure 1. IntOuts are categorized into two main types: emissions and End-of-Life (EoL) waste. Emissions encompass all gaseous releases, such as carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and methane, including water vapor. EoL waste includes all solid and liquid outputs. Data for these outflows were either obtained from Eurostat waste statistics or estimated through modeling and mass-balancing with input flows (further details are available in supporting information S1 and supplementary data S2 online). Emissions are non-recyclable and directly contribute to domestic processed output (DPO). A portion of the total EoL waste, specifically those reported as RCV_B (recovery other than energy recovery—backfilling) and RCV_O (recovery other than energy recovery—except backfilling) in Eurostat (2017b) waste statistics, re-enters the socioeconomic system as secondary materials. It's important to note that secondary materials that are imported or exported, like scrap or waste paper, are not separately tracked as secondary materials but are instead included within trade flows, thus not impacting circularity indicators. The remaining EoL waste, after accounting for secondary materials, is released into the environment as DPO waste, destined for landfilling, incineration, or intentional application (e.g., as manure or fertilizer). DPO is the sum of DPO emissions and DPO waste.
To achieve a closed material balance between inputs and outputs, a combination of statistical reporting (ew-MFA and waste statistics) and modeling was employed. This balancing was performed independently for both energetic use (eUse) and material use (mUse) components through two distinct calculations. Equations 1 and 2, respectively, outline the mass balancing methodologies for eUse and mUse.
The mass balance for materials used for energy (eUse) was closed by assuming their complete conversion into domestic processed output (DPO) emissions, including water vapor, and solid waste within the same year of extraction. To determine the solid waste component, we utilized waste statistics data for combustion byproducts and estimated solid waste from human and animal metabolism (excrements) using coefficients for indigestible food and feed fractions. DPO emissions were then derived by subtracting this solid waste outflow from the total eUse. Crucially, balancing items like atmospheric oxygen uptake during combustion and water consumed by humans and livestock were excluded. Consequently, all eUse outflows represent only the material content of the original processed materials (PM), such as the carbon or sulfur content in CO2 or SO2, or the average water content of excrements from food and feed intake. This method of balancing means that any discrepancies arising from statistical data inaccuracies or assumptions are absorbed into the DPO emissions (DPOe). A cross-check of DPO emissions from fossil energy carrier combustion against emission statistics revealed our estimate to be approximately 6% higher, which is considered acceptable given the incomplete coverage of all emissions in standard statistics.
To achieve a complete material balance, given the absence of precise data on existing in-use stocks, a specific methodology was employed. Initially, it was necessary to accurately differentiate between end-of-life (EoL) waste originating from material use (mUse) and waste streams from demolition, discard, and throughput materials. The overall EoL waste from mUse was obtained from waste statistics. However, it's important to note that the construction and demolition waste reported in these statistics doesn't fully encompass all EoL waste from discard and demolition, as the latter also includes items like old furniture, vehicles, and electronics. Subsequently, the quantity of waste from discard and demolition was determined by subtracting the portion of throughput materials (those with a lifespan under one year, such as packaging or food waste) within mUse from the total EoL waste from mUse reported in the statistics. Finally, Net Additions to Stocks (NAS) were computed as the disparity between new additions to stocks and the combined discard and demolition waste. This method of balancing implies that any discrepancies between input and output flows, stemming from statistical inaccuracies or assumptions, are absorbed by the demolition and discard flows, thereby affecting the calculated NAS. While direct validation of NAS for the EU28 was not feasible due to a lack of independent data, our calculated figure of 5.2 tons per capita per year for the EU28 in 2014 falls within a credible range when compared to Krausmann et al.'s (2017b) estimate of 6.7 tons per capita per year for industrial countries in the same year, acknowledging the differences in country groupings and methodologies.
The framework's indicators and material flows were determined for the four primary material categories recognized in economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA): nonmetallic minerals, metal ores and metals, fossil energy carriers, and biomass. This categorization presented difficulties because Eurostat's waste statistics, governed by Regulation (EC) No 2150/2002, classify waste based on economic sectors (NACE classification), collection methods, or hazard levels. Consequently, a single waste category often encompassed several ew-MFA material types, necessitating a careful allocation of output flows to corresponding input flows. To ensure mass balance between input and output, adjustments were made to waste flow data from official statistics to align with the system boundaries defined in ew-MFA.
Our framework for monitoring a circular economy (CE) is guided by a specific definition, initially proposed by Braungart et al. (2007) and later summarized by UNEP (2012). This definition characterizes a CE as an economic system where material flows consist of either biological materials, which re-enter ecological cycles after disposal, or materials engineered for continuous circulation within the socioeconomic system (MacArthur 2013). Drawing from this foundational definition, we established six pairs of indicators to quantify advancements in material cycle closure within a CE (Table 1). These indicators are rooted in economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA) principles and incorporate insights from prior research (Nuss et al. 2017; Haas et al. 2015; Hashimoto et al. 2004; Kovanda 2014). We differentiate between "scale indicators," which assess the overall magnitude of socioeconomic metabolism (O’Neill 2015), and "circularity rates," which evaluate socioeconomic and ecological cycling in relation to input and output flows. The necessity of independent input and output flow measurements arises from the delaying impact of in-use material stocks on output flows.
Our framework employs three pairs of indicators to quantify the scale of material and waste flows. The first pair includes Domestic Material Consumption (DMC), which serves as a proxy for the environmental pressure exerted by a national economy's direct material use, and Domestic Processed Output (DPO), which quantifies the total waste and emission outflows. To account for displacement effects stemming from international trade, we incorporate a consumption-based indicator: Raw Material Consumption (RMC). Provided by Eurostat (2017a), RMC is analogous to the material footprint (Wiedmann et al. 2015) and reflects global material use linked to domestic final consumption. The third pair of indicators, unlike traditional economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA), considers secondary material flows. Processed Materials (PM) on the input side represents the sum of DMC and secondary material inputs, while Interim Output (IntOut) on the output side measures wastes and emissions before materials are diverted for recycling or downcycling. Notably, the growth of material stocks in industrial nations means that current interim outflows are significantly less than PM, thus impeding immediate loop closure and delaying future recycling potential.
To gauge the extent of material loop closure, we introduce three sets of circularity rates, which assess material flows in relation to both processed materials (PM) and interim outputs (IntOut). The socioeconomic cycling rates quantify how much secondary material contributes to PM (Input Socioeconomic Cycling Rate, ISCr) and the proportion of IntOut that is repurposed as secondary materials (Output Socioeconomic Cycling Rate, OSCr). For biomass, developing precise circularity indicators is more complex. Lacking a clear definition and established criteria for sustainable biomass production, along with insufficient data, we define the input ecological cycling rate potential (IECrp) as the proportion of primary biomass (biomass Domestic Material Consumption, DMC) within PM. Similarly, the output ecological cycling rate potential (OECrp) represents the share of domestic processed output (DPO) from biomass within IntOut. The development of robust data and indicators for ecological cycling is essential, as it is a key component of circular economy strategies, allowing both socioeconomic and ecological cycling rates to reflect the economy's overall circularity. Finally, noncircularity indicators quantify the proportion of material flows that cannot be socioeconomically or ecologically cycled. These measure the share of fossil energy carriers used for energetic purposes (eUse) within PM and IntOut. Materials like fertilizers or de-icing salt were not categorized as noncircular due to unreliable data on their dissipation rates. The remaining percentage after accounting for these three circularity rates indicates the untapped potential for further socioeconomic cycling.
To track economy-wide circularity, the proposed indicators aim to identify both improvements and potential trade-offs. Given the interconnectedness of various materials and categories (Bleischwitz et al. 2018), boosting circularity for one material can influence the consumption of others and energy. For instance, recycling plastics, construction minerals, or metals often necessitates substantial energy expenditure for operations, infrastructure, and transportation. Therefore, an increase in recovery rates, such as for plastic waste, could paradoxically diminish overall circularity by increasing energy consumption. Our approach utilizes the proportions of socioeconomic, ecological, and noncircular flows within PM and IntOut to serve as circularity indicators, enabling the monitoring of system-wide effects stemming from circularity efforts.
Assessing uncertainty in economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA) is a nascent field. Studies indicate that global domestic extraction (DE) figures from different material flow accounts can vary by 5% to 20% due to diverse data sources and estimation methods. Furthermore, waste statistics face significant challenges, including inconsistent reporting across countries and varying data quality within the EU28.
To evaluate the impact of data variations on our Circular Economy (CE) assessment, we utilized a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis, as outlined by Saltelli et al. (2000). This involved applying a ±20% sensitivity test to the primary statistical data and allocation methods within our framework. Specifically, we examined how changes affected four scale indicators (PM, IntOut, DPO, DPOe) and two circularity indicators (ISCr and OSCr). Our tests covered five key areas: (a) economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA) data, (b) waste statistics, (c) secondary material data, (d) the division between energetic and material use, and (e) the alignment of waste flows with ew-MFA classifications. For the fifth test, sensitivity analysis was conducted separately for industrial and construction minerals, reflecting the modeling framework's structure. The outcomes, depicted in Figure 2, illustrate the relative change in each indicator resulting from these ±20% variations in the identified uncertainty sources. Further detailed results are available in Table S3 of the supporting information S1 online.
The assessment's indicators exhibited the greatest sensitivity to alterations in the foundational statistical data. Specifically, material flow data (sensitivities a–c) generated the most significant sensitivities. DPOe displayed the highest sensitivity, changing by ±26% with a ±20% variation in ew-MFA data. PM, DPO, and IntOut showed comparatively lower sensitivities. A ±20% change in waste statistics led to sensitivities under 10% for all scale indicators, while similar variations in secondary material quantities resulted in sensitivities below 4%. The ISCr and OSCr reacted nonlinearly to input data changes, with sensitivity magnitudes decreasing from 'a' to 'd', except for OSCr's sensitivity 'd'.
Changes in allocation schemes had a minor impact on indicators. Output-related scale indicators exhibited sensitivities between ±1.4% and ±2.6% when PM allocation assumptions varied by ±20%, with OSCr showing even lower sensitivity (under 1.5%). Other allocation sensitivities remained below ±1.1% for the chosen indicators.
The sensitivity analysis revealed that the CE indicators and assessment outcomes are largely resilient to changes in how waste flows are categorized by material. However, the most significant relative fluctuations in the evaluated flows and indicators stemmed from the quality of the input data.
In 2014, the EU28 processed a total of 7.4 gigatons (Gt) of materials, according to the Circular Economy (CE) monitoring framework. The primary sources for these processed materials (PM) included 5.8 Gt from domestic extraction, 0.9 Gt from net imports, and 0.7 Gt from secondary materials. A significant portion of these processed materials, 3.1 Gt, was allocated for energy provision, while 4.3 Gt served material uses. Within the material use category, 0.7 Gt comprised throughput materials, including extractive waste. However, the largest share, 3.5 Gt, was dedicated to expanding and maintaining the EU28's in-use stocks, such as buildings, infrastructure, and other long-lasting products. This resulted in a net addition of 2.6 Gt to these stocks, meaning almost one-third of all processed materials in 2014 contributed to increasing the EU28's material inventory. These additions are expected to remain in use for extended periods, influencing future waste streams and material loop closure opportunities, though their operation will also demand energy. Regarding outflows, 2.2 Gt of end-of-life (EoL) wastes were generated from demolition, discard, throughput materials, and solid wastes from energetic use. Approximately one-third of this EoL waste was recovered and re-utilized as secondary materials. The remaining two-thirds were either landfilled or incinerated, ultimately being released into the environment. Additionally, domestic processed output (DPO) emissions to the air totaled 2.5 Gt, with 1.1 Gt originating from food, feed, and biomass energy, and 1.4 Gt from fossil energy carriers.
In 2014, nonmetallic minerals constituted the most significant material flow within the EU28, accounting for 3.5 gigatons (Gt), or half of all processed materials. A substantial portion of these nonmetallic minerals, specifically 2.4 Gt, contributed to net additions to material stocks, primarily for maintaining and expanding societal infrastructure. This considerable stock growth influenced the recycling rates. While one-third (0.35 Gt) of the total end-of-life waste from nonmetallic minerals was recovered, leading to a material-specific output cycling rate of 33.8%, the input cycling rate was notably lower at 9.5%. These findings, along with data for fossil energy carriers, biomass, and metal ores and metals, are detailed in Figure 4a-d, which illustrates the material flows for these four primary categories in Gt/yr. It's important to note that the scale for metal ores is five times smaller, and balancing flows of oxygen and water are excluded from these figures.
Fossil energy carriers, totaling 1.7 Gt, were primarily consumed for energy generation, resulting in 1.5 Gt of atmospheric emissions (excluding oxygen but including water vapor), a significant contributor to climate change. Only a minor portion (0.05 Gt) of these processed carriers served non-energetic functions. While 0.15 Gt of solid and liquid residues were generated from fossil energy use, with 42% recovered (predominantly bitumen and plastics), their material-specific output circularity remained low at 4.1% due to the overwhelming energetic use. Consequently, these 1.7 Gt of fossil energy carriers did not contribute to socioeconomic cycling.
In 2014, the EU28 processed 2 Gt of biomass, with 90% originating from domestic extraction. A significant portion, 78% (1.6 Gt), was utilized for energy, including feeding livestock and humans, and generating technical energy, resulting in 1.1 Gt of emissions. Material uses, primarily wood, accounted for approximately one-fifth of the processed biomass (0.3 Gt). Of the biomass end-of-life waste, 0.2 Gt (26%) was recovered, while the remaining 0.6 Gt was disposed of in landfills or incinerated, becoming domestic processed output waste.
Metals and metal ores possess significant economic and thermodynamic value, alongside strategic importance. In 2014, 90% (0.21 Gt) of processed metals were imported, highlighting their global origin. While domestic metal extraction was minor (0.02 Gt of 0.22 Gt PM), it produced substantial extractive waste (0.17 Gt). Most processed metals, including almost a third from secondary sources, were incorporated into material stocks.
In 2014, a significant portion of processed materials (PM), specifically 65% or 4.8 Gt, became interim outputs (IntOut). The remaining 35% of PM contributed to the expansion of in-use stocks for infrastructure, buildings, and durable goods, resulting in a net addition to stocks (NAS) of 2.6 Gt. Despite almost a third of end-of-life (EoL) waste being recovered for secondary resources, the output socioeconomic cycling rate (OSCr) was a modest 14.8%. The input socioeconomic cycling rate, reflecting the proportion of secondary materials reprocessed into the domestic economy, was even lower at 9.6%. In contrast, ecological cycling showed higher theoretical potential, with the input ecological cycling rate potential (IECrp) at 24.6% and the output ecological cycling rate potential (OECrp) reaching 35.3%. The substantial reliance on fossil energy carriers in the EU28's primary energy supply, which are non-recyclable, resulted in an input noncircularity rate (INCr) of 21.2% and an output noncircularity rate (ONCr) of 32.8%.
Although our study focused on a single year, Eurostat data on material flow analysis and waste statistics offer insights into broader trends. By examining consistent data for Domestic Material Consumption (DMC) and the flow of recycled and downcycled materials from 2010 and 2012, we were able to calculate the Input Socioeconomic Cycling Rate (ISCr). Our findings revealed a modest increase in ISCr between 2010 and 2014, primarily driven by a slight reduction in DMC, rather than significant improvements in socioeconomic cycling, which remained relatively stable. Assessing ecological loop closing proved more complex, but an observed rise in biomass's share within DMC suggests an expanding potential for ecological cycling.
In 2014, our study assessed the state of the circular economy (CE) within the EU28, establishing key indicators for both the magnitude and circularity of material and waste flows. The analysis revealed that out of 7.4 gigatons (Gt) of processed materials (PMs), 6.7 Gt originated from primary resources, ultimately generating 4.8 Gt of interim outputs (IntOuts). Notably, all circularity indicators were higher when measured against outflows, a discrepancy attributed to the expanding in-use stocks within the EU28 economy. Secondary resources constituted a modest portion of these flows, representing nearly one-tenth (9.6%) of all PMs and 14.8% of all IntOuts. While acknowledging the complexities of calculating ecological cycling rates, we identified potential input and output ecological cycling rates of 24.6% and 35.3%, respectively. Our findings indicated lower socioeconomic cycling compared to previous research, specifically the 13% input socioeconomic cycling rate (ISCr) estimated by Haas and colleagues (2015) for the EU27 in 2005. This earlier study, however, relied on a simplified model for stock outflows and assumed all end-of-life stocks became waste, overlooking "hibernating stocks." This methodological difference likely led to an overestimation of waste flows and, consequently, circularity rates in their assessment.
Despite the EU28's stringent waste regulations, sophisticated collection and recovery systems, and impressive material-specific recovery rates (e.g., 25% for biomass to 70% for metals), the observed socioeconomic cycling rates for both input and output were unexpectedly low. This finding underscores that a circular economy necessitates more than just enhanced reuse and recycling of end-of-life waste, though these improvements remain crucial. Significant impediments to boosting socioeconomic cycling include the continuous growth of in-use material stocks and the substantial proportion of non-circular fossil energy carriers within processed materials (INCr). Therefore, strategies aimed at extending product lifetimes and promoting more intensive utilization of material stocks are vital for advancing a circular economy. Furthermore, addressing challenges such as improving recycling technologies and designing products for better recyclability is essential. Reducing fossil fuel consumption is also imperative for climate change mitigation and would concurrently enhance socioeconomic circularity. For renewable biomass, CE strategies should prioritize efficient, less wasteful, and cascaded uses, alongside fostering production systems that support ecological cycles, rather than merely increasing biomass input as a substitute for other materials.
Our research presents an economy-wide, mass-based framework utilizing existing material flow and waste statistics to track circular economy progress through key indicators. However, this approach has limitations and weaknesses that must be addressed to enhance its potential and ensure more reliable future assessments of a circular economy.
The integration of data from various statistical sources highlights a critical need for enhanced statistical reporting and a clearer acknowledgment of uncertainties in reported figures. Establishing an official concordance between economy-wide material flow accounts (ew-MFA) and waste statistics would be highly advantageous, mirroring practices for other statistical datasets. The quality of waste statistics, in particular, requires significant improvement, as the completeness of waste flow coverage across different countries is uncertain, and the reported data often exhibit inconsistent quality. A lack of understanding and reporting regarding the discrepancies between recovered materials and those actually recycled to replace primary materials results in an inflated estimation of socioeconomic cycling rates. For instance, research by Schiller and colleagues (2017) indicates that in Germany, only 48% of concrete and brick outflows are suitable for high-quality recycling, limiting their contribution to fresh concrete to a maximum of 32%. Similarly, a Swiss study (Haupt et al. 2017) found that actual recycling rates for PET bottles, tinplate, aluminum, paper, cardboard, and glass are considerably lower than officially reported, a trend the authors anticipate in other nations. Therefore, the EU28's ongoing pursuit of a circular economy necessitates improvements and harmonization in its statistical reporting.
Better understanding of material stocks is crucial for enhancing waste statistics and validating mass balances. This improved knowledge would allow for a more accurate consideration of "hibernating stocks" and offer insights into future end-of-life (EoL) waste trends. Current waste statistics are incomplete, as they only track EoL materials entering treatment facilities, omitting on-site downcycled waste and hibernating stocks. This leads to an inflated estimate of net additions to stocks (NAS) and an underestimation of material outflows, significantly impacting the reliability of socioeconomic loop closing indicators. Without data on the aging of in-use stocks, it's impossible to predict future EoL flows or their spatial distribution, information vital for effective waste management and circularity strategies. Addressing this knowledge gap necessitates detailed records of material stock vintages and service lifetimes, achievable through various modeling techniques depending on data availability.
The framework outlined in this paper offers mass-based indicators to track progress toward a circular economy by monitoring material loop closure. Its primary advantage lies in its system-wide perspective, which comprehensively illustrates how materials cycle within society. These indicators are valuable for identifying problem shifting and establishing targets to reduce industrial metabolism, a key objective of a circular economy, alongside specific interventions for problematic material flows. However, a limitation of these aggregated indicators is their tendency to obscure the movement of individual materials or substances. Such specific flows might be crucial for CE policies due to their criticality or environmental impact, even if their mass share is small. For instance, plastics are categorized under fossil energy carriers, and critical metals fall under metal ores and metals. To monitor changes in these specific flows, additional data from more detailed material flow analyses are necessary and should be consistently incorporated into the framework. Despite this, the mass-based indicators provide a strong data foundation that can be expanded to include more in-depth assessments of individual materials, as well as social, economic, and environmental indicators. For example, the framework could be augmented with an environmentally weighted material consumption indicator (EMC) to quantify the total environmental impact of material flows, as suggested by Van der Voet and colleagues (2009). Similarly, it could integrate CE core indicators for organizations, as proposed by Pauliuk (2018), which partly rely on material flow analysis methods. Furthermore, the framework can be adapted to model energy flows more explicitly, including differences in energy quality. A deeper understanding of the greenhouse gas implications of ecological cycling is also essential.
The assumption that all utilized biomass contributes to ecological loop closing is only valid if its production and disposal preserve the regenerative capacity of ecosystems. However, a thorough evaluation of ecological loop closing is currently hindered by insufficient data and suitable indicators. Key considerations for such an assessment include soil degradation, the overexploitation of water resources, effective management of plant nutrients and livestock manure, the regional scope of ecological cycling, and net carbon losses from cultivation. This challenge is particularly significant given that climate change mitigation and bioeconomy strategies increasingly promote biomass utilization.
While our study examines material flows and circularity solely within the EU28, a comprehensive understanding of circularity in national assessments necessitates incorporating its global, cross-country aspects. The growing trade in waste and secondary resources highlights this need. Although consumption-based indicators like RMC already account for the global impact of domestic consumption, improved documentation and integration of waste and secondary material exports and imports are crucial for the assessment framework and its indicators.
The Circular Economy (CE) serves as a crucial framework for connecting resource utilization, waste generation, and emissions, thereby facilitating the integration of environmental and economic policies. Addressing the environmental impacts stemming from industrial processes necessitates coordinated efforts across these areas. Enhanced cooperation between currently separate policy domains can yield benefits for the environment, employment, and resource security. Furthermore, advancing the CE requires close collaboration between policymakers and industries, encompassing everything from production to waste management. All these initiatives, in turn, depend on robust monitoring frameworks to evaluate the CE's contribution to sustainability goals at various levels. Integrating a systematic, mass-balanced approach with consistent statistical data demands improvements in data quality, standardization of waste statistics, and their consolidation with economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA) data. These advancements will lead to a more comprehensive understanding of both the extent and quality of circularity, ultimately clarifying its true impact on sustainability.
