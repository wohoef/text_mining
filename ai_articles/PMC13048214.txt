Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a significant global cause of vision loss, with intravitreal injections of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapies serving as the primary treatment. This study aimed to examine how anti-VEGF agents are prioritized for wet AMD within Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) Program and to assess their clinical results. The research included patients diagnosed with active choroidal neovascularization due to AMD who met NHI reimbursement criteria for anti-VEGF therapy between August 1, 2014, and May 31, 2015. The study analyzed factors influencing the choice of treatment agent and compared clinical outcomes between ranibizumab and aflibercept, along with their respective treatment protocols. A total of 166 treatment applications from 166 eyes across 159 patients were analyzed. The selection of an anti-VEGF agent was significantly influenced by patient age, the laterality of the condition, the presence of retinal pigment epithelial detachment, and a history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, or cerebral vascular accidents. While treatment patterns and clinical outcomes were comparable between ranibizumab and aflibercept, patients receiving aflibercept required significantly fewer injections over the follow-up period. Ultimately, under Taiwan's restrictive insurance framework, aflibercept was the more frequently chosen treatment for wet AMD by both patients and ophthalmologists. Nevertheless, in practical clinical application, no substantial differences in efficacy or overall clinical outcomes were observed between ranibizumab and aflibercept.

Intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections represent the primary treatment for age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a major global cause of blindness. Ranibizumab and aflibercept are the most frequently utilized anti-VEGF medications. While some research suggests aflibercept therapy effectively reduces retinal pigment epithelial detachment (RPED) and may enhance systemic absorption due to its recombinant fusion protein structure, other studies, including the VIEW 1 and VIEW 2 trials and real-world data, have found no significant differences in clinical efficacy or systemic side effects between the two drugs. Consequently, the selection of anti-VEGF agents for AMD treatment continues to be a subject of debate.
In Taiwan, the National Health Insurance (NHI) program approved ranibizumab for AMD treatment in January 2011, followed by aflibercept in August 2014. The NHI covers 3 to 7 doses of either anti-VEGF agent per eye within a two-year period, upon an ophthalmologist's recommendation. Crucially, switching between these agents is not permitted, forcing an initial choice that cannot be altered based on subsequent treatment results.
This study investigated real-world treatment patterns and outcomes for AMD patients under Taiwan's NHI program. Researchers aimed to identify patient characteristics and clinical factors influencing the choice between anti-VEGF agents. A secondary goal was to assess the clinical outcomes resulting from these treatment decisions.
NHI reimbursement for anti-VEGF agents in Taiwan required meeting specific criteria.
This retrospective hospital-based chart review included patients diagnosed with AMD at Taipei Veterans General Hospital between August 1, 2014, and May 31, 2015, who met NHI reimbursement criteria for anti-VEGF therapy. Patients with incomplete medical records, insufficient ophthalmic data, or less than a 6-month follow-up were excluded. The institutional review board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital approved the study.
The study analyzed ophthalmologist and patient characteristics to identify factors influencing anti-VEGF agent selection. Eight experienced retinal specialists at Taipei Veterans General Hospital participated, with seniority defined as over 10 years of practice. Factors examined included patient age, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), central macular thickness (CMT), previous AMD in the fellow eye, presence of retinal pigment epithelial detachment (RPED), and pre-existing cardiovascular disease.
Clinical outcomes and treatment patterns were compared between patients receiving ranibizumab and aflibercept. Researchers analyzed the presence of RPED at the final visit, total treatment dosage, final central macular thickness (CMT) and its change from baseline, and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) at four and six months post-injection.
Data analysis between groups was conducted using SPSS version 18.0, employing the chi-squared test and two-sample t-test. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
The study reviewed 166 treatment applications from 159 patients, with 43 for ranibizumab and 123 for aflibercept. Reimbursement was approved for 69.9% of applications. Disapprovals were primarily due to suspected fibrovascular scarring or polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy.
Aflibercept applications significantly outnumbered ranibizumab applications (p = 0.01). Univariate analysis revealed that patients opting for aflibercept were generally younger, had AMD in their other eye, presented with RPED, and lacked a history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, or cerebral vascular accidents, in contrast to those receiving ranibizumab. No significant disparities were observed between the two drug groups concerning best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), central macular thickness (CMT), or the ophthalmologist's seniority or sex.
The National Health Insurance program's dose limitations meant that only 46.4% of ranibizumab patients and 34.1% of aflibercept patients completed the full three monthly injections for their initial loading dose. Notably, patients treated by junior or female ophthalmologists were more likely to receive fewer than the three loading injections. Despite this, no significant differences in clinical or anatomical outcomes were observed between those who received the full loading dose and those who did not.
Within six months, patients receiving aflibercept required significantly fewer injections than those treated with ranibizumab (2.8 vs 3.0). No notable differences were found in the improvement of retinal pigment epithelial detachment, final central macular thickness, changes in CMT, or best-corrected visual acuity at four and six months post-treatment between the two agents.
Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy offers significant benefits for wet AMD, preventing blindness in many patients. However, treatment approaches can vary widely, influenced by healthcare systems, reimbursement policies, patient selection criteria, and the number of allowed injections. Despite these factors, there's limited understanding of how anti-VEGF agents are prioritized and selected in real-world scenarios, particularly under insurance program restrictions. This study aimed to shed light on the practical treatment strategies for wet AMD within Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) Program. Notably, the full reimbursement offered by the NHI program eliminated cost as a variable in the decision-making process.
When deciding which anti-VEGF therapy to use, initial best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and central macular thickness (CMT) were not primary factors. While aflibercept applications were generally more frequent, ranibizumab saw increased use in older patients or those with a history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, or cerebral vascular accidents. This indicates that systemic safety is a significant concern, given that anti-VEGF injections can elevate the risk of systemic thrombotic cardiovascular events. Aflibercept has a reported plasma half-life of about 5 to 6 days after intravitreal administration, whereas ranibizumab has a much shorter systemic half-life of approximately 0.09 days. Despite these pharmacokinetic differences, major randomized trials like VIEW 1 and VIEW 2, which compared the two drugs for AMD treatment, did not find significant variations in systemic or ocular safety. Consequently, a definitive consensus on differences in systemic exposure or side effects between intravitreal aflibercept and ranibizumab remains elusive.
Patients with a history of AMD in their fellow eye showed a preference for aflibercept over ranibizumab for treating their current AMD. This preference may stem from prior treatments for the other eye, which could have involved ranibizumab, bevacizumab, or other therapies, as aflibercept's reimbursement in Taiwan only began in August 2014. While ranibizumab and aflibercept have demonstrated comparable efficacy, prior research suggests aflibercept may offer improved anatomical and functional outcomes in cases where ranibizumab treatment was not fully satisfactory. The researchers propose that this observation might lead some patients and ophthalmologists to opt for aflibercept in a newly affected eye if the contralateral eye's response to ranibizumab was suboptimal.
The exact relationship between retinal pigment epithelial detachment (RPED) and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) remains unclear. However, RPED has been identified as a significant predictor of vision loss in AMD patients. Studies indicate that approximately half of individuals with AMD and newly diagnosed RPED experience a vision decline of more than three lines within a year. Large randomized trials have shown that aflibercept treatment leads to greater RPED flattening compared to ranibizumab over a 52-week period. Additional research has also demonstrated positive responses with aflibercept regarding RPED height, size, and overall regression. These findings likely contribute to the higher number of aflibercept applications for patients with RPED in their affected eyes. Nevertheless, it is also possible that selection bias could account for the observed lack of difference in RPED improvement between the two treatment groups.
Despite evidence suggesting that an initial loading dose of anti-VEGF injections can lead to greater visual acuity improvements, only 34% to 46% of patients in this study received the full three loading doses. This is attributed to the treatment burden and the limited number of approved doses under the reimbursement policy, leading Taiwanese ophthalmologists to prefer a pro re nata strategy to reduce the overall number of injections. Interestingly, no significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed between patients who received the loading doses and those who did not. A prior study also indicated that while loading doses are crucial for maximizing initial vision gains, they do not appear to affect the rate of visual acuity decline over a two-year period. This finding further supports the observed trend of over half of AMD patients receiving their medication under a pro re nata approach.
In a 6-month period, patients treated with aflibercept received significantly fewer injections than those treated with ranibizumab. While VIEW 1 and VIEW 2 trials suggested aflibercept offered longer efficacy, other observational studies found no difference in injection frequency between the two agents. Furthermore, no other clinical outcome differences, such as improvements in BCVA or CMT at 4 or 6 months post-treatment, were observed between ranibizumab and aflibercept.
The retrospective nature of this study presents several limitations. Selection bias is a potential concern, as factors like subretinal fluid volume and height, which were not significantly different between groups at baseline, might have influenced treatment choices and ultimately impacted outcomes. Furthermore, the National Health Insurance (NHI) program in Taiwan imposed restrictions on treatment strategies. This prevented an evaluation of switching between anti-VEGF agents or the use of combination therapies with other treatments like steroids or photodynamic therapy. The insurance program's limit of seven reimbursed injections also meant that patients requiring more frequent treatment might have experienced less favorable outcomes, which could skew the observed results.
Key factors influencing the choice of anti-VEGF drug for wet AMD, according to this study, included patient age, whether the condition affected one or both eyes, the presence of retinal pigment epithelial detachment (RPED), and a history of cardiovascular disease. Within Taiwan's restrictive National Health Insurance program, aflibercept was more frequently selected by both patients and ophthalmologists, likely due to a belief in its superior efficacy. However, real-world clinical practice revealed no significant differences in effectiveness or overall outcomes between ranibizumab and aflibercept. A notable finding was that aflibercept-treated patients received fewer injections. Further large, prospective research is recommended to explore the long-term efficacy and safety of both drugs, aiming to balance treatment burden with clinical results.
