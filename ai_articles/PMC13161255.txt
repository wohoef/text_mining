Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), a condition that can arise after injury or surgery, currently lacks reliable biomarkers, making diagnosis challenging. Our research aimed to address this by identifying and characterizing antibodies against p29ING4, initially discovered through protein array technology. For this study, we analyzed anti-p29ING4 antibodies in a diverse set of patient samples. This included 36 sera from individuals with early-stage type 1 CRPS, 66 from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, 53 from axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) patients, 29 from psoriatic arthritis (PsA) patients, 22 from trauma controls (patients with radial fractures but no CRPS), and 100 from healthy blood donors (BD). To achieve this, we developed ELISAs utilizing seven distinct p29ING4 antigens and various secondary antibodies targeting IgG, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, and IgM. Additionally, two separate tests were employed to detect immune complexes (IC) formed by p29ING4 with either IgG or IgG1. Our findings revealed promising diagnostic potential. For instance, using IgG as a secondary antibody, the A1-23 antigen showed a sensitivity of 19% and 100% specificity, yielding a likelihood ratio (LR) greater than 19 when comparing CRPS to trauma controls. Similarly, the A120-165 antigen, also with IgG, achieved 17% sensitivity and 100% specificity (LR = 17). When IgA was used with A120-165, the sensitivity was 31% with 95% specificity (LR = 6.2). Immune complexes of p29ING4 and IgG were detected in 31% of CRPS sera (11/36), 27% of RA sera (17/64), 25% of SpA sera (13/53), 17% of PsA sera (5/29), 5% of trauma control sera (1/22), and 4% of BD sera (4/100). For p29ING4 and IgG1 immune complexes, the prevalence was 39% in CRPS sera (14/36), 30% in RA sera (19/64), 25% in SpA sera (13/53), 3% in PsA sera (1/29), 9% in trauma control sera (2/22), and 4% in BD sera (4/100). Given the current absence of other specific biomarkers for type 1 CRPS, these p29ING4 autoantibodies could prove valuable in its diagnostic evaluation.

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), also known as Sudeck disease or sympathetic reflex dystrophy, is a common post-trauma or post-operative complication, affecting approximately 30 out of 100,000 individuals. Its incidence can reach 20% in patients with distal radial fractures. CRPS significantly impacts society due to the severe, long-term disability it often causes.
Current diagnostic criteria differentiate CRPS into type 1 (no nerve lesion) and type 2 (nerve lesion present).
CRPS is currently identified through clinical assessment, requiring the recognition of specific symptoms and their distinction from typical post-trauma or post-surgery effects, as well as other medical conditions. Early after an injury, this differentiation is challenging due to the overlap between CRPS and injury-related symptoms. Consequently, CRPS diagnosis is often delayed, sometimes by months or even years. However, research indicates that early intervention significantly improves treatment outcomes.
Type 1 CRPS and other neuropathic pain syndromes involve mechanical hyperalgesia, potentially mediated by cytokines like TNF-alpha. This neurogenic inflammation in the affected limb, alongside sympathetic dysfunction, is considered a primary pathogenic mechanism of type 1 CRPS.
The presence of autoantibodies in CRPS patients suggests a novel autoimmune basis for the condition, which could significantly influence diagnosis and treatment. Blaes and colleagues identified surface-binding autoantibodies in a subset of both type 1 and type 2 CRPS patients. These antibodies targeted antigens within the autonomic nervous system, evidenced by their reaction with sympathetic and myenteric plexus neurons, as well as cholinergic neuroblastoma cells, when exposed to patient sera. However, the exact origin and specificity of these antibodies remained undetermined. It was also unclear if these autoantibodies existed prior to the traumatic event. Such autoantibodies could potentially serve as a new diagnostic marker or as a target for immunosuppressive therapies like TNF-alpha inhibitors or rituximab.
Given the necessity for antibody-based tests specific to CRPS to aid in distinguishing it from other post-traumatic conditions, our research employed protein array technology. This approach allowed us to screen for autoantibodies in individuals with type 1 CRPS, ultimately leading to the identification of p29ING4 as a relevant antigen. P29ING4 is a tumor suppressor protein found universally in human cells. It plays a role in regulating cell growth and apoptosis by interacting with TP53 and EP300/p300 through its PHD-finger domain. This interaction can upregulate p65 and NF-kappa alpha, suggesting a potential involvement in inflammatory processes.
To assess diagnostic potential as early markers, various ELISAs were developed for broader control evaluation.
For this study, blood samples were gathered from several patient groups and healthy individuals. These included 36 patients diagnosed with early-stage type 1 Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS), and 22 trauma controls who had experienced distal forearm and hand fractures but showed no CRPS symptoms. Additionally, sera were collected from 64 individuals with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), 53 with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), and 29 with psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Two groups with febrile infectious diseases were also included: 68 without chronic infection (FID CI-) and 44 with chronic infection (FID CI+). Finally, 100 blood donors (BD) served as a control group. All participants provided informed consent, and the study received approval from the local ethical committee (project number 4928).
The diagnostic criteria used were Budapest Criteria 2007 for CRPS, ACR/EULAR 2010 for RA, ASAS for axSpA, and CASPAR for PsA.
Patient demographic, laboratory, and disease activity information was collected from the Medical University Hannover's Departments of Immunology and Rheumatology, Pain Medicine, and Trauma.
Patient information gathered comprised age, sex, serology, treatment, imaging, physical exam, acute phase reactants, and IASP, DAS28, and BASDAI criteria.
Blood samples from CRPS and radial fracture patients were drawn from the unaffected arm. All samples were then stored at -20°C for a maximum of two years.
To identify autoantibodies, six early-stage type 1 CRPS patient sera were analyzed using a high-density protein array (hEx1, Source BioScience LifeSciences, Germany). This method, previously detailed in our work [23], employed specific p29ING4 peptides (A1-23, A24-44, A45-70, A71-93, A94-114, A120-165) and a recombinant partial-length p29ING4 protein as antigens.
Biomatik in Wilmington, DE, USA, synthesized the peptides, which were 97-99% pure.
The partial recombinant p29ING4 protein, sourced from an Escherichia coli expression system (Source BioScience LifeSciences, Germany), comprised amino acids 109-225. SDS-PAGE confirmed its purity exceeded 90%.
ELISA tests were conducted using 96-well Maxisorb plates. Each well was coated overnight with either 1 µg of synthetic p29ING4 peptide or 0.2 µg of p29ING4 protein, both diluted in PBS. Following this, the plates were blocked for 30 minutes at room temperature with 300 µl per well of Aesku sample buffer. Subsequently, 100 µl of diluted patient sera (1:100 in Aesku sample buffer) was added to each well and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Finally, the plates underwent three washes with 300 µl of 0.05% Tween-20 PBS.
Following the wash step, 100 μl of a secondary HRP-conjugated goat anti-human antibody, prepared in Aesku sample buffer, was introduced into each well. The specific antibodies employed were IgG Fc HRP (Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe Ltd., Suffolk, UK) at a 1:10000 dilution, IgA HRP (Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe Ltd., Suffolk, UK) at 1:5000, and IgM HRP (Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe Ltd., Suffolk, UK) at 1:5000. Additionally, IgG1, IgG2, and IgG3 (all from Abcam, Cambridge, UK) were used at a 1:2000 dilution, and IgG4 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 1:1000.
Following a 30-minute pre-dilution, antibodies were added to the plates. Plates were then incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by three washes with PBS-T. The colorimetric reaction, using TMB, proceeded for up to 30 minutes as per manufacturer guidelines, with optical densities measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader.
This section details the ELISA method for detecting p29ING4 immune complexes with IgG or IgG1.
To conduct the ELISA tests, 96-well Maxisorb plates were prepared by coating each well overnight with 100 µl of a mouse monoclonal antibody targeting human p29ING4, which was diluted in PBS. Following this, the plates were blocked for 30 minutes at room temperature using 300 µl per well of PBS containing 1% rabbit serum. Subsequently, the plates underwent three washes with 300 µl of 0.05% Tween-20 PBS.
Following the wash steps, 100 µl of secondary HRP-conjugated goat anti-human antibody, diluted in 1% rabbit serum PBS, was introduced to each well. The specific antibodies employed were IgG H+L HRP (1:25000) and IgG1 (1:5000).
Following a 30-minute incubation at room temperature, plates were washed thrice with PBS-T. The colorimetric reaction was then initiated using TMB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DK) for up to 30 minutes, as per manufacturer guidelines. Optical density was subsequently measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader.
Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis determined the ELISA cut-offs. For this, CRPS patients represented the true positive rate, while post-trauma patients served as the false positive rate. All ELISAs incorporated eight samples with varying levels as standards and eight blood donors as negative controls.
Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value below 0.05. Differences within groups were assessed using Fisher's exact test, while relationships between variables were examined with Spearman's correlation.
GraphPad Prism 5 software was used to evaluate areas under the curve (AUC) and determine the diagnostic significance of the tests through ROC curve analysis.
Multivariate analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 22.0) to compare positive samples from RA, SpA, and CRPS patients, and to differentiate between positive and negative RA patients.
Patients had a median age of 55 (range 22–78) years, with 17 males and 19 females. Their mean disease duration was 2.5 months (SD 1.9 ms). C-reactive protein levels were not elevated, averaging 3 mg/l (SD 3.2 mg/l). No patients were receiving DMARDs or glucocorticoid treatment.
Blood samples were collected from 22 trauma patients (12 male, 10 female) within three months post-injury. Their median age was 39, with a range of 16 to 80 years.
Rheumatoid arthritis patients (n=64) had a median age of 60 (range 22-80) years and a median disease duration of 13 (range 1-32) years, with 50 being female. Seven patients lacked both RF and ACPA. Mean DAS28 was 3.2 ± 1.8, ESR was 17 mm ± 29 mm, and CRP was 4 mg/l ± 44 mg/l. Treatments included GCs (39 patients), conventional DMARDs (46 patients), and biological DMARDs (30 patients).
Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) patients (n=53) had a median age of 48 (range 23-65) years and a median disease duration of 16 (range 1-38) years. The cohort was predominantly female (32/53) and 26/53 were HLA-B27 positive. Ankylosing spondylitis was diagnosed in 32/53 patients. Mean BASDAI was 5.2 ± 2.3, and mean CRP was 3.2 ± 12.2 mg/l.
Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA) patients, with a median age of 50 (ranging from 34 to 68) years, included 19 females out of 29. Their median disease duration was 12.5 years (2–29), and the mean CRP level was 3.5 ± 4.9 mg/l.
Patients with febrile infectious diseases without chronic infection (FID CI−) had a median age of 54 (18–84) years. Of these, 38 were female, and their average CRP was 96.8 ± 105.1 mg/l.
Patients with infectious diseases and chronic infection (FID CI+) had a median age of 44 years (range 29–85). The majority (38/44) were male. HIV was present in 37/44, hepatitis B in 8/44, and hepatitis C in 6/44, with 10/44 co-infected with HIV and either hepatitis B or C. Their mean CRP was 92.1 mg/l ± 101.9 mg/l.
Blood donors, 53 males and 47 females, had a median age of 42 years, ranging from 18 to 60.
Analysis of CRPS patient sera revealed binding to 5 to 20 distinct autoantigens per patient, with fewer than five shared among more than two individuals. Notably, p29ING4 antibodies were identified in 4 out of 6 CRPS patients. In comparison to prior studies, these antibodies were present in 3/12 rheumatoid arthritis patients, 2/8 axial spondyloarthritis patients, and none of the 4 blood donors.
To investigate the presence of autoantibodies, we analyzed sera from 26 patients diagnosed with early-stage Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) and 22 trauma control patients who had experienced radial fractures but showed no clinical signs of CRPS. Our testing utilized a range of p29ING4-related antigens, specifically a partial length recombinant protein and several synthetic peptides: A1-23, A24-44, A45-70, A71-93, A94-114, and A120-165. Additionally, p29ING4 immune complexes (IC) were assessed. For detection, we employed various secondary antibodies targeting different human immunoglobulin classes and subclasses: IgG, IgA, IgM, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4. The results of these analyses are detailed in Table 1. This table presents the frequency of positive findings in the CRPS patient group for each antigen and antibody combination. Crucially, Table 1 also incorporates four distinct cut-off values, which were determined based on the number of positive results observed in the 22 trauma control patients. These cut-offs are expressed as 0/22, 1/22, 2/22, and 3/22, indicating the number of trauma controls (T) that tested positive for a given antibody and antigen combination. This approach allowed for a comparative evaluation of antibody prevalence in CRPS patients against a relevant non-CRPS trauma group, helping to establish the diagnostic potential of these autoantibodies.
Building on initial findings, a broader cohort of patients was subsequently assessed. This expanded evaluation included individuals with CRPS, spondyloarthritis (SpA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), febrile infectious diseases with and without chronic infection (FID CI+ and FID CI−, respectively), and blood donors (BD). The specific markers analyzed were IgG anti-A1-23, IgG anti-A120-165, IgA anti-A120-165, p29ING4 IgG immune complexes (IC), and p29ING4 IgG1 IC. The frequencies of these markers across the different patient groups are detailed in Tables 1 and 2.
For differentiating CRPS from trauma controls, several markers showed high likelihood ratios. IgG antibodies targeting A1-23 and A120-165 both achieved 100% specificity, with sensitivities of 19% (LR > 19) and 17% (LR = 17) respectively. IgA antibodies against A120-165 had a 91% specificity and 42% sensitivity (LR = 4.7). Additionally, IgG p29ING4 immune complexes (IC) offered 95% specificity and 31% sensitivity (LR = 6.2), while IgG1 p29ING4 IC yielded 91% specificity and 39% sensitivity (LR = 4.4).
No correlation was found between A1-23 IgG autoantibodies and patient clinical parameters.
In RA patients, Spearman correlation revealed a link between IgG antibodies targeting A120-165 and both DAS28 (p=0.04, r=0.2598) and ESR (p=0.04, r=0.3288).
Multivariate analysis revealed that rheumatoid arthritis patients with IgG anti-p29ING4 A120-165 antibodies exhibited significantly more tender and swollen joints compared to RA patients without these antibodies (both p < 0.001).
In RA patients, IgA autoantibodies targeting A120-165 showed correlations with several disease activity markers through Spearman's analysis. These included DAS28 (p=0.002), tender joint count (p=0.003), CRP (p=0.004), and ESR (p=0.004). However, when multivariate analysis was applied, only the tender joint count remained significantly different between RA patients with and without these IgA autoantibodies (p=0.042). No similar correlations were found for acute phase reactants or disease activity in other patient groups, such as those with febrile infectious diseases (FID).
Immune complexes (IC) involving p29ING4 and IgG were detected in 31% of CRPS patients (11 out of 36), significantly higher than the 5% (1 out of 22) observed in trauma controls (p = 0.02, LR 6.72). Similarly, IgG1 p29ING4 ICs were present in 39% of CRPS patients (14 out of 36) compared to 9% (2 out of 22) in trauma controls (p = 0.02). Combining both IgG and IgG1 IC tests boosted sensitivity to 44% in CRPS patients (16 out of 36) and 14% in trauma controls (3 out of 22) (p = 0.02, LR 3.14).
P29ING4 immune complex levels did not significantly distinguish CRPS from inflammatory rheumatic conditions.
Patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) exhibiting p29ING4 IgG1 immune complexes (IC) demonstrated a statistically significant elevation in their DAS28 scores (p = 0.025). Further analysis using Spearman's correlation revealed strong associations between these immune complexes and several indicators of disease activity in RA patients. Specifically, significant correlations were observed with DAS28, the count of swollen joints (p = 0.001, r = 0.488), the number of tender joints (p = 0.04), Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores (p < 0.0001), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (p = 0.003), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (p = 0.008). In contrast, no such correlations between CRP or ESR and p29ING4 IgG1 IC were found in other patient groups. Notably, all CRPS patients in the study presented with normal CRP levels.
For axSpA patients, no notable links were found with p29ING4 IgG immune complexes.
For psoriatic arthritis (PsA) patients, those with p29ING4 IgG immune complexes did not receive TNFi treatment, unlike a significant portion of those without.
IgG1 p29ING4 ICs were more common in febrile infectious diseases with chronic infection compared to those without (p = 0.029).
Currently, type 1 CRPS lacks a definitive diagnostic marker, relying instead on clinical assessment, including patient history, physical examination, X-rays, and three-phase bone scintigraphy. This necessitates diagnostic methods to rule out other conditions. Delayed diagnosis can worsen CRPS prognosis. Consequently, our research aimed to discover early-stage type 1 CRPS biomarkers, leading to the identification of p29ING4 as a relevant antigen.
The presence of various autoantibodies in CRPS patients has historically pointed to a potential autoimmune basis for the condition. For instance, Korr et al. reported that 30-40% of CRPS patients exhibited autoantibodies targeting inducible autonomic nervous system autoantigens. It remains unknown if these autoantibodies pre-exist the clinical manifestation of CRPS. Importantly, these specific autoantibodies were not detected in control groups, including healthy individuals, those with non-inflammatory neuropathy, or peripheral nerve lesions without CRPS. This suggests that trauma or nerve damage alone does not trigger this particular type of autoimmunity. In a related concept, Goebel et al. introduced "IRAM" (injury-triggered, regionally restricted autoantibody-mediated autoimmune disorder with minimally destructive course) as a novel autoimmune prototype exemplified by CRPS.
P29ING4 immune complexes (IC) were present in all inflammatory joint disorders. While p29ING4 antibodies distinguished CRPS from trauma controls, they were not useful for differentiating CRPS from febrile infectious diseases (FID).
While not designed for it, the study unexpectedly found a correlation between various p29ING4 test methods and disease activity in rheumatoid arthritis.
The protein p29ING4 can undergo citrullination by PAD4, which disrupts its connection with p53. While PAD4's role in RA etiology, particularly its induction by smoking, is established, citrullinated p29ING4 has not yet been identified as an autoantibody target. Furthermore, there's no current evidence linking citrullination or PAD4 to CRPS.
Even with p29ING4 antibodies present in other inflammatory conditions, these autoantibodies could aid early CRPS diagnosis. Patient history, biomarkers, and imaging readily distinguish PsA, SpA, and RA.
P29ING4 autoantibodies could aid in diagnosing early-stage type 1 CRPS, given the absence of other specific biomarkers. These antibodies were detected in 17-42% of CRPS patients, with an overall frequency of 39%, compared to 0-9% in trauma controls. This suggests their potential as a marker for both joint inflammation and early CRPS.
