Research indicates that 3,3’,5-triiodo-L-thyronine (T3) can inhibit tumor progression and possesses chemosensitizing, differentiating, and immunomodulatory effects. Conversely, L-thyroxine (T4), through its receptor on plasma membrane integrin αvβ3, stimulates solid tumor growth and neoangiogenesis, suggesting that reducing T4 levels slows tumor growth. This paper details two cases: a patient with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer and another with metastatic pancreatic cancer. Both patients experienced benefits from treatment solely with antithyroid drugs and exogenous T3 (liothyronine). Notably, in these instances, tumor progression was associated with a depletion of plasma T3, potentially serving as a new indicator of disease advancement.

The connection between thyroid function and tumor growth is well-established. Preclinical research indicates that T3 directly inhibits tumor progression, exhibiting chemosensitizing, differentiating, and immunomodulatory effects. Furthermore, type 3 deiodinase, an enzyme that inactivates T3, is an oncofetal protein found in various tumors, linked to cancer development, progression, and recurrence.
Conversely, L-thyroxine (T4) has been shown to stimulate solid tumor growth and neoangiogenesis by interacting with a thyroid hormone receptor on plasma membrane integrin αvβ3. Reducing T4 levels has been demonstrated to decrease tumor growth rates and improve responses to radiation and chemotherapy. In contrast, 3,3’,5-triiodo-L-thyronine (T3) at physiological concentrations is substantially less potent (10–100 fold) in initiating proliferative actions via the same plasma membrane receptor. Consequently, achieving depleted blood free thyroxine (FT4) alongside exogenous T3 supplementation presents a potentially viable and attainable therapeutic strategy, as indicated by recent findings.

This paper details two patients with breast and pancreatic cancers who received experimental treatment involving methimazole-induced hypothyroxinemia and exogenous T3. Their tumors showed temporary stabilization, and a new correlation was found between free T3 levels and tumor serum markers. This suggests that declining free T3 might indicate cancer progression.
In September 2012, a 67-year-old woman with a history of hypertension and endometrial polyps was diagnosed with invasive ductal breast carcinoma. The 1.7 cm tumor in her right breast showed aggressive features (Nottingham Histological Score III/III, negative ER/PR and HER2, Ki67 50%) and had spread to 8 out of 10 axillary lymph nodes. She underwent a tumorectomy and a three-level axillary lymphadenectomy, followed by a second lymphadenectomy in December 2012 due to a newly identified affected lymph node. Following surgery, she received chemotherapy from December 2012 to May 2013, initially with docetaxel and carboplatin, later switching carboplatin to cyclophosphamide due to hematologic toxicity. Radiotherapy to the breast and armpit was completed in August 2013.
In April 2014, the patient experienced a relapse with multiple bilateral lung metastases, the largest measuring 0.8 cm, identified by CT scan and confirmed with F-FDG TC/PET. Initial chemotherapy involved paclitaxel (later nab-paclitaxel due to allergy) and bevacizumab. This regimen was switched to capecitabine-bevacizumab in November 2014 due to severe neuropathy. Treatment continued until March 31st, 2015, when progression was noted with new left axillary and retropectoral adenopathies.
Prior to a documented progression in April 2014, the patient underwent an experimental I-131 scan in February 2015 to assess for potential radioactive iodine treatment. Before this procedure, her thyroid function was confirmed as euthyroid, with TSH, total T4, and total T3 levels within normal ranges. The preparation for the scan involved a three-day regimen of methimazole (15 mg/day) to inhibit thyroid production and liothyronine (25 mcg every 8 hours). While the scan itself did not reveal tumor uptake of radioactive iodine, a significant decrease in the tumor marker Ca125 was noted 12 days later. A subsequent repeat of the preparation protocol, this time without the I-131 administration, also resulted in a comparable reduction in Ca125 levels within seven days. This observation prompted a literature search, leading to the identification of induced hypothyroxinemia, as described by Hercbergs et al., as a potential explanation. Consequently, the authors contacted these researchers to explore further experimental treatment along these lines.

The patient received methimazole at 45 mg daily, alongside escalating doses of liothyronine (L-T3). This regimen continued until her TSH reached the lower limit of the normal range, a state achieved with 6.25 mcg of liothyronine every eight hours. For three months, her disease remained stable. In July 2015, an increase in Ca-125 levels was noted. This coincided with the detection of enlarged left axillary adenopathy on CT scans. Investigations revealed a consistent correlation: each rise in Ca-125 was linked to a decrease in serum free-T3. However, increasing the oral liothyronine dosage and subsequently raising plasma free-T3 levels led to a stabilization of Ca-125. The treatment proceeded with further progressive increases in liothyronine. Despite these adjustments, the doses eventually became insufficient to sustain plasma free-T3 levels. By September 2015, CT scans indicated progression in the liver, mediastinum, and lungs. Doxorubicin chemotherapy was initiated at this juncture, but it was halted after its second cycle in October 2015 due to an infusion reaction, with no discernible tumor response. Concurrently in October 2015, liothyronine administration was discontinued at a dose of 150 mcg/day due to severe trembling symptoms, which resolved after stopping the medication. The patient continued with chemotherapy and other experimental treatments until her death in April 2016, likely from a hospital-acquired infection, while hospitalized with evident hepatic progression. Throughout her illness, she was managed with antihypertensive drugs and vitamin D supplementation. Bisoprolol was added during the thyroid treatment phase to mitigate potential cardiac side effects.
A second patient, an 89-year-old female with a history of hypertension, depression, and a prior stomach adenocarcinoma treated with subtotal gastrectomy in 2010, presented in June 2015 with asthenia and depression. A CT scan revealed pancreatic cancer involving the splenic vein and lung metastases. Her serum Ca19.9 level was significantly elevated at 1255 UI/mL. Following a tumor board discussion where standard oncologic treatment was deemed unsuitable, she was offered an experimental treatment involving induced hypothyroxinemia. Initiating treatment on July 29th, 2015, she received methimazole (45 mg/day) and escalating doses of liothyronine (L-T3), administered in 2-3 daily doses. Within two weeks, at a liothyronine dose of 37.5 mcg/day, her Ca19.9 levels stabilized. After eight weeks, with the liothyronine dose increased to 43.75 mcg/day, a substantial decrease in Ca19.9 was noted, and a previously appearing skin metastasis resolved. Methimazole was discontinued due to neutropenia. Within ten days of stopping methimazole, a sharp rise in Ca19.9 occurred, coinciding with a drop in serum free-T3 levels, while T4 levels remained suppressed. The L-T3 treatment was ultimately discontinued in December 2015 due to lack of efficacy, and the patient passed away in January 2016 without further imaging. During the liothyronine treatment, she was taking vitamin D supplementation and the beta-blocker bisoprolol.
An inverse correlation between serum free-T3 and tumor markers was noted in both patients. Exogenous L-T3 elicited a biochemical response, independent of chemotherapy. Notably, tumor progression and rising serological markers aligned with a decrease in plasma free-T3, even with continued or increased L-T3 administration. This suggests a potential link between declining free-T3 and disease advancement.
The exogenous administration of T3 implies that its elevated plasma clearance must stem from either increased consumption or inactivation. This phenomenon is likely attributable to the expression of deiodinase III (DIO3) within tumor cells. DIO3 activity could concurrently lead to reduced serum free-T3 levels and the development of treatment resistance. In the first patient, this resistance was temporarily managed by increasing the exogenous L-T3 dosage. However, in vitro data indicates that a six-fold supranormal T3 concentration is required to overcome DIO3 expression. Therefore, while deiodinase III is a plausible explanation for the observed resistance, further investigation is warranted. Given the clinical difficulty in measuring DIO3 expression, monitoring blood reverse T3 (rT3) could serve as a non-invasive indicator of tumoral type 3 deiodinase activity in similar scenarios.
Tumors have been observed to consume thyroid hormones, a phenomenon previously documented in hepatic hemangiomas and attributed to the expression of tumoral DIO3. This current study, however, represents the first reported instance of a link between T3 clearance from the plasma and the progression of non-vascular tumors. The thyroid hormone fluctuations in these patients were an incidental finding during experimental treatments affecting thyroid function. It is suggested that similar occurrences might be present in other euthyroid cancer patients whose thyroid function is not routinely monitored, and that such monitoring could potentially become a standard practice.
Both patients in this study demonstrated a biochemical response to the experimental treatment, with the second patient even experiencing regression of a cutaneous metastasis. Previous research by Hercbergs et al. documented a survival advantage in patients undergoing chemotherapy who received induced hypothyroxinemia and maintenance liothyronine doses, maintaining TSH in the lower normal range. However, the tumor response observed in our patients appeared to be more closely linked to changes in serum free-T3 levels rather than serum free-T4. Historical data on high-dose T3 treatment in humans includes a 1962 study where it was found beneficial for breast cancer patients when combined with steroids. Additionally, in 1976, T3 was successfully employed as a radiosensitizing agent for high-grade gliomas. While these earlier cases did not involve chemotherapy, L-T3 is known to possess chemosensitizing properties and can indirectly enhance chemotherapy response by lowering T4 levels. Future investigations are warranted to determine if combining L-T3 with chemotherapy can prolong the observed tumor responses.
In two patients receiving exogenous L-T3, plasma free-T3 levels inversely correlated with tumor markers. While suppressive L-T3 doses appeared to inhibit tumor growth directly, early resistance developed. This resistance and tumor progression coincided with increased plasma free-T3 clearance.
