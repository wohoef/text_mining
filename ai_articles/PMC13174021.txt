In the manufacturing industry, industrial robots represent a significant source of energy consumption. Given the growing emphasis on sustainable manufacturing practices, improving the energy efficiency of these robots is crucial. A fundamental tool for analyzing and enhancing robot energy efficiency is an accurate energy consumption model. This paper addresses the analysis of industrial robot energy consumption characteristics and the development of such models. A critical step in energy consumption modeling is the identification of inertial and friction parameters. However, the joint torque typically required for this identification may not be accessible for all industrial robots. To overcome this limitation, this research proposes a novel approach utilizing software-simulated power data for parameter identification and subsequent energy consumption modeling. The effectiveness of this proposed method for parameter identification and the resulting energy consumption model are then validated through comprehensive simulations. Furthermore, the study investigates the relationship between robot speed and its energy consumption.

The concept of sustainable development gained prominence following the United Nations' 1987 "Our Common Future" report. Energy efficiency in manufacturing is a crucial component of this sustainability. As a foundational global industry, manufacturing is a significant energy consumer. Within this sector, industrial robots are increasingly vital, yet they also represent a substantial energy drain. For instance, in the automotive industry, robots account for approximately 8% of the total energy consumed throughout a vehicle's lifecycle during its production phase. Consequently, understanding and modeling the energy consumption of industrial robots is essential for optimizing their energy efficiency.
Recent research has extensively explored the energy consumption of industrial robots, identifying key energy-consuming components such as the controller, cooling fans, motors, and joint friction. Various approaches have been proposed to enhance energy efficiency. For instance, Mohammed et al. suggested selecting energy-efficient robot configurations. Rassolkin et al. and Brossog et al. investigated electricity consumption patterns under diverse operating conditions, including variations in tool weight, trajectories, workpiece placement, and speed. More detailed energy consumption models have also been developed. Meike et al. presented a comprehensive model encompassing converters, the DC bus, inverters, motors, and brakes, though it requires numerous parameters that are often proprietary and difficult to acquire. Pellicciari et al. focused on modeling energy consumption and deriving energy-optimal trajectories, assuming prior knowledge of the robot's inertial parameters. Additionally, Brossog et al. utilized Modelica-based simulations to create digital robot models, refining their accuracy with experimental data and examining the influence of operational parameters like payload and speed on energy usage.
Understanding an industrial robot's energy consumption necessitates an examination of its dynamic behavior. Crucially, the inertial and friction parameters, fundamental to this analysis, are typically proprietary and unavailable to users. Consequently, parameter identification is essential for obtaining these values, a challenge that has spurred considerable research. Existing methods, such as those proposed by Swevers et al., leverage periodic excitation to derive high-quality joint velocity and acceleration data. This data then facilitates parameter identification, often employing maximum likelihood estimation, which can be simplified to weighted linear least squares. A common requirement for these identification processes is access to joint torque data. Alternative approaches have explored indirect measurements. Paes et al., for instance, utilized feedback motor current, but this method relies on detailed, often unavailable, technical specifications like motor torque constants and transmission ratios to derive joint torques. Christoforou's work involved using sensors to measure base reaction forces and torques for online identification of inertial parameters, primarily aimed at enhancing trajectory tracking controllers.
Despite extensive research into energy consumption modeling and parameter identification for industrial robots, certain areas require further investigation. A significant challenge is the unavailability of joint torque data for some robots, including ABB models, which is typically essential for parameter identification. This paper addresses this by proposing a novel approach that utilizes software-simulated power data as a substitute for torque measurements. This method simplifies power sensing requirements, as only the total robot power is needed. Consequently, the dynamic and energy consumption models can be derived. Furthermore, the effectiveness of energy consumption models built upon dynamic models has not been thoroughly validated. This research aims to bridge this gap by testing and demonstrating the efficacy of such models.
Industrial robots typically draw AC power, with their total energy consumption (Pt) divided between the control system (Pc) and auxiliary components (Pa). These auxiliary components, such as the teach pendant, air compressor, cooling fans, and interaction PCs, consume a relatively constant amount of power. The control system's power usage (Pc) comprises power losses (Pc,loss) from components like converters, the DC bus, and inverters, as well as the power supplied to the robot's motors (Pmi). This motor power (Pmi) is further split into the power used to drive the motors (Pm) and the power lost within the motors themselves (Pm,loss).
Industrial robots commonly employ high-efficiency permanent magnet synchronous motors (83-92%). Motor power losses, as detailed in equation (1), encompass copper, iron, mechanical, and stray losses.
Motor power loss (Pm,loss) comprises iron loss (PFe), copper loss (Pcopper), mechanical loss (PF), and stray loss (Pstray).
Iron and mechanical losses within a motor remain largely constant, irrespective of the robot's load. These losses are influenced by factors such as motor materials, manufacturing processes, input voltage, and structural design. Specifically, iron loss stems from eddy currents and hysteresis within the stator, while mechanical losses arise from bearing friction and air resistance. In contrast, copper and stray losses are load-dependent, originating from the resistance of the stator and rotor. These load-dependent losses are converted into heat. Stray losses are further attributed to leakage fluxes passing through the stator winding's resistance.
Motor output power drives robot links. Figure 3 illustrates the drive system.
The torque driving link $i$, denoted as $\tau_{d,i}$, originates from the motor shaft, passes through the transmission system, and reaches the link. This torque is composed of dynamic torque ($\tau_{dyn,i}$) and friction torque ($\tau_{f,i}$), which are functions of joint positions, velocities, accelerations, and robot parameters.
The torque driving link *i* is composed of two components: inertial torque and friction torque. The inertial torque, denoted as $\tau_{dyn,i}$, is derived from the robot's dynamics, typically expressed using the Newton-Euler equation. It depends on the joint positions ($q$), joint velocities ($\dot{q}$), and inertial parameters ($\phi$), represented by the inertia matrix ($D$), Coriolis-centrifugal matrix ($C$), and gravitational vector ($G$). The friction torque ($\tau_{f,i}$) accounts for joint friction, modeled as a combination of Coulomb friction ($f_{c,i}$) and viscous friction ($f_{v,i}$), acting on the joint velocity ($\dot{q}_i$). The power required to drive link *i* ($P_{d,i}$) is then the product of the total driving torque ($\tau_{d,i}$) and the joint velocity ($\dot{q}_i$), which can be further broken down into the power needed to overcome inertia and friction.
The power required to drive each joint ($P_{d,i}$) is composed of two components: the power needed to counteract inertia ($τ_{dyn,i}q̇_i$) and the power to overcome joint friction ($τ_{f,i}q̇_i$). During acceleration, the motor supplies power to the joint. Conversely, during deceleration, the motor acts as a generator, converting the robot's kinetic energy into electrical energy. While this generated energy can theoretically be recovered by capacitors to assist other joints, industrial robots have limited energy recovery capabilities due to small capacitor values. Ultimately, this generated electrical energy is dissipated as heat through a drain resistor in the servo system. Furthermore, a portion of $P_{d,i}$ is lost as heat due to motor inefficiencies and joint friction. This paper specifically addresses the modeling of motor energy consumption, represented by the integral equation $E = \int_{0}^{t} \sum_{i=1}^{n} P_{d,i} \mu_i dt$, where $μ_i$ is the motor efficiency.
The total energy consumed is the integral of motor power, accounting for motor efficiency. This article models motor power to develop the energy consumption model.
Identifying the inertial and friction parameters, crucial for calculating motor efficiency (µ) in equation (4), is necessary. These parameters, however, are typically unknown to users. While joint torque is commonly used for this identification, it's not always accessible. To address this limitation, this paper introduces a novel method for parameter identification utilizing simulated power data, as illustrated in the flowchart in Figure 4.

To simplify parameter identification, Equation (2) is linearized into Equation (5), which relates joint torque to robot parameters.
The observation matrix is denoted as W(q,q·,q··), with its i-th row being (W(q,q·,q··))i. Φ represents the vector of inertial and friction parameters.
The paper employs least squares for parameter identification, yielding estimated parameters Φ^ = (WᵀW)⁻¹Wᵀτd + γ, where γ represents the parameter identification error.
Each robot link possesses ten inertial parameters and two friction parameters. The inertial parameters include components of the inertia tensor and the first moment of the link. Some inertial parameters are redundant or do not influence the robot's dynamics and can be removed. To streamline parameter identification and enhance its accuracy, determining the minimal set of inertial parameters is crucial. This minimal set can be derived using the method proposed by Gautier and Khalil.
Finite Fourier series are commonly used for robotic parameter identification trajectories.
For excitation trajectories, the joint position $q_i(t)$ is represented by a finite Fourier series. This series includes an offset $q_{0,i}$, a base frequency $f$, and coefficients $a_{i,j}$ and $b_{i,j}$ for $N$ terms. The trajectory's period is $T=1/f$. To effectively identify inertial parameters, the excitation frequencies must be sufficiently high, but they must also steer clear of the robot's structural resonance frequencies.
To enhance parameter identification accuracy, the excitation trajectory is optimized. This optimization uses the condition number of the observation matrix Φ as the primary criterion; a smaller condition number indicates better identification quality. The optimization objective, detailed in equation (8), is subject to the robot's physical limitations. These constraints include limits on joint position, angular velocity, and angular acceleration, as well as ensuring the trajectory remains within the robot's workspace.
The excitation trajectory is optimized using MATLAB's `fmincon` function. This optimization aims to minimize the condition number of the observation matrix, considering the robot's workspace and kinematic constraints.
Since joint torque is often unavailable for industrial robots, motor power, which is more accessible, is proposed as a practical alternative. Equation (9) relates motor power to dynamic torque, motor efficiency, and joint velocity. Combining this with the linearized dynamic model (Equation 5) leads to Equation (10), which expresses motor power in terms of a new observation matrix and identified parameters.
The new observation matrix $M$, its $i$-th row $(M)_i$, and the calculated $M$ and $\hat{\Phi}$ are defined by equations (11) and (12).
The identified parameters, denoted as $\hat{\Phi}$, incorporate the motor efficiencies ($\mu$), which are treated as a constant vector.
Since the delivered power ($P_{d,i}$) is difficult to isolate from the motor power ($P_{m,i}$), the latter is used for parameter identification. This necessitates the creation of new parameters, $\hat{\Phi}$, which are proportional to the actual inertial and friction parameters. Motor efficiency ($\mu$) is absorbed into these new parameters, impacting motor power calculations. Using equations (10)-(12), robot parameters can be identified as $\hat{\Phi}' = (M^T M)^{-1} M^T P_m + \gamma'$.
The vector of motor powers is denoted by $P_m$, and the parameter identification error vector is $\gamma'$.
Simultaneous identification of all robot link parameters is possible if individual motor power data is available. Alternatively, a sequential approach can be employed. This involves first identifying parameters for the last link (link n), and then recursively identifying parameters for preceding links (link i) using the previously obtained results for links i+1 through n.

During manufacturing, a robot's tool and load can vary. This study incorporates these changes by treating the tool and load as part of link 6. Consequently, parameter identification is re-executed. The methodology for identifying parameters and modeling energy consumption remains consistent, regardless of whether the robot is carrying a tool and load or not.
The ABB IRB 1200, a 6-DOF industrial robot, serves as the testbed for demonstrating parameter identification and energy consumption modeling using power data. While power can be directly measured, this study leverages the total motor power data conveniently provided by the RobotStudio simulation software for parameter identification. The accuracy of this simulated power data directly influences the accuracy of the parameter identification results, an aspect noted for future evaluation. Alongside power data, joint positions and total motor energy consumption were also acquired from RobotStudio, as depicted in the simulation setup in Figure 7.
The parameter identification process in this study is conducted step-by-step, beginning with the robot's final link and progressing backward to the first. This sequential approach means that results from earlier identification steps inform subsequent ones. Consequently, identifying parameters for the ABB IRB 1200 requires six distinct steps. During the parameter identification for link *i*, excitation is applied to joints *i*+1 through *n*. When simulating scenarios with loads, these loads are incorporated as part of the last link.
To generate the excitation trajectory, a base frequency of 0.1 Hz was selected, incorporating five terms from the Fourier series. Figure 8 illustrates an example of such an optimized trajectory for step 6, designed according to equation (8). The robot was then instructed to follow this optimal path, as depicted in Figure 9. During this movement, both the total motor power and the joint positions were recorded at a sampling rate of 10 Hz. To mitigate noise, the recorded joint position data was smoothed using the Fourier series from equation (7). Subsequently, joint velocity and acceleration were derived through differential calculations on the processed joint position data. This collected information was then utilized for parameter identification, as outlined in equation (13).

To analyze the impact of robot speed on energy consumption during a manufacturing task, a pick-and-place operation was simulated. The robot followed a specific path, denoted by points A through F, while equipped with a tool. During this task, the robot's load varied. It started without a load, then picked up a load at point C, released it at point A, acquired a heavier load at point A, and finally released the load again after reaching point E. This controlled variation in load allowed for the examination of speed's influence on energy usage.
Figure 13 illustrates the simulated motor power at a speed of 0.5 m/s, highlighting key aspects of the robot's energy consumption. Green text denotes trajectory via points, while pink text indicates the power consumption in Watts. Notably, during the movement from point A to B (segments 1-2), the robot operates without a load, consuming 67.255 W. Similarly, the power consumption between points E and A (segments 12-13) remains comparable when no load is present. The impact of load becomes evident when comparing segments. From B to A (segments 4-5), the robot carries a load, resulting in a power consumption of 69.516 W, exceeding the no-load scenario. Furthermore, a heavier load between points A and E (segments 9-10) leads to even greater power consumption. These observations confirm that the simulated power accurately reflects the robot's motor power characteristics. Future work will involve a quantitative comparison between these simulation results and measured data.
The impact of robot speed and load on energy consumption was investigated. Tasks were performed at various speeds (0.05 to 1 m/s) and with different loads (0, 2, and 4 kg), with the results presented in Figure 14. Findings indicated that energy consumption escalates with increasing load. For a constant load, energy consumption fluctuated with speed, with both excessively high and low speeds leading to increased energy use. This suggests an optimal speed exists for minimizing energy consumption. However, achieving this optimal speed might compromise productivity, necessitating a balance between energy efficiency and production output.
This paper investigates the energy consumption characteristics of industrial robots, with a particular emphasis on modeling motor energy usage. This modeling approach allows for optimization of robot trajectories without requiring physical modifications to the robot itself. A novel method for parameter identification is introduced, utilizing power data instead of joint torque, which is often unavailable. The ABB IRB 1200 robot serves as a case study to demonstrate this technique. Simulation results from RobotStudio confirm the efficacy of the proposed parameter identification method. Subsequently, an energy consumption model for the robot is developed using these identified parameters, and simulated results indicate its satisfactory accuracy. Future work will involve a quantitative comparison between simulated and measured power data. Furthermore, the study analyzes the relationship between robot speed and energy consumption. Findings suggest that optimal energy efficiency is achieved at moderate speeds, neither too high nor too low. However, this optimal speed must be balanced against productivity considerations, presenting a trade-off that warrants further investigation.
