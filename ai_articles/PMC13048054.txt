This prospective study investigated the effectiveness of integrated interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and team-efficiency (TE) training, specifically focusing on nursing trainees. The primary objective was to determine if workshops involving the creation of scenarios and videos offered extra advantages for developing IPC-TE skills in these trainees. The study design involved an initial pre-intervention assessment of IPC-TE skills using a mock simulation with two integrated IPC and TE (IIT) scenarios. Following this, intervention groups participated in basic and advanced workshops where they developed discipline-specific scenarios and videos. Thirty-six nursing trainees were randomly assigned to teams of five, each comprising three nursing students, one standardized medical student, and one standardized trainee from another profession. These teams were allocated to either intervention groups (scenario plus video, or scenario only) or a control group. Post-intervention, all groups underwent a formal simulation-based assessment using two different IIT scenarios. Evaluation of team performance in both mock and formal IIT simulations was conducted by instructors using the Interprofessional Team Collaboration Scale (AITCS). Additionally, trainees completed self-assessments of their attitudes and program value scores at all stages, utilizing the Attitudes Toward Interprofessional Health Care Teams Scale (ATIHCTS). The findings indicated high satisfaction among nursing trainees in the intervention group regarding the IIT intervention. Compared to the control group, intervention groups showed a more significant improvement in instructor-assessed team performance across the "partnership," "cooperation," and "shared decision making" domains of the AITCS. Similarly, self-assessed "quality of care delivery" and TE domains of the ATIHCTS also saw greater increases in the intervention groups. Notably, the "scenario plus video" group demonstrated superior overall improvement compared to the "scenario only" group. These positive changes in nursing trainees were sustained at a 4-week follow-up. In conclusion, the study suggests that scenario creation-based training significantly enhanced nursing trainees' IPC and TE behaviors and attitudes. Furthermore, the process of creating videos for newly developed nurse-specific scenarios particularly strengthened partnership and cooperation among nursing trainees and their interprofessional team members.

Within the intricate modern healthcare landscape, effective interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and team efficiency (TE) are crucial for delivering high-quality patient care. These are fundamentally built upon strong partnership, cooperation, coordination, and shared decision-making among nursing and other healthcare teams. While simulation-based TE training has been shown to reduce surgical mortality in clinical environments, its application has historically centered on junior doctors. However, the Institute of Medicine's influential report, "To Err Is Human," emphasized the necessity of TE training for all healthcare professionals. For nursing teams specifically, teamwork and TE are foundational for successful IPC, with competencies developed through collaborative processes like partnership, cooperation, coordination, and shared decision-making.
The impetus for this research stemmed from 2016 course evaluations where nursing trainees highly valued interprofessional collaboration (IPC) training focused on team efficiency (TE). The training was almost universally recommended by participants. However, some trainees suggested that integrated IPC and TE (IIT) training, tailored to specific disciplines, should be extended to all healthcare professionals. This is crucial for delivering comprehensive care to patients with complex conditions, necessitating improved understanding of professional terminology and capabilities across different healthcare disciplines. Furthermore, the experiential learning model indicates that engaging in creative activities enhances learning outcomes.
This study assessed how effective scenario/video-created workshops were in an IIT program for developing nursing trainees' IPC-TE skills.
From March 2017 to February 2018, this prospective study on integrated IPC and team efficiency (IIT) was carried out. The institutional Ethics Committee granted ethical approval (IRB 2016-12-010B), ensuring adherence to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki research principles.
The IIT intervention program recruited voluntary participants from internal medicine nurses and other health professionals, all possessing between one and three years of clinical experience. Nursing trainees, once randomly assigned, formed teams within three distinct groups: a scenario plus video group, a scenario group, and a control group. Each team comprised five individuals. Specifically, every nursing team on training day included three nursing trainees, one standardized medical student, and one standardized professional from another discipline (e.g., a pharmacist, social worker, or radiologist). For the final data analysis, a total of 36 nursing trainees were included, with 12 trainees drawn from each of the three groups (four teams per group).
To minimize variability, 24 standardized partners (12 medical students, 12 cross-professional trainees) for the nurses' IIT program underwent a 2-hour training by senior instructors. This training, conducted before both mock and formal assessments, involved watching pilot scenario videos and practicing their roles in the IIT simulation.
For both pre- and post-intervention simulation assessments, the educational committee selected two from four pre-existing Integrated Interprofessional Collaboration and Team-Efficiency (IIT) scenarios. This committee comprised experts skilled in IIT scenario development, video production, workshop organization, and program evaluation. Throughout the study's preparation, implementation, and follow-up phases, IIT team members convened monthly to refine program specifics. The nursing IIT scenarios used in simulations included anaphylactic shock, stable atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation, and acute coronary syndrome. These scenarios were executed using standardized clinical case details, simulator orientation, environmental setup, equipment, and engagement rules. Before video production, all IIT scenarios underwent testing by a nursing group to ensure content accuracy and to allow for modifications, if needed, with a focus on team efficiency, partnership, cooperation, coordination, and shared decision-making.
Prior to the IIT intervention workshops, nursing teams underwent pre-intervention mock assessments. This included a standardized introduction to simulation, covering a safe learning environment, the simulation/debriefing process, and familiarization with the SimMan 3G simulator. To enhance team efficiency and interprofessional collaboration, each mock assessment team comprised three nursing trainees, one standardized medical student, and one standardized trainee from another profession. These participants collaboratively assessed and treated two simulated patients during the two IIT scenarios.
Physician instructors, specializing in chest, internal, and family medicine, assessed team performance using specific AITCS items during the pre-intervention phase. These instructors, experienced in simulation-based teaching, then led a debriefing session, introducing the ATHCTS. Following this, workshops tailored to nurses, involving scenario and video creation, commenced.
Prior to the post-intervention evaluations, both the "scenario plus video" and "scenario" groups participated in two supplementary four-hour workshops. These sessions were designed to develop their proficiency in creating scenarios and, for one group, producing videos. The initial two hours of the basic workshop focused on familiarizing participants with the framework of an Integrated Interprofessional Collaboration and Team Efficiency (IIT) scenario and its accompanying training video. Following this, a distinct training scenario, different from those previously tested, was discussed and rehearsed during the subsequent hour. The third hour was dedicated to the development of a new, nurse-specific scenario. The final hour involved testing participants' Interprofessional Collaboration and Team Efficiency (IPC-TE) knowledge using these newly developed scenarios. The themes for these nursing IIT scenarios were derived from common clinical situations necessitating care from multiple healthcare disciplines. Nursing students formulated the content of these IIT scenarios, which were then validated by experienced nursing and medical faculty.
Medical education instructors oversaw the development and testing of scenarios. An advanced four-hour workshop, held a week after the basic one, allowed teams to refine their new nurse-specific scenarios through small group discussions and role-playing in an IIT simulation. Teams in the "scenario plus video" group also created a ten-minute training video during this advanced workshop. Two weeks following these workshops, all teams underwent the post-intervention IIT simulation assessment.
Following the intervention, a final IIT simulation assessed changes in team IPC and TE. All teams participated in two distinct IIT scenarios, different from the pre-intervention phase. Subsequently, an instructor-led debriefing session, guided by Rudolph et al.'s "debriefing with good judgment" framework, facilitated reflection, discussion, and a review of key learning objectives.
Twelve nursing trainees, all in their first year of clinical practice, were assigned to four teams within the "scenario plus video" group. Each team also included eight partners: four standardized medical students and four standardized students from other healthcare professions. Collaborating as a unit, these teams determined clinically relevant topics for their IIT scenarios. With guidance from nursing and medical faculty, four unique nurse-specific scenarios were created. Trainees were also prompted to utilize their personal devices, such as smartphones or tablets, for filming and editing their videos, drawing inspiration from prior examples.
The scenario group, like the video group, created four nurse-specific scenarios, but without producing videos.
The control group, unlike the intervention groups, did not participate in the extra workshops. They followed their usual training, serving as a baseline to assess the workshops' impact.
The Assessment of Interprofessional Team Collaboration Scale (AITCS) serves as a diagnostic tool to evaluate interprofessional collaboration (IPC) within teams. Comprising 48 statements, the AITCS categorizes IPC characteristics into four distinct subscales: Partnership (14 items), Cooperation (15 items), Coordination (7 items), and Shared Decision Making (12 items). Participants rate their agreement with each statement on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from "Never" to "Always," yielding total scores between 48 and 240. For the specific purpose of this study, a subset of 13 AITCS items was utilized to assess team efficiency (TE) in the nursing IIT scenario. This selection included two items from the Partnership domain (items 2 and 7), eight from Cooperation (items 3, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13, and 15), one from Coordination (item 7), and two from Shared Decision Making (items 2 and 3). A higher score on these selected items indicated greater efficiency of the nursing team.
The Attitudes Toward Interprofessional Health Care Teams Scale (ATHCTS), a 14-item instrument, comprises three subscales: "Quality of care delivery," "Patient-centered care," and "Team efficiency." For this study's self-assessment of interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and team efficiency (TE) attitudes, only four items from the "quality of care delivery" domain and three from the "team efficiency" domain were utilized, both pre- and post-intervention.
Following both the initial mock and subsequent formal IIT simulations, nursing trainees evaluated group performance, individual attitudes, and program value using AITCS and ATIHCTS assessments. Four weeks post-intervention, participants also completed follow-up ATIHCTS and satisfaction questionnaires. While AITCS scores measured group-level improvement, individual progress was gauged through self-reported ATIHCTS scores.
To ensure consistent evaluation, instructors underwent training using lectures, discussions, and videos. This training focused on differentiating team performance across specific AITCS and ATHCTS items. Regular meetings facilitated consensus among instructors on rating nursing team performance in areas like quality of care, team efficiency, partnership, coordination, shared decision-making, and cooperation during simulations.
Mean and standard deviation were used for data presentation. A significance level of p = 0.05 was set. Changes in team AITCS and individual ATHCTS scores from pre- to post-intervention were calculated and compared across groups. Additionally, post-intervention and follow-up satisfaction and ATHCTS scores were inter-group compared.

Similarly, there were no significant differences between the groups regarding prior work experience or previous exposure to IPC or TE training (Table 2). To evaluate the collaborative abilities of the nursing students, the team members participating in the post-intervention assessment were distinct from those in the pre-intervention assessment within each group. For instance, nursing trainees from the scenario plus video group were randomly paired with other professional trainees from the same group for the post-intervention assessment, a process mirrored in the scenario and control groups. Furthermore, the instructors demonstrated strong inter-rater reliability during both the mock and formal assessments, with acceptable kappa values (0.71, 0.67, 0.78, and 0.69) observed across the four scenarios.
Overall, nursing trainees expressed satisfaction with the intervention program. The scenario plus video group reported higher satisfaction with both interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and team efficiency (TE) aspects compared to the scenario group. While the scenario group showed greater satisfaction specifically with the IPC component, the scenario plus video group's satisfaction extended to both IPC and TE. Furthermore, the scenario group's IPC satisfaction was higher than that of the control group.
Nursing trainees in both the scenario and scenario plus video groups showed significant post-intervention improvements in attitudes toward "quality of care delivery" and "team efficiency" compared to pre-intervention levels, unlike the control group. The scenario plus video group exhibited a greater percentage increase in these positive attitudes than the scenario group. This intervention generally had a beneficial impact on nursing trainees' attitudes regarding interprofessional collaboration and team efficiency.
Following the intervention, the integrated interprofessional collaboration and team efficiency (IPC-TE) performance of both intervention groups (scenario plus video and scenario) surpassed their pre-intervention levels (Fig. 4A). Significant enhancements were observed in the "partnership," "cooperation," and "shared decision making" aspects of the AITCS. The scenario plus video group showed a more pronounced improvement compared to the scenario group (Fig. 4B). However, no notable difference was found in the "coordination" domain of the AITCS across any of the three nursing groups.
The intervention's positive impact on nursing trainees' attitudes toward "quality of care delivery" and "team efficiency" endured post-training (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, both the scenario and scenario-plus-video groups maintained high program-value scores for four weeks following the new interventional program (Fig. 2C).
Effective multiprofessional healthcare relies on streamlined team processes and the cultivation of partnerships, cooperation, coordination, and shared decision-making—all fundamental to both interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and team efficiency (TE). Prior multicenter research has shown that team training can significantly decrease surgical mortality by 18%. Beyond simply gaining knowledge and skills, simulation-based training has the capacity to alter a learner's performance. Participants in multicenter TE training programs, both medical and non-medical, frequently reported that simulation improved their ability to deliver safer patient care. Consequently, this study establishes integrated IPC and TE (IIT) training as a viable and effective method for educating nursing trainees, ultimately aiming to ensure high-quality patient care.
Simulation scenarios are effective for consistent training and assessment because simulated behavior reflects real-world performance and attitudes.
Our study's design was rooted in the principles of experiential learning theory (ELT). The process of developing a scenario guides nursing trainees through the ELT cycle. This begins with concrete experience, where a learning need is identified by reinterpreting existing experiences to create a new scenario. The second stage involves reflective observation, prompting trainees to note any discrepancies between their experience and understanding during a scenario-based simulation. Abstract conceptualization follows, as reflection from the previous stage, combined with feedback, review, and self-evaluation post-simulation, generates new ideas. Finally, active experimentation occurs, where a video is produced by applying and integrating the newly developed scenario. This comprehensive process of scenario creation encompasses identifying learning needs, engaging in simulated activities, receiving feedback, self-evaluating, applying knowledge in live situations, and planning for skill transfer.
Our study categorized nursing trainees into three distinct groups: a control group, a scenario group, and a scenario plus video group. All groups underwent pre- and post-assessments. The scenario group participated in basic and advanced workshops focused on creating nurse-specific IIT scenarios, engaging with the first three stages of the experiential learning cycle. The scenario plus video group, however, completed all four stages of this cycle, which included the additional step of video production. In contrast, the control group did not participate in any workshops involving scenario or video creation, thus bypassing the experiential learning cycle entirely. The intervention groups (scenario and scenario plus video) benefited from more extensive learning and practice opportunities compared to the control group. This aligns with Experiential Learning Theory, which posits that progressing through the experiential learning cycle enhances training effectiveness, thereby explaining the greater improvements observed in the intervention groups.
Even without extra workshops, the control group trainees reacted positively to the new IIT components in both pre- and post-assessments. This progressive improvement in their post-intervention performance and attitude underscores the effectiveness of our educational program.
To maximize participant diversity, our study incorporated principles of complexity theory by forming teams with nursing trainees and other standardized professionals. This diverse composition was crucial for developing discipline-specific IIT scenarios and videos. During the creation of these nurse-specific scenarios and videos, nursing trainees within the same team engaged in meaningful interactions, allowing them to share, develop, and critically evaluate each other's ideas. The mock and formal IIT assessments utilized cases specifically designed to require the application of nurse-specific knowledge. Healthcare professionals, in their roles, are expected to embrace new technologies and innovative approaches to problem-solving. Video production, encompassing scripting and acting, served as a team reflection method, fostering creativity and enabling teams to assess their own performance by reviewing their created scenarios. Consequently, the intervention, particularly the scenario plus video group, demonstrated greater benefits compared to the scenario-only group, a finding supported by the positive feedback from most intervention group nursing trainees regarding the creative aspects of scenario and video development.
This study collected data corresponding to the initial three levels of Kirkpatrick's training evaluation model: reaction, learning, and performance. The fourth level, results, was not assessed. Reaction data came from post-intervention and follow-up questionnaires. Learning was measured through nursing trainees' self-assessments of attitudes using ATIHCTS before, after, and at follow-up. Instructor assessments of team performance in simulated IIT scenarios, using AITCS, provided performance data. All training and assessments for the groups concluded within a six-month period.
Our study observed that effective partnerships among nursing and other healthcare professionals were linked to strong cooperation and shared decision-making. This aligns with prior research indicating that diverse team compositions, beyond creative activities like scenario and video creation, significantly shape team dynamics.
Physician instructors evaluated nursing trainees' performance during both pre- and post-intervention simulations. The chosen ATIHCTS item for assessing "coordination" focused on consistent communication regarding patient care. Our findings revealed no improvement in coordination skills post-training. This suggests a need for educators to re-evaluate interprofessional training approaches and consider adjusting the nurse's role in clinical practice, as physicians typically handle coordination among healthcare team members.
The simulation teams in this study comprised three nursing trainees alongside a diverse group of other professionals, including medical students, social workers, respiratory therapists, pharmacists, radiologists, traditional medicine practitioners, dietitians, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychotherapists, medical laboratory technicians, and speech therapists. Achieving effective coordination among such varied participants proved challenging for the nursing teams. This highlights the need for additional training to enhance the coordination skills of nursing trainees.
Healthcare demands are growing more intricate worldwide, necessitating collaborative care delivery by nurses and other healthcare professionals. This evolving landscape presents a challenge for organizations to uniformly train all healthcare workers as a cohesive team, ensuring high-quality care across diverse clinical environments. Our research indicates that integrated Interprofessional Collaboration (IPC) and Team-Efficiency (TE) training effectively enhances these skills in varied teams of nursing and other healthcare professionals. The positive outcomes of our intervention are clear, evidenced by high program satisfaction, improved attitudes, and behavioral shifts, all stemming from active engagement of nursing trainees in simulation-based assessments, and the creation of scenarios and videos. Future studies should investigate the long-term effects of this program on clinical practice and team dynamics within and between nursing and other professional teams.
