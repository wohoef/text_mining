To enable effective personalized cancer treatment, it is crucial to understand both the metabolic characteristics and genetic stability of individual tumors. This study introduces a novel link between DNA repair mechanisms and the detoxification of aldehyde compounds, which has significant implications for predicting patient outcomes and guiding treatment strategies. Our research employed Western blot and qPCR analyses on both normal and cancerous lung and liver cell lines. These experimental findings were complemented by bioinformatics analysis of data from The Cancer Genome Atlas, focusing on lung and hepatocellular cancer patients. Through these combined biochemical and bioinformatics methods, we identified a correlation between the expression levels of aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2), an enzyme responsible for detoxifying aldehydes, and XRCC1, a key protein in DNA base excision repair. We observed an inverse relationship: when the expression of one gene was low, the expression of the other tended to be high across various cancer types. Notably, a combination of low ALDH2 expression and high XRCC1 expression was found to predict a poorer overall survival, particularly in lung and liver cancer patients. Furthermore, we discovered that Mithramycin A, an inhibitor of XRCC1 expression, effectively eliminates cancer cells that exhibit low ALDH2 levels. These findings suggest that lung and liver cancers depend on robust single-strand break repair for their growth, which is facilitated by a reduced capacity for aldehyde detoxification. Consequently, the ratio of XRCC1 to ALDH2 expression levels may serve as a valuable prognostic indicator for these cancer types.

Aldehydes, ubiquitous organic molecules, are acquired through diet or generated internally as metabolic byproducts. Acetaldehyde (AcAl) is a prominent cellular aldehyde, especially recognized for its role in cancer metabolism. AcAl arises endogenously from alcohol metabolism and sugar processing. These reactive molecules can induce various DNA modifications, including strand and protein crosslinks. To mitigate their toxicity, cells employ detoxification enzymes like aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2), which converts AcAl to acetate. The cellular response to AcAl-induced DNA damage was largely unknown until recently. Emerging research indicates that the complex nature of AcAl-induced DNA damage necessitates processing by multiple DNA repair pathways. While pathways for repairing DNA crosslinks and double-strand breaks have been linked to AcAl damage, they cannot address base modifications. AcAls also cause base modifications, with N2-ethylidenedeoxyguanosine being a primary adduct. Its reduced form, N2-ethyldeoxyguanosine, has been directly associated with ALDH2, as its levels rise in ALDH2 knockout mice exposed to ethanol.
The primary pathway for repairing most endogenous DNA damage, including base modifications caused by acetaldehyde (AcAl), is base excision repair (BER). This well-established DNA repair pathway begins when a DNA glycosylase identifies a damaged base and breaks the bond connecting it to the sugar-phosphate backbone. This creates an abasic site, which is then acted upon by an AP endonuclease (APE1 in humans). APE1 cleaves the phosphodiester bond adjacent to the abasic site, resulting in a DNA strand break with a hydroxyl group at the 3' end and deoxyribose phosphate at the 5' end. Subsequently, DNA polymerase β, utilizing its AP-lyase activity, removes the deoxyribose phosphate from the 5' end and inserts a nucleotide at the 3' end of the nick. The final step involves sealing the DNA ends by a complex consisting of XRCC1 and DNA ligase IIIα. XRCC1 plays a crucial role as a scaffold protein, vital for forming and stabilizing the ternary complex of DNA polymerase β, XRCC1, and DNA ligase IIIα, which is necessary for completing BER. Consequently, cells lacking XRCC1 exhibit impaired DNA repair and heightened genomic instability.
Our prior research demonstrated that a deficiency in Base Excision Repair (BER) leads to metabolic alterations, specifically an increase in the one-carbon cycle. This cycle is known to produce and process formaldehyde, a highly reactive aldehyde. Consequently, BER-deficient cells might experience elevated formaldehyde levels, both as a metabolic byproduct and an intermediate. Based on this, we hypothesized that these cells would exhibit higher aldehyde concentrations. To manage this increased toxicity from acetaldehyde (AcAl) and formaldehyde, we proposed that BER-deficient cells would require enhanced aldehyde detoxification mechanisms, such as ALDH2. This study aimed to investigate this hypothesis and its implications for cancer aggressiveness and patient outcomes.
The study utilized several cell lines. The non-transformed bronchial epithelium cell line LIMM-NBE1 was previously characterized. Normal human fibroblasts, TIG-1 and WI38, were sourced from the Coriell Institute Cell Repository. For cancer models, the non-small cell lung carcinoma line H1299 was obtained from ATCC, and the liver carcinoma line JHH4 was provided by Prof. Ricky Sharma. All cell lines were maintained in DMEM with either 15% FBS (LIMM-NBE1) or 10% FBS (cancer lines) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere, with regular mycoplasma checks. Mithramycin A was procured from Enzo Life Sciences.
Cells were transfected with 30 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, analyzed 72 hours post-transfection. Sequences for XRCC1, TDP1, and ALDH2 were obtained from Eurogentec. Control transfections utilized a non-targeting siRNA (NC).
Whole cell extracts were prepared for Western blotting. Antibodies against XRCC1, ALDH2, β-actin, and α-Tubulin were used. Detection and quantification were performed using an Odyssey image analysis system.
To assess DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs), LIMM-NBE1 cells were cultured to full confluency in 10-cm dishes. This achieved G1 phase synchronization, as XRCC1 siRNA treatment arrests the cell cycle at this stage. A modified rapid DNA adduct recovery assay was employed for DPC detection. The procedure involved lysing 1.5 to 2 × 10^6 cells in 1 ml of M buffer (MB), which contained 6 M Guanidine Thiocyanate, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 20 mM EDTA, 4% Triton X100, 1% Sarkosyl, and 1% Dithiothreitol. DNA was then precipitated with 1 ml of 100% ethanol and subjected to three washes in a wash buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 150 mM NaCl, and 50% ethanol). Subsequently, the DNA was solubilized in 1 ml of 8 mM NaOH. A portion of the recovered DNA was digested with 50 μg/ml proteinase K for 3 hours at 50 °C. Quantitation was performed using PicoGreen dye following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration was further verified by slot-blot analysis and immunodetection with an anti-dsDNA antibody. For DPC quantification, DNA was digested with benzonase for 30 minutes at 37 °C. Proteins were then precipitated using the standard Trichloroacetic Acid protocol, dissolved in Laemmli buffer, separated by SDS-PAGE, and visualized through silver staining.
To visualize ALDH2, immunofluorescence was performed using standard protocols. Cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 minutes. Following this, permeabilization was achieved with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 minutes at 4°C. Cells were then blocked for 1 hour with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Incubation with an anti-ALDH2 antibody (catalog number 102-10,056, Cambridge Biosciences) was conducted in 5% BSA-PBS containing 0.01% Tween 20. Indirect detection of the antigens was accomplished using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were visualized with Hoechst 33,342. Images were captured using confocal microscopy on a Zeiss LSM 710 microscope.
To quantify gene expression, total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy kit and converted to cDNA with a SuperScript RT-PCR system. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was then performed using a Fast SYBR® Green Master Mix. The comparative CT method was employed for quantification, with GAPDH and B2M serving as endogenous controls. Specific primers were utilized for ALDH2, GAPDH, B2M, TBP, XRCC1, APE1, and TDP1. Reactions were conducted on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System.
For survival assays, 500 cells were plated per well in 6-well plates, with three technical replicates. In experiments shown in Figures 8D and 8E, siRNA transfections preceded cell seeding by 24 hours. For Figure 9A, cells were allowed to adhere for a minimum of 18 hours before the addition of the Sp1 inhibitor at specified concentrations. The Sp1 inhibitor was applied for a total of 48 hours, with a replenishment at the 24-hour mark. Following treatment, surviving cells were cultured for 10–14 days to allow for colony formation. Colonies were then fixed for 30 minutes using 0.4% methylene blue in methanol. Only colonies exceeding 50 cells were quantified using the automated GelCount™ system. All presented graphs represent the average of at least three independent experiments.

Our prior research demonstrated that cells with deficiencies in Base Excision Repair (BER) adapt their metabolic processes to combat oxidative stress by generating antioxidant molecules like glutathione (GSH). Building on this, we hypothesized that BER-deficient cells might employ a similar strategy to protect against DNA damage induced by aldehydes, by enhancing their detoxification mechanisms. To investigate this, we experimentally reduced the expression of XRCC1, a crucial protein in the BER pathway, in the non-transformed cell line LIMM-NBE1 (referred to as NBE1) using small interfering RNA (siRNA). Following 72 hours of this knockdown, we observed a significant elevation in ALDH2 expression at both the messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that the localization of ALDH2 remained unchanged, with the increased expression appearing in the cytoplasm. This upregulation of ALDH2 in response to XRCC1 depletion was also evident in two other normal human fibroblast cell lines, TIG1 and WI38. While APE1, POLβ, and XRCC1 are all vital components of BER, APE1 possesses a broader range of cellular functions, and its depletion leads to severe toxicity and diverse DNA lesions, from apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites to double-strand breaks. Cells with POLβ deficiencies are BER-impaired, but to a lesser extent than those lacking XRCC1. Consequently, we considered XRCC1 knockdown to be an optimal model for studying BER deficiency. However, BER deficiency can also be simulated by inducing unresolved single-strand breaks (SSBs). This can be achieved by depleting tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1), an enzyme responsible for processing stalled topoisomerase I complexes at the 3-prime end of DNA single-strand breaks. Similar to XRCC1 depletion, TDP1 knockdown also resulted in an increase in ALDH2 expression. This finding suggests that the accumulation of SSBs is a trigger for ALDH2 upregulation. Subsequently, we explored whether this compensatory mechanism was connected to the detoxification of aldehydes and their resulting products, specifically DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs). Despite the enhanced ALDH2 levels, we detected a slight but statistically significant increase in DPCs in XRCC1-deficient cells compared to control cells treated with a non-targeting siRNA. This observation implies that even with increased ALDH2, DPCs continue to form more frequently in BER-deficient cells. In a positive control experiment, inhibiting aldehyde detoxification with the broad-spectrum ALDH inhibitor Disulfiram led to a substantial accumulation of DPCs in NBE1 cells, confirming that ALDH2's detoxification activity can indeed prevent DPC formation. Based on these results, we concluded that BER-deficient cells accumulate SSBs. These SSBs may, either directly or indirectly by increasing reactive aldehydes, lead to an increase in DPCs. In response to this increased DPC burden, cells appear to upregulate ALDH2 expression, likely as a protective measure to prevent further accumulation of these damaging crosslinks.
DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs) pose a significant risk to DNA replication and overall genomic stability. Their accumulation has been documented in aging, neurodegenerative conditions, and the development of cancer. To investigate why cancer cells struggle to prevent DPCs and other DNA damage induced by reactive aldehydes, we conducted bioinformatics analyses of ALDH2 mRNA levels across various cancer types using the Oncomine® database. Our findings revealed a striking trend: when ALDH2 mRNA expression is significantly altered, it is predominantly downregulated. Specifically, ALDH2 mRNA levels were notably low in melanoma, sarcoma, leukemia, and bladder cancer. This observation is particularly relevant given that ALDH2 mRNA is naturally expressed at its highest levels in tissues like the liver, brain, and lungs. In cancerous forms of these tissues, ALDH2 mRNA levels were found to be reduced. Concurrently, we observed that XRCC1 mRNA expression levels generally mirrored ALDH2 levels in reverse, showing higher expression in most cancerous tissues. This inverse relationship was more pronounced in sarcoma and bladder cancer, and notably in liver, brain, and lung cancers. On average, across all cancer types examined, ALDH2 was downregulated by more than twofold, while XRCC1 was upregulated by more than twofold. These collective analyses suggest that the increased presence of DPCs in cancer cells may stem from a deficiency in ALDH2 expression. This deficiency, however, is accompanied by elevated XRCC1 expression, a protein crucial for the base excision repair (BER) pathway. This interplay between reduced aldehyde detoxification and increased DNA repair capacity is a key finding of our study.
To further investigate the significance of the relationship between XRCC1 and ALDH2 gene expression in cancer, we examined whether the observed pattern of low ALDH2 and high XRCC1 mRNA levels in most cancers (as depicted in Fig. 2) had an impact on patient outcomes. We utilized provisional mRNA data from "The Cancer Genome Atlas" (TCGA), accessed through the cBioPortal database. Our analysis focused on lung cancer (522 samples, detailed in Table S2) and liver cancer (422 samples, detailed in Table S3), where substantial differences in ALDH2 and XRCC1 mRNA levels were evident (illustrated in Figs. 3A and 4A). For comparative purposes, we also included data from esophageal cancer (186 samples, detailed in Table S4), a cancer type characterized by very similar XRCC1 and ALDH2 mRNA levels (as shown in Fig. 5A). Our examination of the 5-year overall survival rates revealed that XRCC1 mRNA levels, when considered independently, did not demonstrate any prognostic value across these cancer types (as seen in Figs. 3B, 4B, and 5B). In contrast, a significant finding emerged regarding ALDH2 expression. We observed that low ALDH2 mRNA levels, determined by stratifying patient data at the median value, strongly predicted a poorer prognosis in both lung and liver cancer patients (supported by Figs. 3C and 4C). This prognostic significance was notably absent in esophageal cancer, where ALDH2 mRNA levels showed no predictive value (as indicated in Fig. 5C). Therefore, while XRCC1 mRNA levels alone do not serve as a prognostic indicator, low ALDH2 expression is associated with a worse 5-year overall survival, but this association is specifically limited to cancers exhibiting a pronounced disparity between ALDH2 and XRCC1 expression.
In liver cancer patients, ALDH2 mRNA expression significantly impacted overall survival, while XRCC1 mRNA levels alone did not serve as a predictive factor (Fig. 4). To explore this further, we divided patients into groups based on their XRCC1 mRNA expression levels (low and high) and examined if this stratification enhanced the prognostic significance of ALDH2 mRNA expression. A striking observation was that ALDH2 mRNA levels held no prognostic value within the low XRCC1 mRNA expression group (Fig. 6A). However, in patients with high XRCC1 mRNA expression, low ALDH2 mRNA expression was strongly associated with a very poor prognosis (Fig. 6B). This finding was further supported by observations that XRCC1 mRNA levels were elevated in samples with low ALDH2 mRNA, and conversely, ALDH2 mRNA levels were reduced in samples with high XRCC1 mRNA (Fig. 6C and D). These results corroborated the previously identified inverse relationship between XRCC1 and ALDH2 expression (Fig. 1). To strengthen the link to our in vitro findings from Fig. 1, we also assessed the role of APE1 and TDP1 mRNA levels in stratifying ALDH2 expression and prognosis in liver cancer (Fig. S1). We noted that APE1, whose depletion does not solely induce single-strand breaks (SSBs) [25], did not stratify the prognostic value of ALDH2 (Fig. S1A and S1B). In contrast, TDP1, whose depletion effectively mimics XRCC1 loss (Fig. 1F), was found to stratify ALDH2 prognosis similarly to XRCC1, albeit with a less pronounced effect (Figs. S1C and S1D). POLβ levels, however, did not demonstrate any prognostic value in liver carcinomas (data not shown). Subsequently, we investigated the potential of the XRCC1 mRNA to ALDH2 mRNA expression ratio as a predictive factor. Our analysis revealed that this ratio was significantly more effective in predicting the 5-year overall survival rate for both lung (Fig. 7A, compare to 3C) and liver (Fig. 7B, compare to 4C) cancers compared to ALDH2 levels alone. In oesophageal cancer, where XRCC1 and ALDH2 mRNA levels were comparable (Fig. 5A), the XRCC1 mRNA/ALDH2 mRNA ratio did not offer any improvement in predicting overall survival (Fig. 7C, compare to 5C). Therefore, we conclude that combining XRCC1 and ALDH2 mRNA levels for stratification can significantly improve the prediction of 5-year overall survival rates in liver and lung cancer patients, potentially aiding in the refinement of their treatment strategies.
Our findings indicate a relationship between XRCC1 and ALDH2 mRNA levels, where a high XRCC1 to low ALDH2 ratio appears to correlate with increased tumor aggressiveness. This led us to investigate whether disrupting this balance could negatively impact cancer cell survival. To explore this, we utilized two cancer cell lines: H1299, derived from lung cancer and exhibiting low ALDH2 expression, and JHH4, a liver cancer cell line with high ALDH2 expression (Figure 8A). We then proceeded to reduce the expression of XRCC1, ALDH2, or both, and assessed cellular survival through a clonogenic assay. The H1299 cells, characterized by low ALDH2 levels, demonstrated a poor tolerance to reductions in both XRCC1 and ALDH2 protein levels (Figure 8B and 8D, last column). In contrast, JHH4 cells, with high ALDH2 expression, managed better following a combined knockdown of XRCC1 and ALDH2 (Figure 8C and 8E, last column). This resilience in JHH4 cells is likely attributable to their ability to retain some ALDH2 expression even after the knockdown (Figure 8C). Interestingly, reducing ALDH2 expression alone led to increased survival rates in both H1299 (Figure 8D, column 3) and JHH4 cells (Figure 8E, column 3). This observation supports our earlier finding that lower ALDH2 levels are advantageous for cancer cell proliferation (Figure 2). Furthermore, a single knockdown of XRCC1 did not significantly reduce the survival of H1299 cells, even with their already low ALDH2 levels (Figure 8D, second column). We hypothesize that this might be due to residual Base Excision Repair (BER) activity resulting from incomplete siRNA-mediated knockdown of XRCC1 expression [25]. Consequently, we propose that H1299 cells could be sensitized by a comprehensive shutdown of the BER pathway. Our recent research has revealed that inhibiting the Sp1 transcription factor leads to decreased expression of the BER protein APE1 [25], along with other components of this pathway [30]. Based on this, we investigated the survival of H1299 and JHH4 cells when treated with Mithramycin A, an Sp1 inhibitor. We observed that H1299 cells were highly sensitive to very low doses of Mithramycin A, whereas JHH4 cells exhibited resistance (Figure 9A). Following Sp1 inhibition, we also confirmed reduced expression of XRCC1 and APE1 in both JHH4 (Figure 9B) and H1299 (Figure 9C) cells via qRT-PCR. Given that Sp1 regulates the entire BER pathway, its inhibition proved more effective in eliminating H1299 cells than depleting XRCC1 alone. These findings provide proof of principle that, although low ALDH2 expression confers a growth advantage to cancer cells (Figure 8D and 8E), they may still remain dependent on an efficient BER pathway (Figure 9A). In summary, our study identified a common profile in several cancer types characterized by reduced ALDH2 and elevated XRCC1 expression, which is associated with a poor prognosis. Cancer cells exhibiting this profile could potentially be targeted using BER enzyme inhibitors or by inhibiting the pathway through suppression of BER gene expression.
Cancer development is fundamentally linked to metabolic alterations. Advances in understanding cancer metabolism highlight the importance of identifying pathways that hinder tumor progression, aiming to develop effective clinical strategies for patients. Specifically, targeted pathways should represent unique mechanisms cancer cells employ to enhance their proliferation and survival. These metabolic pathways often leave behind discernible waste products, which can serve as indicators for novel metabolic routes utilized by cancer cells. The Base Excision Repair (BER) pathway is among the earliest DNA repair mechanisms to become deregulated during cancer progression. In this study, we utilized XRCC1 levels as a measure of BER efficiency. XRCC1 depletion was chosen as a model due to the distinct type of lesion it causes, in contrast to APE1 depletion, and the significant BER deficiency it induces, compared to POLβ depletion. Our prior research demonstrated that loss of XRCC1 initiates metabolic changes mirroring those observed in many cancers. We hypothesized that modifications in aldehyde metabolism might contribute to these changes. Aldehydes, including acetaldehyde (AcAl), methylglyoxal, and formaldehyde, have recently garnered considerable attention for their role as metabolic byproducts in cancer. When linked to metabolic pathways that benefit cancer, aldehyde production presents a dual threat, associated with significant genotoxicity. Cancer cells depend on detoxification enzymes like ALDH2 to neutralize aldehydes and maintain a degree of metabolic balance. However, cancer cells frequently experience an overabundance of aldehydes, leading to a high incidence of aldehyde-related DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs) and genetic instability. Our bioinformatics analyses revealed a surprising trend in most cancers, particularly lung and liver cancers: a reduction in ALDH2 expression coupled with an overexpression of XRCC1. This finding aligns with accumulating evidence linking a high occurrence of DPCs to genetic instability in cancer. Notably, DPC accumulation in the liver is especially oncogenic, as evidenced by the development of aggressive hepatocellular carcinomas in patients lacking the DNA-protein crosslink repair metalloprotease SPRTN/DVC1. Our findings demonstrate that inhibiting ALDH2 increases DPC occurrence, and low ALDH2 expression is associated with a poorer prognosis in liver cancer patients. Despite the presence of various ALDH isoforms, only ALDH2 expression significantly impacted liver cancer prognosis, suggesting its paramount role in aldehyde detoxification.
Several hypotheses, which are not mutually exclusive, can be proposed regarding the role of Base Excision Repair (BER) in managing aldehyde-related DNA damage. One possibility is that BER directly participates in repairing damage caused by aldehydes. Aldehydes can modify DNA bases, and BER is known to fix such alterations. Furthermore, research suggests that BER might also be involved in repairing DNA inter-strand crosslinks, another type of damage that aldehydes can induce. While direct evidence linking BER to the repair of DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs) is limited, it is noteworthy that XRCC1, a key BER protein, along with TDP1, plays a role in processing lesions caused by trapped Topoisomerase I (TOP1). If the mechanism for removing TOP1-induced lesions shares similarities with DPC repair, BER could function downstream of SPRTN/DVC1 after the removal of the crosslinked protein. Collectively, these findings suggest that the BER pathway could be implicated in repairing aldehyde-induced DPCs, which might explain why cancer cells with low ALDH2 expression exhibit increased BER activity. An alternative perspective suggests that a deficiency in BER could actually lead to aldehyde-related toxicity. Our previous work demonstrated that the loss of BER triggers metabolic shifts, including an enhanced activity of the one-carbon cycle. Recent studies have highlighted the significant role of endogenously produced formaldehyde in this cycle. Consequently, if BER-deficient cells upregulate the one-carbon cycle, it is plausible that this would lead to an increased concentration of formaldehyde within the cell, acting as both a metabolic byproduct and an intermediate. Interestingly, we previously observed that BER-deficient cells display elevated levels of the antioxidant glutathione (GSH). It is established that GSH can serve as an alternative pathway for aldehyde detoxification. Therefore, we propose that BER-deficient cells increase ALDH2 expression to counteract aldehyde-induced DNA damage. This implies that cancer cells with low ALDH2 expression face a heightened risk of aldehyde overload if their BER pathway is not sufficiently robust. A third hypothesis posits that BER deficiency, by generating a substantial number of single-strand breaks (SSBs), some of which may have aldehyde adducts at their termini, could readily lead to DPC formation. This notion is supported by the observation that both XRCC1 and TDP1 depletion, the models employed in this study, result in a significant accumulation of SSBs. Regardless of the precise role of BER, and acknowledging that any or all of these hypotheses might hold true, we present a proof-of-principle finding: cells may not survive the simultaneous loss of both BER and ALDH2 activities. By inhibiting Sp1, a primary regulator of BER protein expression, we observed that cells with low ALDH2 expression exhibit increased sensitivity to BER loss. While Mithramycin A, a Sp1 inhibitor, has significant in vivo toxicity and is not directly applicable for patient treatment, the development of less toxic Mithramycin A derivatives or the exploration of other BER inhibitors could offer novel strategies for targeting specific metabolic vulnerabilities exploited by certain cancer cells.
A novel link between the Base Excision Repair (BER) pathway and aldehyde detoxification has been identified. This connection holds significant prognostic implications for liver and lung cancer patients and suggests new avenues for targeting cancer metabolism.
