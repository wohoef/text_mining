This study aimed to investigate the concept of "field carcinogenesis" in oral cancer by comparing the genomic aberration profiles of visually normal oral fields (ODFs) and oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs). The researchers sought to provide evidence for this model by analyzing these profiles using high-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) and array-Comparative Genomic Hybridization (a-CGH). A secondary objective was to determine if specific CGH aberrations were linked to DNA aneuploidy. Nineteen patients with single OPMLs were included. Samples were collected from both the OPMLs, which comprised 11 non-dysplastic leukoplakias (nd-OPMLs) and 8 dysplastic leukoplakias (d-OPMLs), and from distant ODFs. DNA aneuploid nuclei, identified by hr DNA-FCM, were isolated using a DNA-FCM-Sorter. These purified cell populations were then subjected to DNA extraction and a-CGH for a comprehensive analysis of DNA copy number aberrations (CNAs). The findings revealed a significant difference in DNA aneuploidy frequencies, with ODFs showing 5.3% and OPMLs exhibiting 32%. While aneuploid values in ODFs and nd-OPMLs were near-diploid, 40% of d-OPMLs displayed high-aneuploid values. The average number of CNAs was considerably lower in ODFs (1.9) compared to OPMLs (6.5). A common CNA, the gain of the 20q13.33-qter region, was observed in 37% of both ODFs and their corresponding OPMLs. Other shared regions with gains included 7p22.2-pter, 11p15.5-pter, and 16p13.3-pter. Notably, gains in 20q13.31–q13.33 and 5p13.33-pter, along with a loss in 9p21.3, were frequently found exclusively in d-OPMLs. Crucially, the loss at 9p21.3, gain at 5p13.33-pter, and gain of 20q13.31–q13.33 were significantly associated with DNA aneuploidy. In conclusion, the study suggests that common CNAs in specific chromosomal regions between ODFs and OPMLs indicate early events in oral carcinogenesis, supporting the field effect model and potentially contributing to the transition from ODFs to OPMLs. Furthermore, specific aberrations, including loss at 9p21.3 and gains at 5p13.33-pter and 20q13.31–q13.33, appear to play a role in the development of DNA aneuploidy.

Cancer development and progression are widely understood to involve the accumulation of genetic and genomic alterations. These aberrations disrupt the normal functions of genes responsible for critical cellular processes such as cell cycle control, programmed cell death, chromosomal stability, blood vessel formation, and the ability to invade and spread. Epidemiological and experimental findings strongly suggest that carcinogen exposure, particularly from tobacco smoking, plays a significant role in the origin and advancement of most oral cancers. Research using advanced sequencing technologies has identified a substantial number of somatic mutations, including those within coding regions of genes. The numerous chemicals present in tobacco smoke are known to bind to and alter DNA in oral mucosa cells, potentially leading to widespread "field cancerization." This phenomenon involves the creation of an extensive area of genetically altered cells throughout the oral cavity. Furthermore, specific gene mutations may impair DNA repair mechanisms, increasing DNA damage and contributing to chromosomal instability and aneuploidy, thereby elevating the risk of oral cancer development. The theory of field cancerization, supported by recent genetic insights, has led to a compelling genetic progression model for oral cancer. A key stage in this model is the transformation of a localized area of stem cells with shared genetic aberrations into a broader field characterized by numerous alterations, which can manifest as visible lesions like leukoplakias. Consequently, numerous studies have investigated various types of DNA aberrations in oral cancer tissues and adjacent normal-appearing tissues, employing techniques such as loss of heterozygosity analysis, in situ hybridization, immunohistochemistry, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, and DNA image and flow cytometry.
Oral cancer is associated with chromosomal aberrations in specific loci such as 1p36.3, 3q26, 7p, 8q, 9p, 11q13, 16p, 16q, 17q, and 20q. Our previous work using high-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) suggested a model of DNA aneuploidization in oral fields of visually normal mucosa (ODFs), oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs), and oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCCs). This study focuses on investigating ODFs located distant from non-dysplastic (nd-OPMLs) and dysplastic (d-OPMLs) lesions.
Our study employed high-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) to identify DNA aneuploid sublines, which are characterized by a DNA Index (DI) difference of 2-4% compared to diploid control cells. Following detection, a DNA-FCM-Sorter physically isolated these aneuploid epithelial sublines. The extracted DNA was then subjected to genome-wide array-Comparative Genomic Hybridization (a-CGH) to analyze DNA copy number aberrations (CNAs). This integrated approach aimed to provide evidence for the transition of visually normal oral fields (ODFs) into visible leukoplakias (nd-OPMLs and d-OPMLs), representing an expanding field of genetic alteration. Specifically, we sought to determine if ODFs and OPMLs shared common chromosomal changes and to correlate chromosomal aberrations with DNA aneuploidy, thereby identifying genomic regions involved in aneuploid subline development.
Patients with oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs) were recruited from two medical centers: the University of Turin and the "S. Martino Hospital" in Genoa. All participants provided written informed consent, which was approved by the respective Institutional Ethics Committees. During interviews, their tobacco and alcohol consumption habits were documented.
This study enrolled nineteen patients, comprising nine males and ten females, with a mean age of 57.9 years (ranging from 32 to 76 years), who presented with either non-dysplastic (nd-OPMLs) or dysplastic (d-OPMLs) oral potentially malignant lesions. All participants provided informed consent. Their tobacco consumption habits were also documented, revealing that eight patients were non-smokers (NS) and eleven were smokers (S). Tissue samples were collected from both the OPMLs and from distant, corresponding orally visually normal fields (ODFs) in eighteen of the nineteen patients. An exception occurred with patient 16, whose ODF sample was obtained from the buccal mucosa, while the OPML was located on the tongue. Tissue collection was performed using incisional biopsy and/or micro-biopsy techniques, as previously described [29]. Care was taken to induce slight bleeding, ensuring the collection of basal epithelial layers. A portion of each sample was preserved in formalin for histological assessment of dysplasia using routine hematoxylin and eosin staining. The remaining multiple samples were immediately snap-frozen and stored at -20°C for subsequent analysis by high-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) and array-Comparative Genomic Hybridization (a-CGH). The OPMLs, all diagnosed as leukoplakias, were classified according to internationally recognized criteria with diagnostic certainty levels of C3-C4 [30]. Histological evaluation for dysplasia was conducted by a pathologist trained in WHO guidelines [31]. In eighteen of the nineteen cases, the ODFs and OPMLs were located in the same anatomical subsites, with the buccal mucosa being the most frequent site. The distance between ODFs and their corresponding OPMLs varied. In instances where OPMLs were situated on a cheek, ODF samples were taken from the contralateral cheek, representing an optimal "contralateral" or "mirror" positioning. However, when lesions were located on the anterior floor of the mouth, ODFs might have been in close proximity to the lesion. Consequently, OPMLs situated near the midline were excluded from this study. A detailed breakdown of the anatomical subsites for the ODFs and OPMLs is provided in Table 1.
Nuclei suspensions, stained with DAPI, were prepared from multiple tissue samples using a modified Otto et al. method [32]. High-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) was then performed using a CyflowML cytometer (Partec GmbH, Muenster, Germany). This process generated DNA content histograms and allowed for the evaluation of the DNA Index (DI) [33]. Gender-specific human lymphocytes served as DNA diploid controls, exhibiting a mean CV of 1.2 ± 0.2% for their G0-G1 peaks. The technique could detect DNA near-diploid aneuploid sublines, characterized by a DI difference of 2-4% compared to diploid controls [20]. Subsequently, DNA aneuploid sublines (DI ≠ 1) were isolated with approximately 99% purity using a Cyflow Space FCM with a PPCS unit (Partec GmbH).
DNA was extracted using an ArchivePure DNA kit with modifications. Following protein precipitation, DNA was purified via phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation, using glycogen as a carrier. DNA quality was then evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer.
To conduct the array-Comparative Genomic Hybridization (a-CGH), an oligonucleotide Human Genome CGH 105 K array platform from Agilent Technologies was utilized. Pooled normal male or female DNA from Promega served as the reference DNA. Genomic DNA was amplified using either the GenomePlex Whole Genome Amplification Kit WGA2 (Sigma-Aldrich) for samples with at least 50 ng of DNA, or the Ovation WGA System (NuGEN) for samples with less than 50 ng. Following amplification, 2 μg each of test and reference DNA were labeled using the Bioprime Labelling Kit (Invitrogen). Hybridization and washing of the slides adhered to the manufacturer's instructions. Spot fluorescence was captured by an Agilent G2565BA Scanner, and images were processed with Feature Extraction software (version 9.5.3.1) from Agilent Technologies. Analysis of the data files was performed using Agilent CGH Analytics Software (version 3.5.14) with the ADM-2 algorithm. Experimental quality was verified using a QC metric tool. Oligonucleotides were mapped to the human genome build NCBI35/Hg17 (May 2004). The raw data has been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus database (accession number: GSE28906). Commercially available pooled normal DNA from healthy donors was used. Copy number variations (CNVs) identified as large segment copy number polymorphisms in the Genomic Variants Database were excluded from the analysis.
To ensure accuracy, various tests were conducted to rule out any influence of WGA, DAPI staining, or DNA-FCM-Sorting on the a-CGH results.
Data were managed and analyzed using Microsoft Office Excel and SPSS 16.0. Associations between variables in contingency tables were assessed using the Fisher exact or chi-squared tests. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

The OPML group comprised 11 non-dysplastic leukoplakias (nd-OPMLs) and 8 dysplastic leukoplakias (d-OPMLs); no dysplasia was found in the 19 ODFs. DNA aneuploidy occurred in 5.3% of ODFs and 32% of OPMLs. While aneuploid DI values in ODFs and nd-OPMLs were near-diploid (DI ≤ 1.4), 40% of d-OPMLs exhibited high aneuploidy (DI > 1.4).
Copy number aberrations (CNAs) were detected in 58% of OPMLs (11 out of 19) and 37% of ODFs (7 out of 19). Conversely, no aberrations were found in 63% of ODFs (12 out of 19) and 42% of OPMLs (8 out of 19). Notably, 31.6% of ODFs and their corresponding lesions exhibited a consistent lack of aberrations. In 29% of cases (5 out of 19), ODFs and their matched OPMLs shared specific CNAs. However, 11% of ODFs (2 out of 19) displayed unique CNAs not present in their corresponding non-dysplastic OPMLs. The incidence of DNA aneuploidy rose from 5.3% in ODFs to 32% in OPMLs, correlating with an increase in the average number of CNAs from 1.9 to 6.47. Furthermore, the presence of dysplasia in OPMLs was linked to a higher average number of CNAs.
Across both ODFs and OPMLs, gains in copy number aberrations (CNAs) were observed more frequently than losses. Notably, CNA losses occurred in 32% of OPMLs but were absent in ODFs (p = 0.02). Furthermore, the presence of DNA aneuploidy in all samples correlated with a greater number of CNAs, encompassing both gains and losses. Segmental chromosomal aberrations were found in both DNA diploid and aneuploid sublines. However, numerical chromosomal aberrations, such as the gain or loss of entire chromosomes, were exclusively detected in the presence of DNA aneuploidy.
Table 4 details the copy number aberration (CNA) gains in six minimal common regions, including their segment sizes and gene counts. The most prevalent common aberrant region was the gain of 20q13.33-qter, found in 7 out of 19 cases (37%) in both ODFs and OPMLs. This specific region was more frequently observed in dysplastic OPMLs (d-OPMLs) at 62.5% compared to non-dysplastic OPMLs (nd-OPMLs) at 18%. Additionally, the gain of 7p22.2-pter occurred slightly more often in OPMLs (32%) than in ODFs (21%), and the gain of 8q24.3-qter showed a similar trend, appearing in 37% of OPMLs versus 16% of ODFs. A combination of three CNA gains—7p22.2-pter, 8q24.2-qter, and 20q13.33-qter—was identified simultaneously in 50% of d-OPMLs, compared to 18% of nd-OPMLs and 16% of ODFs.
Among the 19 oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs) studied, 8 exhibited dysplasia (d-OPMLs). These d-OPMLs were distinguished by unique copy number aberrations (CNAs) that were infrequent or absent in other samples. Table 5 details these specific aberrations, including gains in regions 5p13.33-pter, 16q24.2-qter, and 20q13.31–q13.33, as well as a loss in 9p21.3. The frequencies of these CNAs within the d-OPML group were 50%, 37.5%, 62.5%, and 50%, respectively.
In this study, ten distinct aberrant chromosomal regions were identified across 19 ODFs and 19 OPMLs. Table 6 details the association between these copy number aberrations (CNAs), comprising nine gains and one loss, and DNA ploidy status in a cohort of 31 diploid and 7 aneuploid samples. Statistically significant associations with DNA aneuploidy were observed for chromosomal regions 5p13.33-pter (p = 0.0005), 20q13.31–q13.33 (p = 0.01), and 9p21.3 (p = 0.00004). Notably, the gain in the 20q region, extending to 20qter and present in 37% of ODFs/OPMLs, showed only a weak association with DNA aneuploidy (p = 0.077).
The concept of field cancerization in oral cancer, widely discussed in the literature, posits that premalignant changes can arise anywhere in the oral mucosa exposed to carcinogens. Dakubo et al. define field cancerization as a transformation process where genetically altered cells, appearing histologically normal, precede or coexist with malignant cells, regardless of clonality. This theory highlights that such changes can occur throughout the oral mucosa.
Tobacco smoking introduces over 60 chemicals that can mutate numerous genes, fostering widespread oral field cancerization. This concept underpins an appealing genetic progression model for oral cancer. A key transition within this model involves the evolution from an early precursor to an expanded field exhibiting multiple aberrations, which can manifest as leukoplakias or oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs).
Recent advancements in Comparative Genomic Hybridization (CGH), including array-CGH, have enabled genome-wide analysis of DNA copy number aberrations (CNAs). These studies suggest that CNA gains and losses can lead to gene hyper- or hypo-expression. Research primarily focusing on oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCCs) and, less frequently, oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs), has identified numerous aberrant chromosomal loci. These include regions such as 1p36.3, 3q26, 5p, 7p, 8q, 9p, 11q13, 16p, 16q, 17q, and 20q, significantly enhancing our understanding of oral cancer development and its clinical implications.
Our study revealed that many aberrant chromosomal regions were present in both oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs) and their corresponding visually normal oral fields (ODFs). Notably, specific copy number aberrations (CNAs), such as loss at 9p21.3 and gain at 20q13.31–q13.33, occurred at high frequencies (40-60%) in dysplastic OPMLs but were rare or absent in other samples.
High-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) offers significant advantages. It enables the identification and physical separation of DNA aneuploid sublines from diploid components using DNA-FCM-Sorting. This yields a purer population of aneuploid nuclei for a-CGH analysis. Consequently, this combined approach allows for a comparison of DNA aberrations detected by both hr DNA-FCM and a-CGH, potentially pinpointing genomic regions involved in the development of DNA aneuploid sublines.
Our analysis identified ten distinct aberrant chromosomal regions present in both oral fields of visually normal mucosa (ODFs) and oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs). Among these, a loss at 9p21.3, along with gains at 5p13.33-pter and 20q13.31–q13.33, showed a significant association with DNA aneuploidy (p = 0.00004; p = 0.0005; p = 0.01, respectively). Despite the relatively small sample size (n=38), these findings are novel and suggest that candidate genes within these chromosomal regions may contribute to chromosomal instability and aneuploidy. The chromosomal loss at 9p21.3 is particularly noteworthy. This region is small, spanning only 2.16 Mb and containing just nine genes. Its significance is further underscored by the fact that 50% of dysplastic OPMLs (d-OPMLs) exhibited a loss in this area, and it demonstrated a strong correlation with DNA aneuploidy. Identifying the specific gene(s) responsible for this profound change, such as the tumor suppressor gene CDKN2A (also known as TP16), which is frequently inactivated in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) carcinogenesis, requires further specialized investigation. Similarly, the chromosomal region 5p15.33-pter, with a segment size of 1.9 Mb, also showed an association with DNA aneuploidy. Within this region, the hTERT gene (Telomerase reverse transcriptase) is relevant, as it has been linked to telomerase-mediated chromosomal instability and imbalances in cell growth and apoptosis.
The RTEL1 gene, found in the 20q13.33-qter region common to ODFs and OPMLs, is crucial for telomere maintenance, chromosomal stability, and DNA repair. It also negatively regulates apoptosis and has been amplified in various human cancers.
Identifying genes that drive chromosomal instability and aneuploidization during the early stages of oral carcinogenesis, as seen in ODFs and OPMLs, is a crucial goal. Our a-CGH data prompted us to investigate whether ODFs and OPMLs share common chromosomal aberrations. We observed that certain copy number aberrations (CNAs) in specific chromosomal regions of ODFs (including 1p36.32-pter, 7p22.2-pter, 8q24.2-qter, 11p15.5-pter, 16p13.3-pter, and 20q13.33-qter) were also present, often with increasing frequency, in OPMLs. This suggests these aberrations may contribute to the ODF-OPML transition and indicate a clonal relationship, with ODFs potentially serving as early precursors to OPMLs within the same patient. However, the presence of CGH aberrations in two ODFs alongside the absence of aberrations in their corresponding nd-OPMLs suggests that the origins of ODFs or nd-OPMLs might be polyclonal. It is important to acknowledge that these aberrant chromosomal regions are extensive and likely encompass numerous genes beyond those directly involved in the ODF-OPML transition.
Regarding the high-resolution DNA flow cytometry (hr DNA-FCM) findings, only one of the 19 oral fields of visually normal mucosa (ODFs) examined exhibited a DNA Index (DI) value other than 1, indicating the presence of a DNA aneuploid subpopulation (5.26%). In contrast, DNA aneuploid subpopulations were identified in 6 out of 19 oral potentially malignant lesions (OPMLs), representing 32% of these samples. This suggests that DNA aneuploidization is a relatively common early event in OPMLs, and occasionally, such early aberrations can also be found in ODFs within the same anatomical location. However, no common DNA aneuploid subline, characterized by the same DI value, was detected in both ODFs and their corresponding OPMLs across any of the cases. Further studies with a larger sample size are necessary to definitively address this observation. These findings align with previous research using DNA-FCM alone, which reported DNA aneuploid ODFs and OPMLs at rates of 8.5% and 28%, respectively.
Pure DNA diploid samples lacked a DNA aneuploid component, preventing epithelial cell enrichment. Consequently, diploid stromal cells coexisted with epithelial cells. Insufficient material also precluded obtaining and sorting epithelial-specific antibody-marked DNA diploid cell suspensions for subsequent nuclei suspension and DNA extraction. The epithelial cell presence (at least 50%) in DNA diploid samples was confirmed morphologically from parallel tissue samples.
Specific aberrant chromosomal regions identified by high-resolution a-CGH in visually and histologically normal oral fields, as well as in potentially malignant lesions, suggest early events in oral cancer development. These findings are linked to the early establishment of field cancerization and the emergence of DNA aneuploidy. Future research is needed to pinpoint specific genes within these common copy number aberration regions that may drive the transition from normal fields to potentially malignant lesions and contribute to DNA aneuploidization. Detecting these genomic aberrations, including DNA aneuploidy which is amenable to routine analysis, could aid in identifying patients with potentially malignant lesions at higher risk for developing oral squamous cell carcinoma. This may also inform alternative management strategies for these individuals. However, validating these hypotheses will necessitate long-term prospective studies with sufficient patient numbers.
