This study investigated the relationship between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cancer mortality in a substantial group of hypertensive individuals. While both CKD and cancer are prevalent in older populations, the extent to which CKD contributes to cancer risk has been uncertain. The research utilized post-hoc analyses of data from the Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial (ALLHAT), encompassing both in-trial and post-trial observations. The study population consisted of participants aged 55 years or older, all of whom had hypertension and at least one additional risk factor for coronary heart disease. The primary predictor examined was baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Cancer mortality, identified through national databases reporting cancer-related deaths during and after the trial, served as the outcome measure. Hazard ratios (HRs) were computed using Cox proportional hazard models, adjusted for potential confounding variables and stratified by baseline GFR. The average age of participants was 66.9 years. Over a mean follow-up period of 8.9 years, a total of 2,338 cancer-related deaths were recorded. A key finding was that participants with an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 exhibited an elevated risk of cancer mortality compared to those with an eGFR of 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 or higher (adjusted HR 1.54; 95% CI 1.22 – 1.94; p-value for trend 0.004). This association remained consistent across various subgroups, including those defined by race, gender, and diabetes status. Specifically, individuals with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 faced a greater risk of mortality from colon cancer (p-value for trend 0.048, HR 2.28 (1.12 – 4.62)) and urinary tract cancer (p-value for trend 0.001, adjusted HR 2.95 (1.14 – 7.65)). A limitation of this study is its post-hoc nature, as it involved re-analyzing existing clinical trial data. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that in this large cohort of hypertensive patients, an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 is linked to an increased risk of cancer-related mortality.

The clinical trial was registered on October 27, 1999 (NCT 00000542).
Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects over 26 million Americans, with a disproportionately high prevalence among the elderly; almost one-third of individuals over 70 years old have CKD. While prior studies primarily examined CKD's influence on end-stage kidney disease (ESRD) progression and cardiovascular health, recent evidence indicates its broader impact on areas like frailty, cognitive function, and quality of life. Cancer, a significant concern in the aging demographic, is also prevalent in older patients, and both conditions contribute substantially to morbidity and healthcare expenditures. Epidemiological data suggest an elevated cancer risk in ESRD patients and kidney transplant recipients. Investigating whether CKD itself increases cancer risk is crucial, given the profound clinical, research, and public health implications of such a link.
The link between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cancer is gaining attention, particularly as recent research highlights cancer as a significant cause of death in CKD patients. While the occurrence of kidney disease in cancer patients, often due to nephrotoxic chemotherapy, is well-documented, the precise role of CKD in cancer development remains debated, with existing data presenting inconsistencies. Some studies have indicated a correlation between elevated urinary albumin-creatinine ratios and increased cancer incidence, though this finding is not universal. Further epidemiological investigations are crucial to clarify this relationship and pave the way for focused pathophysiological studies to uncover the underlying mechanisms.
This paper aimed to investigate the link between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cancer-related mortality. It utilized data from the Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial (ALLHAT), a study that randomized 42,418 hypertensive patients and collected extensive follow-up information. This large cohort offered a valuable chance to explore the association between CKD status and cancer deaths.
The ALLHAT trial's methodology and key findings have been documented elsewhere [20, 21, 22]. This study aimed to assess whether certain antihypertensive treatments could reduce the incidence of fatal coronary heart disease or nonfatal myocardial infarction in high-risk hypertensive patients aged 55 or older. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a thiazide-type diuretic (chlorthalidone), an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (lisinopril), a calcium channel blocker (amlodipine), or an alpha-blocker (doxazosin). Recruitment spanned from February 1994 to January 1998 across 623 locations in the USA, Canada, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands. The trial, registered as NCT 00000542 on ClinicalTrials.gov in October 1999, adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, with all sites securing institutional review board approval and all participants providing informed consent.
Participants with symptomatic heart failure, a left ventricular ejection fraction under 35%, or a serum creatinine level exceeding 2.0 mg/dL were excluded from the study. Baseline clinical data were gathered through interviews, questionnaires, and laboratory tests, all conducted at a single central laboratory using standardized methods. Serum creatinine measurements, crucial for estimating kidney function, were performed with the VITROS chemistry system, demonstrating a coefficient of variation of approximately 2%. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation, and participants were categorized into CKD stages 1, 2, 3A, and 3B based on these eGFR values. The active treatment phase of the trial involved a mean follow-up of 4.9 years. The doxazosin treatment arm was discontinued prematurely because it showed no benefit for the primary endpoint (coronary heart disease) and was associated with a higher incidence of cardiovascular disease, particularly heart failure, compared to the chlorthalidone arm. Consequently, participants in the doxazosin arm were not included in the present analyses, aligning with previous studies on ALLHAT’s long-term outcomes. After the remaining trial arms concluded in March 2002, an additional 4 years of extended follow-up were conducted using national databases like the Social Security Administration and National Death Index for passive surveillance. This resulted in an overall mean follow-up period of 8.9 years.
The study's primary outcome was cancer-related mortality, tracked from participant randomization in ALLHAT. While investigators determined cause of death during the trial, the National Death Index (NDI) was utilized for all mortality data throughout the entire study period. Death certificates confirmed patient identification for NDI-discovered deaths. Causes of death were coded using ICD-10 via NDIPlus. Further details on ALLHAT mortality are available elsewhere.
From the initial 42,418 ALLHAT participants, 10,522 were excluded due to doxazosin assignment or missing baseline serum creatinine. Canadian participants were also excluded because long-term mortality data was unavailable, as were those censored by transplant. Ultimately, cancer mortality data from 31,349 patients were included in the analysis, with all remaining randomized groups combined.
An additional analytical approach involved examining a combined endpoint of cancer events during the ALLHAT study's active treatment phase, encompassing both physician-reported cancer diagnoses and cancer-related deaths. Nonfatal cancer incidence was determined through documented clinician reports, while cancer mortality was identified via physician reports or the National Death Index, as previously detailed. For fatal organ-specific cancers reported by investigators, which included categorical options supported by pathology reports or death certificates, these were cross-referenced with International Classification of Disease-9 modification (ICD-9) codes to integrate with post-trial data. In instances where a clinic investigator did not identify cancer mortality, the National Death Index (NDIPlus) database served as the source. Deaths occurring from 1999 onwards, initially recorded with ICD-10 codes, were converted to ICD-9 for consistency. The specific ICD-9 codes used for NDIPlus-identified organ-specific cancer mortality were: prostate (185), lung (162), colon (153–154.1), breast (in women, 174), urinary tract (188–189), hematopoietic (200–208), and "other" (141–199, 235–239). Further information regarding the post-trial follow-up objectives, procedures, and key findings through 2006 has been published. Deaths occurring after the trial concluded were identified using administrative databases. Cancer classifications, based on physician reports or NDIPlus diagnoses for participants lost to follow-up, categorized primary cancer sites, including prostate (men), lung, colon, breast (women), urinary tract (kidney, ureter, ureterovesical junction, bladder), and a broad "Other" category encompassing numerous less common or unspecified primary diagnoses.
The methodology employed for data analysis involved several statistical approaches. To begin, baseline characteristics were compared across different eGFR levels. For continuous variables, a one-way analysis of variance was utilized, while categorical data were assessed using contingency table analysis. The study's primary objective was to evaluate the relationship between kidney function and cancer mortality. This was achieved through the application of Cox proportional hazards regression models, which also incorporated trend tests. These models considered each participant's complete trial experience, extending through the post-trial follow-up phase. Participants who experienced death during the follow-up period were censored at their time of death. To quantify this relationship, hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from both univariate and multivariable-adjusted Cox models. The multivariable adjustments accounted for a comprehensive set of baseline factors, including age, race, ethnicity, smoking status, diabetes, history of coronary heart disease, body mass index, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, glucose, estrogen therapy, aspirin use, and the assigned antihypertensive treatment group. HRs for overall cancer mortality, stratified by eGFR levels, were calculated using both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted models. Estimated GFR was treated as both a continuous variable and a categorical variable, with participants grouped into specific eGFR ranges: ≥ 90, 60 – 89.9, 45 – 59.9, and < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. A cutoff point of < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 was selected due to the limited sample size within the GFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum. Variables demonstrating a statistically significant association with cancer (p < 0.05) in the unadjusted analyses were subsequently included in the multivariable-adjusted models. Notably, smoking status was retained in all models, given its established role as an independent risk factor for cancer. To explore potential effect modification, two-way interaction terms were incorporated between the predictor variables (eGFR) and all other covariates. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to examine differences across various demographics and health conditions, specifically: men versus women, participants aged < 65 versus ≥ 65 years, Black versus non-Black participants, diabetic versus nondiabetic participants, and individuals with or without baseline coronary heart disease. Furthermore, adjusted spline models were developed to investigate nonlinear relationships between CKD and cancer, comparing each decile of GFR against the highest decile. The survival analysis follow-up period spanned from the time of study randomization (1994 – 1998) until cancer mortality, encompassing both the randomized trial (mean 4.9 years) and the subsequent 4-year extension period. A p-value of < 0.05 was designated as the threshold for statistical significance. However, given the extensive number of proportional hazard, subgroup, and interaction analyses performed, the interpretation of statistical significance at this level was approached with caution.
During the clinical trial, we also analyzed physician-reported new cancer diagnoses or cancer-related deaths as an outcome, using similar analytical methods.
Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of ALLHAT participants, categorized by eGFR. Significant variations were observed across eGFR groups: individuals with reduced eGFR tended to be older, female, and have pre-existing coronary heart disease, while being less likely to be Black. However, the allocation to antihypertensive therapy arms showed no statistically significant differences among these eGFR-defined groups.
A total of 2,338 participants experienced cancer-related mortality during the combined trial and post-trial follow-up, which extended until December 31, 2006. As detailed in Supplemental Table 1, cancer mortality rates varied by eGFR, with lower GFR categories exhibiting higher rates. Specifically, the 10-year cancer mortality rates per 100 individuals were 7.90 for eGFR ≥ 90, 7.71 for 60 – 89.9, 10.11 for 45 – 59.9, and 13.19 for < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table 2). A clear trend indicated that the risk of cancer mortality increased progressively with decreasing GFR strata (p for trend < 0.004), as illustrated in Table 3 and Figure 2. The highest adjusted risk for cancer mortality was observed in participants with an eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, showing an adjusted HR of 1.54 (95% CI 1.22 – 1.94) when compared to those with eGFR ≥ 90. This elevated risk was also consistently noted for specific cancer types: colon cancer (adjusted HR 2.28 (95% CI 1.12 – 4.62)), urinary-tract cancer (adjusted HR 2.95 (95% CI 1.14 – 7.65)), and "Other" cancers (adjusted HR 1.70 (95% CI 1.17 – 2.46)).
The relationship between eGFR and cancer mortality was not straightforward. While a continuous eGFR variable, when adjusted, showed no independent link to overall cancer mortality (HR 1.00 (95% CI 0.98 – 1.03)) or specific cancer types (Supplemental Table 2), this finding held true across various subgroups including age, gender, diabetes, race, and CHD (Supplemental Table 3). However, a more complex, U-shaped pattern emerged in the adjusted spline model, indicating that both extremely high and extremely low eGFR values were associated with an elevated risk of cancer mortality (Figure 3). Furthermore, when competing risks were factored into regression models, the increased risk of cancer mortality observed in the GFR < 45 group, compared to the GFR > 90 group, was reduced (1.21 (0.96 – 1.54) (Supplemental Table 4)).
During the clinical trial phase, 2,529 participants experienced either a physician-diagnosed new cancer or cancer-related death. The incidence of these events showed a clear pattern: individuals with lower eGFR levels exhibited higher rates, with a progressive increase observed across decreasing eGFR strata (as detailed in Supplemental Table 5 and Supplemental Figure 1). Specifically, the risk of incident cancer or mortality during the trial period was elevated in patients within lower eGFR categories (Supplemental Table 6). After accounting for potential confounding factors, participants with an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 faced a 43% greater risk of incident cancer or mortality compared to those with an eGFR of 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 or higher (Hazard Ratio 1.43, 95% Confidence Interval 1.13 – 1.79). When eGFR was analyzed as a continuous variable in the adjusted model, a 2% increase in the risk of incident cancer or cancer mortality was noted for every 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 reduction in eGFR (Supplemental Table 7). This relationship remained consistent across subgroups defined by gender, diabetes, race, and coronary heart disease. However, a significant interaction with age was observed (p = 0.03); for participants aged 65 years or older, each 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 decrease in eGFR corresponded to a 4% higher risk of incident cancer or cancer mortality, a finding not present in younger individuals.
Our study of a large hypertensive cohort revealed a significantly elevated risk of cancer mortality for individuals with an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, compared to those with an eGFR above 90 mL/min/1.73 m2. This heightened risk was specifically observed for colon, urinary tract, and "other" cancers, but not for lung, breast, or hematopoietic malignancies. These consistent findings were evident across various subgroups, including age, race, gender, diabetes status, and presence of coronary heart disease.
The relationship between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cancer has garnered significant attention recently, particularly as cancer is a primary cause of death among individuals with CKD. However, prior research in this area has often been limited by small sample sizes and specific populations, leading to inconsistent findings. For instance, an Australian prospective study identified an elevated cancer risk only in men with at least stage 3 CKD (eGFR < 55 mL/min/1.73 m2), but not in women. Similarly, a Swedish cohort, despite its limited number of CKD patients, indicated a link between kidney function and cancer, exclusively in young men. Conversely, some studies involving diabetic CKD patients found no such association. More recent investigations have offered varied insights. One study reported a higher cancer mortality risk (HR 1.91 (95% CI 1.32 – 2.76)) for individuals with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 compared to those with eGFR > 75 mL/min/1.73 m2. A study conducted within the Kaiser healthcare system in Northern California, a large and representative population, observed a greater incidence of renal-cell cancer in patients with eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, relative to those with normal kidney function. However, this Kaiser study did not establish an overall association between CKD and all cancers. Our current study contributes significantly to this evolving field by demonstrating an independent link between CKD stages and cancer mortality within a large, multiethnic cohort. Our results indicate that an eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 is associated with an elevated risk of cancer-related mortality when compared to an eGFR of 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 or higher. Unlike many previous studies that focused on broad eGFR categories or specific malignancies, our research, leveraging the ALLHAT population's substantial size and diverse eGFR distribution, allowed for a more precise determination of the eGFR level associated with increased cancer risk. Furthermore, our analysis of site-specific cancers advances understanding by documenting an increased risk of gastrointestinal and urinary-tract cancers in CKD patients, consistent with prior reports. Notably, we found no association between eGFR and lung or breast cancers, which are prevalent in the United States.
The ALLHAT cohort's substantial size facilitated significant subgroup analyses. Unlike other recent large cohort studies predominantly featuring Caucasian participants, the ALLHAT sample was notably diverse, including 34% Black and 19% Hispanic individuals. A key discovery was the consistent association between CKD and cancer, irrespective of race/ethnicity, gender, or diabetes. The observation that the link between eGFR and cancer was more pronounced in older patients warrants further investigation. While dialysis or transplantation might have impacted cancer risk, very few participants progressed to end-stage renal disease.
Coexisting risk factors might partly account for the observed link between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cancer in this study. Baseline characteristics varied among ALLHAT participants based on their estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). For instance, individuals with lower eGFR were, on average, older (71 years) than those with preserved eGFR (63 years). Despite adjusting for age and other relevant risk factors, the connection between CKD and cancer remained statistically significant, though somewhat weakened. The relationship between eGFR and cancer mortality might not be straightforwardly linear, as indicated by spline analyses. A potential explanation for this non-linearity is that chronically ill or frail patients, even those with seemingly higher eGFR due to reduced muscle mass, could face an elevated cancer risk. Furthermore, the diminished risk observed in competing risk models highlights the intricate interplay of various clinical outcomes in older CKD patients. Nevertheless, traditional regression models are generally considered more appropriate for characterizing etiologic associations. Consistent with cancer mortality data, physician-diagnosed cancer or cancer mortality during the trial also showed a trend of higher risk with lower GFR, though this trend did not reach statistical significance.
The precise biological pathways connecting CKD and cancer remain unclear. However, potential contributing factors include impaired solute clearance by the kidneys, inflammatory processes, and alterations in the gut microbiome. These findings, combined with other recent research, highlight the need for further investigation into the mechanisms underlying this association.
The study's findings are broadly applicable due to the diverse participant population, which included significant numbers of older individuals, diabetics, and Black participants. A key strength is the extensive follow-up period within this large cohort. Furthermore, the consistent results from both physician reports and national databases bolster the conclusions regarding the link between CKD and new cancer cases.
Several limitations warrant consideration. The ALLHAT study was not originally designed to investigate the link between CKD and cancer, meaning these findings are retrospective and require further validation. A direct assessment of pre-existing cancer in participants at the study's outset was not feasible. Nevertheless, individuals with incurable malignancies expected to cause non-cardiovascular death were excluded, making it improbable that those with undiagnosed advanced cancers were included. The study population exclusively comprised hypertensive patients, so applying these results to non-hypertensive individuals should be done cautiously. Furthermore, the exclusion of participants with serum creatinine exceeding 2.0 mg/dL restricts the generalizability of the data to patients with more severe CKD. An observed demographic imbalance showed that participants with GFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 were disproportionately white and female. While adjustments were made for these differences, their potential influence on the association between low eGFR and cancer remains unclear, as does any connection to the eGFR estimation equation. The inherent limitations and potential bias of the MDRD equation, especially at higher GFR values, could also have affected the observed associations.
This study found that an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, indicative of stage 3B chronic kidney disease or worse, is independently linked to approximately a 50% increase in cancer-related mortality when compared to an eGFR of 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 or higher. This association remained consistent across various demographic and health subgroups, including age, race, gender, and diabetes. The substantial public health impact of both CKD and cancer underscores the importance of further research into the underlying biological mechanisms and potential risk-modifying strategies.

The ALLHAT study participants, whose baseline characteristics were analyzed for cancer mortality across both trial and post-trial phases, were categorized by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). A total of 31,349 individuals were included in this analysis. The largest group, comprising 17,778 participants, had an eGFR between 60 and 89.9 mL/min/1.73 m2, while 8,027 had an eGFR of ≥ 90. Smaller groups included 4,293 participants with eGFR between 45 and 59.9, and 1,251 with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. Significant differences (p < 0.001) were observed across these eGFR groups for several baseline characteristics. Participants with lower eGFR values tended to be older, with a mean age of 71.7 years in the < 45 group compared to 63.3 years in the ≥ 90 group. The proportion of women increased with decreasing eGFR, from 46.7% in the highest eGFR group to 55.9% in the lowest. Conversely, the percentage of Black participants was highest in the ≥ 90 eGFR group (50.4%) and lowest in the 45-59.9 group (25.8%), though it slightly increased to 32.2% in the < 45 group. Hispanic representation also varied, being highest at 22.0% in the ≥ 90 group and lowest at 15.5% in the 45-59.9 group. Other notable variations included education levels, with the lowest mean years of education (10.4) found in the < 45 eGFR group. The prevalence of diabetes was highest in the ≥ 90 group (51.2%) and lowest in the 45-59.9 group (37.4%). Both coronary heart disease (CHD) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) showed increasing prevalence with lower eGFR, reaching 30.0% and 60.2% respectively in the < 45 eGFR group. Smoking history was also significantly different across groups. Mean body mass index generally decreased with lower eGFR, while diastolic blood pressure also showed a decreasing trend. Total, LDL, and HDL cholesterol levels, as well as triglycerides and fasting glucose, also exhibited significant differences across the eGFR categories. No statistically significant differences were found in the distribution of participants across the various antihypertensive randomization arms.
Table 2 presents unadjusted cancer mortality event rates, combining both in-trial and post-trial data, categorized by eGFR levels and specific cancer types. A total of 2,338 cancer-related deaths were observed. The overall 10-year cancer mortality rate was 8.25 per 100 persons. This rate varied significantly across eGFR groups: 7.90 for eGFR ≥ 90, 7.71 for 60-89.9, 10.11 for 45-59.9, and 13.19 for eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. For specific cancer types, lung cancer accounted for the highest number of deaths (719), followed by "Other" cancers (802). Prostate cancer deaths totaled 191, colon cancer 230, breast cancer 107, urinary tract cancer 110, and hematopoietic cancer 179. Notably, the highest 10-year event rates for prostate, colon, and urinary tract cancers were observed in the eGFR < 45 group (2.70, 1.29, and 1.12 per 100 persons, respectively). The "Other" cancer category also showed its highest 10-year rate in the lowest eGFR group, at 5.58 per 100 persons. In contrast, lung, breast, and hematopoietic cancers did not consistently show their highest 10-year rates in the lowest eGFR category.
Table 3 details the relationship between baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and cancer mortality, encompassing both in-trial and post-trial data. Hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are presented for different eGFR categories, with eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 serving as the reference group (HR = 1.00). The analysis includes both unadjusted and adjusted models, with adjustments accounting for various factors such as age, gender (except for prostate cancer), race, ethnicity, smoking status, diabetes, history of coronary heart disease, body mass index, blood pressure, total cholesterol, glucose, aspirin use, and antihypertensive treatment arm. For breast cancer, the adjusted model also specifically included estrogen therapy history. A significant trend (p < 0.001) was observed in unadjusted cancer mortality, indicating an increasing risk with lower eGFR. Specifically, individuals with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 faced an unadjusted HR of 1.88 (1.55 – 2.28) for overall cancer mortality. After adjustment, this risk remained significant, with an HR of 1.54 (1.22 – 1.94) and a p-value for trend of 0.004 for eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 compared to the reference. For specific cancer types, unadjusted prostate cancer mortality showed a strong trend (p < 0.001), with the lowest eGFR group having an HR of 3.01 (1.54 – 5.87). After adjustment, this group's HR for prostate cancer was 1.83 (0.81 – 4.13), with a trend p-value of 0.028. Colon cancer mortality also exhibited a significant unadjusted trend (p = 0.048), with an HR of 2.15 (1.17 – 3.94) for eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. The adjusted HR for colon cancer in this group was 2.28 (1.12 – 4.62), though the trend p-value became 0.238. Urinary tract cancer mortality showed a highly significant unadjusted trend (p = 0.001), with the lowest eGFR group having an HR of 3.99 (1.90 – 8.38). This remained significant after adjustment, with an HR of 2.95 (1.14 – 7.65) and a trend p-value of 0.023. In contrast, unadjusted and adjusted analyses for lung, breast, and hematopoietic cancers did not consistently show statistically significant trends or increased risks in the lower eGFR categories. For "Other" cancers, the unadjusted HR for eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 was 2.01 (1.47 – 2.75) with a trend p-value of 0.005, which, after adjustment, became 1.70 (1.17 – 2.46), with a trend p-value of 0.259.
