This study aimed to identify factors influencing the success of cephalomedullary nailing for unstable intertrochanteric fractures (ITFs) in older adults, particularly introducing a novel radiological measurement. While cephalomedullary nails are the standard treatment for these fractures (AO/OTA 31-A2 and A3) in the geriatric population, a consistent radiological method for assessing the nail's entry point has been lacking. To address this, a retrospective analysis was performed on 136 elderly patients who underwent Gamma-3 nail fixation for unstable ITFs between July 2011 and December 2014, with a minimum one-year follow-up. Researchers collected demographic information, various radiological measurements, and a newly defined parameter: the side-difference of the distance from the piriformis fossa to the greater trochanteric tubercle in the anteroposterior view (dPG). These data were statistically compared between patients experiencing successful union and those with treatment failure, utilizing Student’s t-test, χ2 test, and multiple logistic regression. The findings revealed an overall failure rate of 13.23% (18 patients). Multivariate analysis indicated that patient sex, tip-apex distance (TAD), and the novel dPG measurement were significantly associated with treatment outcomes. Specifically, a dPG greater than 6 mm was linked to a 7.64 times higher odds of failure compared to a dPG less than 6 mm. This research therefore reinforces the impact of sex and TAD on ITF outcomes following nailing, and importantly, establishes dPG as a valuable new radiological predictor of treatment failure.

Intertrochanteric fractures (ITFs) in older adults pose a significant challenge to healthcare systems, accounting for almost 30% of all fracture-related hospitalizations in the United States. Surgical stabilization typically involves either dynamic hip screws (DHS) or cephalomedullary nailing. While the optimal fixation method remains debated, cephalomedullary nails are generally considered theoretically more stable than DHS due to their shorter moment arm and robust lateral support.
Despite the use of nailing for intertrochanteric fractures (ITF), failure rates persist, ranging from 6.5% to 16.5%. These failures often manifest as proximal fragment subsidence or lag screw cut-out. Key risk factors identified include insufficient tip-apex distance (TAD), incorrect lag screw placement, female sex, and poor entry point selection.
Given the limited research on radiologic parameters for cephalomedullary nails and their entry points, this study introduced dPG (side-difference of the distance from the piriformis fossa to the greater trochanteric tubercle in the anteroposterior view) to assess improper entry point characteristics. The investigation also included an evaluation of other relevant radiological and clinical risk factors.
From July 2011 to December 2014, geriatric patients (over 65) with unstable ITFs treated with cephalomedullary nailing and at least one year of follow-up were included. Exclusions comprised individuals with pathological fractures, non-ambulatory status, prior hip surgeries (ipsilateral or contralateral), unidentifiable radiological landmarks, or those lost to follow-up.
Unstable intertrochanteric fractures (ITFs) were characterized by an inadequate lateral wall, compromised medial support (involving the lesser trochanter), or a reverse oblique fracture pattern. These were further categorized using the AO/OTA classification system, with 31-A2 denoting lateral wall fractures and 31-A3 indicating reverse oblique fractures. Treatment for these unstable ITFs involved the use of Gamma-3 nails, with procedures conducted by a team of four trauma surgeons or supervised senior residents.
Post-operative assessments included clinical follow-ups and radiographs (pelvic AP and hip lateral views) at 4 and 8 weeks, then 3, 6, and 12 months. Outcomes were categorized as union or failure based on final radiological reports, with failure encompassing lag screw cut-out, nonunion, or osteonecrosis.
We analyzed patient characteristics like age, sex, injury side, and ASA classification, alongside radiological measures such as reduction quality (AP and lateral views), TAD, and AO/OTA classification. These factors were compared between patients who achieved union and those who experienced treatment failure.
Two independent orthopedic trauma surgeons assessed the newly proposed radiological parameter, dPG, for its predictive ability regarding failure and its reliability. They utilized smartIRIS version 13.0 for all radiographic reviews.
Postoperative radiographs were used to assess reduction quality, classifying it as good, acceptable, or poor. This system, initially described by Baumgaertner et al. and later adapted by Kashigar et al., relied on two distinct criteria. A "good" reduction satisfied both conditions: an AP view neck shaft angle between 120° and 135° with less than 20° of lateral angulation, and displacement under 4mm in both AP and lateral views. If only one criterion was met, the reduction was deemed "acceptable." Reductions failing both criteria were categorized as "poor."
Lateral view reduction was classified using Tsukada et al.'s system. Type 1 indicated no displacement. Type 2 involved anterior displacement of the head and neck fragment, while type 3 denoted posterior displacement of these fragments.
The study utilized two key measurements: TAD and dPG. TAD, or tip-apex distance, was determined using Baumgaertner et al.'s established method, categorized as either greater than or less than or equal to 25 mm. The novel parameter, dPG, involved calculating the side-difference in the distance between the piriformis fossa's deepest point and the greater trochanteric tubercle's tip, based on postoperative day one pelvic AP views. Patients with extensive comminuted fractures hindering landmark identification were excluded.
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 17.0. Continuous data underwent Student's t-tests, while categorical data were analyzed with χ2 tests, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Significant factors were then subjected to multiple logistic regression. To assess interobserver reliability for continuous data, the intraclass correlation coefficient with a 95% CI was employed. The discriminative power of the test was determined using the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), and the optimal cutoff point was identified via the Youden index.
Between July 2011 and December 2014, 193 elderly patients with unstable ITFs were initially assessed. Ultimately, 136 subjects were enrolled in the study. Exclusions totaled 53 patients, comprising 8 with prior hip surgeries, 5 with pathological fractures, 21 with medical complications or mortality, 4 with insufficient radiological landmarks, and 19 lost to follow-up.
Among the 136 patients, 60 were male and 76 were female. The majority (103) had AO/OTA 31-A2 fractures, while 33 had 31-A3. Treatment failure occurred in 18 patients (13.23%), primarily due to varus change and screw cut-out, with one case of osteonecrosis.
Initial analysis revealed several factors significantly linked to treatment failure: female sex, AO/OTA classification, TAD, dPG, reduction quality, and lateral displacement (all p < 0.05). Conversely, age, ASA classification, and injury side showed no significant difference between groups. Subsequent multivariate analysis confirmed that female sex, TAD, and dPG maintained their statistical significance.
The average dPG in the union group was 4.68 ± 4.58 mm, with values spanning from -4.1 to 17.5 mm. For the failure group, the mean dPG was notably higher at 9.10 ± 4.35 mm, ranging from 2.2 to 17.7 mm. Visual examples of elevated dPG values are provided in Figure 2. A small subset of four patients presented with comminuted fractures, which unfortunately obscured the trochanteric tubercle or piriformis fossa, preventing dPG measurement. However, in instances of lateral wall breakage and greater trochanteric fractures, the fracture lines typically lay inferior to the greater trochanteric tubercle, allowing for clear landmark identification on postoperative AP radiographs. Similarly, when the greater trochanter was displaced, the avulsed fragment was consistently positioned superior to the landmark, and no cases involved a fracture directly affecting the greater trochanteric tubercle itself.
The dPG measurement demonstrated good interobserver reliability. The intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.70 (single-measure, 95% CI: 0.60-0.77) and 0.82 (average-measure, 95% CI: 0.75-0.88), confirming consistent results between observers.
An optimal dPG cutoff of 6 mm was determined from the ROC curve, yielding an area under the curve of 0.796. This threshold demonstrated a specificity of 76.47% and a sensitivity of 70.16%. Patients exhibiting a dPG exceeding 6 mm faced a significantly elevated failure risk, with an odds ratio of 7.64 (95% CI, 2.33 to 24.99).
Our findings indicate that female sex, a TAD exceeding 25 mm, and a dPG greater than 6 mm are significant risk factors for failure following unstable intertrochanteric fracture nailing. The dPG measurement proved to be a reliable and strong predictor of these failures.
Despite comparable outcomes for unstable fractures treated with either DHS or cephalomedullary nails, the latter offers benefits in avoiding anatomical deformities. Its clinical adoption for unstable ITFs has surged, with usage rates for intramedullary nails climbing from 3% to 67% between 1999 and 2006.
While sliding hip screw failures and their risk factors are well-documented, the failure mechanisms and associated risks for cephalomedullary nails require more investigation. Prior research indicates that intramedullary nails commonly fail due to varus reduction changes and screw cut-out. Our study similarly observed this particular failure pattern.
Our study identified sex as a risk factor for cephalomedullary nail failure in ITFs, aligning with prior research. We hypothesize that women's increased osteoporosis and smaller bone structure contribute to this outcome.
In our study, univariate analysis revealed that AO/OTA 31-A3 fractures were associated with poor outcomes. However, this risk is typically higher with sliding hip screws and lower with intramedullary devices. The cephalomedullary nail's robust lateral support likely mitigated displacement in these 31-A3 fractures, thereby reducing the observed failure rate in our patient cohort.
Our study further supports existing evidence that Tip-Apex Distance (TAD) is a significant predictor of failure. This holds true for both Dynamic Hip Screws (DHS) and intramedullary nailing outcomes, as shown in numerous previous studies.
The impact of reduction quality, as assessed in the anteroposterior (AP) view, on patient outcomes has been a subject of varying findings across different research. While several studies have highlighted its significant influence, others, particularly those employing multivariate analysis, have concluded that reduction quality does not independently affect outcomes. Our current investigation aligns with some prior research, showing that reduction quality was a statistically significant factor in univariate analysis but lost this significance when subjected to multivariate analysis. Similarly, regarding displacement observed in the lateral view, previous work by Tsukada et al. and Ito et al. indicated that posterior displacement of the head and neck fragment was a predictor of substantial fracture sliding. In line with this, our study also identified lateral view displacement as a significant factor in univariate analysis. However, due to a limited number of cases exhibiting such displacement, we could not confirm this finding in multivariate analysis.
The entry point for intramedullary nails is widely recognized as critical for preventing malreduction in intertrochanteric fractures. Despite this, a specific radiological parameter to assess the anatomical changes linked to entry point selection has been lacking. To address this gap, our study introduced dPG, a novel radiological parameter designed to quantify the post-nailing anatomical alterations in the trochanteric region. After surgery, the trochanteric tubercle, identifiable as the lateralmost point of the greater trochanter in an AP view, and the piriformis fossa were clearly visible for measurement. Our findings indicate that an elevated dPG correlates with a greater risk of treatment failure, a conclusion supported by both univariate and multivariate analyses. Furthermore, the dPG measurement exhibited good and consistent interobserver reliability. An increase in dPG suggests that the piriformis fossa and the proximal fragment have shifted away from the distal fragment during the nailing procedure. This displacement is likely a consequence of either insufficient nail preparation or an improperly chosen entry point. When dPG exceeds 6 mm, the odds of failure are significantly higher (7.64) compared to a TAD greater than 25 mm (5.75), with satisfactory sensitivity and specificity for this threshold.
Despite promising initial findings, this study has limitations. Its retrospective nature introduces potential biases and confounding factors. While radiographic measurements were occasionally unfeasible, the interobserver reliability for dPG was good, and such instances were rare. Future research should include a larger sample size to reinforce these conclusions, especially given the infrequent occurrence of nailing failures.
This research introduces dPG as a novel radiological indicator for post-nailing greater trochanter geometry. A higher dPG strongly correlates with treatment failure, suggesting varus deformity of the proximal fragment or distal fragment lateralization, both linked to entry point and canal preparation.
