This study examines Switzerland's 2012 waste management system through a material flow analysis, focusing on the recoverable resources within waste. Approximately half of the municipal solid waste (MSW) undergoes material recycling, while the remaining half is thermally processed to recover energy. Enhancing resource efficiency by circulating materials is crucial for an industrial ecosystem. Recycling rates (RRs) serve as a common metric for assessing a circular economy's effectiveness in material circulation. The research offers a detailed breakdown of recycling for specific MSW components in Switzerland: paper, cardboard, aluminum, tinplate, glass, and polyethylene terephthalate (PET). It differentiates between collection rates (CRs), which represent material entering the recycling process, and RRs, which quantify the secondary resources generated. For PET, the study found a 45% closed-loop CR and a 40% open-loop CR, yielding RRs of 31% and 37% respectively (this includes exported materials and the recycling of polyethylene and metals from collected waste). Official collection figures for paper and cardboard are notably high at 97%. However, this study's findings, which incorporate exports, show CRs of 74% and 89% and RRs of 59% and 81% for paper and cardboard, respectively. A significant observation is that the calculated rates for most separately collected materials are considerably lower than the officially reported figures. This discrepancy, coupled with the lack of information on secondary material availability in official rates, obscures opportunities for improving resource recovery. The concept of an industrial ecosystem, first proposed by Frosch and Gallopoulos in 1989, draws parallels with biological ecosystems. This vision emphasizes highly efficient energy and material use, minimal waste and pollution, and the repurposing of residues as raw materials. Central to this ideal is enhancing resource efficiency through material circulation. This idea has recently evolved into the widely discussed "circular economy," which aims for production and consumption systems with minimal material and energy loss through extensive reuse, recycling, and recovery, as highlighted by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation (2013) and the EEA (2014). Material flow analysis (MFA) serves as a tool to quantify a society's material efficiency and identify areas for improvement. By mapping national waste flows, MFA enables both the tracking of material pathways and the closure of material loops, as noted by Brunner and Rechberger (2004). In Switzerland, the focus of this study, several MFAs have examined the national waste management system. Previous work by Liechti (2009) and Dettli and colleagues (2014) explored the overall material and energetic recovery potential of waste streams, while Steiger (2014) analyzed the composition and energy content of Swiss municipal solid waste (MSW). However, these earlier studies lacked detailed modeling of the recycling system, failing to trace the ultimate destination of separately collected materials. Consequently, they did not provide comprehensive insights into actual resource conservation or its potential for improvement within Switzerland's waste management framework.
Recycling rates, a common metric for societal resource efficiency, currently lack a standardized definition across various national statistics and organizations (e.g., EC 2008, 2015; FOEN 2013a; VKU 2014; EUWID 2012). Often, these national figures represent a "collection rate," which measures the collected material against the total goods consumed (EUWID 2012). This approach, however, only quantifies the material entering recycling systems, not the actual secondary material generated. The European Union's 2015 circular economy action plan aims to standardize recycling rate definitions, proposing a focus on the material input at the final recycling stage, after impurity removal (EC 2015). Similarly, Rigamonti and colleagues (2012) advocate for a material recovery indicator that prioritizes the output of secondary materials over mere collection volumes. A significant limitation in most existing studies is the omission of secondary material quality, which hinders accurate assessment of the displacement effects of recycling (Geyer et al. 2015). Furthermore, recycling rates frequently combine "closed-loop" and "open-loop" recycling into a single figure, despite their distinct implications. Closed-loop recycling involves reusing secondary material in its original product application, while open-loop recycling repurposes it for different products. This aggregation is problematic because open-loop recycling often utilizes recovered materials in applications with lower quality demands. Consequently, enhancing recycling efficiency for a circular economy necessitates a shift towards more closed-loop processes (Graedel et al. 2011). It's important to note that the environmental advantages of recycling aren't inherently tied to the open- or closed-loop distinction; rather, they depend on the comparative environmental impacts of producing equivalent goods from primary versus secondary materials (Geyer et al. 2015; Meylan et al. 2014).
This study conducts a detailed material flow analysis (MFA) of Switzerland's waste management, focusing on recycled municipal solid waste (MSW) components like paper, cardboard, glass, PET bottles, tinplate, and aluminum. It breaks down recycling rates (RRs) into open- and closed-loop collection and recycling categories. This granular approach, considering RRs and material quality, offers a more accurate assessment of how much primary material can be replaced, revealing the MSW system's role in a circular economy and potential areas for improvement.
The Material Flow Analysis (MFA) of Switzerland's 2012 waste management system draws upon existing data from Dettli and colleagues (2014) concerning overall waste generation. This analysis incorporates various waste treatment methods, including incineration, industrial furnaces, and cement production, as well as exported waste quantities. The study's geographical scope is limited to waste streams originating within Switzerland, deliberately excluding imported waste, as its processing falls outside the core functions of the Swiss waste management system. The MFA categorizes waste into several key types. Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) encompasses all household waste and similar waste from small businesses, including their respective recycling fractions. Construction and Demolition (C&D) waste includes metals, minerals, and combustible materials from the building sector, with the exception of unpolluted excavated soils. Hazardous waste, covering both household and industrial sources, is quantified using statistics from the Federal Office of the Environment (FOEN 2013b), with major components being chemical waste, contaminated soil, and various treatment residues and slurries. Industrial wastes that are either directly reused within the plant for energy recovery (e.g., waste at paper mills) or serve as raw materials in other processes (e.g., sawmill waste for board production, as per FOEN 2013c, or local agricultural wastes) are not included in this analysis. Several adjustments were made to the data from Dettli and colleagues (2014), particularly regarding MSW incineration. Impurities found within separately collected fractions are now accounted for in the recycling waste streams (illustrated as flow 'a' in Figure 1). This inclusion allows for a more comprehensive assessment of the quality of separate collection systems. These impurities, once separated during recycling activities, are subsequently directed to MSW incineration (MSWI), as depicted in Figure 2. Furthermore, the recovery of metals from MSWI bottom ash, a practice that is becoming increasingly common, is integrated into the analysis, building upon the work of Hügi (2012) and augmenting the data from Dettli and colleagues (2014). Figure 1 provides a simplified representation of the waste management system for separately collected recyclable materials. It outlines key processes and distinguishes between removed impurities and yield losses ('li') and materials recovered from MSWI bottom ash that are then recycled ('f'). When materials enter the recycling system, they can be processed for either open-loop or closed-loop product applications (g1 and g2), or they may be sorted out as impurities, lost (l1 to l3), or re-routed to a different recycling process as a distinct material (h1 and h2, such as polyethylene caps in PET bottle recycling). The dotted line in Figure 1 delineates the system boundaries for the collection rate (CR), while the dashed line indicates the boundaries for the recycling rate (RR).
This study conducts detailed Material Flow Analyses (MFAs) for specific Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) recycling systems in Switzerland, focusing on paper, cardboard, PET bottles, glass, tinplate, and aluminum. The data for these analyses were gathered from various sources, including national statistics, the recycling industry, and associated organizations, with all specific data sources detailed in Supporting Information S1. Due to inconsistencies in the available data across different waste streams, a process of data reconciliation was undertaken. This involved additional data mining conducted collaboratively with the relevant industries and the Swiss Federal Office of the Environment (FOEN). Based on this in-depth examination of recycling processes, the study re-evaluates the official recycling rates (official-RR) previously published by FOEN (FOEN 2013d). These recalculated rates are further broken down into domestic closed-loop and open-loop collection and recycling rates, as well as export rates. To clarify the methodology, a simplified diagram (Figure 1) illustrates the MFA scheme for recycling systems, highlighting the distinction between the Collection Rate (CR) and the Recycling Rate (RR), a concept adapted from UNEP (2011). This simplified scheme forms the foundational structure for all the detailed MFAs presented in Figures 3 through 6. The CR, as depicted in Figure 1, represents the proportion of material collected separately (flow c) relative to the total waste generated within a given period (the sum of flows b and c). In contrast, the RR is defined as the ratio of recycled materials (flows g1, g2, h1, and h2) to the total waste generated (flows b+c). When the overall RR is further categorized into open-loop and closed-loop RRs, the recycling of other materials (flows h1 and h2) is presented as a distinct rate and is consequently not integrated into either the open-loop or closed-loop RR. It's important to note that in Switzerland, the official-RR for certain materials are, in practice, equivalent to CRs, while for other fractions, Intermediate Recycling Rates (iRRs) are employed. These iRRs correspond to the ratio d/(b+c) in Figure 1. Comprehensive definitions of the official-RR can be found in Figure 7 and Supporting Information S1. The mass flows illustrated in Figures 3 through 6 encompass impurities and other non-target materials, not solely the specific recyclables under investigation. Impurities originating from the product's use phase and during collection are accounted for in flows b, c, d, e, and f. Gray arrows within these figures indicate impurities that have been removed and yield losses that occur throughout the processing chain. Exports, categorized as either CR-export or RR-export, can occur at two stages: either after separate collection or after the recycling process, when the material is a ready-to-use secondary resource. Figure 3, for instance, provides a detailed MFA of PET bottle recycling in Switzerland for 2012. This figure shows the collected material, including all contaminants, as input. All resulting materials and flows directed to subsequent treatment processes, such as Municipal Solid Waste Incineration (MSWI), are presented as exports. The flows are quantified in tonnes per year, with the thickness of the lines visually representing the relative magnitude of these flows. The data sources for the values in this figure are also available in Supporting Information S1.
The potential future uses of a material, or its "opportunity space," define whether its recycling is considered closed-loop or open-loop. Closed-loop recycling involves processing a material so it can be reused in its original product application. In contrast, open-loop recycling produces a secondary material that is unsuitable for its previous product form. This distinction is crucial for understanding a material's next life cycle. The ultimate destination of exported materials, whether for closed-loop or open-loop recycling, is specified where data allows, with all underlying assumptions detailed in Table 1.
In 2012, Switzerland generated a total of 21 million metric tonnes of waste, equating to roughly 3 tonnes per person annually. Construction and demolition (C&D) waste constituted the largest portion, exceeding 13 million tonnes (58% of the total), with most of this material being recycled into products like concrete granulate and other mineral fillers. Other separately collected waste streams, excluding municipal solid waste (MSW), made up 6% of the total, while hazardous waste accounted for 7%. A significant portion of both these separately collected materials (42%) and hazardous waste (65%) was sent abroad for processing. Regarding MSW, 48% (2.6 Mt) was processed in municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) plants. Conversely, 52% (2.9 Mt) of MSW recyclables were collected for recycling. However, during the recycling processes, 6% of the collected material was discarded as impurities or rejected waste, ending up in MSWI, cement kilns, or paper/cardboard production furnaces. Consequently, the actual recovered fraction of Swiss MSW decreased to 50% (2.7 Mt), with 27% of this recovered material being exported for further recycling. Following the overall MSW analysis, detailed material flow analyses (MFAs) are provided for PET, glass, and combined systems of paper/cardboard and aluminum/tinplate. These combined MFAs are necessary due to the strong interconnections in their material cycles, often involving mixed collection methods.
The detailed Material Flow Analysis (MFA) for PET recycling in Switzerland outlines a multi-stage process, beginning with separate collection, primarily at retail locations. This collected material then undergoes sorting, typically at one of five Swiss facilities, before proceeding to recycling. Following initial sorting by color, blue and colorless PET fractions are directed towards closed-loop recycling. A caustic soda washing process enables these materials to be re-manufactured into new bottles, potentially using 100% recycled content. In contrast, PET fractions of other colors are designated for open-loop recycling. Their color prevents their use in new bottle production due to aesthetic design constraints, so they are instead converted into PET fibers or food packaging, such as cookie trays. During both recycling pathways, impurities like polyethylene caps and metals, which are collected alongside the PET, are separated. These sorted-out materials are then sent to their respective recycling processes, with the assumption of closed-loop recycling for them. Any residues generated during the sorting phase are either incinerated in municipal solid waste incinerators (MSWI) or utilized in cement kilns. Waste produced during the recycling process itself is exclusively used as an alternative fuel in cement kilns. A portion of the high-quality, bottle-grade PET resulting from closed-loop recycling is exported. However, this finished product is categorized within the closed-loop Recycling Rate (RR) rather than the export rate, as its recycling transformation occurs within Switzerland. The Collection Rate (CR) for closed-loop PET recycling stands at 45%. Yet, only 26% of the initial PET waste ultimately becomes granulate suitable for new bottle production, reflecting the closed-loop RR. This reduction is partly due to "fines," which constitute 20% of the closed-loop recycling input; these are sorted out and diverted to the open-loop recycling system. This diversion diminishes the closed-loop RR while maintaining a constant open-loop rate, despite the removal of these residues. The aggregated CR, encompassing material for both open- and closed-loop systems, as well as exports, reached 85% in this study. This contrasts with the official-RR of 81%, which is derived similarly to the intermediate Recycling Rate (iRR). The calculated iRR for PET was 72%. The discrepancy between the official-RR and the iRR is attributed to how impurities are assessed: the official-RR uses a fixed percentage for assumed impurities, whereas this study utilized actual material flow data from 2012. Additionally, 9% of the collected PET, including impurities, was exported to other countries, where open-loop recycling is anticipated.
To streamline logistics and cut transportation expenses, aluminum and tinplate from municipal solid waste (MSW) are increasingly collected together as a mixed metal fraction. However, the precise proportions of metals collected as mixed versus single fractions (e.g., aluminum or ferrous metals) from households remain unclear, as data on collection systems are not sourced from recyclers or traders. Municipalities or local traders gather this material from collection points and then deliver it to recyclers, either within or outside Switzerland. The collected mixed material undergoes sorting using magnetic and eddy current separators. Since Switzerland lacks an aluminum smelting plant, aluminum scrap is exported for recycling, primarily to Germany and Italy, with smaller quantities going to Austria and France (Swiss-Impex 2012). Conversely, a portion of ferrous scrap (60% of the collection) is detinned in Switzerland before being utilized in Swiss steel works. The remaining 40% of collected ferrous scrap is exported for remelting without prior detinning. Detinned can scrap is then employed in Swiss electric arc furnaces to produce liquid steel, which has diverse applications. If material is not detinned, the recycling process necessitates diluting the tin with higher-quality scrap, resulting in dilution losses (Verhoef et al. 2008; Nakamura et al. 2012; Ohno et al. 2014). Consequently, only the melting of detinned material is categorized as closed-loop recycling. The quantity of tinplate in MSW incinerators (MSWI) is largely unknown. This study assumed 2,084 tonnes per year based on the official recycling rate, though this collected amount diverges from official statistics when calculated using internal reports from the relevant recycling association. In contrast, Steiger (2014) estimated, through a sampling campaign, that 19,000 tonnes of ferrous metals are sent to MSWI, with 9,000 tonnes potentially recyclable. However, this estimate carries significant uncertainties (ranging from 0.7 to 7.0 kilograms per year per person across 33 municipalities) and was therefore not adopted. While assuming 2,084 tonnes in MSWI yields a recycling rate of 71% (official-RR, 86%), an assumption of 9,000 tonnes in MSWI would result in a collection rate (CR) of 43%.
While metals are often considered infinitely recyclable, post-consumer aluminum scrap typically finds open-loop applications, primarily in automotive castings, due to its higher impurity levels. This contrasts with wrought alloys, a key product of the primary aluminum industry, which demand less than 10% alloying elements and cannot be made from old scrap. To mitigate impurities like alloying elements from prior use or collection, remelting processes often involve dilution with primary aluminum or superior-quality scrap. The separate collection of used beverage cans (UBCs) is crucial for aluminum recycling because this source separation also sorts by alloy type. Such segregated UBCs can be remelted into new beverage cans. However, mixed scrap fractions are destined for other products. It's important to note that while official recycling rates (official-RR) focus solely on UBC collection, the collection rates (CR) and recycling rates (RR) discussed here encompass all aluminum packaging. In 2012, 43% of aluminum from municipal solid waste (MSW) was collected separately as UBCs, representing a closed-loop CR-export. Another 42% of aluminum was source-sorted as a mixed fraction, also a CR-export, sometimes combined with other metals. While detailed modeling of overseas treatments was not undertaken, it was presumed that exported material contained 5% impurities, mirroring those found in Swiss mixed collections, which would result in melting losses. To account for yield losses and impurity removal during ferrous scrap melting, an 88% efficiency was adopted, based on Remus and colleagues (2013). For aluminum, a 94.4% efficiency was used, derived from a UBC study by da Silva et al. (2010).
While not typically factored into official recycling rates, materials salvaged from Municipal Solid Waste Incineration (MSWI) contribute to a circular economy. For instance, if 80% of ferrous scrap were recovered from MSWI bottom ash (a figure supported by a 2009 study and Danish data from 2015), the intermediate recycling rate (iRR) for tinplate would rise by 12%, and the overall recycling rate (RR) by 9%, reaching 80%. Similarly, aluminum recovery from bottom ash, with an average efficiency of 62% (Allegrini et al., 2014) and a 70% recycling efficiency, would boost its iRR by 9% and RR by 6%, bringing aluminum's total RR to 85%.
Households in Switzerland dispose of paper and cardboard through either mixed or separated collection, with methods varying by municipality. While mixed materials can be sorted by recyclers or paper industries, or directly used in cardboard production, the exact amount of mixed material separated at an intermediate stage remains unclear. This is because data collection occurred at recycling industries, not at the municipal level, meaning the Material Flow Analysis (MFA) only accounts for a small portion of mixed material that directly reaches recycling industries. The collected cardboard encompasses diverse qualities like folding boxboard, chipboard, and old corrugated containers. Cardboard negatively impacts paper recycling, so recyclers aim to limit its presence in paper recycling to a maximum of 2%. Conversely, paper is suitable for cardboard recycling. Industry experts note that imperfect sorting leads to approximately 7% post-consumer paper in the sorted cardboard fraction. This contamination elevates the open-loop Recycling Rate (RR) for paper from 17% (paper directly used in cardboard production) to 23%. Since data was sourced from paper industries that often purchase pre-sorted materials, the quantity of paper collected mixed with cardboard and subsequently separated by recyclers is unknown, leading to an underestimation of paper's downcycling.
Of the collected material, 20% is non-recyclable, primarily short fibers, which the paper industry uses for heat. Additionally, 13% of collected paper and 32% of cardboard are exported, presumed to undergo closed-loop recycling.
This study's calculated collection rates (CR) for paper and cardboard in 2012 were considerably lower than the officially reported recycling rates (official-RR). The Swiss association for paper and cardboard recycling (RPK) has since updated its models for calculating official-RR, which were previously presented as CR, in cooperation with the authors of this research. While these revised recycling rates were officially released from 2015 onwards, corrected figures for 2012 are now accessible. A key factor contributing to the lower CRs in both this study's findings and the RPK's updated figures is the inclusion of non-recyclable toilet and kitchen tissues. This inclusion significantly reduces the overall collection rates. For a visual comparison of the Federal Office for the Environment's (FOEN) official-RR with the closed-loop, open-loop, and export rates determined in this study, refer to Figure 7. The figure also clarifies that "not separately collected" material goes to municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) from households, and "not recyclable" material is sent to MSWI or cement kilns after separate collection. Importantly, materials recovered from MSWI bottom ash are not reflected in official-RRs.
In Swiss municipalities, glass collection primarily occurs in two ways: 70% is color-separated, while 29% is collected as mixed-colored waste glass to streamline logistics. This collected glass encompasses not only bottles but also other packaging like jars. A small fraction, 1%, consists of returnable bottles designated for reuse. Regarding recycling, the closed-loop collection rate (CR) for glass, excluding exports, stands at 24%. From this, 92% is suitable for producing new glass. However, 4% becomes collection residues, and another 4% is fine material that is subsequently downcycled into open-loop recycling. The open-loop recycling rate (RR) for Switzerland is 7%. A significant discrepancy exists between the total recycling rate calculated in this study (84%) and the officially reported rate (97%). This difference largely stems from the varying methodologies for estimating glass quantities entering municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI). The Federal Office for the Environment (FOEN) extrapolates from consumed glass bottles to all packaging glass, whereas this study utilizes residual waste composition data from Steiger (2014). Consequently, this research included 60,000 tonnes of glass in residual waste sent to MSWI, a figure considerably higher than the 14,000 tonnes anticipated by official calculations. Furthermore, an estimated 16,000 tonnes of glass packaging imported through cross-border shopping, not accounted for in official statistics, partially explains these deviations. Estimating non-bottle glass packaging waste also introduces substantial uncertainties. Notably, mixed-color cullet is processed into foam glass domestically, while exported waste glass, after color sorting, is used for packaging glass, suggesting it enters closed-loop recycling.
Based on the detailed material flow analyses (MFAs), this study determined open-loop, closed-loop, and export recycling rates (RRs), which are presented in Figure 7. The methodology for these calculations is provided in Supporting Information S2. The official recycling rates (official-RR) for 2012, encompassing collection rates (CR) or intermediate recycling rates (iRR), were sourced from FOEN (2013b) and are also included in Figure 7. For PET, the analysis revealed a 45% closed-loop CR, a 31% open-loop CR, and a 9% CR-export. These collection figures translated to respective RRs of 26% for closed-loop, 30% for open-loop, and 7% for export. Notably, the open-loop recycling rate increased from CR to RR, a phenomenon attributed to the incorporation of lower-quality materials from closed-loop collection. In contrast to official figures, the RRs for paper and cardboard derived in this study were considerably lower. Paper showed a total RR of 59% and a CR of 74%, while cardboard had an RR of 81% and a CR of 89%. The official-RR for paper and cardboard combined was 97%. It is important to note that the RPK's calculation model, which informs the FOEN's published rates, has since been revised in collaboration with the study's authors. Consequently, the official-RR for paper and cardboard was adjusted to 70% in 2014, a figure that integrates the CR and CR-export for both materials and is also reflected in Figure 7 (RPK 2012).
Switzerland's municipal solid waste (MSW) generation is among the world's highest, reaching 700 kilograms per person annually. In 2012, roughly half of this waste was incinerated, while the remaining half underwent material recycling. Among the recycled MSW fractions, paper and cardboard constituted the largest portion by mass at 47%. Biogenic waste followed at 30%, with glass packaging making up 12%. Waste electronic and electric equipment (WEEE) accounted for 7%, while PET and other waste plastics, along with textiles, each represented 2%. Metals (aluminum and tinplate) comprised the smallest fraction at 1%. Detailed analysis of WEEE, textiles, and waste plastics recycling was not undertaken due to system complexity, data scarcity, and the nascent stage of large-scale household plastics recycling, respectively.
Current official recycling metrics, such as those from the Swiss Federal Office of the Environment (FOEN), frequently rely on collection rates (CRs) or intermediate recycling rates (iRRs). While iRRs account for impurity removal, they often overlook recycling yields. Similarly, the European Union predominantly uses CRs, though a shift to iRRs and a discussion of recycling rates (RRs) are underway. However, only RRs accurately reflect the quantity of material genuinely recovered from waste management systems. Therefore, RRs are crucial performance indicators for a circular economy, as they incorporate the efficiency of recycling processes. Implementing RRs necessitates establishing specific targets for each material, considering technical feasibility and actual recycling yields. Neither current Swiss nor European rates adequately convey the amount of secondary material actually produced. To provide a more comprehensive understanding, closed-loop and open-loop RRs should be reported distinctly, given their differing resource recovery approaches. Open-loop recycling facilitates the processing of diverse material qualities, thus maximizing the volume of recycled waste. Conversely, closed-loop recycling prioritizes quality, aiming for the secondary material to be used in applications identical to the original product. Closed-loop recycling offers greater flexibility for future treatment, whereas open-loop recycling is irreversible, leading to material degradation and limiting subsequent recycling possibilities. It's important to note that the environmental benefits of open- versus closed-loop recycling are not universally superior; they depend on the specific materials and products being substituted.
The study reveals that actual recycling rates (RRs) for most materials are considerably lower than officially reported figures, a trend likely extending beyond Switzerland. Existing recycling metrics are problematic because they fail to accurately indicate the quantity of secondary materials produced and obscure opportunities for waste management enhancement. Policy decisions, often relying on collection rates (CR), are therefore made with incomplete information. The lack of a consistent definition for national recycling rates renders current comparisons ineffective. Consequently, the European action plan for a circular economy advocates for standardized recycling rates. A globally harmonized definition would facilitate meaningful comparisons and pinpoint worldwide areas for improved material recovery.
Uncertainty often clouds the quantity of material sent to Municipal Solid Waste Incineration (MSWI). For instance, official recycling rates indicate 14,000 tonnes of glass go to Swiss MSWI, yet Steiger (2014) reported 60,000 tonnes of glass from household MSW entering MSWI. Better monitoring of these material flows is crucial for more accurate collection and recycling rate calculations.
The study accounts for material recycling both within Switzerland and abroad, with 73% of source-separated, recovered material processed domestically. While detailed knowledge of overseas treatment and its open- or closed-loop nature is limited, likely treatments were determined through industry discussions and incorporated into the Material Flow Analysis. This inclusion of exported fractions is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the impacts and advantages of Swiss waste management.
This study's detailed material flow analyses (MFAs) reveal how product design significantly impacts recycling outcomes. For instance, the color of PET bottles directly affects their suitability for closed-loop recycling into new bottles; only blue and colorless PET can be used for this purpose. Consequently, choosing to produce green PET bottles, for example, restricts their recycling options to open-loop uses like non-beverage packaging or fibers, preventing their return to bottle production. Beyond design, the quality of collected recyclables, influenced by consumer sorting habits, dictates their ultimate fate. PET mixed with other plastic packaging, such as shampoo or detergent bottles, often suffers from high contamination. This contamination typically prevents its closed-loop recycling, leading to its use as alternative fuel in cement kilns (as a mixed residual fraction) or in open-loop recycling. While increasing collection rates is desirable, it can inadvertently lower the quality of separated materials, thereby limiting their secondary use and increasing the processing effort required for recycling, including sorting, baling, and shredding.
The European Commission's 2015 circular economy action plan sets goals for material recycling and reuse to enhance waste management's role in a circular economy. To facilitate comparisons between different regions or countries, a standardized indicator is essential. One proposal under consideration involves defining recycling rates based on the material input at the final production stage, which for certain materials would align with the intermediate recycling rates (iRR) previously discussed. However, this approach lacks incentives for improving overall recycling efficiency. For instance, reducing the mineral content in ferrous scrap from municipal solid waste incinerator (MSWI) bottom ash would be discouraged, as it would lead to a lower iRR. Consequently, recycling rates (RRs) are suggested as a more appropriate metric for international comparison. RRs encompass the entire recycling process, thereby encouraging optimization across all stages and providing valuable data on the secondary materials generated from waste.
A central question in the circular economy is whether its main objective is to provide alternative resources from secondary sources or to minimize environmental impact. If the former is the priority, then Recycling Rates (RR) serve as an effective performance indicator. Differentiating between open- and closed-loop recycling helps in quantifying the extent of material circulation, though this categorization doesn't necessarily align with overall environmental benefits (Geyer et al. 2015). However, if the primary goal is environmental impact reduction, then a more comprehensive evaluation of recycling processes is required. This would involve detailed assessments, including modeling substitution and displacement effects (Zink et al. 2015; Geyer et al. 2015). While more data-intensive than a simple Material Flow Analysis (MFA), this approach offers a broader perspective crucial for developing environmentally optimal waste management systems. Current metrics often concentrate solely on material cycle closure, frequently overlooking environmental benefits and impacts. The European Union's Waste Framework Directive (EC 2008) mandates the use of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) to determine when deviations from the traditional waste hierarchy (avoid, reuse, recycle, recover, landfill) are justified. Therefore, a holistic understanding of environmental impacts is essential for informing future policy and crafting effective waste management strategies. Future research will focus on a complete LCA of the Swiss Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) management system, building upon the detailed recycling pathway investigation provided by this study's MFA, given that environmental benefits largely depend on the materials substituted by secondary resources and their displaced product systems. Comparing LCA results with RR could also highlight potential trade-offs between resource recovery and environmental impact reduction, such as when increased recycling efforts disproportionately arise from incorporating low-quality materials.
While current recycling rates typically exclude material recovered from Municipal Solid Waste Incineration (MSWI) bottom ash, this recovery method is a promising strategy for a circular economy. The ongoing necessity for thermal treatment, particularly with complex product designs that prevent complete source separation, means that various residual fractions will continue to be produced. Therefore, the thermal recovery of energy, followed by material recovery, presents a valuable approach, provided the quality of the recovered materials is suitable for further use. Given MSWI's significant contribution to metal recycling, these material flows are crucial for accurately assessing a society's circularity. A discussion is needed to determine if MSWI material recovery should be integrated into national recycling rates for source-separated materials or reported as a distinct thermal recycling rate.
Transitioning from a linear to a circular economy necessitates a focus on material quality. For instance, the long-term viability of closed-loop recycling is jeopardized if materials like aluminum or steel degrade in quality with each use cycle due to accumulating alloying elements. Research by Kral and colleagues (2013) emphasizes the importance of removing harmful substances during recycling to maintain closed-loop systems. Furthermore, studies (Haupt et al. 2016) indicate that using low-quality scrap in metal recycling can increase electricity consumption, revealing a conflict between material recycling and energy efficiency.
Current recycling metrics, such as collection rates (CRs) and intermediate recycling rates (iRRs), are inadequate for evaluating progress towards a circular economy. CRs, while commonly reported, offer no insight into the actual volume of secondary resources generated or their ultimate use. Similarly, iRRs, which account for material entering the recycling process after impurity removal, still fall short. They do not incentivize improvements in recycling efficiency or the reduction of contaminants in recyclable goods, thus failing to accurately represent the amount of material truly retained within circular systems. A more effective approach to gauge waste management's contribution to a circular economy would involve reporting not just CRs or iRRs, but also distinct closed-loop and open-loop recycling rates (RRs).
