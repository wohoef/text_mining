This study investigated chromosome 17 alterations in male breast cancer (MBC), a topic previously explored in only a limited number of small cohorts. In female breast cancer (FBC), HER2 amplification, located on chromosome 17, is linked to aggressive disease, high grade, and poor prognosis, and involves complex chromosomal rearrangements. However, HER2 amplification occurs less frequently in MBC. To characterize these changes, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) were employed on a cohort of 139 MBC samples. The findings were then compared with FBC data and correlated with patient outcomes and clinicopathological features. The research revealed that MBC exhibits fewer chromosome 17 copy number changes and less intricate rearrangement patterns compared to FBC. Specifically, MBC showed gains on 17q and losses on 17p, but no instances of whole chromosome 17 polyploidy. Two recurring amplicons were identified on chromosome 17: one at 17q12, including the NEUROD2, HER2, GRB7, and IKZF3 genes, and another at 17q23.1, involving the MIR21 and RPS6KB1 genes. While whole arm copy number gains of 17q were associated with reduced 5-year survival rates (p = 0.010), HER2 amplification, though linked to high tumor grade, did not independently predict patient survival. Both HER2 and NEUROD2 copy number gains correlated with high tumor grade, elevated mitotic count, and decreased 5-year survival (p = 0.015). However, only tumor size and NEUROD2 copy number gains emerged as independent prognostic factors. In conclusion, MBC displays simpler and fewer chromosome 17 rearrangements than FBC, with frequent 17q gains (including two distinct amplicons) and 17p losses, but no whole chromosome 17 polyploidies. The independent prognostic significance of NEUROD2 gains suggests distinct roles for chromosome 17 aberrations in male versus female breast cancer development.

Prior research utilizing multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) and comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) has highlighted significant distinctions in gene copy number alterations between male breast cancer (MBC) and female breast cancer (FBC), suggesting divergent carcinogenic pathways [1, 2]. Chromosome 17q copy number variations have been a focal point of research across various cancer types, including FBC, largely owing to the presence of the ERBB2 oncogene (HER2) in this region. HER2 amplification, observed in approximately 10–20% of FBC cases, typically results in elevated protein expression. This amplification and overexpression are associated with aggressive tumor characteristics, including high grade, increased mitotic activity, and a poorer prognosis, though it also indicates a positive response to trastuzumab therapy [3–8]. Beyond HER2, chromosome 17 hosts other oncogenes like TOP2A and PPM1D [9–11]. While correction for chromosome 17 polysomy is a common practice in assessing HER2 amplification via in situ hybridization, studies in FBC indicate that true polysomy is rare and centromere copy number status is an unreliable proxy for chromosome 17 copy numbers. Instead, FBC often presents with intricate chromosome 17 rearrangements [12–15]. In MBC, HER2 amplification is less frequent than in FBC, occurring in 2–8% of cases compared to FBC's 10–20% [1, 6, 14, 16–18]. To date, limited studies, primarily using CGH on small MBC cohorts, have investigated chromosome 17 alterations, and their impact on disease outcome remains largely unexplored [2, 19].
This study characterized chromosome 17 copy number changes in a large male breast cancer (MBC) cohort. We utilized a specialized MLPA kit, previously employed for chromosome 17 polysomy research in female breast cancer (FBC). HER2 CISH was also performed, and findings were correlated with clinicopathological features and patient outcomes.
The study enrolled a total of 139 male breast cancer (MBC) cases, chosen because their paraffin blocks contained sufficient tumor cells for DNA isolation. These specimens were collected consecutively from surgical invasive MBC cases between 1986 and 2011. The collection spanned seven pathology laboratories: four in the Netherlands (St. Antonius Hospital Nieuwegein, Diakonessenhuis Utrecht, University Medical Center Utrecht, and Laboratory for Pathology East Netherlands) and three in Germany (Paderborn, Cologne, and Kassel). To ensure diagnostic accuracy, four experienced observers (PJvD, RK, AM, ML) independently reviewed hematoxylin and eosin (HE) stained slides. This review confirmed the diagnosis and allowed for tumor typing and grading according to current World Health Organization (WHO) standards. Clinicopathological data for these 139 cases were extracted from pathology reports, encompassing patient age, tumor size, and lymph node status. The average age of MBC patients in this cohort was 67 years, with ages ranging from 32 to 89 years. Tumor sizes varied from 0.2 to 7.2 cm. Lymph node status was determined for 114 cases through either axillary lymph node dissection or sentinel node procedures, revealing lymph node metastases in 56% of these patients. The predominant histological type, identified according to WHO criteria, was invasive ductal carcinoma. Most tumors were classified as grade 2 or grade 3 based on the modified Bloom and Richardson score. Mitotic activity was also assessed, with a mean mitotic index of 12 per 2 mm². Furthermore, the expression of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), along with HER2 amplification status, were re-evaluated for all cases. ER and PR expression were detected using immunohistochemical staining on tissue microarray (TMA) slides. For HER2 copy number assessment, chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) was employed, specifically using the SPoT-Light HER2 CISH kit from Invitrogen. HER2 amplification was defined by two criteria: low-level amplification, indicated by 5–10 single dots or small clusters of dots per nucleus in over 50% of tumor cells; and high-level amplification, characterized by more than 10 single dots or large clusters of dots per nucleus in over 50% of tumor cells.
Tumors were categorized into five intrinsic subtypes using immunohistochemical staining, as previously detailed. These included luminal A (ER/PR positive, HER2 negative, low Ki-67), luminal B (ER/PR positive, HER2 positive or high Ki-67), HER2-driven (HER2 positive, ER/PR negative), basal-like (ER/PR/HER2 negative, CK5/6 or CK14 or EGFR positive), and unclassifiable triple negative (all six markers negative).
DNA extraction and subsequent Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) analysis were central to this study. Initially, tumor areas were carefully identified on HE-stained slides and then microdissected from 8 μm paraffin sections, a method previously established. The extracted DNA underwent an overnight incubation with proteinase K (10 mg/ml; Roche, Almere, The Netherlands) at 56 °C, followed by a 10-minute boiling step and centrifugation for clarification. For MLPA analysis, 5 μl of the prepared DNA solution was utilized. This process adhered to the manufacturer's guidelines (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) and was carried out using a Veriti 96-well thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). A specific P004-C1 kit from MRC Holland was employed, featuring five probes targeting 17p genes and twenty-six probes for seventeen 17q genes, alongside twelve additional reference probes. To ensure reliability, all tests were duplicated. Each MLPA run incorporated negative controls, consisting of normal breast and blood cells, as well as a positive control, which was a tumor sample exhibiting high-level HER2 amplification confirmed by CISH, consistent with prior studies. Following PCR, the products were separated via electrophoresis on an ABI 3730 capillary sequencer (Applied Biosystems). Gene copy number ratios were then computed using Genescan v4.1 (Applied Biosystems) and Coffalyser v9.4 (MRC-Holland) software. For genes represented by multiple probes, an average of the duplicate copy number ratios was calculated. Established cut-off values were applied: values between 0.7 and 1.3 were deemed normal. A gene copy number increase, or total gain, was defined as values exceeding 1.3, encompassing both gain (>1.3 to 2.0) and amplification (>2.0). Conversely, values below 0.7 indicated a loss. Whole chromosome arm loss or gain was determined if over 75% of all probes showed a copy number loss, mirroring previous array-CGH methodologies. A partial gain on the long arm of chromosome 17 was identified by any probe showing an increase in copy number. The chromosome 17 copy number data from male breast cancer (MBC) cases were subsequently compared with previously reported data from 111 female breast cancer (FBC) cases, acknowledging that the FBC data originated from a different MLPA design.
For statistical analysis, IBM SPSS for Windows version 20.0 was utilized. To determine correlations between gene copy numbers and clinicopathological characteristics, Pearson Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were employed for categorical variables. Standard dichotomization was applied to grade, tumor size, and mitotic count. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering, performed with the R statistical program, identified significant clusters using the maximum distance and Ward’s method, with cluster stability assessed by pvclust. Prognostic and therapeutic data were sourced from the Integral Cancer registration of the Netherlands (IKNL). Survival data, available for 100 cases with a 5.6-year mean follow-up, informed analyses based on 5-year survival rates. Univariate survival estimates were generated via Kaplan-Meier plots and log-rank tests. Cox regression was then used for multivariate survival analyses, incorporating variables significant in univariate tests. Holm-Bonferroni corrections addressed multiple comparisons.
Our investigation utilized Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) to determine the copy number status of chromosome 17 across a cohort of 139 male breast cancer (MBC) cases. A comparative analysis with female breast cancer (FBC) cases, detailed in Figure 1 and Supplementary Table A, revealed notable differences. Specifically, MBC exhibited a reduced frequency of copy number alterations and less intricate genomic rearrangement patterns when contrasted with FBC. A significant portion of MBC cases, 51 out of 139 (36.7%), showed no copy number changes in any of the genes examined by the MLPA assay. When alterations were present, copy number increases predominantly occurred on chromosome 17q, affecting 78 out of 139 cases (56%). Conversely, copy number losses were most prevalent on chromosome 17p, observed in 36 out of 139 cases (26%). Further analysis indicated that a complete gain of the 17q arm was present in six MBC cases (4%), yet no instances of whole chromosome 17 gain were identified. The genes most frequently amplified were NEUROD2, IKZF3, HER2, and MIR21. Furthermore, MIR21 and RPS6KB1 were the genes most commonly associated with copy number gains. On the other hand, copy number losses were predominantly observed for the MNT, TP53, and PMP genes, all situated on 17p (Figure 2).
MLPA analysis was performed on eight male breast cancer (MBC) cases to determine their HER2 status, representing 5.8% of the total 139 MBC cases studied. Among these eight, half (four cases) exhibited MLPA amplification ratios between 2.0 and 2.5, while the remaining four cases showed ratios exceeding 2.5. A comparison with Chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) results revealed inconsistencies: the four cases with MLPA ratios between 2.0 and 2.5 did not show HER2 amplification by CISH. Conversely, four cases identified as HER2 amplified by CISH also displayed MLPA amplification ratios greater than 2.5. Notably, one case, categorized as low-level amplification by CISH, registered only a gain (not high-level amplification) through MLPA.
Among the eight cases with HER2 amplification detected by MLPA, three (37.5%) displayed a whole arm gain of 17q, which included the WSB1 gene near the centromere. Notably, two of these three cases also presented with partial gains on the short arm, alongside losses in other short arm loci. A significant proportion, 75% (6/8) of HER2-amplified cases, exhibited amplification within the 17q12 region, encompassing the NEUROD2, GRB7, and IKZF3 genes. Within this group, two cases further showed amplification in the RARA/TOP2A gene region at 17q21.2. The genes NEUROD2, HER2, GRB7, and IKZF3 were frequently observed to have copy number gains, with no instances of copy number loss in this region. Additionally, a separate region of frequent copy number gain was identified at 17q23.1, involving the MIR21 and RPS6KB1 genes. In summary, two recurring amplicons were identified: one at 17q12 (containing NEUROD2, HER2, GRB7, and IKZF3) and another at 17q23.1 (containing MIR21 and RPS6KB1).
Table 3 reveals associations between increased copy numbers of several genes on chromosome 17q and adverse clinicopathological characteristics. Specifically, elevated mitotic counts and high tumor grades were linked to copy number increases in NEUROD2, HER2, GRB7, IKZF3, RPS6KB1, PPM1D, AXIN2, and UNC13D. SGCA copy number increases correlated with both high grade and larger tumor size, while BRCA1 and MIR21 increases were associated solely with a high tumor grade. Further analysis of gene amplification showed that NEUROD2 amplification was connected to both a high mitotic count and a high grade. Amplification of HER2 and GRB7 genes was associated with a high tumor grade, and IKZF3 amplification correlated with a high mitotic count. After applying corrections for multiple comparisons, the significant relationship between NEUROD2 gene copy number gains and both a high mitotic count and a high grade persisted, as detailed in Table 3.
Unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis revealed a significant co-clustering of the NEUROD2, HER2, GRB7, and IKZF3 genes (p < 0.001). Within the male breast cancer cohort, a distinct cluster of 12 cases exhibited whole arm copy number gains on chromosome 17, alongside amplifications of these four genes. This specific cluster displayed a notably higher proportion of luminal type B cases compared to luminal type A cases (p = 0.010). No significant differences were observed in other clinicopathological characteristics, such as age, grade, mitotic index, tumor size, or lymph node status, when comparing this cluster to the rest of the cases.
Among 100 male breast cancer cases with an average follow-up of 5.6 years, several factors were linked to reduced 5-year survival. These included high tumor grade (Grade 3), an elevated mitotic count (over 8 mitoses/2 mm2), larger tumor size (exceeding 2.0 cm), luminal type B classification, and a positive HER2 status as determined by CISH (both low and high amplification). Additionally, gains in NEUROD2 copy number, HER2 copy number, and entire 17q arm gains were associated with poorer survival outcomes. Interestingly, the previously identified clusters did not show a correlation with survival. When subjected to multivariate Cox regression analysis, only tumor size and NEUROD2 gene copy number gain emerged as independent predictors of prognosis.
This study employed MLPA to identify and describe chromosome 17 copy number alterations within a substantial group of male breast cancer (MBC) patients. A significant portion of MBC cases exhibited these aberrations, primarily involving gains on 17q (56%, 78/139 cases) and losses on 17p (26%, 36/139 cases). Notably, whole chromosome 17 gains were absent, consistent with prior research on female breast cancer (FBC). Furthermore, only a small fraction (4%, 6/139 cases) displayed a complete 17q arm gain. When compared to FBC, MBC presented with fewer chromosome 17 copy number changes and less intricate genomic rearrangement patterns.
Earlier investigations employing chromosome CGH examined copy number alterations in MBC [2, 19]. Rudlowski et al. [19] identified 17q gains in 36% of MBC cases, though these gains lacked correlation with any clinicopathological features. Our current research observed 17q gains in 58% of MBC, with 14% exhibiting high-level amplifications. Consistent with our findings, Tommasi et al. [2] reported that both gains and losses on chromosome 17 were less pronounced in MBC compared to FBC.
Frequent co-occurrence of whole 17q arm gains and WSB1 gene gains (near the centromere) was observed with HER2 gene amplification in 37.5% of cases. Conversely, the 17p arm in these cases exhibited only partial copy number changes or no alterations, indicating an absence of whole chromosome 17 polysomies in MBC, consistent with prior FBC findings. This suggests that using centromere probes for FISH polysomy correction could lead to inaccurate HER2 gene status assessments, a phenomenon also noted in FBC.
A large 17q amplicon, encompassing HER2, NEUROD2, GRB7, and IKZF3, was identified in 75% of MBC cases, despite its overall rarity. This co-amplification and clustering of genes distinguishes it from previously observed FBC patterns. The shared prognostic significance of NEUROD2 and HER2 copy number gains in this study supports the presence of this combined amplicon.
Our study revealed that HER2 copy number gains were present in 21% of male breast cancer (MBC) cases. However, only 5.8% of these cases showed true amplification when assessed by MLPA. This MLPA-detected amplification rate aligns with earlier HER2 expression research [16, 18]. Notably, this frequency is lower than the 30% amplification reported by Tommasi et al. [2] in MBC using CGH, a difference potentially attributable to their distinct methodology and smaller sample size. Compared to prior MLPA studies in female breast cancer (FBC), where HER2 amplification was observed in 20% of cases [6], our findings indicate a reduced frequency (5.8%) in MBC. Consistent with our previous work in both FBC and MBC, MLPA results for HER2 amplification demonstrated a strong correlation with those obtained via CISH [1, 14].
A diminished 5-year survival rate was linked to a positive HER2 status, identified through both CISH (p = 0.039) and MLPA (p = 0.015. However, HER2 amplification itself was infrequent, occurring in only 5.8% of cases, and did not independently predict survival in univariate analyses. This limited predictive power is likely attributable to the small sample size of cases exhibiting HER2 amplification. Conversely, gains in NEUROD2 copy number were also associated with reduced 5-year survival (p = 0.015), a trend mirrored by whole 17q arm gain (p = 0.010). When subjected to multivariate Cox regression, NEUROD2 copy number gains emerged as an independent prognostic factor, alongside tumor size. While NEUROD2 encodes a protein crucial for neuronal differentiation and cell fate, it is improbable that NEUROD2 itself directly influences breast cancer prognosis. Instead, it is more plausible that other genes within the same amplicon drive this prognostic effect. Potential candidate genes in close proximity to NEUROD2 include MED1, MED24, and DARPP-32. MED1, a subunit of the Mediator/TRAP co-activator complex, is a vital ERα co-activator. The MED1/MED24 complex has been observed to be frequently over-expressed in FBC, contributing to breast cancer cell growth via the RAS-MAPK pathway. Research also suggests MED1's presence within the HER2 amplicon and its potential role in HER2-mediated tamoxifen resistance. The DARPP-32 gene, another neighboring gene, encodes a Dopamine and cAMP-regulated phosphoprotein. Its over-expression has been implicated in resistance to TRAIL receptor-targeted cancer therapies. Further investigation into the copy number status and prognostic significance of these adjacent genes in MBC is warranted.
The second identified amplicon includes the RPS6KB1 gene. This gene, which codes for a ribosomal protein S6 kinase, showed copy number gains in 30% of male breast cancer cases in this study. In female breast cancer, RPS6KB1 has been previously reported to be amplified and over-expressed in 10-30% of cases. Its protein product, involved in protein synthesis, cellular growth, and proliferation via the PI3K and mTOR pathways, represents a potential therapeutic target. However, the precise significance of RPS6KB1 copy number alterations in MBC requires further investigation.
TOP2A, a nuclear protein crucial for DNA replication and mitosis, is the primary target of anthracycline-based chemotherapy. While frequently amplified in FBC, its prognostic significance in FBC remains debated. In MBC, we observed a lower rate of TOP2A copy number gain (15%) compared to FBC (27%). Furthermore, TOP2A gain or amplification appears to lack prognostic value in MBC.
Our current study found PPM1D amplification in a small fraction (2.9%) of MBC cases, with gains observed in 16.5%. While PPM1D gain tended to correlate with high tumor grade and mitotic count, it did not predict survival in MBC, consistent with previous FBC findings. This contrasts with its frequent amplification (25%) in FBC.
Our study concludes that male breast cancer (MBC) exhibits distinct chromosomal alterations on chromosome 17. Specifically, we observed frequent copy number gains on the long arm (17q), featuring two unique amplified regions, alongside copy number losses on the short arm (17p). Consistent with findings in female breast cancer (FBC), no instances of whole chromosome 17 polysomy were detected in MBC. Compared to FBC, MBC displayed a less intricate arrangement of chromosome 17 and fewer overall copy number changes. This suggests that chromosome 17 plays differing roles in the development of breast cancer in males versus females. We found that a complete gain of the 17q arm correlated with HER2 copy number gain. Both HER2 and NEUROD2 copy number gains were linked to aggressive tumor characteristics, including high grade, increased mitotic activity, and reduced five-year survival. While NEUROD2 copy number gain emerged as an independent prognostic indicator, it is improbable that NEUROD2 itself drives this amplified region. Future investigations should focus on evaluating copy number changes in adjacent genes to understand their potential prognostic and therapeutic significance in MBC.
