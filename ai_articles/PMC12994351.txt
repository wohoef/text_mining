While mitotic count is a well-established prognostic indicator for invasive breast cancer generally, its utility for triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC) has received limited research attention. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of absolute mitotic counts in TNBC, employing both manual and deep learning methodologies. A retrospective cohort of 298 TNBC patients was analyzed. Manual mitotic counts were determined by averaging the assessments of three independent observers. For automated analysis, a convolutional neural network was applied to digitized H&E slides. Baseline prognostic models for relapse-free survival and overall survival were established using multivariable Cox regression. These models were then augmented with dichotomized mitotic counts, testing all potential cut-off values, and their performance was assessed using the c-statistic. The findings revealed that averaged manual mitotic counts per 2 mm² varied from 1 to 187 (mean 37.6, SD 23.4), while automatic counts ranged from 1 to 269 (mean 57.6; SD 42.2). Crucially, no mitotic count cut-off, whether from manual or automatic assessment, enhanced the predictive power (c-statistic) of the baseline models. Consequently, the study concluded that mitotic count, as a measure of proliferation, does not serve as a prognostic factor for TNBC, and its use in TNBC patient management is not recommended.

Computer algorithms, developed through recent machine learning advancements, can now analyze whole slide images (WSI) of microscopic tissue sections. These algorithms have demonstrated capabilities in accurately identifying and outlining tumor regions in breast and colon tissues, as well as detecting mitotic figures in breast cancer [1–3]. Beyond their immediate application in research and clinical settings, these algorithms are valuable for re-evaluating established morphological criteria for diagnosis and prognosis. Their ability to perform highly reproducible, automated analysis on numerous tissue sections facilitates the development of 'evidence-based' pathology. This study specifically employed deep learning to assess the prognostic significance of mitosis counting in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).
Triple-negative breast cancers (TNBCs) account for approximately 15% of all breast tumors. These cancers are defined by the lack of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression, as well as no overexpression of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [4]. Patients with TNBCs tend to be younger and face a poorer prognosis compared to those with hormone receptor-positive breast cancers [5]. Standard pathology practice involves histological grading for all newly diagnosed invasive breast tumors [6–8]. This grading, typically using the modified Bloom and Richardson system [9, 10], categorizes tumors into three tiers. The overall histological grade is determined by combining microscopic scores for nuclear pleomorphism, tubule formation, and mitotic count within a 2 mm² area. Grade 1 tumors resemble normal breast tissue, while Grade 3 tumors show the most significant deviation [9, 10].
Triple-negative breast cancers (TNBCs) predominantly present as histological grade 3, with grade 1 cases being uncommon [11–14]. Despite this, the prognostic utility of histological grade for TNBC has been questioned [11, 15]. TNBC tumors exhibit a broad spectrum of mitotic counts [16, 17], frequently surpassing the threshold for grade 3 in the modified Bloom and Richardson system. Consequently, these tumors are often categorized into the highest mitotic count class, obscuring the extensive variability in their absolute mitotic counts. Currently, there's a lack of research on the prognostic significance of mitotic counts specifically in TNBC. Given the wide range of mitotic figures observed in TNBC, it's uncertain if the existing Bloom and Richardson cut-off values are appropriate, or if TNBC-specific thresholds would be more effective. This study employs deep learning to thoroughly analyze absolute mitotic counts, even when cell densities are exceptionally high.
Building upon our prior work that established histological subtype as an independent prognostic factor for TNBC within a multivariable model [15], this study utilized that same model as a foundation. Our aim was to evaluate whether mitotic count could offer additional prognostic insights for TNBC. Beyond the standard 'grade 3' cutoff from the modified Bloom and Richardson system, we explored the prognostic utility of various other mitotic count thresholds. To ensure objectivity in identifying these alternative cut-off values, we employed both traditional manual assessments, averaged from three independent pathologists, and advanced deep learning techniques. Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs), when integrated with whole slide imaging, have demonstrated significant efficacy in routine pathological tasks like detecting metastases in breast cancer sentinel lymph nodes [19] and identifying mitoses [1].
A retrospective, multicenter cohort of 597 Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC) patients, previously assembled, forms the basis of this study. These patients received their TNBC diagnosis between 2006 and 2014 in the Eastern Netherlands, sourced from both an academic hospital (Radboudumc, Nijmegen) and several general hospitals (Canisius Wilhelmina Hospital, Jeroen Bosch Hospital, Bernhoven Hospital, Hospital Pantein). Data for this cohort was initially gathered from the Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Registry (IKNL), a national database documenting all malignancies. Excluded from the study were patients presenting with stage IV disease or those who had undergone neoadjuvant therapy. For each tumor, a single representative tissue block was chosen after examining archival sections to confirm tumor presence and the transition zone between tumor and normal tissue (the "invasive margin"). Subsequently, a new H&E stained slide was prepared from each chosen block at the Radboudumc pathology department following standard procedures. Central histopathological review, adhering to current guidelines, was performed on all tumors to confirm histological subtype and grade.
Patient follow-up and clinical information were gathered from two sources: the Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Registry provided data for overall survival (OS), while local patient records supplied relapse-free survival (RFS) information. RFS was specifically defined as the time from initial TNBC diagnosis (via core needle biopsy or fine needle aspiration) to the first clinical or pathological detection of TNBC recurrence. Importantly, any subsequent diagnosis of hormone receptor-positive or HER2-positive breast cancer was classified as a new primary tumor, not a recurrence. For patients without recurrence, data was censored at their last follow-up date. OS, conversely, represented the duration from TNBC diagnosis until death or the final follow-up. The study adhered to the REMARK guidelines for prognostic marker reporting and the STARD guideline for diagnostic accuracy.
The Radboudumc's institutional review board granted a waiver for ethical approval (case number 2015–1711). All patient data and materials were handled in accordance with the Code of Conduct for the Use of Data in Health Research [24] and the Code of Conduct for responsible use of human tissue in health research [25].
Three independent observers, including a pathology resident (MCAB) and two pathologists specializing in breast cancer (WV and PCC), visually counted absolute mitotic figures for each tumor. They exhaustively assessed a 2 mm2 area, chosen based on the modified Bloom and Richardson grading system. The raw mitotic count was recorded, not categorized into predefined classes. All observers were unaware of clinical, pathological, or other observers' scores.
To automate mitosis counting, a deep learning algorithm, previously detailed in reference [1], was employed. This algorithm's training involved 18 tumors from the TNBC cohort. For each of these tumors, an extra H&E slide was prepared and scanned at a resolution of 0.25 μm/pixel using a Pannoramic 250 Flash II slide scanner. Following this, the H&E slides were destained and then re-stained with an anti-phosphorylated histone H3 (PHH3) antibody, which specifically highlights mitotic figures. These PHH3-stained slides were subsequently scanned with the identical scanner and settings. The resulting H&E and PHH3 whole slide images (WSIs) were then co-registered, ensuring pixel-level alignment between the two image sets. This meticulous alignment process enabled the precise identification of PHH3-positive cells within the H&E sections, as illustrated in Figure 1. The extensive collection of mitotic figure images generated through this destaining and restaining method served as a comprehensive training dataset. This dataset was crucial for developing a highly advanced algorithm capable of accurately detecting mitoses in H&E-stained slides, addressing the typical requirement of deep learning algorithms for large, labeled example sets.
For automated mitosis counting, the deep learning algorithm was applied to the H&E whole slide images (WSIs) of the TNBC cohort. These slides were scanned with the identical Pannoramic 250 Flash II slide scanner and settings used during the algorithm's training phase. The algorithm's function was to automatically identify all mitotic figures within these WSIs, as illustrated in Figure 2a and 2b. To replicate the manual counting process, the system was designed to calculate mitotic density within 2 mm² circular areas across the entire WSI. From all possible locations, the algorithm automatically pinpointed and selected the region exhibiting the highest concentration of mitotic figures (Figure 2b, c). The total count of mitotic figures within this automatically identified "hotspot" was then recorded as the automatic mitotic count (AMC). Figure 2c further details a high-magnification view of a selected area within such a hotspot, highlighting detected mitotic figures, though it also notes instances where the algorithm missed some (indicated by blue arrows).
The algorithm's inability to distinguish between benign and malignant mitotic figures sometimes resulted in the 2 mm² hotspot appearing outside the invasive tumor area, particularly in cases with low mitotic density (52 out of 597 tumors). To address this, a pathology resident (MCAB) manually outlined the tumor boundaries. The algorithm was then re-applied, ensuring the hotspot was located within the defined tumor region. All automatically identified mitotic figures and the 2 mm² circle were visually superimposed on the H&E whole slide images for review.
To determine the necessary sample size for manual mitotic counting, a power analysis was conducted, acknowledging the labor-intensive nature of visual mitosis assessment. Given the absence of existing literature on the prognostic utility of mitotic counts specifically in TNBC, the power calculations drew upon studies that reported hazard ratios (HRs) for mitotic counts within the broader breast cancer population [27–30]. These studies indicated a range of HRs between 1.5 and over 8. For TNBC, it was hypothesized that the mitotic count's HR would fall within the lower end of this spectrum, leading to an assumed HR of 2.5 for the power analyses. With an alpha significance level set at 0.05 and a desired power of 80%, the required sample size varied depending on the outcome. For a 20% risk of recurrence within five years (which was 19.6% in the current cohort), a sample size of 292 was needed to detect an HR of 2.5. When considering an overall five-year mortality risk of 25% (25.1% in the present cohort), the required sample size was 200. Based on these calculations, 50% of the previously established cohort (n = 298) [15] was chosen for this study. This selection involved ranking all patients by their incidence date (diagnosis date of TNBC via histology or cytology) and then including every second patient. A comparison of selected versus unselected cases was performed using cross-tabulation to assess the distribution of variables of interest and event numbers. No significant differences were found (p > 0.05; data not shown) using the Pearson Chi-Square test. Similarly, independent sample T-tests confirmed no significant differences (p > 0.05; data not shown) in mean time to events between the selected and unselected groups. Interobserver variability among the human observers, and between the convolutional neural network (CNN) and the observers, was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). A 2-way random-effects model was employed to test for absolute agreement, with reliability calculated from a single measure, corresponding to ICC(2,1) as per the Shrout and Fleiss convention [31]. For each tumor, the mean manual mitotic count (MMC) was derived by averaging the counts from the three observers. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was then utilized to evaluate the prognostic value across various cut-off values for both MMC and automatic mitotic count (AMC), with relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) serving as the primary endpoints. The baseline prognostic models for TNBC RFS and OS, developed in the researchers' prior study [15], were used. These baseline models incorporated variables such as age, primary tumor stage, regional lymph node stage, histological subtype, primary surgical treatment, adjuvant systemic therapy, and adjuvant radiotherapy. To investigate whether the prognostic value of the mitotic count varied with other causal variables, interaction terms between mitotic counts and available clinicopathological variables for RFS and OS were computed. Cox analysis was performed by adding the dichotomized MMC and AMC as a variable to the baseline model, testing a range of different cut-off values. The lowest and highest 10% of MMC and AMC values were excluded from consideration as cut-off values. The tested range also encompassed the cut-off value of 15, which is used to differentiate between classes 2 and 3 of the modified Bloom and Richardson mitotic score [10].
To evaluate the model's performance, the c-statistic was employed, reflecting a regression model's discriminative power. This statistic was calculated using the Harrell et al. method, which is favored for long-term risk prediction studies where not all subjects experience the event. Additionally, an experiment was conducted using the automatic mitotic counts (AMC) on the entire cohort of 597 TNBC tumors, following the previously outlined procedure.
Statistical significance was determined by a p-value below 0.05, with 95% confidence intervals. All computations utilized SPSS (version 24.0) and R (version 3.5.1) software.
The characteristics of the patients and their tumors, as detailed in Table 1, reveal key demographic and clinical features of the selected cohort. Most patients, specifically 64.8%, were aged 50 years or older at the time of their diagnosis. Tumor size analysis indicated that 54.0% of the tumors measured less than 2 cm. The predominant histological classification observed was invasive carcinoma of no special type, accounting for 88.6% of cases. Regarding chemotherapy, patients received either anthracyclines alone or anthracyclines combined with taxanes. Notably, no survival advantage was identified for patients treated with taxanes in addition to anthracyclines when compared to those receiving only anthracyclines. The study also confirmed that none of the patients were administered older chemotherapy regimens such as CMF (cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and 5-fluorouracil) or platinum salts. The clinical outcomes showed that approximately one in five patients (20.1%) experienced a recurrence of TNBC, while a quarter of the patients (25.5%) died during the follow-up period. For those who developed a recurrence, the median time until a clinically detected recurrence was 28.8 months from the initial TNBC diagnosis. Following a recurrence diagnosis, the median time to TNBC-specific death was 3.7 months, with an average of 8.0 months.
The initial multivariable Cox regression analysis identified several factors associated with poorer survival outcomes. Specifically, a more advanced primary tumor stage, a higher regional lymph node stage, and the absence of adjuvant therapy were all linked to reduced survival. This analysis, detailed in Table 2, established baseline c-statistic values for two key models: the relapse-free survival (RFS) model, which had a c-statistic of 0.745, and the overall survival (OS) model, which had a c-statistic of 0.761. These c-statistic values represent the discriminative power of the respective baseline models for the selected triple-negative breast cancer cohort (n=298). The findings indicate that these established clinical and pathological variables are significant predictors of patient prognosis in this specific breast cancer subtype.
The manual mitotic count (MMC) exhibited a range from 1 to 187, with a median of 34.7, a mean of 37.6, and a standard deviation of 23.4. The consistency among the three independent observers for MMC was strong, as indicated by an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.60, with individual ICCs varying between 0.585 and 0.616. For the purpose of establishing cut-off values for MMC, the boundaries were set at 12 for the lower limit and 66 for the upper limit. In contrast, the automatic mitotic count (AMC) spanned a broader range, from 1 to 269, with a higher median of 50.5, a mean of 57.6, and a standard deviation of 42.2. The agreement between the AMC and the human observers was also assessed, with ICC values ranging from 0.497 to 0.626. For AMC, the chosen cut-off value boundaries were 12 at the lower end and 110 at the upper end. To evaluate the prognostic utility of these mitotic counts, the discriminative capacity of the models was analyzed using the c-statistic, as visually represented in Figure 3. This figure illustrates the c-statistic of the baseline prognostic model (depicted by a red line) in comparison to the c-statistic of the baseline model augmented with the mitotic count as an additional variable (shown by a green line), for both MMC (left panel) and AMC (right panel). For relapse-free survival (RFS), as presented in Figure 3a-b, the c-statistic of the baseline prognostic model remained largely unchanged even with the inclusion of the mitotic count, across all tested cut-off values. This suggests that the mitotic count, whether manual or automatic, did not enhance the predictive power of the existing RFS model. Similarly, for overall survival (OS), depicted in Figure 3c-d, the c-statistic curve for the model incorporating the mitotic count showed only minor fluctuations around the baseline model's c-statistic curve. This further supports the finding that mitotic count does not significantly improve the prognostic model for OS. To strengthen these findings, the deep learning algorithm for automatic counting was subsequently applied to the entire unselected cohort of 597 TNBC cases. The results from this expanded analysis, which utilized the automatic counts as input for the multivariable Cox regression model, similarly indicated no additional prognostic value for the mitotic count (Figure 4). This study investigated the prognostic utility of mitotic count in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Building upon a previous study that identified histological subtype as an independent prognostic factor for relapse-free survival (RFS) in TNBC, the current research utilized a pre-existing multivariable Cox regression model as a baseline. To evaluate the potential additional prognostic value of mitotic count, this baseline model was augmented by incorporating mitotic count data. The discriminative power of these expanded Cox regression models for both RFS and overall survival (OS) was then assessed across a broad spectrum of mitotic count cut-off values. The findings indicated that including mitotic count did not enhance the prognostic capability of the models for either RFS or OS, regardless of the cut-off value applied.
Triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC) are defined by the lack of ER, PR, and HER2 expression. This broad classification encompasses tumors with diverse histopathological and genomic profiles, leading to varied disease progression. Consequently, clinicians face difficulties in treatment selection due to the limited availability of established prognostic factors for TNBC. While research efforts are underway to discover immune-based and molecular biomarkers for prognosis and prediction in TNBC, the prognostic significance of proliferation in this specific subtype remains largely unexplored in existing literature. Despite TNBC tumors exhibiting high levels of proliferation at both mRNA and protein levels, a robust proliferation-based prognostic indicator for this cancer type has yet to emerge. Our prior work identified histopathological subtype as an independent prognostic factor for TNBC, potentially pinpointing a small subgroup (up to 30%) with a poorer prognosis. However, the current study demonstrates that mitotic counting, a standard component of general breast cancer grading and an established prognostic factor, offers no prognostic information for TNBC. These findings suggest a need to re-evaluate current TNBC grading practices and highlight the urgent requirement for alternative prognostic factors for this disease. This study is novel in its focus on the prognostic value of absolute mitotic counts specifically for TNBC.
While the prognostic significance of mitotic count in invasive breast cancer has been extensively investigated, research into the absolute mitotic count is less common. Traditionally, mitotic counts are categorized into three groups (low, middle, high) using cut-off values derived from the modified Bloom and Richardson grading system. Kronqvist et al. offered a different approach, analyzing a spectrum of mitotic count thresholds in a cohort of 364 breast cancer patients. Their method involved identifying an optimal cut-off by evaluating Chi-square values from log-rank tests across various patient subgroups for each threshold. Our study shares some methodological similarities with Kronqvist et al.'s work. However, a key distinction lies in our focus on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a subtype known for its significantly higher mitotic counts compared to general breast cancers. Furthermore, unlike Kronqvist et al.'s univariable analyses, our research employed a multivariable approach to account for other prognostic factors.
Mitotic figure counting is known to be subjective, influenced by a lack of standardized procedures and issues like fixation artifacts. To mitigate this subjectivity in our study, three independent observers manually counted mitoses for each tumor. Their individual counts were then averaged to create a mean mitotic count, which effectively reduced interobserver variability. Furthermore, we employed a deep learning algorithm for automated mitotic counting, offering a more objective and reproducible measurement. This automated approach was crucial, as the labor-intensive nature of manual counting for large numbers of mitoses would have limited our ability to analyze the full cohort of nearly 600 cases. The use of this deep learning algorithm in our research highlights its potential for objectively re-evaluating established histopathological characteristics across large datasets.
Deep learning is poised to extend beyond research into clinical histopathology. Algorithms like the one employed here could greatly benefit clinical practice by pre-analyzing digitized tissue sections before a pathologist begins diagnosis. The widespread adoption of whole slide imaging technology will accelerate the integration of these algorithms. Once whole slide images are processed, these systems can then help pathologists by minimizing observer bias, boosting accuracy, and improving overall efficiency. A preliminary prospective study demonstrated that pathologists, when aided by a deep learning system, achieved faster and more accurate detection of metastases in breast cancer sentinel lymph nodes.
This research offers several key advantages. The triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cohort, drawn from five hospitals over multiple years, accurately represents the varied TNBC patient population. A significant strength lies in the meticulous manual counting of absolute mitotic figures in nearly 300 tumors by three observers, a process known to be both labor-intensive and time-consuming. This effort is particularly valuable given the absence of prior studies on the prognostic utility of mitotic counts in TNBC. Furthermore, the study's findings, supported by both human observation and computer-based algorithmic assessments of mitotic counts, consistently indicate that mitotic count holds no prognostic value for TNBC. The comparable prognostic outcomes from the automated analysis reinforce this conclusion. While acknowledging the limitations inherent in a retrospective study design, the researchers diligently ensured high-quality, comprehensive follow-up data through a robust national cancer registry and thorough patient file reviews. Ultimately, by examining a broad spectrum of cutoff values, the study demonstrates that mitotic count does not enhance the prognostic capability of existing multivariable models for relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) in TNBC. These findings suggest that mitotic count lacks prognostic significance for TNBC patients. As a pioneering investigation into this specific area, these results warrant validation in independent TNBC cohorts. Additionally, the study highlights the promise of deep learning algorithms for objectively assessing histopathological features across extensive datasets.
