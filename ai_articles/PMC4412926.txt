The Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway is recognized for its significant contribution to tumor progression, and its inhibition has demonstrated potential in suppressing tumor growth. However, a key question remains regarding the optimal strategy for achieving maximum anti-tumor efficacy: is blocking the TGF-β pathway in isolation sufficient, or does the tumor microenvironment play a crucial, potentially synergistic, role? To address this, our study investigated the impact of blocking TGF-β signaling within the context of the tumor's microenvironment. We utilized a panel of patient-derived xenografts (PDX) and treated them with galunisertib, a selective TGF-β receptor kinase inhibitor. Both in vitro and in vivo effects were subsequently evaluated. In vitro clonogenic assays revealed that galunisertib inhibited growth in only 6.3% (5 out of 79) of the tested PDX. Further analysis of gene expression profiles, encompassing both canonical and non-canonical TGF-β signaling pathways, indicated that the majority of PDX displayed alterations in TGF-β downstream signaling. Subsequently, 13 of these PDX were assessed in vivo, where distinct response patterns emerged. These findings suggest that factors beyond intrinsic tumor characteristics, specifically extrinsic or microenvironmental influences, can impact the response to galunisertib. Notably, pSMAD2 protein expression and TGF-βRI mRNA expression levels were found to correlate with the in vivo outcomes of galunisertib treatment. Based on these results, we conclude that intrinsic, tumor-specific TGF-β signaling alone does not fully account for the anti-tumor effects of galunisertib. Therefore, in vivo xenograft models, which better recapitulate the tumor microenvironment, may be more suitable than in vitro clonogenic assays for evaluating the anti-tumor activity of TGF-β inhibitors like galunisertib.

The TGF-β signaling pathway exerts diverse effects in both healthy and cancerous tissues, crucially influencing tumor-stroma interactions. Its canonical activation begins with a ligand (TGF-β1, TGF-β2, or TGF-β3) binding to TGF-β receptor II (TGF-βRII). This triggers heterodimerization with TGF-β receptor I (TGF-βRI/ALK5), leading to transphosphorylation of their kinase domains. Subsequently, SMAD2 and SMAD3 are recruited and phosphorylated. This complex then initiates the SMAD-dependent cascade, culminating in nuclear translocation and gene transcription. Beyond this canonical route, less understood non-canonical pathways exist, involving signaling through JNK, p38 MAPK, ERK, or MEKK, which do not rely on SMAD proteins.
Several small molecule inhibitors targeting the TGF-βRI serine/threonine kinase activity have been developed, such as LY2157299 monohydrate (galunisertib). This compound has demonstrated the ability to inhibit pSMAD2 expression in various tumor models. Galunisertib is currently undergoing clinical trials and has recently shown anti-tumor effects in patients with glioblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma. Given the limited number of TGF-β inhibitors in clinical development, establishing appropriate preclinical models is crucial for accurately determining their mechanisms of action and guiding future drug screening efforts.
Galunisertib has demonstrated moderate anti-tumor activity in conventional models like xenografts derived from established cell lines and in vitro cell viability assays. To gain a deeper understanding, this study utilized patient-derived xenografts (PDX) instead of established cell lines. Unlike cell lines, primary patient-derived cells generally preserve their original characteristics. While initially employed for assessing cytotoxic agents, these primary cells have more recently proven valuable for evaluating the anti-tumor effects of cytostatic or immunomodulatory compounds. Furthermore, the outcomes of anti-tumor drug treatments in PDX-based clonogenic assays have shown a strong correlation with clinical responses in patients with various solid cancers. Consequently, we posited that PDX models would be suitable for investigating both the in vitro and in vivo impacts of galunisertib across diverse primary tumor cell types, thereby clarifying the contributions of both intrinsic and extrinsic TGF-β signaling activities to the observed responses.
Galunisertib, a small molecule inhibitor of TGF-βRI serine/threonine kinase activity, was supplied by Eli Lilly and Company. Initial testing involved concentrations from 0.03 to 10.0 μM. For selected tumor xenografts, higher concentrations ranging from 1.0 to 80.0 μM were subsequently employed. Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO at 3.0 or 24.0 mM and stored frozen in the dark. Immediately before use, final dilutions were made in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium.
Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) samples were obtained following informed patient consent and ethical review board approval. These samples originated from tumors grown subcutaneously in NMRI nu/nu mice, sourced from Elevage Janvier, France, or Taconic Europe, Denmark. The established protocol involved sterile removal of solid tumor xenografts from mice. These were then mechanically broken down and incubated for 45-60 minutes at 37°C in RPMI-1640 medium containing an enzyme cocktail. This mixture included collagenase type IV (41 U/ml), DNase I (125 U/ml), hyaluronidase type III (100 U/ml), and dispase II (1.0 U/ml). Subsequently, single cells were filtered through 200 and 50 μm mesh sieves and washed twice with sterile PBS. Cell viability was assessed using trypan blue exclusion staining in a hemocytometer.
To assess the effects of galunisertib, tumor clonogenic assays were conducted using a modified soft agar method, originally described by Hamburger & Salmon [23]. These assays involved a three-layered system within each test well. The bottom and top layers consisted of semi-solid medium, while the middle layer contained the supernatant with or without the test compound. The base layer was prepared using IMDM, enriched with 20% fetal calf serum (FCS), 0.01% gentamicin, and 0.75% agar. Cells were then seeded at a density ranging from 7.5 × 10⁴ to 2 × 10⁵ cells/ml in the same culture medium, but with a reduced agar concentration of 0.4%, and plated onto this bottom layer. Continuous exposure to the test compound was achieved through a drug overlay in the culture medium. Incubation of these cultures took place at 37°C with 7.5% CO₂ in a humidified environment for a period of 7 to 20 days. Colony growth was closely monitored using an inverted microscope, with colonies defined as those exceeding 50 μm in diameter. Once maximum colony formation was observed, automated counting was performed. This involved staining vital colonies for 24 hours with a sterile aqueous solution of 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-phenyltetrazolium chloride (1 mg/ml, 100 μl/well) [24] before evaluation by an automatic image analysis system (OMNICON 3600, Biosys GmbH, Germany). In parallel, colony viability, serving as a proxy for colony formation, was quantified using a CellTiter-Glo® viability assay (Promega, Madison, WI). Luminescence readings from an EnVision® Xcite Multilabel Reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) provided a measure of metabolically active and viable cells. All assays were standardized, and galunisertib's efficacy was evaluated against untreated controls containing only cells and the solvent (DMSO at 0.3%). The efficacy was categorized based on concentration-response relationships: inhibition was defined as a T/C ratio of ≤ 75%, no response as a T/C ratio between 75% and 125%, and stimulation as a T/C ratio of ≥ 125%.
PDX were derived from primary patient material, while CDX originated from in vitro cultured cells. Both types were grown subcutaneously in nude mice, serially passaged, and randomized when tumors reached 72–120 mm³.
For in vivo studies, galunisertib was formulated as a suspension containing 1% NaCMC, 0.5% SLS, 0.05% Antifoam, and 0.085% PVP C-30. This suspension was administered orally via gavage twice daily for 14 days at a dosage of 75 mg/kg to groups of 12 mice. A control group received the same vehicle without galunisertib. Tumor growth was monitored twice weekly using caliper measurements. The absolute tumor volume, calculated using the formula a × b² × 0.5 (where 'a' is the largest diameter and 'b' is the perpendicular diameter), served as the basis for further analysis. Relative tumor volume on any given day 'x' (RTVx) was determined by dividing the absolute tumor volume on that day by the initial tumor volume at randomization (day 0), expressed as a percentage: RTVx [%] = (Tx/T0) × 100. Tumor growth curves were generated using median RTVs for each group to evaluate treatment efficacy. Quantitative assessment of tumor growth response was achieved by calculating the Area Between the Curves (ABC) percentage, which compared the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the control group's tumor growth to that of the treated group: ABC (%) = [(AUCcontrol - AUCtreatment) / AUCcontrol] × 100.
Positive ABC values signify tumor growth curves below the control, indicating inhibition. Responses were categorized: < -20% ABC meant growth stimulation, -20% to 20% indicated no change, and > 20% represented growth inhibition compared to the control. All animal studies adhered to German welfare regulations.
To analyze gene expression, total RNA was extracted from frozen tissues pooled from four untreated mice per xenograft model. The mirVana miRNA Isolation kit was used for extraction, and genomic DNA was removed with an RNase-free DNase Set. RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer, and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 6.5 were selected for subsequent gene expression profiling.
DNA was isolated from snap-frozen, untreated tumor samples. Initially, proteinase K digested the samples overnight at 55°C, followed by RNase A digestion of the resulting lysates. Subsequently, DNA was extracted using a phenol:chloroform:isoamylalcohol mixture and precipitated with ethanol. The DNA pellets were washed and then resuspended in TElow buffer. Finally, DNA integrity was assessed via 1.3% agarose gel electrophoresis, and purity was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 system.
Gene expression profiling was conducted on total RNA samples using Affymetrix HGU133 plus 2.0 arrays. These analyses were performed by AROS Applied Biotechnologies or DNA vision, adhering to Affymetrix protocols for sample preparation, including first- and second-strand synthesis, biotin labeling, fragmentation, and hybridization. Data evaluation and normalization were carried out using the "R" statistical computing environment (version 2.15.3). Specifically, the gcRMA method from Bioconductor was employed for normalization to generate summary expression values for each probe set. A single probe set per gene was selected based on the methodology described by Li et al. [25]. Gene expression levels were analyzed logarithmically and reported in arbitrary units (U), with values below 6 U considered background noise.
To determine the mutation status of critical cancer genes, mass array sequencing panels from Sequenom, Inc. (OncoCarta panels I, II, and III) were employed across all samples. These findings were subsequently verified through Sanger sequencing of individual exons or whole exome sequencing. In addition, whole exome sequencing was performed on 64 out of 79 patient-derived xenograft (PDX) samples. For this process, exonic regions from Oncotest DNA samples were targeted using Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon kits (38 MB for 60 samples, 51 MB for 4 samples). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq-2000 platform, generating 100 bp paired-end (PE) reads with an anticipated coverage of approximately 80×. To eliminate mouse stroma contamination, PE reads that aligned more strongly to the mouse genome (mm10) than the human genome (hg19) were excluded from the human-aligned read dataset. This filtering was based on the Burrows-Wheeler Alignment mapping score and executed using PicardTools. Variant detection was carried out independently by three distinct variant callers: GATK’s UnifiedGenotyper, the combined Samtools mpileup and bcftools caller, and Freebayes. Only variants identified by all three tools, supported by at least 3 variant-supporting reads and exhibiting a minimum variant frequency of 5%, were subjected to further analysis.
The Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.0 was utilized, featuring 1.8 million genetic markers. This included over 906,600 SNPs and more than 946,000 probes for copy number variation detection. Standard manufacturer protocols were followed. Affymetrix guidelines dictated contrast Quality Control (QC) thresholds above 0.4 and Median Absolute Pairwise Difference (MAPD) thresholds above 0.35.
Copy number variations were identified using Affymetrix Genotyping Console™ v4.1 and PICNIC software from the Welcome Trust Sanger Institute.
To prepare native tumor lysates for Western blotting, untreated patient-derived xenograft (PDX) samples were homogenized. This process involved using a Tissue Lyser (Qiagen) and a cell lysis buffer. The buffer composition included Tris pH7.4, NaCl, EDTA, NP40, deoxycholate, Na4O7P2 10H2O, Na3VO4, NaF, PMSF, Benzonase, and a protease inhibitor mix. Following homogenization, the lysates were clarified, and their protein concentrations were determined using a Protein Assay (Bio-Rad). Subsequently, the protein samples underwent polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and membrane transfer. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies. These antibodies targeted TGF-β1 (clone 56E4), Smad2 (clone D43B4), phosphorylated Smad2 (pSmad2, ser465/467, clone 138D4), and GAPDH, which served as a loading control. Antibody binding was detected using a secondary HRP-coupled antibody. This was followed by incubation with an ECL Western blotting detection reagent and visualization with a GE Healthcare Image Quant LAS 4000 CCD camera system. Protein expression quantification was achieved by subtracting the GAPDH signal intensity from the intensity of the protein of interest. This analysis was performed using Image J software, with the resulting protein quantities expressed in arbitrary units (AU). To account for variations between experimental plates, an Oncotest tumor lysate pool, comprising a mixture of 380 different PDX lysates, was utilized as a normalization control.
The TGF-β inhibitor galunisertib underwent evaluation in various patient-derived xenografts (PDX) and cell line-derived xenografts (CDX) through a series of clonogenic assays. This testing panel encompassed eleven distinct tumor types, including glioblastoma, melanoma, colon, gastric, liver, non-small cell lung cancers (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell lung carcinoma), small cell lung cancer, mammary, ovarian, pancreatic, and renal cell carcinomas, as detailed in Table 1. Additionally, established cell lines derived from hematologic malignancies, such as leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma, were included. Initial screening of diverse tumor types is a common practice to ascertain the spectrum of anti-tumor efficacy for compounds and to understand their differential activities. The majority of PDX models utilized in this study originated from metastatic tumor lesions (53% or 42 out of 79) and were characterized by poor differentiation. Predominantly, the tumors were sourced from male patients (58% or 46 out of 79), with the average patient age being 52 years (median age 55 years, ranging from 11 to 82 years). Anti-tumor effects were quantified by measuring the inhibition of colony formation relative to untreated controls, expressed as T/C values. While concentrations exceeding 10 μM are generally associated with non-specific activity of galunisertib, higher concentrations were employed in this study to explore anti-tumor effects beyond the pharmacologically targeted range.
In initial experiments, galunisertib's effectiveness was evaluated across a panel of 66 tumor models using a clonogenic assay. When tested at concentrations up to 10 μM, the compound demonstrated inhibitory effects on colony growth in only one sample (1.5%). The majority of samples showed no response (75.8%), while a significant portion exhibited growth stimulation (22.7%). Further investigation with a subset of 24 samples, tested at higher concentrations up to 80 μM, revealed a slightly increased inhibition rate in 20.8% of cases. However, a substantial number of these samples still displayed growth stimulation (41.7%) or no response (37.5%). The most responsive xenograft samples included CXF 742 (colon cancer), LXFS 650 and LXFE 1422 (small cell lung cancer), and the hematologic xenografts LYXF MYLA (T-ALL) and LYXF RAJI (Burkitt’s lymphoma). Notably, no correlation was found between the observed responses and the histopathological characteristics of the different samples. The efficacy of galunisertib was categorized based on concentration-response as inhibition (T/C ≤ 75%), no response (75% < T/C < 125%), or stimulation (T/C ≥ 125%).
Galunisertib was further evaluated in a panel of melanoma patient-derived xenografts (PDX), as prior research indicated TGF-β1 signaling plays an autocrine role in melanoma growth. In these experiments, galunisertib demonstrated no inhibitory effects. Up to a concentration of 10 μM, 70.6% of the melanoma PDX samples showed no response, while 29.4% exhibited stimulated colony formation. Even at a higher concentration of 30 μM, 64.7% of the samples still showed no response, and 35.3% displayed stimulated colony formation.
Given the limited success of galunisertib in clonogenic assays, our next step was to examine the canonical TGF-β signaling pathway within these PDX models. We analyzed the expression and mutation status of genes involved in both canonical pathways, such as TGF-β1, TGF-β2, SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD4, SMAD7, TGF-βRI, and TGF-βRII, and non-canonical pathways, including MAPK and AKT. This investigation aimed to characterize the molecular profiles of these pathways and determine if specific genes could predict drug sensitivity. Additionally, we examined the expression of TP53 and MED12, proteins previously linked to TGF-β-mediated drug resistance.
In an analysis of 70 out of 79 patient-derived xenograft (PDX) samples, gene copy numbers for TGF-β1, TGF-β2, TGF-βR1, and TGF-βR2 were examined. No significant rearrangements were detected. Mutations in TGF-β1 were identified in two samples (CXF 260, A350V and MEXF 989, S138L), and in TGF-β2 in two others (CXF 269, S365R, and CXF 260, P387H). Three samples harbored mutations in TGF-βR1 (MAXF 401, E242D; CXF 260, A125V; and LXFA 737, E111K), all of which were found in non-responding tumors. TGF-βR2 mutations occurred in four samples: one from the growth-inhibited group (LXFE 1422), one from the stimulated group (LXFA 1041), and two from the no-response group (LXFA 526 and CXF 1103). Despite these findings, no correlation was established between the mutation status of these genes and the response to galunisertib.
To further explore the TGF-β pathway's role, we examined the mRNA expression of TGF-β1, TGF-β2, TGF-βR1, and TGF-βR2 genes in 77 out of 79 patient-derived xenografts (PDX) (Figure 2a). A wide range of expression levels was observed for these four genes across the tested samples. Crucially, no significant correlation was found between these mRNA expression levels and the observed responses to galunisertib. Despite this variability, some consistent patterns emerged. The expression of TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 genes was generally low or undetectable in most samples. In contrast, the TGF-βR1 gene demonstrated robust expression in the majority of PDX samples. However, the TGF-βR2 gene showed low or undetectable expression in a notable subset of these samples. Figure 2a visually represents these mRNA expression patterns, with PDX samples ordered according to their response to galunisertib: inhibited (green), no response (yellow), or stimulated (pink). Grey bars highlight PDX samples with mutations in the gene of interest. Expression levels below 6 were considered background and are not depicted.
The study investigated the SMAD-dependent activation of the TGF-β1 pathway. Gene copy number alterations were largely absent, with the exceptions of SMAD3 deletion in LXFA 749 and SMAD4 deletion in LIXF 575, both from the "stimulated" group. Sequence analysis identified mutations in SMAD1 (CXF 260, A262V) and SMAD2 (LXFE 397, S287C), but no SMAD3 mutations were found. SMAD4 exhibited the highest mutation frequency, with nine mutations occurring in samples categorized as "no response" (seven instances) and "stimulated" (two instances). SMAD6 mutations were present in three samples from the "inhibited" and "no response" groups. SMAD7 was mutated in CXF 260 and MAXF 449. While all SMAD genes displayed varied expression patterns, these did not correlate with galunisertib response. Notably, SMAD7 expression was minimal across most samples, whereas SMAD2, SMAD3, and SMAD4 showed comparable expression levels and ranges, albeit with inconsistent patterns.
Further investigation focused on non-canonical, SMAD-independent genes through genomic and transcriptomic analyses. Regarding the AKT1 gene, no significant genomic alterations were found. AKT1 mRNA was detected in the majority of samples, with expression levels ranging from 5.5 to 11 Units and a mean of 7.9 Units. This expression did not show a significant association with galunisertib response. The MAPK1 gene was expressed in 30% of the samples examined, and its expression levels were not found to correlate with galunisertib responses. Additionally, E-cadherin (CDH1) expression was observed in most of the tested samples.
To further explore drug resistance mechanisms linked to TGF-β signaling, we examined specific genes (Fig. 3). While no MED12 gene copy number gains were observed, 21 samples showed a loss of one MED12 copy without impacting mRNA expression. MED12 mutations occurred in 8 samples, with 6 showing undetectable mRNA levels. Crucially, none of these MED12 alterations correlated with galunisertib response. Additionally, TP53 mutations were common across most samples, but these also failed to demonstrate a significant association with galunisertib efficacy, as indicated by a Kruskal-Wallis p-value of 0.13.
To confirm the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway in PDX samples prior to treatment, we examined pSMAD2 protein expression. Our findings indicated that pSMAD2 was present in the majority of tested samples. Interestingly, this expression was sometimes detected even when TGF-βR1 protein levels were low or absent. Western blot analysis, utilizing GAPDH as a loading control and a mixed lysate pool for normalization, revealed TGF-β1, SMAD2, and pSMAD2 protein levels across 79 untreated PDX samples.
Thirteen of the 79 samples were chosen for in vivo evaluation. Galunisertib's effectiveness was determined using Area Between the Curves (ABC) calculations. These calculations revealed ABC values ranging from -23.63% (indicating tumor growth stimulation) to 28.32% (indicating tumor growth inhibition) (Figure 5a). The mRNA expression levels of TGF-β pathway genes were then compared against these ABC values (Figure 5a and b). Out of the 13 selected samples, two (15.4%), specifically lung cancer PDX LXFA 737 and prostate cancer PDX PRXF MRIH 1579, demonstrated tumor growth inhibition. The majority of samples, 8 out of 13 (61.5%), showed no significant change in tumor growth. Conversely, 3 out of 13 samples (23.1%) exhibited tumor growth stimulation. Subsequently, the study investigated potential associations between these observed in vivo responses and the concurrent expression levels of TGF-β1 and SMAD2 genes, as well as the activation status of the TGF-β signaling pathway, measured by pSMAD2 protein levels (Figure 5a and c). While TGF-β1 and SMAD2 gene expression levels did not correlate with the in vivo response, elevated TGF-βRI transcript levels and pSMAD2 protein levels were found to be associated with in vivo anti-tumor responses. This correlation was statistically significant, with Spearman correlation coefficients of 0.67 (p-value = 0.017) for TGF-βRI mRNA and 0.68 (p-value = 0.025) for pSMAD2 protein.
This study revealed discrepancies between the in vitro and in vivo anti-tumor effects of galunisertib, a TGF-β receptor kinase inhibitor. Notably, galunisertib did not demonstrate broad efficacy across the tested tumor panel, a characteristic that distinguishes it from many cytotoxic anti-cancer agents. These findings, considered unusual for anti-cancer drugs, coupled with galunisertib's observed activity in patients, led to the hypothesis that the tumor microenvironment plays a crucial role in supporting TGF-β-dependent tumor growth. Several factors may explain these observations. Firstly, the effectiveness of galunisertib in tumor clonogenic assays could be influenced by its selectivity. Galunisertib is known to selectively inhibit TGF-βRI (ALK5) at concentrations up to 1 μM. Higher concentrations, exceeding 10 μM, are generally not administered to humans due to potential toxicity. However, in this study, concentrations as high as 80 μM were employed to explore effects beyond the pharmacologically relevant range. Crucially, no significant differences in efficacy were observed between the lower and higher concentrations, suggesting that drug selectivity had minimal impact. Only five patient-derived xenografts (PDX) exhibited minor inhibitory effects, and these were not linked to any specific tumor type. The majority of other tumors, including melanomas—which are thought to rely on a TGF-β-dependent autocrine loop for growth—did not respond to galunisertib. For the purpose of this study, galunisertib responses were categorized as "inhibited," "no response," or "stimulated." Secondly, to understand why most PDX showed either "no response" or a "stimulated" response in vivo, we investigated the integrity of the canonical or non-canonical TGF-β1 signaling pathways. Canonical, SMAD-dependent TGF-β signaling was found to be impaired in approximately 30% of the PDX samples. This finding suggests that galunisertib might have interacted with unintended kinases, leading to non-specific activity in the clonogenic assays. While TGF-βR1 and TGF-βR2 transcripts were detected in most PDX samples, TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 transcripts were present at low levels and in only half of the samples. In such cases, autocrine loops might still be active despite low TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 levels. The SMAD1, SMAD2, and SMAD4 genes were all expressed, with SMAD4 being the most frequently mutated gene, while others were rarely affected. Notably, inhibitory effects of galunisertib were observed in samples expressing both TGF-βR1 and SMAD4 genes. Therefore, the observed responses could not be definitively attributed to a specific or intact signaling pathway, whether canonical or non-canonical. Thirdly, the clonogenic assay might underestimate the anti-tumor activity of galunisertib or other agents targeting the tumor microenvironment. Such agents may not induce cell death but rather arrest tumor progression by inhibiting cell migration and invasion. It is also plausible that galunisertib targets non-tumor cell-dependent signals, such as immune or other microenvironmental signals. This has been demonstrated in a syngeneic mouse model where a monoclonal antibody against TGF-βRII exhibited anti-tumor activity, which was dependent on the presence of specific immune cells like CD8-positive T-cells and NK cells. We also evaluated galunisertib in vivo using 13 PDX samples. Two of these samples (LXFA 737 and PRXF MRIH 1579) demonstrated growth inhibition. Given the presence of residual NK cell functions in nude mice, it is hypothesized that in these two models, blocking TGF-β-dependent signaling facilitated NK cell activity against tumor cells. Furthermore, the investigated PDX samples contained varying proportions of human tumor cells and murine stroma components, which can include a fibroblast-rich microenvironment or genomically unstable tumor cells. Although the amount of stroma did not directly correlate with in vivo response rates, the presence of stroma influenced the overall response rate. Importantly, the in vivo response patterns showed a stronger correlation with the molecular characteristics of the PDX models, specifically pSMAD protein and TGF-βR1 mRNA expression. In conclusion, models that accurately reflect normal physiological conditions, encompassing all aspects of the microenvironment, appear to be more suitable for assessing TGF-β1 inhibitors. In the absence of such comprehensive models, the results from experiments must be interpreted with caution.
To accurately assess TGF-β1 inhibitors, models mirroring normal physiological conditions and the complete microenvironment are more suitable. Without such models, experimental results should be interpreted cautiously.
