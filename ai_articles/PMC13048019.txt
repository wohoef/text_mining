Neuropathic pain (NeP) frequently goes unrecognized, leading to inadequate pain management. To address this, the 10-item Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4-T) questionnaire, a Taiwanese version, was developed. Its purpose was to identify patients with NeP within a diverse group experiencing pain. To achieve this, a prospective, nonrandomized, multicenter study was carried out across the Neurology Departments of four Taiwanese medical centers. The study aimed to develop and validate the DN4-T questionnaire as a diagnostic instrument for NeP. Participants who had experienced pain for over 30 days were categorized into neuropathic, nociceptive, or mixed pain groups. Both patients and physicians completed the DN4-T questionnaire. The assessment of DN4-T scores involved calculating an optimal cut-off score using a receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve. Sensitivity, specificity, and reliability, determined by the Cronbach alpha coefficient, were also statistically evaluated. The study included 318 patients who completed the DN4-T questionnaire. Of these, 189 were diagnosed with NeP, seven with mixed pain, and 122 with nociceptive pain. For analytical purposes, patients were grouped into either NeP (combining neuropathic and mixed pain) or non-neuropathic (nociceptive) pain (non-NeP). An optimal DN4-T cut-off score of ≥3, derived from a maximum c index value of 1.54, was identified. At this threshold, the DN4-T scores demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.77 and a specificity of 0.78 for predicting NeP. The questionnaire's predictive power for diagnosing NeP was 0.83, as indicated by the area under the ROC curve. Furthermore, the DN4-T scores were significantly predictive of pain type (p < 0.0001), showing a concordance of 0.785 and discordance of 0.129. A Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.7 suggested the DN4-T questionnaire's utility as a predictive tool for NeP diagnosis. In conclusion, the DN4-T questionnaire has been reliably translated into Mandarin Chinese and can serve as a diagnostic tool for NeP when used alongside clinical evaluation.

Chronic pain, affecting 5% to 8% globally, is a severe condition requiring precise diagnosis for effective treatment. It can manifest as a symptom of various debilitating diseases, including diabetes, arthritis, and fibromyalgia. Patients experience pain categorized into three main types: neuropathic, nociceptive, or mixed. Neuropathic pain (NeP) stems from damage or disease within the nervous system's somatosensory pathways. This differs from nociceptive pain, which arises from signals transmitted from injured tissues like those in fractures or inflammation. Mixed pain, conversely, involves a combination of both neuropathic and nociceptive components, where the neuropathic element can trigger inflammatory processes that lead to nociceptive pain.
Currently, no definitive test exists for identifying neuropathic pain (NeP). Clinicians rely on patient history, physical and neurological exams, and imaging or electrodiagnostic results. NeP is often under-recognized and undertreated, highlighting a need for improved identification. Accurate diagnosis of pain types is crucial for administering targeted treatments.
Several pain assessment questionnaires exist to aid clinicians in identifying neuropathic pain (NeP), offering varying sensitivity and specificity. As pain is subjective, questionnaires relying on patient descriptions can serve as valuable diagnostic tools. These instruments, including LANSS, NPQ, Pain DETECT, ID-Pain, STEP, McGill Pain Questionnaire, and Brief Pain Inventory, empower specialists, general practitioners, and nurses to distinguish NeP from non-neuropathic pain.
Developed in 2005 by the French Neuropathic Pain Group, the 10-item Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4) questionnaire was designed for use alongside a clinician. It assesses patients experiencing chronic pain linked to neurological issues or somatic tissue damage. The questionnaire comprises seven questions about sensory experiences, including burning, painful cold, electric shock, tingling, pins and needles, numbness, and itching. Additionally, it includes three components derived from a physical examination of the painful area: tactile hypesthesia, pinprick hypesthesia, and allodynia. The DN4 demonstrated effectiveness in distinguishing neuropathic pain (NeP) from nociceptive pain within a French-speaking population. However, for global applicability, the questionnaire requires standardized translation processes that account for cultural adaptation, psychometric validation, and local pain terminology. Consequently, the DN4 has undergone cultural adaptation and translation into numerous languages. A meta-analysis of NeP screening tools identified the DN4 as the most appropriate for clinical NeP evaluation.
This study aimed to develop and validate the Taiwanese DN4-T questionnaire for diagnosing neuropathic pain (NeP) in a mixed pain population across four Taiwanese medical centers.
A prospective, nonrandomized, multicenter study was conducted in four Taiwanese neurology departments. Its purpose was to assess the validity of an interview-based DN4 questionnaire and its utility as a diagnostic tool for neuropathic pain (NeP).
To create the DN4-T for Taiwan, a pain specialist translated the DN4 into Mandarin Chinese. An independent bilingual researcher then back-translated it to English, comparing it to the original for clarity. Finally, an expert panel validated and finalized this Taiwanese version.
Patients underwent clinical assessments, including general, neurological, and musculoskeletal examinations, to pinpoint pain locations and characteristics.
To be included in the study, patients had to be over 18 years old and experiencing pain for more than 30 days, excluding headaches. They also needed to be able to complete the DN4-T questionnaire and have no history of participating in other pain studies within the last 30 days. Individuals with psychosocial issues, unstable medical conditions, or specific pain types like piriformis-induced sciatica, lower back pain, or nerve injuries of unknown origin or cause were excluded. Cancer pain was also an exclusion criterion. After a comprehensive explanation of the study's purpose and procedures, eligible participants provided written informed consent. Their pain was then categorized as neuropathic, nociceptive, or mixed.
Physicians administered the DN4-T questionnaire, with patients responding to seven symptom-related questions. "Yes" answers scored one point, and "No" answers scored zero. Clinicians assessed the remaining three items. Total scores, ranging from 0 to 10, indicated the degree of neuropathic pain.
For statistical analysis, clinical diagnoses of neuropathic pain (NeP), nociceptive pain, and mixed pain were grouped into NeP (neuropathic and mixed) and non-NeP (nociceptive) categories.
To assess the DN4-T questionnaire's effectiveness, its results were analyzed against clinical diagnoses to identify the best threshold for predicting neuropathic pain (NeP). A receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve was employed for this purpose. The optimal cut-off point was identified by finding the maximum sum of sensitivity and specificity, referred to as the index c. The DN4-T questionnaire's cut-off score was then used to predict NeP or non-NeP. Differences between physician diagnoses and the questionnaire's predictions were evaluated using a chi-squared test. Furthermore, the internal consistency and reliability of the DN4-T questionnaire were quantified using Cronbach's alpha coefficient.
A total of 330 patients were initially screened. Of these, 318 patients and their physicians successfully completed the DN4-T questionnaire, with three failing screening and nine excluded due to incomplete responses. Neurologists or pain specialists assessed the participants. The diagnoses included 189 patients (59%) with neuropathic pain (NeP), seven patients (2%) with mixed pain, and 122 patients (38%) with nociceptive pain. Within the NeP group, postherpetic neuralgia was the most frequent diagnosis at 21%. For the nociceptive pain group, myofascial pain (65%) and osteoarthritis (34%) were the most common.
For statistical analysis, patients were grouped into neuropathic pain (NeP) or non-neuropathic pain (non-NeP), combining mixed pain with NeP.
In the non-neuropathic pain group, a score of 0 was most common. Conversely, scores ranging from 3 to 5 were most frequently reported in the neuropathic and mixed pain groups. DN4-T score variability was similar across all three pain classifications.
Patients diagnosed with neuropathic pain (NeP) exhibited significantly higher DN4-T scores compared to those without NeP (p < 0.001). Statistical analysis revealed that 77% of NeP patients (150 out of 196) had a DN4-T score of 3 or greater, whereas only 17% of non-NeP patients (27 out of 122) reached this threshold (p < 0.001).
The optimal cut-off score for the DN4-T questionnaire in predicting neuropathic pain (NeP) was identified using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. This curve assessed sensitivity and specificity to predict a positive NeP diagnosis. The maximum index c value, representing the sum of sensitivity and specificity, was used to establish the optimal cut-off point. This point was determined to be a score of 3, corresponding to a maximum c index value of 1.54. The DN4-T's predictive power for diagnosing NeP was found to be 0.83, as indicated by the area under the curve (AUC).
The DN4-T questionnaire demonstrated good reliability, evidenced by a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.7, which falls within the acceptable range for such measures. Utilizing a cut-off score of 3 or higher for identifying neuropathic pain (NeP) yielded a sensitivity of 0.77 and a specificity of 0.78. Further analysis using ordinary least-square regression confirmed that DN4-T scores significantly predicted NeP versus non-NeP (p < 0.0001). With a concordance rate of 0.785 and discordance of 0.129, the questionnaire proved to be a valuable predictive instrument for distinguishing between these pain types, comparable to expert clinical diagnoses.
Diagnosing neuropathic pain (NeP) clinically is intricate, necessitating a comprehensive approach that integrates a patient's medical history with thorough neurological and physical examinations. Questionnaires like the DN4 have proven invaluable in both diagnosing and managing NeP. While various self-administered and clinician-guided pain assessment tools exist, a meta-analysis highlighted the DN4 questionnaire as the most appropriate for the clinical diagnosis of NeP, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity. This extensive, multicenter study involving 318 patients revealed that individuals diagnosed with NeP exhibited significantly higher DN4-T scores compared to those without NeP (p < 0.001). At a cut-off score of ≥3, the DN4-T achieved a sensitivity of 0.77 and a specificity of 0.78. Furthermore, the DN4-T questionnaire demonstrated good reliability, evidenced by a Cronbach's alpha score of 0.7, underscoring its utility as a diagnostic instrument for NeP in clinical settings.
The findings from our study align with those of other translated DN4 questionnaires, including versions in Spanish, Arabic, Korean, Farsi, Dutch, Portuguese, Greek, and Turkish. These multilingual versions consistently demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity, indicating the DN4's strong capacity for translation and its effectiveness in diagnosing neuropathic pain (NeP) across various languages. Notably, even with differing cut-off scores used in other countries—specifically, a score of 3 in one nation, 4 in seven nations, and 5 in two nations—the DN4 questionnaire maintained high performance. Its ability to differentiate between NeP and non-NeP remained robust, with sensitivity ranging from 75% to 100% and specificity from 78% to 95.8%.
Differences in specificity and sensitivity across translated DN4 questionnaires can arise from several validation factors. Translating between vastly different cultures necessitates input from healthcare and linguistics experts. Furthermore, clinical researchers, pain specialists, and physicians must validate both the translated and back-translated versions.
Variations in how patients were recruited and the criteria for inclusion in studies can affect DN4 questionnaire results. Some studies exclusively enrolled patients from pain clinics, while others included individuals attending for their initial visit. Patients previously assessed and managed by pain specialists might possess greater familiarity with neuropathic pain terminology, potentially leading to more precise responses on the DN4 compared to those seen for the first time. This difference could introduce bias into the overall DN4 questionnaire scores.
The International Association for the Study of Pain defines neuropathic pain (NeP) as pain stemming directly from damage or disease within the somatosensory system. More recently, a 2016 literature review suggested a revised grading system for classifying NeP. This system allows for clinical diagnoses to be categorized as possible, probable, or definite, based on the thoroughness of the clinical evaluation. Confirmation of NeP occurs when diagnostic tests identify a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system as the pain's origin. While a definitive gold standard for NeP diagnosis would be ideal, the most suitable criteria for defining it are still under consideration.
A limitation of this study was the absence of a uniform diagnostic algorithm across the participating sites. Ideally, a thorough diagnostic evaluation, encompassing clinical history, neurological examination, quantitative sensory testing, and supplementary tools like electrodiagnostic studies, MRI, and laboratory tests, would be employed for NeP diagnosis. However, practical constraints related to time and resources often make this approach unfeasible. Furthermore, the study did not assess intra- and inter-rater reliabilities. Prior research has established these reliabilities, suggesting they should not pose a significant obstacle to the clinical application of the DN4-T.
The DN4-T questionnaire has been reliably translated into Mandarin Chinese for use in Taiwan. It can serve as a valuable diagnostic tool for screening and assessing neuropathic pain, when used alongside clinical evaluation by a specialized healthcare professional.
