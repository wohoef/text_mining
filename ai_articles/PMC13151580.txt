L-threonine, a technically pure feed additive, is manufactured through fermentation utilizing a genetically modified (GM) Escherichia coli strain (CGMCC 7.58). Initially, the EFSA's FEEDAP Panel assessed its safety and efficacy, but found insufficient characterization of the recipient strain and its genetic modification, specifically regarding the presence or absence of antibiotic resistance genes. This lack of information prevented the FEEDAP Panel from concluding on the safety of l-threonine produced by this recombinant E. coli for target animals, consumers, users, or the environment. Additionally, concerns were raised about the inhalation risk to users due to the product's endotoxin levels and dusting potential. Subsequently, the European Commission requested a new EFSA opinion on l-threonine's safety for all animal species. This request was prompted by the applicant providing further data on the additive's characterization and the production strain's genetic modification. The updated assessment confirmed the absence of recombinant antibiotic resistance genes in both the production strain and the final product. Consequently, l-threonine, when produced by E. coli CGMCC 7.58, no longer poses safety concerns for target species, consumers, users, or the environment in relation to the genetic modification. However, a risk of endotoxin exposure for handlers is still anticipated, and potential dermal sensitization remains a concern.

This application seeks approval for l-threonine, a nutritional additive (amino acids, salts, and analogues) for all animal species. It is produced using a genetically modified E. coli strain (CGMCC 7.58) and is intended for use in feed.
The product was previously assessed in 2015, but the production strain's identity and the genetic modification, specifically the lack of antibiotic resistance genes in the strain and product, were not sufficiently confirmed.
New information was provided regarding the production strain's genetic modification, the additive's physical and chemical characteristics, and the manufacturing process.
Improvements to the manufacturing process have significantly lowered the bacterial endotoxin levels in the final product. Previously, the average endotoxin activity was 927 IU/mg, based on two analyzed batches. The downstream process was enhanced by altering the ceramic membrane filtration, reducing its pore size from 300 kDa to 20 kDa. Additionally, reverse osmosis was implemented to decrease endotoxins in the process water. As a result of these changes, recent data from three batches show a much lower endotoxin activity, ranging from less than 0.50 to 1.44 IU/mg, as determined by the European Pharmacopoeia 2.6.14 method.
During the 2015 evaluation, a single batch exhibited over 10,000 CFU/g in its total aerobic count, prompting a recommendation to refine the production process to minimize microbial contamination. Subsequently, the applicant submitted new microbiological data from three batches. These analyses revealed no colony growth after incubating 100 μL of a 5% solution on LB medium at 37°C for 48 hours. The production strain, serving as a positive control, functioned correctly. While the earlier assessment noted low levels of yeasts (< 100 CFU/g) and filamentous fungi (≤ 150 CFU/g), and the absence of Salmonella spp. in 25 g samples, the current application lacked updated information on these specific parameters.
To reduce dustiness by increasing crystal size, the applicant modified the downstream process. This involved lowering the evaporation temperature (75–72°C), decreasing the evaporation rate (2.5–2.0 m3/h), and extending both evaporation (12–15 h) and cooling (3–5 h) times.
New analyses have been conducted to determine the particle size and dusting potential of the updated final product. The dusting potential, measured across three batches using the Stauber–Heubach method, was found to be between 0.74 and 1.25 g/m3. This represents a reduction compared to the 2015 assessment, which reported a dusting potential of 2.4 g/m3 for one batch. Regarding particle size distribution, laser diffraction analysis of three new batches revealed that 59–60% (v/v) of particles were smaller than 100 μm, 24–26% (v/v) were under 50 μm, and 3% (v/v) were less than 10 μm in diameter, with an average particle diameter of approximately 85 μm. In contrast, the previous product, analyzed across five batches by an unspecified method, had a higher proportion of particles under 100 μm (69–75%) and a lower proportion under 50 μm (12–15%). An additional batch of the former product, analyzed by sieving, showed about 16% (w/w) of particles smaller than 50 μm.

The acronyms used include CGMCC for China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center, CFU for colony-forming unit, and IU for International Unit of endotoxin activity, which equals one EU. EFSA's FEEDAP and GMO Panels are also referenced, along with DECOS and HSE.
A prior EFSA FEEDAP Panel opinion (2015) detailed the impact of inhaled endotoxins and their exposure limits. This included determining maximum acceptable exposure levels for feed additives.
According to EFSA guidelines (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012b), the estimated exposure duration for additives in premixtures is based on 40 daily exposure periods, each lasting 20 seconds, totaling 800 seconds per day. Applying an uncertainty factor of 2, the maximum inhalation exposure extends to 1,600 seconds (0.444 hours) daily. Given a respiration volume of 1.25 m3/h, the inhaled volume potentially carrying endotoxin-laden dust would be 0.556 m3 per day. To remain within safety limits, this inhaled volume should not exceed 900 International Units (IU) of endotoxin. Consequently, the dust generated from the product must contain no more than 1,619 IU/m3 of endotoxin.
Assessing the respiratory risk linked to a product's endotoxin levels necessitates two primary measurements. These include the product's dusting potential, quantified in grams per cubic meter (g/m3), and the endotoxin activity of the dust, measured in International Units per gram (IU/g) using the Limulus amoebocyte lysate assay. Should specific dust data be unavailable, the endotoxin content of the product itself can serve as an alternative. To determine the endotoxin concentration in airborne dust (c IU/m3), one multiplies the additive's endotoxin content (a IU/g) by its dusting potential (b g/m3). This calculated value then informs the assessment of potential inhalation exposure for users handling the additive.
