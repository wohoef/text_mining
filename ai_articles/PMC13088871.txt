Maize DAS-40278-9, created through direct Whiskers-mediated transformation, was engineered to produce the aryloxyalkanoate dioxygenase-1 (AAD-1) protein. This protein provides resistance to 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) and aryloxyphenoxypropionate (AOPP) herbicides. The EFSA GMO Panel conducted a comprehensive assessment of this genetically modified (GM) maize. Molecular characterization of DAS-40278-9 revealed no safety concerns. Field trials comparing DAS-40278-9 with its non-GM counterpart showed no significant differences in agronomic, phenotypic, or compositional traits that would raise food, feed, or environmental safety issues. Specifically, the AAD-1 protein itself was deemed safe, with no concerns identified regarding its potential toxicity or allergenicity. Furthermore, the genetic modification did not appear to alter the overall allergenicity of the maize. The nutritional profile of DAS-40278-9 is expected to be comparable to non-GM maize varieties, making post-market monitoring for food/feed unnecessary. The GM maize was found to be as nutritious as its non-GM comparator and other commercial non-GM varieties. Environmental risk assessment indicated no increased likelihood of feral DAS-40278-9 plants establishing or spreading, unless exposed to the specific herbicides it was designed to tolerate. Even in such a scenario, the environmental impact would not differ from conventional maize. Interactions with the biotic and abiotic environment were not considered problematic within the application's scope. The possibility of horizontal gene transfer from DAS-40278-9 to bacteria, though theoretically possible, was deemed unlikely and not a safety concern. The proposed post-market environmental monitoring plan and its reporting schedule were found to be appropriate for the application's scope. In summary, the EFSA GMO Panel concluded that the provided information adequately addressed Member States' scientific comments. Maize DAS-40278-9, as presented in the application, is considered as safe as its non-GM comparator and reference varieties for human and animal health, and for the environment, within the context of its intended use.

Dow AgroSciences LLC submitted an application (EFSA-GMO-NL-2010-89) under Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003 for genetically modified maize DAS-40278-9. The EFSA GMO Panel was tasked with providing a scientific safety opinion on this herbicide-tolerant maize. This application specifically covered the import, processing, and use of maize DAS-40278-9 as food and feed within the European Union, but it did not include cultivation within the EU.
The GMO Panel assessed maize DAS-40278-9 according to its established risk assessment guidelines for GM plants. This comprehensive evaluation included examining the molecular details of the inserted DNA and protein expression, alongside comparative analyses of the plant's composition, agronomic traits, and phenotypic characteristics. Furthermore, the safety assessment covered potential toxicity, allergenicity, and nutritional aspects of both the new proteins and the entire food/feed. The environmental risk and post-market environmental monitoring plans were also reviewed.
Immature maize embryos underwent direct Whiskers-mediated transformation to create maize DAS-40278-9. This modification enables the expression of the aryloxyalkanoate dioxygenase-1 (AAD-1) protein, which provides resistance to 2,4-D and AOPP herbicides. Molecular analysis confirmed the presence of a single, functional aad-1 expression cassette, with no other plasmid components detected. Bioinformatic and genetic stability studies were conducted, revealing no safety concerns. The levels of the AAD-1 protein in the maize were accurately measured and documented.
Field trials revealed no significant differences in agronomic or phenotypic traits between GM maize DAS-40278-9 and its non-GM counterpart, apart from plant height and silking time. Furthermore, compositional analyses of forage and grain showed no differences necessitating further food and feed safety evaluation.
The safety evaluation of maize DAS-40278-9 found no issues concerning the potential toxicity of the AAD-1 protein. This conclusion was supported by a 28-day subacute toxicity study, which showed no negative effects even at the highest dose, and by analyses of the AAD-1 protein's structure and function, including bioinformatic assessments. Furthermore, the GMO Panel found no evidence suggesting allergenicity or adjuvant effects associated with the AAD-1 protein, nor any changes to the overall allergenicity of the maize itself. Comparative analysis indicated that the nutritional profile of food and feed from maize DAS-40278-9 is comparable to that of non-GM maize varieties. Consequently, the GMO Panel determined that maize DAS-40278-9 is as safe and nutritious as its non-GM counterparts and reference varieties, making post-market monitoring of its food and feed products unnecessary.
The GMO Panel determined that maize DAS-40278-9 is unlikely to pose environmental safety concerns, even if viable GM maize grains are accidentally released. This conclusion considers the plant's inherent low survival capacity, which means minor differences in "plant height" and "time to silking" are not expected to enhance its fitness, invasiveness, or ability to establish and spread as feral plants. While exposure to 2,4-D or AOPP herbicides could give these plants a selective advantage, the Panel believes this would not lead to environmental impacts different from conventional maize. Furthermore, the Panel did not identify risks from potential interactions with biotic or abiotic environments. The theoretical possibility of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from maize DAS-40278-9 to bacteria was also assessed and found not to be a concern, given the introduced traits, exposure routes, and limited exposure levels. The post-market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plan submitted by the applicant, including its proposed reporting intervals, aligns with the application's scope and the Panel's guidelines for GM plants.
The GMO Panel's scientific opinion on maize DAS-40278-9 was formed after considering the application EFSA-GMO-NL-2010-89, supplementary data from the applicant, scientific feedback from Member States, and pertinent scientific literature. Ultimately, the Panel determined that the provided information adequately addressed Member State concerns. They concluded that maize DAS-40278-9 is as safe as its non-GM counterparts for human and animal health, and the environment, within the application's defined scope.

Maize DAS-40278-9 was created by transforming immature Hi-II maize embryos, a hybrid of A188 and B73 inbred lines. This direct Whiskers-mediated transformation utilized a specific 6,236 bp DNA fragment. This fragment, which confers tolerance to 2,4-D and AOPP herbicides via an aad-1 expression cassette, was isolated from plasmid pDAS1740. The isolation process involved FspI digestion of pDAS1740, yielding five fragments. After digestion, the desired 6,236 bp fragment was separated from two ~1 kb fragments (containing parts of the ampicillin resistance gene) and two smaller 9 bp fragments using column chromatography.
The 6,236 bp DNA fragment contained several genetic components. It included the ZmUbi1 promoter from *Zea mays* and a synthetic, plant-optimized *aad-1* gene, originally from *S. herbicidovorans*. A terminator sequence, the ZmPer 3′ untranslated region (UTR) from *Z. mays*, was also present. To enhance *aad-1* gene expression, the entire cassette was bordered by identical matrix attachment regions (RB7 MAR v3 and RB7 MAR v4) sourced from *Nicotiana tabacum*.
The FspI-digested plasmid pDAS1740 contained pUC19 backbone sequences, including an ampicillin resistance gene, which were not intended for transfer into the maize genome.
To assess maize DAS-40278-9, molecular characterization employed Southern analysis, PCR, and DNA sequencing. This determined the inserted sequences' copy number, size, and organization, and verified the absence of plasmid backbone sequences. The methodology was deemed adequate in both scope and precision.
Maize DAS-40278-9 was shown to possess a single insert, comprising one copy of the aad-1 expression cassette from pDAS1740, through Southern analysis. This finding, including the insert's copy number, was corroborated by various restriction enzyme and probe combinations, encompassing both the 6,236 bp inserted fragment and its adjacent regions. Further confirmation came from PCR analyses. Additionally, Southern analysis, employing backbone-specific probes, verified the absence of vector backbone sequences.
Sequencing of maize DAS-40278-9's insert and its adjacent 5' (1,873 bp) and 3' (1,868 bp) regions was conducted. This analysis confirmed that the insert is a 4,816 bp segment from pDAS1740, encompassing a complete aad-1 expression cassette. Additionally, a 249 bp partial MAR v3 was found at the 5' end and a 1,096 bp partial MAR v4 at the 3' end. The aad-1 sequence within DAS-40278-9 maize matched that of pDAS1740, with the exception of a single T to C base pair alteration in the non-coding 3'UTR of the aad-1 gene.
Bioinformatic analyses were conducted to assess whether the insertion in DAS-40278-9 maize might have disrupted known endogenous maize genes. This involved examining the pre-insertion locus and the genomic sequences adjacent to the insert. Comparing the flanking region sequences of DAS-40278-9 with the pre-insertion locus revealed a 21 base pair insertion and a 2 base pair deletion at the 5' junction, along with a 1 base pair insertion at the 3' junction. These analyses suggested that the insert likely integrated into a region resembling a Grande retrotransposon. Crucially, no evidence indicated the interruption of any known endogenous genes within the maize genome.
Segregation and bioinformatic analyses confirmed the insert's presence within the nuclear genome.
Following EFSA guidance, updated bioinformatic analysis of the AAD-1 protein's amino acid sequences showed no substantial resemblances to known toxins or allergens.
Bioinformatic analyses of new open reading frames in the insert and at its junctions showed no significant resemblance to known toxins or allergens.

These samples encompassed various plant parts and developmental stages, including leaves (V2–V4, V9, R1), roots (R1), pollen (R1), forage (R4), whole plants (R6), and mature grain. Both herbicide-treated (quizalofop, 2,4-D, or both) and untreated samples were analyzed. Table 1 presents the average and range of AAD-1 protein levels found in the grain and forage.
Southern analysis across five generations confirmed the stable inheritance of the single DAS-40278-9 insert and its flanking regions. The chosen restriction enzyme and probe combinations adequately demonstrated this genetic stability.
Segregation analysis of quizalofop tolerance in maize DAS-40278-9 demonstrated phenotypic stability. This analysis indicated a single, Mendelian-segregating insertion.
Maize DAS-40278-9's molecular characterization confirmed a single insert: one copy of the aad-1 expression cassette. Bioinformatic analysis of the newly expressed proteins and other open reading frames (ORFs) within the insert or at its genomic junctions revealed no safety concerns. The stability of the inserted DNA and the herbicide tolerance trait was consistently observed across multiple generations. Additionally, AAD-1 protein levels were accurately measured and documented.
Field trials conducted at eight U.S. sites in 2009 provided data on the agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional characteristics of maize DAS-40278-9, as detailed in application EFSA-GMO-NL-2010-89.
To evaluate the agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional traits of maize DAS-40278-9, field trials were carried out across eight locations in major maize-producing regions of the USA during 2009. These sites encompassed a range of agricultural practices and environmental conditions. Each location featured a randomized complete block design with four replicates, including the GM maize under various herbicide treatments (untreated, 2,4-D, quizalofop, and a combination of 2,4-D and quizalofop). Additionally, a non-GM comparator (XHH13 × 7SH382), genetically similar to DAS-40278-9, and three commercial non-GM maize reference varieties were planted. All plants received standard maintenance pesticides, including conventional herbicides. In total, six non-GM maize reference varieties were utilized across all trial sites.
Field trial data from 2009, encompassing agronomic, phenotypic, and compositional aspects, underwent statistical analysis in accordance with GMO Panel guidelines. This involved two primary tests for each of the four maize DAS-40278-9 treatments: a difference test comparing the GM maize to its non-GM counterpart, and an equivalence test against a range of non-GM commercial varieties. The equivalence test yielded results categorized into four levels, from equivalent to non-equivalent. A total of 28 agronomic and phenotypic traits were assessed.
For 16 agronomic/phenotypic endpoints, where statistical test requirements were not met, a non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. No significant differences were found for these endpoints.
Among the 12 parametrically analyzed endpoints, plant height showed significant differences between GM maize and its non-GM counterpart in one treatment (2,4-D+quizalofop). However, the equivalence test for plant height was not feasible due to minimal variation within non-GM reference varieties.
For the 11 endpoints amenable to both difference and equivalence testing, various outcomes were observed. When untreated, maize DAS-40278-9 showed statistically significant differences from its non-GM counterpart in early stand count at V1 and time to silking. However, in comparison to non-GM reference varieties, early stand count at V1 was categorized as equivalence category I, while time to silking fell into equivalence category II. When treated with 2,4-D, DAS-40278-9 exhibited statistically significant differences across seven endpoints: early stand count at V1 and V4, final stand count, yield, and pollen color and shape at both 30 and 60 minutes. All these differences were classified under equivalence category I when compared to non-GM reference varieties. Similarly, DAS-40278-9 treated with quizalofop displayed significant differences in three endpoints: yield, pollen color at 30 minutes, and pollen shape at 60 minutes, all of which also met equivalence category I. Finally, the combination treatment of 2,4-D and quizalofop resulted in five statistically significant differences for DAS-40278-9, including yield, pollen color at 30 and 60 minutes, and pollen shape at 30 and 60 minutes. These, too, were all assigned to equivalence category I.
The DAS-40278-9 maize, when untreated, showed a significantly different "time to silking" compared to its non-GM counterpart, placing it in equivalence category II. Similarly, "plant height" for DAS-40278-9 treated with 2,4-D+quizalofop also differed significantly from the non-GM comparator, though it couldn't be categorized for equivalence. These differences will be further evaluated for environmental impact.
In 2009, field trials in the USA yielded maize forage and grain. These samples were analyzed for 82 compounds, including 9 in forage and 73 in grain, encompassing key constituents recommended by OECD (2002).
The applicant's studies on AAD-1 protein's substrate specificity revealed its capacity to oxidize maize's natural compounds, trans-cinnamic acid and indole-3-acetic acid. The GMO Panel observed that phenylalanine and coumaric acid, both metabolically linked to trans-cinnamic acid and involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, were among the compositional parameters analyzed.
Statistical analysis omitted 17 grain components, as over half of their measurements fell below the quantification limit.
Due to minimal variation among non-GM reference varieties, the equivalence test was not performed for six compounds (one forage, five grain). Of these, arginine content in the GM maize was significantly different from the non-GM comparator.
For the 59 remaining compositional parameters, both difference and equivalence tests were performed. When comparing untreated maize DAS-40278-9 with its non-GM comparator, 20 constituents (16 in grain, 4 in forage) showed statistically significant differences. Among these, 15 constituents were deemed equivalent (Category I or II) to non-GM reference varieties, while 5 grain constituents were categorized as non-equivalent (Category III or IV). For maize DAS-40278-9 treated with 2,4-D, 20 constituents (16 in grain, 4 in forage) exhibited significant differences from the non-GM comparator. Of these, 16 were found equivalent (Category I or II) to non-GM reference varieties, and 4 grain constituents were non-equivalent (Category III or IV). In the case of maize DAS-40278-9 treated with quizalofop, 26 constituents (21 in grain, 5 in forage) displayed significant differences. The equivalence test revealed that 19 of these were equivalent (Category I or II), while 7 grain constituents were non-equivalent (Category III or IV). Finally, for maize DAS-40278-9 treated with both 2,4-D and quizalofop, 14 constituents (11 in grain, 3 in forage) showed significant differences. Among these, 10 were equivalent (Category I or II) to non-GM reference varieties, and 4 grain constituents were non-equivalent (Category III or IV). All these findings are detailed in Table 3.
The GMO Panel concluded that the AAD-1 enzyme is unlikely to negatively impact endogenous plant metabolism. This was based on the observation that coumaric acid, a compound related to trans-cinnamic acid, showed no differences, and the minor phenylalanine difference was not a safety concern.
The GMO Panel evaluated all compositional variations between GM and non-GM maize. Based on the biological function and the extent and nature of observed changes, no further food and feed safety assessment was deemed necessary.
No further food and feed safety assessment is needed for maize DAS-40278-9. The GMO Panel found no relevant differences in its grain, forage composition, or agronomic and phenotypic characteristics compared to non-GM maize.
Field trials showed maize DAS-40278-9's agronomic and phenotypic traits were comparable to non-GM maize. Only plant height and time to silking differed, and these were further evaluated for environmental impact.
The comparative assessment (Section 3.2) indicates that food and feed products derived from maize DAS-40278-9 processing will be indistinguishable from those of conventional non-GM maize varieties.
The thermal stability of the AAD-1 protein was assessed by heating solutions at 50, 70, and 95°C for 30 minutes. This treatment significantly reduced enzymatic activity to below 3% and immunoreactivity to only 1%. Despite these functional changes, the protein's molecular mass remained consistent, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE, indicating its structural integrity was maintained even after heat exposure.
The impact of pH on the AAD-1 protein's in vitro activity was evaluated using 2,4-D as a substrate. The bacterial AAD-1 protein, expressed in maize DAS-40278-9, showed optimal activity at a pH of 7.9, with activity diminishing at both higher and lower pH levels. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that the AAD-1 protein concentration stayed constant across the tested pH range, implying that the reduction in enzymatic activity was due to structural changes rather than protein degradation.
Due to challenges in obtaining sufficient AAD-1 protein from plants for safety tests, the protein was produced in *Pseudomonas fluorescens*. The bacterial and maize-derived AAD-1 proteins were then analyzed and compared for their physical, structural, and functional characteristics.
The AAD-1 proteins, whether produced in plants or microbes, exhibited similar characteristics. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed their molecular weight was approximately 33 kDa, aligning with expectations. Western blot analysis further showed that both protein types reacted similarly with AAD-1 specific antibodies. Glycosylation detection indicated that neither the plant-derived nor the microbe-derived AAD-1 proteins were glycosylated. Amino acid sequencing, performed using MALDI-TOF MS and ESI-LC/MS, confirmed that both proteins possessed the anticipated AAD-1 sequence. However, the N-terminal methionine was found to be truncated in both versions. The plant-derived protein displayed additional minor truncations, up to alanine 4, and a small fraction (3%) was N-acetylated. Conversely, the C-termini of both proteins were identical and perfectly matched the theoretical AAD-1 sequence. A biochemical in vitro activity assay established functional equivalence, demonstrating that both protein sources had comparable activity and specificity towards the target herbicides. Furthermore, the microbial AAD-1 protein was assessed for its interaction with endogenous plant substrates, and the results suggested that AAD-1 activity is unlikely to significantly impact the metabolism of transgenic plants. The equivalence between the microbially produced AAD-1 and the DAS-40278-9 expressed AAD-1 was also confirmed through MS analysis and enzymatic activity assays, supporting the use of the microbial protein in a 28-day oral toxicity study in mice.
The GMO Panel approved using microbial AAD-1 protein for safety studies, noting it was a novel assessment. Bioinformatic analyses were also conducted.
Bioinformatic analysis of the AAD-1 protein's amino acid sequence in maize DAS-40278-9 showed no significant resemblance to known toxins. Additionally, in vitro studies demonstrated rapid degradation of the AAD-1 protein by pepsin.
The AAD-1 protein, derived from bacteria, was rapidly degraded by pepsin within 30 seconds at pH 1.2, as determined by SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis of samples taken over time. Acute oral toxicity testing was also conducted.
Male and female Crl:CD1(ICR) mice received the bacterial AAD-1 protein orally at 2,000 mg/kg body weight. No AAD-1-related effects were observed in these mice.
The GMO Panel believes acute toxicity tests for new proteins offer minimal extra value in assessing risks from repeated consumption of GM plant-derived food and feed by humans and animals. This perspective informs the design of 28-day repeated dose toxicity studies.
The initial 28-day oral toxicity study on AAD-1 protein in mice, submitted by the applicant, was deemed insufficient by the GMO Panel. Concerns were raised regarding the study's design, specifically the low dosage of AAD-1 protein administered (a maximum of approximately 45 mg/kg body weight per day) and the small group size of five animals per sex, which hindered adequate statistical power. Furthermore, the absence of coagulation analyses was noted as a deficiency. Consequently, the GMO Panel mandated a new 28-day rodent toxicity study. This revised study was required to include a larger animal cohort and employ dosing strategies aligned with OECD TG 407, aiming either to elicit adverse effects at the highest dose or to follow a limit test approach if no toxicity was anticipated.
In response to EFSA's request, the applicant submitted a new 28-day oral repeated dose toxicity study in mice. This study adhered to OECD TG 407 guidelines and Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) principles. The study involved Crl:CD1(ICR) mice, housed individually in groups of 12 per sex. These mice received daily gavage administrations. One group, the AAD-1 protein group, was given the AAD-1 protein at a target dose of 1,000 mg/kg body weight per day. The concentration of AAD-1 protein in the test material (63.5%) was factored in to achieve this target. A vehicle control group received only the vehicle, while a BSA control group received bovine serum albumin (BSA) at a nominal target dose of 1,000 mg/kg body weight per day. Both AAD-1 and BSA formulations were prepared daily. Throughout the study, animals had unrestricted access to feed and water. Daily checks were performed for mortality and general clinical signs, with detailed clinical observations conducted weekly and pre-treatment. Ophthalmoscopy was performed at the beginning and end of the treatment period. Body weights were recorded on specific days (1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 15, 22, 29), and body weight gains were calculated from day 1. Feed consumption was also monitored across several intervals. At the conclusion of the treatment, haematological and clinical chemistry analyses were conducted. All animals were then euthanized for a complete necropsy, which included weighing selected organs. Comprehensive histological examinations were performed on organs and tissues from all animals.
The GMO Panel observed that AAD-1 protein formulations were derived from powdered test material kept at about 4°C. The stability of this lyophilized AAD-1 protein during storage was not assessed in the study, and its long-term concentration of 63.5% remained unverified.
The GMO Panel observed that the applicant performed hematology and clinical chemistry analyses on only six mice per gender per group, with coagulation tests on the remaining six. The applicant cited difficulties in drawing enough blood from individual mice for all tests. However, the Panel highlighted that using mice as test subjects often necessitates more animals per dose group to complete all required assessments. Furthermore, the Panel noted that the animals were not fasted before necropsy and blood collection, a procedure recommended by OECD TG 407.
Statistical comparisons were made between the AAD-1 protein group and the BSA control group, with the latter also compared to the vehicle control group to evaluate the impact of increased protein intake. Before further analysis, all statistically examined parameters underwent Bartlett's test to check for equality of variance (combining sexes), with scale transformations applied if necessary. Subsequently, two types of statistical analyses were conducted. A two-way ANOVA (considering sex and dose as factors, with sexes combined) was performed first. If this yielded a significant result, a one-way ANOVA (with dose as the factor, sexes separated) followed. Additionally, parameters were re-tested using Bartlett's test (with sexes separated) and analyzed via a one-way ANOVA (sexes separated). Dunnett's test facilitated comparisons between individual dose groups and the control group. Descriptive statistics were exclusively reported for body weight gains, globulin, albumin/globulin ratio, RBC indices, and differential WBC counts.
Dose confirmation analyses of the initial mixture showed average recoveries of 75.1% for AAD-1 protein and 79.4% for BSA in 0.5% METHOCEL. These values, based on nominal 100 mg/mL dosing suspensions, fell within the acceptable experimental variation of 70–120%. The GMO Panel determined that the actual AAD-1 protein dose administered in this first mix was 751 mg/kg body weight per day, derived from the measured concentration in the dosing suspension. Homogeneity analyses confirmed that the preparations were uniformly mixed.
The GMO Panel determined that the minor, statistically significant variations between the BSA and vehicle control groups fell within typical biological ranges. Consequently, both the vehicle and BSA control groups were deemed appropriate for comparing and assessing data from the AAD-1 protein group.
The AAD-1 protein group exhibited no mortality, significant clinical observations, or ophthalmic changes. While females in the AAD-1 group showed a statistically significant, but transient, reduction in feed consumption between days 4 and 8 compared to the BSA control, this was deemed incidental by the GMO Panel. No significant differences in body weight or gain were noted between the AAD-1 and BSA control groups.
In male mice treated with AAD-1 protein, haematology analysis revealed a statistically significant, albeit small, reduction in haemoglobin levels compared to the BSA control group. This mean level was comparable to the vehicle control and within historical ranges. Given no other haematological changes, the GMO Panel deemed this difference toxicologically irrelevant.
While male mice receiving AAD-1 protein exhibited statistically higher serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity compared to the BSA control group, the GMO Panel deemed this difference toxicologically irrelevant. This decision was based on the considerable individual variation across all groups and the mean value's proximity to historical control ranges.
While no other significant differences were found in hematological, clinical chemistry, or prothrombin time parameters, the GMO Panel observed a limitation. Only six animals per gender per group were used for these examinations, which did not meet EFSA's recommendation for a larger sample size.
While most organ weights showed no significant changes, male mice treated with AAD-1 protein exhibited slightly increased absolute and relative prostate weights compared to the BSA control group. However, these prostate weight differences were deemed toxicologically irrelevant by the GMO Panel. This conclusion was based on the mean values remaining within historical control ranges and the absence of associated macroscopic or microscopic abnormalities in the prostate gland or other male reproductive organs.
Post-mortem examinations, both macroscopic and microscopic, showed no AAD-1 protein-related pathological changes in organs or tissues. No differences in histopathological findings were observed between treated and control groups.
Despite the absence of adverse effects observed in mice after a 28-day administration of the AAD-1 protein at 751 mg/kg bw per day, the GMO Panel highlighted a limitation. The recommended number of animals for haematological, clinical chemistry, and coagulation examinations (only 6 per gender per group) was not met, falling short of EFSA's guidelines.
Maize DAS-40278-9 showed no novel components beyond the AAD-1 protein. Comparative analysis confirmed no significant compositional alterations in the GM maize.
No further food and feed safety assessment was needed for compositional differences in maize DAS-40278-9, thus animal studies were not mandated. Despite this, the applicant submitted a broiler study, which the EFSA GMO Panel reviewed.
A 42-day feeding study involving 600 day-old Ross 708 chickens, equally split by sex, was conducted to assess the nutritional impact of maize DAS-40278-9. The chickens were divided into five dietary groups, each with 120 chicks, comprising six pens per gender with ten birds each. Diets included maize DAS-40278-9 (confirmed by ELISA to contain AAD-1 and not treated with herbicides), a non-GM comparator, or three commercial non-GM maize varieties (Dekalb 6343, NK 72-G8, and Pioneer 32T16). Maize meal constituted 50% of starter diets (0-14 days), 55% of grower diets (15-28 days), and 60% of finisher diets (29-42 days). Commercial soybean meal (46% protein) was also included. All maize and soybean meal underwent analysis for proximates, ADF, NDF, minerals, amino acids, fatty acids, anti-nutrients, and mycotoxins before feed formulation. The resulting diets were confirmed to be isonitrogenous and isocaloric. Although AAD-1 protein was present throughout the study, its concentration decreased to approximately 30% by the study's conclusion. Mash feed and water were provided freely.
The study monitored chickens for mortality and clinical signs twice daily, with necropsies conducted on deceased birds. Body weights and feed intake were recorded on days 1, 14, 28, and 42 to calculate the feed conversion ratio. Upon trial completion, four birds from each pen underwent carcass evaluation, assessing yield, dressing percentage, and the weight of various parts like thighs, breast, wings, legs, abdominal fat, and liver. Statistical analysis involved ANOVA, treating pen as the experimental unit and considering dietary treatment and gender as fixed effects, followed by Dunnett's test for pairwise comparisons between GM and non-GM groups.
The broiler feeding study revealed no significant adverse effects. Mortality rates remained low, below 2%, and showed no notable variation across treatment groups. Neither final body weight, feed intake, nor feed-to-gain ratio exhibited significant differences among the groups, nor were there significant interactions between treatment and sex for these metrics. While males had a higher average final body weight (approximately 2.8 kg) compared to females (around 2.5 kg), feed-to-gain ratios were comparable (1.65 for females and 1.61 for males). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in carcass characteristics or liver weight between broilers fed GM maize and those fed non-GM maize.
Feeding broilers diets with up to 60% DAS-40278-9 maize meal for 42 days showed no adverse effects. Performance metrics were comparable between groups fed GM and non-GM maize, indicating its safety.
Assessing allergenicity risk involves considering the recombinant protein's origin, its capacity to sensitize or trigger reactions in allergic individuals, and any changes to the modified plant's allergenic characteristics due to transformation.
Predicting allergenicity required a comprehensive "weight-of-evidence" approach, as no single experimental method or data point was sufficient. All available information on the newly expressed protein was considered.
The aad-1 gene comes from S. herbicidovorans, a soil microbe not recognized as a common allergen.
Bioinformatic analysis of the AAD-1 protein's amino acid sequences showed no significant resemblance to known allergens, using a 35% identity threshold over an 80-amino acid window. Further analysis, searching for eight consecutive identical amino acids between AAD-1 and known allergens, corroborated these findings.
The AAD-1 protein's resistance to proteolytic enzyme degradation was previously detailed in Section 3.3.1.2.
The AAD-1 protein's structure or function offers no evidence of an adjuvant effect that could trigger or amplify an IgE response to other proteins.
The GMO Panel found no evidence suggesting that the AAD-1 protein in maize DAS-40278-9 could cause allergic reactions.
Maize isn't currently recognized as a frequent food allergen (OECD, 2002), so the GMO Panel didn't require experimental data on GM maize's allergen profile. The Panel consistently examines published research concerning maize food allergies.
The GMO Panel found no evidence suggesting increased allergenicity in maize DAS-40278-9 food and feed compared to its non-GM counterpart. This conclusion was based on molecular characterization, compositional analysis, and the assessment of the newly expressed protein.
Maize DAS-40278-9 was developed solely for herbicide tolerance, without aiming to change its nutritional content. Comparative analysis showed no compositional differences necessitating further nutritional evaluation for food and feed. Consequently, products from this maize are anticipated to have the same nutritional impact as non-GM and commercial varieties.
The GMO Panel determined that post-market monitoring of GM food/feed is unnecessary, aligning with guidance documents. Maize DAS-40278-9 was found to be as safe as its non-GM counterpart and equally nutritious compared to other non-GM commercial varieties.
The AAD-1 protein, newly expressed in maize DAS-40278-9, showed no potential toxicity. This conclusion was drawn from a 28-day subacute toxicity study, which found no adverse effects even at the highest dose, and from analyses of the protein's structure and function, including bioinformatic evaluations. Furthermore, the GMO Panel found no evidence suggesting allergenicity or adjuvanticity for the AAD-1 protein, nor any indication that the genetic modification altered the overall allergenicity of maize DAS-40278-9. Comparative analysis also indicated that the nutritional profile of food and feed from DAS-40278-9 maize is comparable to that of non-GM maize varieties.
The environmental risk assessment for maize DAS-40278-9, which is not intended for cultivation, focuses on two primary areas. Firstly, it examines the exposure of bacteria to recombinant DNA within animal digestive systems and in environments contaminated by animal waste. Secondly, the assessment addresses the potential for viable GM maize grains to be accidentally released into the environment during transport and processing.
Maize, a highly domesticated crop, generally struggles to persist in the environment without human intervention. Its survival is significantly hampered in colder European climates due to a lack of winter hardiness. While individual maize plants can occasionally appear outside cultivated fields, their ability to thrive is restricted by factors such as limited competitive strength, the absence of a dormant period, and vulnerability to diseases, pests, and cold weather. Although maize volunteers can emerge in fields under specific conditions, like mild winters, and grain has been observed to overwinter in some areas, these volunteers typically exhibit weak growth and asynchronous flowering compared to the main crop. Reports from Spain and other European regions confirm the presence of such volunteers.
A 2009 field trial in eight US locations assessed the phenotypic and agronomic traits of maize DAS-40278-9. Out of ten endpoints showing significant differences between the GM maize and its non-GM comparator, eight were deemed equivalent to non-GM reference varieties. For the remaining two, "plant height" could not be subjected to an equivalence test due to insufficient variation among commercial non-GM maize varieties. "Time to silking" for maize DAS-40278-9 was categorized as "equivalence category II," indicating it was more likely than not equivalent to non-GM maize reference varieties.
The observed difference in plant height between GM maize and its non-GM counterpart was deemed insignificant for increased fitness, considering its characteristics and extent.
The 'time to silking' endpoint showed a difference from the non-GM comparator, suggesting potential unintended effects from genetic modification. However, the equivalence test indicated that this GM maize was more likely equivalent to non-GM reference varieties than not. Crucially, related fitness indicators, such as yield, demonstrated equivalence with non-GM varieties. While seed lot quality could also account for these observations, the available dossier provided no evidence for this.
Accidental environmental release of maize DAS-40278-9 is not expected to increase the establishment or spread of feral plants. While exposure to 2,4-D or AOPP herbicides might offer a transient local advantage, this will not lead to environmental impacts different from conventional maize.
The GMO Panel found no scientific evidence indicating enhanced spread, establishment, or survival of maize DAS-40278-9 or similar maize varieties beyond the applicant's data.
The GMO Panel determined that maize DAS-40278-9 is unlikely to persist in European environments beyond a single season, even if viable GM grains are accidentally released. This suggests that the presence of GM maize plants in the environment would be restricted, aligning with the survival characteristics of conventional maize.
Gene transfer necessitates pathways: horizontal transfer of DNA to bacteria, or vertical transfer through seed spillage and subsequent cross-pollination. This section focuses on plant-to-bacteria gene transfer.
DNA, including its recombinant portions, is found in many maize-derived food and feed products. While DNA is largely broken down during digestion and processing in human and animal guts, some fragments are expected to persist. These remaining DNA fragments can then be exposed to bacteria within the digestive tracts of humans and domesticated animals, as well as in other environments where GM plant material is present.
Under natural conditions, current scientific understanding of bacterial recombination indicates that horizontal gene transfer of non-mobile, chromosomal DNA fragments between unrelated organisms (like plants and bacteria) is unlikely to happen at detectable rates.
For horizontal gene transfer (HGT) to bacteria to be successful, the recombinant DNA must stably integrate into the bacterial genome, providing the host with a selective advantage. Homologous recombination is the sole identified mechanism for non-mobile, chromosomal DNA fragments to transfer to bacterial genomes. This process necessitates shared DNA sequences between the recombining molecules. Such similarity can exist within the coding region of the transgene or in the border regions of the gene cassettes integrated into the plant genome. If the similarity is with the transgene itself, gene replacement occurs. Conversely, if the similarity is in the border regions, additional DNA sequences could be inserted into the bacteria, potentially leading to new characteristics.
The AAD-1 protein's coding sequence in maize DAS-40278-9 is a synthetic gene, originating from the *S. herbicidovorans* *aad-1* gene. This synthetic gene was optimized for plant cell expression by increasing its G+C content. Notably, no bacterial genetic elements are present in the DAS-40278-9 insert. *S. herbicidovorans* and similar bacteria are known for their ability to break down 2,4-D and related compounds using dioxygenase activity. While these bacteria are rarely found in the gastrointestinal tracts of humans or animals, the recombinant genes could potentially appear in environments where *S. herbicidovorans* thrives, such as through manure or feces.
The transfer of the aad-1 gene from maize DAS-40278-9 to environmental bacteria through homologous recombination is theoretically possible, though considered an extremely rare occurrence. This assumption holds if the plant codon optimization doesn't significantly reduce the gene's DNA sequence identity with bacterial counterparts. Such a recombination event would involve gene replacement, substituting existing natural variants of the aad-1 gene rather than introducing a novel trait. Consequently, this gene transfer is not expected to offer a new selective advantage to the recipient bacteria. It's important to note that this theoretical possibility lacks experimental evidence for GM food and feed.
Illegitimate recombination, which doesn't rely on DNA similarity, is a theoretical possibility alongside homology-based processes. However, its transformation rates are significantly lower (10^10-fold) than homologous recombination. Studies, even those with high concentrations of GM plant DNA, have never detected illegitimate recombination between GM plants and bacteria.
The GMO Panel determined that the aad-1 gene from maize DAS-40278-9 could theoretically transfer to S. herbicidovorans and similar bacteria via homologous recombination. However, as these bacteria are not typically found in the gut, exposure to the recombinant DNA from DAS-40278-9 maize is deemed minimal.
The GMO Panel concluded that horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from maize DAS-40278-9 to bacteria is not a safety concern. This is because the gene replacement in S. herbicidovorans or similar bacteria, which naturally possess aad-1 gene variants, is expected to be minimal. Furthermore, the intended use of maize DAS-40278-9 as food and feed, combined with the assessment, supports this conclusion.
Given the application's scope and maize's biology, the risk of recombinant DNA transfer from feral GM maize, arising from spilled imported grains, to sexually compatible plants was evaluated. The presence of such feral GM maize is anticipated to be limited.
In Europe, the transfer of genes from GM maize to other plants is not an environmental concern. This is because there are no known wild relatives of maize in the EU that could sexually cross-pollinate. While cross-pollination with other maize varieties is possible, it would depend on the amount of GM maize accidentally released during transport and processing, and the ability of those plants to establish and flower.
Feral GM maize plants, arising from accidental grain spills during transport and processing, are improbable to release substantial amounts of pollen onto other maize. Studies in Spain on volunteer maize, following GM maize cultivation, showed these plants had poor vitality, infrequently produced cobs, and cross-pollinated nearby plants minimally. Consequently, the chance of cross-pollination between cultivated and feral maize is deemed exceptionally low.
The GMO Panel determined that the spread of genes from this GM maize in Europe is unlikely to differ from conventional varieties, even when exposed to the intended herbicide.
The GMO Panel deemed interactions between the GM plant and target organisms irrelevant, given the application's scope (EFSA-GMO-NL-2010-89) and the absence of target pests.
The GMO Panel deemed interactions between non-target organisms and spilled DAS-40278-9 maize or feral plants from import spills irrelevant. This was due to the application's scope and limited environmental exposure.
The GMO Panel determined that interactions between spilled DAS-40278-9 maize grains or feral plants and the abiotic environment or biogeochemical cycles are not a significant concern. This conclusion is based on the application's limited scope and the low environmental exposure.
A Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plan, as outlined in Annex VII of Directive 2001/18/EC, serves two main purposes. Firstly, it aims to verify the accuracy of assumptions made during the Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA) concerning potential adverse effects of the GMO or its usage. Secondly, it seeks to detect any unforeseen adverse impacts of the GMO or its use on human health or the environment that were not predicted by the ERA.
The EFSA's GMO Panel offers scientific input on the applicant's Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plan. However, the ultimate approval of this plan, being a risk management function, is beyond the EFSA's scope.
The applicant's Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plan for maize DAS-40278-9 outlines a comprehensive strategy. This includes a monitoring approach where operators, such as maize import and processing federations, report any observed adverse effects of GMOs on human health or the environment to the applicants through a centralized system. Furthermore, EuropaBio has established a new coordinating system to gather data from these operators. The plan also incorporates existing surveillance networks. The applicant intends to provide annual PMEM reports, with a final report submitted at the conclusion of the consent period.
The applicant's proposed Post-Market Environmental Monitoring (PMEM) plan aligns with the scope of maize DAS-40278-9. Since the Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA) did not identify adverse environmental effects or cover cultivation, specific monitoring for this case is deemed unnecessary. The reporting intervals suggested in the PMEM plan are also acceptable to the GMO Panel.
The EFSA GMO Panel found no environmental safety concerns stemming from the import and processing of maize DAS-40278-9. Accidental release of viable GM maize grains is not expected to increase the establishment or spread of feral DAS-40278-9 plants, unless they encounter 2,4-D or AOPP herbicides. Even in such cases, the environmental impact would be comparable to conventional maize. The theoretical possibility of horizontal gene transfer from DAS-40278-9 to bacteria was also deemed not to pose an environmental safety risk. Furthermore, the GMO Panel did not consider interactions between maize DAS-40278-9 and the biotic or abiotic environment to be a significant concern, given the application's scope. The applicant's post-market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plan and its reporting schedule align with the intended uses of DAS-40278-9 and the GMO Panel's guidelines for GM plant PMEM.
The EFSA GMO Panel evaluated maize DAS-40278-9 for import, processing, and food/feed use under Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. Molecular characterization data for DAS-40278-9 indicated no safety concerns.
No further food and feed safety evaluation was needed for maize DAS-40278-9, as its compositional, agronomic, and phenotypic differences were deemed insignificant.
The GMO Panel determined that maize DAS-40278-9 is as safe and nutritious as its non-GM counterparts and reference varieties. No concerns were raised regarding the potential toxicity or allergenicity of the new AAD-1 protein. Furthermore, the genetic modification did not appear to significantly alter the overall allergenicity of the maize. The nutritional content of food and feed from DAS-40278-9 is anticipated to be comparable to non-GM maize.
The EFSA GMO Panel determined that a scientific evaluation of potential environmental impacts from cultivating maize DAS-40278-9 was unnecessary, as the application specifically excludes its cultivation. The panel found the probability of environmental effects from the accidental dispersal of viable DAS-40278-9 seeds to be extremely low. Furthermore, the theoretical, albeit improbable, horizontal gene transfer from DAS-40278-9 maize to bacteria was not identified as an environmental safety concern. Given the application's limited scope, the GMO Panel did not consider interactions between DAS-40278-9 maize and the biotic or abiotic environment to be a significant issue. The proposed post-market environmental monitoring plan and its reporting schedule align with the intended uses of DAS-40278-9 maize and the GMO Panel's guidelines.
The GMO Panel concluded that maize DAS-40278-9 is as safe as its non-GM counterparts and other conventional maize varieties. This assessment, based on the provided information, addresses Member State concerns regarding potential impacts on human and animal health, as well as the environment, within the application's defined scope.
