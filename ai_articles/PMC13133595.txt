The integration of patient perspectives, particularly through patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs), is increasingly recognized as crucial for enhancing healthcare quality, especially within oncology. These measures facilitate improved patient-healthcare provider communication and enable individualized care, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes. This necessitates research methodologies capable of accounting for the diverse sociodemographic and clinical backgrounds of patients, and artificial intelligence (AI) offers a promising avenue for automating and accelerating the identification of PROMs and PREMs.

This study aimed to quantify the prevalence of PROMs and PREMs in clinical oncology studies, comparing a traditional expert-based identification method with an AI-enriched approach.

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted utilizing the ClinicalTrials.gov database, focusing on oncology studies registered between 2012 and 2022. Two distinct methodologies were employed for PROM and PREM identification. The first, a traditional expert-based method, involved an algorithm that searched for PROMs and PREMs from a predefined list of 346 oncology-specific PROMs and PREMs (sourced from the PROQOLID database, Mapi Research Trust) and/or 11 generic PROM- and PREM-specific terms. The second, an AI-enriched method, leveraged a bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) model, which was trained on 2399 expert-labeled outcomes. Algorithm performance was assessed by comparing their outputs against expert decisions. Studies were categorized based on their inclusion of PROMs/PREMs, and logistic regression was used to identify influencing factors. A named entity recognition (NER) model was employed to pinpoint frequently utilized measures.

The analysis encompassed 24,491 studies. The traditional expert-based algorithm identified 7,549 (31%) studies incorporating at least one PROM or PREM, whereas the AI-enriched algorithm identified 8,029 (33%) such studies. Both methods indicated an increasing trend in PROM/PREM usage from 2012 to 2022 (P<.001). The AI-enriched algorithm demonstrated superior performance, achieving 90% accuracy (95% CI 88%-92%), significantly outperforming the expert-based algorithm's 84% accuracy (95% CI 82%-86%) (P<.001). Breast and digestive cancers collectively accounted for nearly 50% of all oncology studies utilizing PROMs and PREMs, with the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) being the most prevalent instrument. As anticipated, trials in phases 2 to 4 exhibited a higher frequency of PROM/PREM inclusion compared to preclinical or early phase 1 studies (odds ratio [OR] 1.8, 95% CI 1.1-2.8 for phase 2; OR 3.6, 95% CI 2.3-5.8 for phase 3; and OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.6-4.4 for phase 4). In observational studies, cross-sectional and prospective designs incorporated PROMs and PREMs more frequently than retrospective studies (OR 4.6, 95% CI 3.3-6.4 and OR 3.2, 95% CI 2.5-4.1, respectively).

This study concludes that an AI-enriched algorithm surpasses traditional expert-based methods in accurately identifying PROMs and PREMs within oncology research. The synergistic combination of expert-labeled data and AI facilitates scalable, automated monitoring of clinical trials, thereby supporting efficient research, informing stakeholders, enhancing patient-centered decision-making, and offering potential for extension to other disease areas and databases.

The evolution of healthcare systems has increasingly emphasized the significance of patient perspectives in improving service quality, particularly in oncology. The reliability of data is paramount for evaluating these services. While much data originates from electronic health records, patient-centered measures (PCMs) are gaining prominence. PCMs, such as PROMs and PREMs, are validated tools that directly capture patient information, offering insights into their experiences and outcomes. Healthcare providers utilize PCMs for various purposes, including screening, monitoring, evaluating care, assessing treatment side effects, and aiding decision-making. These measures are typically collected via questionnaires, either paper-based or online, and capture either outcomes (e.g., symptom evolution) or experiences (e.g., satisfaction with treatment duration). Scientifically validated questionnaires are termed PROMs or PREMs, while ad hoc items are developed for specific study purposes. These patient-reported data complement biological and clinician-reported outcomes, such as physical examinations, to optimize patient care planning.

PROMs are standardized questionnaires that quantitatively assess health outcomes directly from patients, without clinician interpretation. These include symptoms, functional status, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and mental well-being, measured at specific time points. Initially developed for research, PROMs now support clinical decision-making, evaluate treatment efficacy in drug trials, prioritize patients for surgery, compare outcomes across providers, stimulate HRQoL improvement, and assess clinical practices. PROMs are categorized as generic or specific. Generic PROMs, like the EQ-5D questionnaire, measure broad health concepts applicable across diverse patient populations, enabling comparisons. The EQ-5D assesses five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, with severity levels evolving from 3L to 5L for enhanced sensitivity. Specific PROMs, such as the EORTC QLQ-C30 for cancer patients or the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) for fatigue, target particular patient groups or conditions. Additional modules, like the EORTC QLQ-LC13 for lung cancer, further refine these measures. Over 300 PROMs have been identified in oncology alone.

In contrast to PROMs, PREMs focus on patients' experiences and are increasingly favored for assessing healthcare quality, exemplified by the validated Kidney PREM questionnaire. PREMs gather information on patient satisfaction with service delivery or their experiences with therapy. Regulatory bodies, including the US FDA and France's Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS), have incorporated PROMs and PREMs into their quality and safety indicators to encourage their use in clinical trials and routine practice. However, the lack of standardized nomenclature for PROMs and PREMs poses a significant challenge to their identification. Natural language processing (NLP) tools may offer a solution. It is therefore essential to develop methods for identifying and tracking PROM and PREM usage in clinical research, both in terms of frequency and instrument selection. We hypothesized that an AI-enriched algorithm would outperform an expert-based algorithm in this task. This cross-sectional study aimed to estimate the proportion of oncology clinical studies incorporating PROMs and PREMs and to characterize these studies using both traditional expert-based and AI-enriched approaches. The performance of both algorithms was evaluated against a manually labeled subsample validated by human consensus.

Our retrospective cross-sectional study utilized data from previously documented clinical studies to comprehensively assess the prevalence and characteristics of PROMs and PREMs in oncology trials from 2012 to 2022. This design was chosen due to the public availability of all study data in the ClinicalTrials.gov registry, allowing for large-scale analysis without new data collection and providing a snapshot of PROM/PREM inclusion. Data were extracted from the "What is the study measuring?" section of study information pages using the 'trials' R package. Inclusion criteria were: availability in ClinicalTrials.gov, study start date between January 1, 2012, and January 1, 2022, oncology focus, and at least one described outcome. Studies lacking outcome descriptions were excluded. Multiple outcomes from a single study were extracted individually. As the study relied solely on public registry data, participant non-participation was not applicable.

Two distinct methods were employed for PROM and PREM identification: an expert-based algorithm and an AI-enriched algorithm. Additionally, a subset of outcomes was manually labeled by human consensus (indicating PROM/PREM presence or absence). This human-labeled dataset served as both training data for the AI-enriched algorithm and the gold standard for evaluating both algorithms' performance.

A comprehensive list of oncology-specific PROMs and PREMs, along with generic PROM- and PREM-related terms, was compiled. First, 346 oncology PROMs and PREMs, including their full names and acronyms, were extracted from the Patient-Reported Outcome and Quality of Life Instruments Database (PROQOLID). Second, two independent groups of three experts developed a list of generic PROM- and PREM-related terms. Through consensus, a final list of 11 generic terms was established. PROM-specific terms included "quality of life," "QoLd," "patient-reported outcome," "psychometric," "patient perspective," "questionnaire," "health related quality of life," and "hrql." PREM-specific terms were "satisfaction," "experience," and "quality of care."

The expert-based algorithm utilized this combined list of 346 oncology-specific PROMs/PREMs and 11 generic terms. It identified outcomes containing PROMs and/or PREMs by searching for these specific terms within the outcome descriptions of oncology studies extracted from ClinicalTrials.gov.

A randomly selected subset of study outcomes was manually labeled by 16 independent experts (not involved in generic term identification). This subset was balanced to include both positive (15% with PROMs/PREMs) and negative (2% without PROMs/PREMs) outcomes, as initially identified by the expert-based algorithm. Experts were divided into eight pairs, each assigned a random sample for labeling. They were instructed to identify descriptions containing a PROM or PREM, defined as a named questionnaire (acronym or full name) directly administered to patients to assess an outcome or care experience. Descriptions mentioning PROM/PREM use without specifying the instrument, ad hoc questionnaires, or clinician-reported outcomes were excluded. Experts were blinded to their partners' identities and labels during initial labeling. Disagreements (initial Cohen κ 0.59) were resolved through detailed discussion and literature review to achieve 100% consensus, establishing the gold-standard labels. Experts remained blinded to the expert-based algorithm's classification throughout this process. While initial agreement was moderate, the final gold standard is fully consensual and reliable.

The AI-enriched algorithm was a text classification model built using bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT). This model was trained on the expert-consensus labeled outcome descriptions (containing or not containing PROMs/PREMs). To ensure balanced classes, a corresponding subset of manually labeled outcomes was used. The dataset was partitioned into 61% for training (2399 samples), 12% for validation (479 samples), and 27% for testing (1065 samples). Using BERT Tokenizer, each textual element was converted into a token and then encoded as a 512-dimensional numerical vector, capturing semantic and contextual information. Model training was performed using the Trainer from the Hugging Face Transformers library. Multiple models were generated by adjusting batch size and learning rate, and their performance was evaluated on the validation subset using accuracy, recall, and F1-score. The model with the highest accuracy was selected.

To evaluate the performance (accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity) of both the expert-based and AI-enriched algorithms, their results were compared against the manually labeled expert consensus dataset (gold standard). This evaluation was conducted on the same performance evaluation subset that was randomly selected during the AI-enriched algorithm's dataset construction.

Each study was classified as either using or not using at least one PROM or PREM, based on the AI-enriched algorithm (or the expert-based algorithm, as detailed in Multimedia Appendix 1). Given the distinct characteristics of observational and interventional studies, analyses were stratified by study type. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate potential associations between PROM/PREM use and other study characteristics, including cancer site, intervention type, clinical trial phase, randomization type, model type, and time perspective.

The ClinicalTrials.gov database, being an open-label field, presented a wide variety of cancer site names, even for identical cancers. To enhance data homogeneity and accuracy, cancer sites were recoded. Initially, k-means clustering grouped cancer sites based on name similarities for each clinical study. The ten most frequent words within each cluster were used to name the clusters. These cluster names (cancer sites) were then used in a subsequent few-shot classification task, employing the SetFit model from Hugging Face. For each cancer site, eight representative sentences were carefully chosen from the dataset, forming a final set of 116 texts. This dataset trained a text classification model, which then classified all cancer sites in the dataset, achieving 96% accuracy.

While the primary AI-enriched algorithm detected the presence of PROMs or PREMs, it did not identify specific instruments. To determine the most frequently used PROMs and PREMs, we utilized SKEP’IA, a previously developed and patented named entity recognition (NER) model. First, over 2500 outcomes containing PROM names were manually annotated using the Doccano open-source tool. Second, these entities were converted into a BIO tagging scheme (beginning, inside, outside) using the spaCy Python library to mark PROM entity boundaries. The NER model was trained on this structured dataset. Third, a BERT-cased model was fine-tuned, with the dataset split into training, validation, and test sets, and hyperparameters optimized with Optuna. Fourth, the final model was evaluated on the test set. This model can recognize any PROM or PREM, whether full text or acronym, and suggest relevant ones based on textual descriptions. SKEP’IA was developed using labeled data from both ClinicalTrials.gov and PubMed. However, the absence of a standardized naming system for PROMs and PREMs limited the exclusive use of SKEP’IA for identifying the most common ones. To address this, a text similarity search approach was implemented using the PROQOLID database. PROMs and PREMs identified by SKEP’IA were compared to the PROQOLID list by calculating text similarity. Finally, both the NER model and the PROQOLID text similarity search were combined.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio software (version 4.4.2; Posit PBC). Descriptive statistics illustrated PROM/PREM use in registered clinical studies, presenting characteristics by numbers and proportions. Continuous variables were analyzed as reported and not categorized. Proportions were compared bivariately using chi-square tests. Missing data were reported and their randomness assessed using a missing completely at random (MCAR) test (RBtest R package). Year-over-year trends in PROM/PREM use were described. Multivariate logistic regression analyzed associations between PROM/PREM use and study characteristics, stratified by interventional or observational study type. Variables from ClinicalTrials.gov deemed relevant were included. Odds ratios (ORs) were presented with forest plots, and statistical significance was assessed using Wald tests (P<.05). Absolute risk translation was not applicable. No subgroup, interaction, or sensitivity analyses were prespecified or conducted, adhering to a predefined analytical framework.

This study did not involve human participants or identifiable human data, thus institutional review board approval, informed consent, and participant remuneration were not applicable. The observational study adhered to STROBE guidelines (checklist in Multimedia Appendix 1). No images or figures in the manuscript or multimedia appendix contain identifiable participant information.

Between January 1, 2012, and January 1, 2022, 28,034 oncology studies were found in ClinicalTrials.gov, with 24,491 having at least one described outcome (Figure 1A). The expert-based algorithm identified 16,954 outcomes using PROMs or PREMs from 7,549 studies, meaning 31% of eligible oncology studies used at least one PROM or PREM (Figure 1B). A subset of 4,770 study outcomes was used to evaluate the expert-based algorithm's performance. Compared to the expert consensus gold standard, it achieved 83% accuracy (95% CI 82%-84%), 86% sensitivity (95% CI 85%-88%), and 81% specificity (95% CI 80%-82%) (Table 1).

The AI-enriched algorithm identified 8,029 studies using at least one PROM or PREM, representing 33% (Figure 1C). In the performance evaluation subset of 1,065 outcomes, against the expert consensus gold standard, the AI-enriched algorithm achieved 90% accuracy (95% CI 88%-92%), 91% sensitivity (95% CI 88%-93%), and 89% specificity (95% CI 86%-92%) (Table 1). Consequently, the AI-enriched algorithm significantly outperformed the expert-based algorithm (chi-square test P<.001) in identifying PROMs and PREMs in oncology studies. Subsequent results focus on characteristics of studies identified by the AI-enriched algorithm; expert-based algorithm results are in Multimedia Appendix 1.

Analysis of year-over-year trends revealed a consistent increase in the proportion of clinical studies incorporating PROMs or PREMs, rising from 27.6% in 2012 to 36.2% in 2021 (Figure S2 in Multimedia Appendix 1).

Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the 24,491 included studies, stratified by PROM/PREM use (n=8029) or non-use (n=16,462), as identified by the AI-enriched algorithm. Of all included studies, 79% (n=19,344) were interventional trials.

Among interventional trials, phase 2 (n=5228, 27%) was the most common stage reporting PROM/PREM use, followed by phase 1 (n=2536, 13.1%), phase 3 (n=1650, 8.5%), and phase 1/2 (n=1528, 7.9%). However, 37.7% (n=7285) of studies had missing data for trial phases (MCAR test indicated missing at random). Randomization was the most frequent allocation type (44.4%, n=8586). Parallel assignment (48.2%, n=9330) and single-group assignment (44.5%, n=8613) were the predominant intervention models. Treatment was the primary purpose for 66.2% (n=12,815) of studies, followed by supportive care (10.6%, n=2058). Among observational studies, cohorts were the most prevalent model (64%, n=3295), with 72.3% (n=3295) being prospective.

Table 2 details key study characteristics for all 24,491 studies, stratified by PROM/PREM use. Characteristics are presented for interventional and observational studies, with proportions compared between studies using and not using PROMs/PREMs. Significant differences were observed across study type, clinical phase, allocation method, interventional model, primary purpose, observational model, and time perspective (all P<.001). MCAR tests indicated that clinical phase data were missing at random, while interventional model, primary purpose, observational model, and time perspective data were missing completely at random.

Of the 24,491 studies, 32.8% (n=8029) incorporated at least one PROM or PREM, as identified by the AI-enriched algorithm. Among these, 84.7% (n=6800) were interventional, and 15.3% (n=1229) were observational. Within interventional studies, phase 2 and phase 3 trials most frequently incorporated PROMs and PREMs, accounting for 19.5% (n=1323) and 12.0% (n=814) respectively. However, 55.4% (n=3767) of interventional studies lacked exploitable data on clinical phases. Interventional studies using PROMs/PREMs were predominantly randomized (n=4401, 64.7%). The main interventional models were parallel assignment (65%, n=4420) and single-group assignment (29.3%, n=1994). Treatment (n=3789, 55.7%) and supportive care (n=1638, 24.1%) were the most frequent primary purposes for interventional studies using PROMs/PREMs. Among observational studies using PROMs/PREMs (n=1229), 67.9% (n=835) were cohort studies, and 12.7% (n=156) were case-only studies. Considering time perspective, prospective studies were the most frequent to incorporate PROMs/PREMs, representing 79.8% (n=981).

Figure S3 in Multimedia Appendix 1 illustrates the proportions of organ-specific oncology studies utilizing at least one PROM or PREM. Breast and digestive cancers collectively accounted for nearly 41% (2818/6918) of all studies employing PROMs or PREMs in oncology. Urogenital, lung, and prostate cancers followed, representing 8% (579/6918), 10% (673/6918), and 11% (756/6918) respectively.

Among the 8029 studies identified by the AI-enriched algorithm as including at least one PROM or PREM, 4823 (61.1%) utilized one or more of the nine most common PROMs or PREMs. The EORTC QLQ-C30 was the most frequently used, appearing in approximately 33.2% (n=1602) of these studies. The FACT-G and EQ-5D-5L questionnaires were next, used in 15.2% (n=734) and 11.5% (n=552) of the analyzed studies, respectively (Table 3). Table 4 categorizes the health dimensions assessed by instruments identified across the 4823 studies using the nine most common PROMs/PREMs. Care-related patient satisfaction was the focus in 40.7% (n=1969) of identified cases, followed by organ-specific quality of life (n=783, 16.2%), and depression/anxiety scales (n=441, 9.1%). This distribution resulted from a zero-shot, multilabel classification model, which often overlapped satisfaction with quality of life, further subdivided into general and cancer-specific categories. No additional filters based on study type or trial phase were applied.

Breast and digestive cancers were most represented in PROM/PREM use in oncology studies, accounting for 22% (1521/6918) and 20% (1307/6918) of cases, respectively. These were followed by prostate (756/6918, 11%), lung (673/6918, 10%), urogenital (579/6918, 8%), and head and neck cancers (408/6918, 6%). In contrast, hematopoietic (193/6918, 3%), brain (118/6918, 3%), thyroid (57/6918, 1%), skin (52/6918, 1%), bone (118/6918, 1%), and neuroendocrine cancers (24/6918, 0.4%) showed less frequent PROM/PREM utilization.

The use of PROMs and PREMs, as identified by the AI-enriched algorithm, varied significantly with several study characteristics (Figure S5A in Multimedia Appendix 1). Among interventional studies, those in phases 2 to 4 incorporated PROMs or PREMs more frequently than preclinical or early phase 1 studies (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.1-2.8 for phase 2; OR 3.6, 95% CI 2.3-5.8 for phase 3; OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.6-4.4 for phase 4). Randomized clinical trials were twice as likely to use PROMs or PREMs compared to nonrandomized trials (OR 2, 95% CI 1.7-2.4). Clinical trials focused on supportive care demonstrated significantly higher odds of using PROMs or PREMs than those with other objectives (OR 4.1, 95% CI 2.7-6.2). Conversely, studies concerning certain organ-specific cancers, such as breast (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.5-0.9), digestive (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.5-0.8), lung (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.4-0.7), hematopoietic (OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3-0.8), and thyroid (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2-0.9) cancers, were less likely to incorporate PROMs or PREMs than non-organ-specific studies. Similarly, PROM/PREM instrument use in observational studies differed based on study characteristics (Figure S5B in Multimedia Appendix 1). Cross-sectional and prospective studies used PROMs or PREMs more often than retrospective studies (OR 4.6, 95% CI 3.3-6.4 and OR 3.2, 95% CI 2.5-4.1, respectively). After adjusting for confounding factors, cohort studies had significantly higher odds of using PROMs or PREMs than case-control studies (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.3-2.1). Additionally, observational studies focusing on organ-specific cancers, including breast (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.5-0.8), digestive (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.3-0.5), head and neck (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.4-0.8), hematopoietic (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.4-0.8), lung (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.3-0.5), thyroid (OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3-0.9), and urogenital (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.3-0.6) cancers, exhibited lower odds of using PROMs or PREMs compared to non-organ-specific studies.

This observational study investigated the capability of an AI-based tool to identify PROM/PREM usage in oncology trials from ClinicalTrials.gov, benchmarked against an expert-based gold standard. Our findings from 24,491 studies demonstrate four key points: (1) an AI-enriched algorithm outperformed an expert-based one in identifying PROMs/PREMs in clinical studies, (2) PROM/PREM usage has increased over time, (3) breast and digestive cancer studies accounted for nearly half of those incorporating PROMs/PREMs, and (4) interventional prospective studies were more likely to use PROMs/PREMs than other study types.

The developed AI-enriched algorithm shows significant promise for researchers seeking to identify both the presence and specific types of PROMs/PREMs in oncology studies. With 90% accuracy, it surpassed manual expert identification in both precision and efficiency. Manual review of 4770 outcomes (representing 890 studies) by 16 highly skilled individuals took several weeks, whereas the algorithm classified all 24,491 studies within minutes to a few hours, depending on computational resources. Although the initial development of the AI-enriched algorithm required substantial investment, this effort yields long-term returns by enabling rapid processing of large datasets. A projected increase in PROM/PREM use in phase 2 to 4 studies is anticipated, driven by growing interest from stakeholders including industry, regulatory bodies, and patient advocacy groups, all committed to value-based healthcare (VBHC). Regulatory agencies like the FDA and HAS encourage PROM/PREM use and have integrated them into quality indicators, though their inclusion in trials is not mandatory. The observed increase in PROM/PREM incorporation in clinical studies supports this optimistic outlook. The AI-enriched tool could be extended to other medical indications, fostering broader research on PCMs. On a larger scale, AI could become an invaluable tool for managing, conducting, and monitoring clinical studies. However, the AI-enriched method carries a risk of misclassifying conceptually similar clinical outcome assessments (e.g., observer-reported or clinician-reported outcomes) as PROMs/PREMs, potentially overestimating their prevalence compared to expert review. Expert-based review, while resource-intensive, offers manual verification for accuracy and contextual understanding.

Among organ-specific studies, PROMs and PREMs were more frequently used in breast and digestive cancers. This trend likely stems from extensive research in these areas due to high prevalence, significant impact on patient quality of life, and the increasing demand for patient feedback as treatments evolve. The diverse patient populations affected by these cancers further emphasize the importance of patient-perceived experiences and outcomes. While not exclusive to these cancers, breast and digestive cancers are prominent in patient-reported outcome research, partly due to their high prevalence and strong advocacy and funding. This visibility has facilitated the integration of VBHC principles, prioritizing patient-centered care. Compared to other cancer sites, breast and digestive cancers feature more prominently in VBHC literature, highlighting a gap for less-studied malignancies. In therapeutic areas where PROMs and PREMs are less common or standardized, the performance gap between AI and expert methods might be smaller, underscoring the importance of context. In our dataset, where PROMs and PREMs were present in 33% of oncology studies, the algorithm achieved strong sensitivity and specificity. However, performance may decrease in contexts with lower or inconsistent PROM/PREM reporting due to their dependence on prevalence. Further studies are needed to assess the robustness and generalizability of AI-driven approaches across diverse conditions beyond oncology.

We also observed that interventional and prospective studies incorporated more PROMs or PREMs than observational or retrospective studies. This trend can be attributed to the growing interest from regulatory bodies and sponsors (e.g., industry, investigators, promoters) in integrating these measures into clinical trials. Regulators, such as the FDA and European Medicines Agency, and Health Technology Assessment bodies, support their use for approvals and decision-making. Some governments have officially recommended PROM/PREM use in interventional studies through guidelines. Interventional studies, by design, have structured protocols for systematic data collection, making them well-suited for PROMs/PREMs. Furthermore, as interventional studies evaluate treatment effects, direct patient insights from PROMs/PREMs are highly relevant. Incorporating PROMs/PREMs into observational studies requires their integration into routine care. Another approach involves leveraging PCMs in registries or real-world data and incentivizing PROM/PREM use through updated funding and publication guidelines. Collaborative efforts, including patient advocacy groups and multidisciplinary teams, can further enhance adoption by emphasizing holistic, patient-centered care and diverse clinical perspectives that value patient-reported measures.

To mitigate potential biases, several measures were implemented. Selection bias was minimized by including all eligible oncology studies from ClinicalTrials.gov. Information bias related to outcome misclassification was reduced through a double-reference approach, training and evaluating the AI-enriched algorithm against an expert-consensus gold standard. Misclassification due to heterogeneous PROM/PREM naming was partially addressed by expert review and consensus labeling during model development. Analytical bias was limited by applying consistent preprocessing and evaluation metrics. However, the absence of prespecified subgroups or sensitivity analyses may limit the exploration of residual heterogeneity.

A significant limitation of our AI-based tool is its susceptibility to false positive or false negative PROM/PREM identification. The model occasionally struggles with multiword PROMs/PREMs (examples in Multimedia Appendix 1). For instance, it might segment "Short Form 36 Health Survey" into separate entities, failing to recognize it as the single, standardized SF-36, leading to non-recognition as a PROM. Additionally, uppercase abbreviations can be misleading; the AI sometimes misidentifies general clinical terms (e.g., "PAIN") as PROMs/PREMs due to their common abbreviation format.

The increased use of PROMs and PREMs in oncology was previously reported by Vodicka et al. (2015), who estimated 29% of oncology trials used PROMs/PREMs between November 2007 and December 2013. Our study identified 33%, potentially due to improved identification methods or an actual increase in usage over time. We utilized the same ClinicalTrials.gov database but with a larger dataset. The primary challenge in identifying PROMs and PREMs stemmed from the lack of standardized naming in ClinicalTrials.gov, leading to varied nomenclature for identical instruments. This observation may explain the data quality issues encountered by our AI-enriched algorithm. Both algorithms (AI-enriched and expert-based) faced this issue, but our AI-enriched algorithm remained more efficient. Therefore, classification heterogeneity appeared to be the major risk of bias in our study. This risk was not assessed; future studies should evaluate the clinical significance of the tool's 10% error rate. Trends in PROM/PREM use and their distribution exhibited less than 100% performance due to cancer misclassification. An AI-based tool supporting researchers in identifying, labeling, and naming PROs could enhance classification consistency and terminology. Our study also faced limitations regarding dataset heterogeneity, reliance on English-language parsing tools, and the absence of full-text scrutiny. A lack of external validation is another limitation; using the entire oncology population from ClinicalTrials.gov necessitates another dataset (different field or database) for generalizability assessment, ideally a large one. Finally, we compared our AI-based algorithm to an expert-based one against a human-based gold standard, but not against other machine learning tools, which is also a limitation. Future algorithms should be benchmarked against ours.

Overall, evidence in oncology suggests that routine PROM/PREM collection enhances patient-provider communication, aids symptom/concern identification, and improves patient satisfaction. However, limited evidence currently demonstrates their impact on healthcare quality or system performance in oncology. Further research is crucial to address these gaps, assess the applicability of this approach across diverse pathologies, and evaluate its adaptability to additional open-access databases. Such investigations would determine the generalizability and scalability of the methodology, facilitating its integration into various healthcare data systems and supporting its widespread application in health data analysis and patient-centered research.

Crucially, this study innovatively combines expert-labeled data with artificial intelligence to automate PROM/PREM identification, overcoming limitations of previous manual or keyword-based approaches. Unlike prior studies, our AI-enriched method enables large-scale, reproducible monitoring of patient-reported measures across thousands of trials, significantly contributing to the field by quantifying trends, highlighting gaps, and identifying areas for improvement in patient-centered data collection.

This approach can assist researchers, regulators, sponsors, and healthcare systems in designing better trials, more easily tracking PROM/PREM integration, informing funding and policy decisions, and ultimately promoting value-based, patient-centered care.

In conclusion, our data unequivocally demonstrate that an AI-enriched algorithm is more accurate, in terms of sensitivity and specificity, and faster than traditional expert-based methods for identifying PROMs and PREMs in oncology clinical studies. PROM and PREM usage is increasing, particularly in interventional and prospective studies. This innovative approach, combining expert-labeled data with AI, enables scalable and reproducible identification of PROMs and PREMs, surpassing previous manual or expert-based methods. We propose that improving PROM/PREM naming and classification, potentially through an AI-based tool to assist researchers, could further facilitate their identification and use. Future studies should extend this methodology to other pathologies and databases, enhancing its utility and supporting broader integration of patient-reported measures in clinical research.
