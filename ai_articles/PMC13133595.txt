The increasing recognition of patient perspectives as crucial for enhancing healthcare quality, particularly in oncology, underscores the importance of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs). These tools facilitate improved patient-provider communication and enable individualized care, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes. This highlights the need for research methodologies that can account for the diverse experiences of patients across varied sociodemographic and clinical backgrounds. To address this, artificial intelligence (AI) offers a promising avenue for automating and accelerating the identification of PROMs and PREMs. This study aimed to quantify the prevalence of PROMs and PREMs in clinical oncology studies using both traditional expert-based and AI-enhanced identification methods. A retrospective cross-sectional analysis was conducted on oncology studies listed in the ClinicalTrials.gov database between 2012 and 2022. Two distinct approaches were employed for identifying PROMs and PREMs. The first, a traditional expert-based method, utilized an algorithm to detect PROMs and PREMs from a predefined list of 346 oncology-specific measures (sourced from the PROQOLID database) and/or 11 general PROM/PREM-related terms. The second, an AI-enriched method, involved a bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) model, which was trained on 2399 expert-labeled outcomes. The performance of both algorithms was assessed by comparing their results against expert classifications. Studies were then categorized by their use of PROMs/PREMs, and logistic regression was applied to pinpoint influential factors. Additionally, a named entity recognition model was used to identify frequently utilized measures. The analysis included a total of 24,491 studies. The traditional expert-based algorithm identified PROMs or PREMs in 7549 studies (31%), whereas the AI-enriched algorithm identified them in 8029 studies (33%). This usage demonstrated an upward trend from 2012 to 2022 (P<.001). The AI-enriched algorithm exhibited superior performance, achieving 90% accuracy (95% CI 88%-92%), significantly outperforming the expert-based algorithm's 84% accuracy (95% CI 82%-86%) (P<.001). Notably, breast and digestive cancers collectively accounted for nearly 50% of all oncology studies incorporating PROMs and PREMs, with the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 being the most commonly employed measure. As anticipated, trials in later phases (2 to 4) more frequently included PROMs and PREMs compared to preclinical or early phase 1 studies (odds ratio [OR] 1.8, 95% CI 1.1-2.8 for phase 2; OR 3.6, 95% CI 2.3-5.8 for phase 3; and OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.6-4.4 for phase 4). Within observational studies, cross-sectional and prospective designs showed a higher inclusion rate of PROMs and PREMs than retrospective studies (OR 4.6, 95% CI 3.3-6.4 and OR 3.2, 95% CI 2.5-4.1, respectively). In conclusion, this study demonstrates that an AI-enriched algorithm surpasses traditional expert methods in identifying PROMs and PREMs within oncology research. The integration of expert-labeled data with AI facilitates scalable and automated monitoring of clinical trials, thereby supporting efficient research, informing stakeholders, and promoting patient-centered decision-making. This methodology holds potential for broader application across various diseases and databases.

Healthcare systems increasingly acknowledge the importance of patient perspectives in enhancing service quality. While much healthcare data originates from electronic health records, patient-centered measures (PCMs) are crucial for further improving care. PCMs, encompassing patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs), gather direct patient input to understand their priorities. Healthcare providers use PCMs for various clinical applications, including screening, monitoring treatment effects, evaluating care effectiveness, and informing treatment decisions. These measures are typically collected via questionnaires, which can be paper-based or digital. They capture either the outcomes of care, such as symptom changes, or the patient's experience with care, like the duration of a specific treatment. Questionnaires with scientifically validated items are termed PROMs when they assess outcomes and PREMs when they assess experiences. Conversely, questionnaires developed for a specific, immediate purpose are considered ad hoc. These patient-reported data complement traditional biological measures and clinician-reported outcomes, such as physical examinations, to facilitate more comprehensive patient care planning. PROMs are standardized questionnaires that quantify health outcomes directly from patients, covering aspects like symptoms, functional status, health-related quality of life, and mental well-being, without clinician interpretation. Initially used in research, PROMs now support clinical decision-making, evaluate treatment efficacy in drug trials, prioritize patients for surgery, compare provider outcomes, and assess healthcare practices. They fall into two categories: generic, which allow comparisons across diverse conditions (e.g., EQ-5D), and specific, tailored to particular patient groups or conditions (e.g., EORTC QLQ-C30 for cancer patients). Over 300 PROMs exist within oncology alone. In contrast, PREMs focus on patient experiences and serve as a key metric for evaluating healthcare quality, exemplified by tools like the Kidney PREM questionnaire. They gather information on patient satisfaction with service delivery or their experiences with therapies. Regulatory bodies, such as the US FDA and France's Haute Autorité de Santé, increasingly advocate for PROM and PREM use in clinical trials and routine care by integrating them into quality and safety indicators. However, the lack of standardized nomenclature for PROMs and PREMs presents a significant challenge for their consistent identification.
Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are standardized questionnaires designed to quantitatively capture health outcomes directly from patients at specific junctures. These instruments gather data on various aspects, including symptoms, functional capabilities, mental well-being, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), without requiring interpretation by clinicians or other parties. While initially developed for research, PROMs now serve diverse applications, such as informing clinical decision-making, evaluating treatment efficacy in drug trials, prioritizing patients for surgical interventions, comparing outcomes across healthcare providers, fostering HRQoL improvements, and assessing healthcare practices. PROMs are broadly categorized into two types: generic and specific. Generic PROMs assess concepts applicable to a wide patient population, facilitating comparisons across different health conditions. An example is the EQ-5D questionnaire, which evaluates five health dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. This instrument uses severity levels, initially three (EQ-5D-3L) and later expanded to five (EQ-5D-5L) in 2010, to enhance sensitivity and mitigate ceiling effects. In contrast, specific PROMs are tailored to particular patient groups or conditions. For instance, the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) specifically measures HRQoL in cancer patients, making it condition-specific. Similarly, the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) is concept-specific, focusing solely on fatigue. Beyond core questionnaires like the EORTC QLQ-C30, additional modules have been developed to address symptoms and outcomes unique to various cancers, such as the EORTC QLQ-LC13 for lung cancer or the EORTC QLQ-H&N35 for head and neck cancer. In oncology alone, over 300 distinct PROMs have been identified.
Patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) offer a distinct focus from patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), specifically capturing patients' experiences and satisfaction with healthcare delivery or treatment plans. These measures, exemplified by instruments like the Kidney PREM questionnaire, are increasingly recognized as crucial for evaluating healthcare quality. Regulatory bodies, such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and France's Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS), have begun incorporating PROMs and PREMs into their quality and safety indicators, actively promoting their integration into clinical trials and routine practice. Despite this growing recognition, a significant challenge remains: the lack of standardized nomenclature for PROMs and PREMs. This absence of consensus complicates their identification within research, underscoring the need for advanced methods. Natural language processing (NLP) tools show promise in addressing this issue. Consequently, developing a robust solution to identify and track PROMs and PREMs in clinical research is essential, not only to quantify their prevalence but also to understand the specific instruments being utilized. This study hypothesized that an artificial intelligence (AI)-enriched algorithm would surpass traditional expert-based methods in accurately identifying PROMs and PREMs within clinical research. To test this, a cross-sectional study was conducted to determine the proportion of oncology clinical studies that incorporate PROMs and PREMs, and to characterize these studies. The investigation employed two distinct approaches: a conventional expert-based method and an AI-enriched approach. The performance of both algorithms was rigorously assessed by comparing their findings against a manually labeled subsample, which was validated through a human-based consensus to establish a gold standard.
This retrospective cross-sectional study investigated the prevalence and characteristics of Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) within oncology trials conducted between 2012 and 2022. The study design leveraged existing, publicly available data from the ClinicalTrials.gov database, specifically the "What is the study measuring?" section of each study's information page. This approach allowed for the analysis of a substantial dataset without the need for new data collection, providing an efficient snapshot of PROM and PREM inclusion across numerous studies. Inclusion criteria for studies were: accessibility in the open-access ClinicalTrials.gov database, a start date within the specified 2012-2022 timeframe, a focus on oncology, and the presence of at least one described outcome. Studies lacking an outcome description were excluded. For studies with multiple outcomes, each was extracted individually. As the research exclusively utilized publicly available registry data, participant nonparticipation was not a relevant factor. The use of the trials R package facilitated data extraction from the ClinicalTrials.gov database.
We employed two methods to identify PROMs and PREMs in oncology study outcomes: an expert-based algorithm and an AI-enriched algorithm. A subset of outcomes was manually labeled by human consensus, serving two purposes. First, this consensus data trained the AI algorithm. Second, it established a "gold standard" for evaluating the performance of both the expert-based and AI-enriched identification methods.
To identify relevant measures, a comprehensive list of oncology-specific Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) was compiled. This began with extracting 346 PROMs and PREMs, including their full names and acronyms, from the PROQOLID database. Subsequently, two separate groups of three experts each developed lists of generic terms associated with PROMs and PREMs. These expert groups then reached a consensus to finalize a combined list of these generic terms. The PROM-specific terms included "quality of life," "QoLd," "patient-reported outcome," "psychometric," "patient perspective," "questionnaire," "health related quality of life," and "hrql." For PREMs, the identified terms were "satisfaction," "experience," and "quality of care."
The expert-based algorithm identified PROMs and PREMs in oncology study outcomes from ClinicalTrials.gov. It searched outcome descriptions for 346 oncology-specific PROMs/PREMs and 11 generic terms. This method aimed to detect the inclusion of these patient-reported measures within the study data.
To establish a gold standard for evaluating PROM/PREM identification, a subset of study outcomes underwent manual labeling by 16 independent experts. These experts were not involved in the initial identification of generic PROM/PREM terms. The selection of this subset was carefully balanced to include both positive and negative cases: 15% of outcomes previously identified as containing PROMs or PREMs by the expert-based algorithm, and 2% of outcomes without PROMs or PREMs. The 16 experts were organized into eight pairs, with each pair assigned a distinct, random portion of the selected subset for labeling. Their task was to determine if each outcome description contained a PROM or a PREM. For an outcome to be classified as containing a PROM or PREM, its description needed to explicitly name a patient-administered questionnaire (either by acronym or full name) that assessed either a patient outcome or a care experience. Descriptions that merely mentioned the use of a PROM or PREM without specifying which one, those employing ad hoc questionnaires, or those referencing clinician-reported outcome measures were explicitly excluded from this classification. Throughout the initial labeling phase, experts operated independently, unaware of their partner's identity or their labeling decisions. After this individual assessment, the experts were unblinded and engaged in a consensus-building process for any disagreements, which initially showed a Cohen's kappa of 0.59. This initial agreement, though moderate, served as a starting point. For every instance of disagreement, experts meticulously discussed the discrepancies and consulted relevant scientific literature until a unanimous consensus was achieved. This rigorous process ensured that all final labels were 100% agreed upon by both experts and firmly rooted in verified evidence, thereby establishing a fully consensual and reliable gold standard for evaluation. Importantly, the experts remained unaware of the classifications generated by the expert-based algorithm during this entire manual labeling and consensus process. This meticulous approach to manual labeling, despite its resource-intensive nature, was crucial for creating a robust and unbiased reference dataset against which the performance of both the expert-based and AI-enriched algorithms could be accurately measured.
A text classification model, built using bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT), formed the core of the AI-enriched algorithm. This model was trained on outcome descriptions that had been manually labeled by expert consensus, indicating the presence or absence of PROMs or PREMs. To ensure balanced classes (an equal number of positive and negative examples), a specific subset of these labeled outcomes was employed. The dataset was divided into three parts: 61% for training (2399 samples), 12% for validation (479 samples), and 27% for testing (1065 samples). The BERT Tokenizer converted each word or text element into a token, which was then encoded as a 512-dimensional numerical vector. This vector effectively captured the semantic and contextual information within the text. Model training was performed using the Trainer from the Hugging Face Transformers library. During this process, various models were generated by adjusting batch size and learning rate to optimize performance. Each model's performance was then evaluated against the validation subset, using accuracy, recall, and F1-score to measure its classification ability. Ultimately, the model with the highest accuracy score was selected as the best performer.
Algorithm performance (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity) was assessed by comparing expert-based and AI-enriched algorithm results against a gold standard dataset. This gold standard comprised outcomes manually labeled by expert consensus, using the same randomly selected subset as the AI algorithm's performance evaluation dataset.
Studies were categorized by whether they utilized at least one PROM or PREM, employing either an AI-enriched or expert-based algorithm. Given their distinct characteristics, observational and interventional studies were analyzed separately. Multivariate logistic regression investigated potential links between PROM/PREM use and factors like cancer site, intervention type, clinical trial phase, randomization, model type, and time perspective. This approach allowed for a nuanced understanding of how these variables influenced the incorporation of patient-reported measures.
To standardize the diverse and open-label cancer site entries within the ClinicalTrials.gov database, a multi-step classification process was implemented to improve data homogeneity and accuracy. Initially, k-means clustering grouped similar cancer site names from each clinical study. The ten most frequent words within each resulting cluster were then used to assign a representative name to that cluster. These newly defined cancer site categories subsequently informed a few-shot classification task, leveraging the setfit model from Hugging Face. For each cancer site, eight carefully chosen, representative sentences from the dataset formed a final collection of 116 texts. This curated dataset served as the training material for a text classification model. Once trained, this model was applied to categorize all cancer sites in the dataset, ultimately achieving a 96% accuracy in its classification. This systematic approach addressed the inherent variability in cancer site reporting, enabling more consistent and reliable analysis.
To identify the specific PROMs and PREMs most frequently employed in clinical research, a named entity recognition (NER) model, SKEP’IA, was utilized. This patented model, developed by our team, was designed to go beyond simply detecting the presence of PROMs or PREMs, instead pinpointing their exact names within text. The development of SKEP’IA involved a multi-stage process. Initially, over 2500 outcomes containing PROM names were manually annotated using Doccano, an open-source annotation tool. These annotations then underwent conversion into a BIO tagging scheme using spaCy, an open-source Python library for natural language processing. This tagging method precisely marked the beginning, internal segments, and boundaries of PROM entities within the text, forming the structured dataset upon which the NER model was trained. Following this, a BERT-cased model was fine-tuned. The dataset was systematically divided into training, validation, and test sets, with hyperparameters optimized using Optuna to enhance model performance. The final model's efficacy was then rigorously evaluated on the test set. This refined model demonstrated the capability to recognize any PROM or PREM within a given text, irrespective of whether it appeared as a full name or an acronym, and could suggest relevant measures based on textual descriptions. SKEP’IA's training data originated from labeled information extracted from both ClinicalTrials.gov and PubMed. A significant challenge in determining the most commonly used PROMs and PREMs arose from the lack of a standardized and official naming convention for these measures. To overcome this limitation, a text similarity search approach was integrated, leveraging the PROQOLID database. PROMs and PREMs identified by SKEP’IA were compared against a pre-existing list from PROQOLID by calculating their textual similarity. This combined methodology, integrating the NER model with the PROQOLID database similarity search, allowed for a more robust and accurate identification of specific PROMs and PREMs in research.
R Studio software (version 4.4.2; Posit PBC) facilitated all statistical analyses. We employed descriptive statistics to characterize the use of PROMs and PREMs in registered clinical studies, presenting study features as numbers and proportions. Continuous variables were analyzed as recorded in the registry, without categorization. Bivariate comparisons of proportions utilized a chi-square test. Missing data were documented in descriptive tables, with their randomness assessed via a missing completely at random (MCAR) test (RBtest R package) and results noted in table footnotes. We also detailed annual trends in PROM and PREM usage. To explore potential links between PROM/PREM use and study characteristics, multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, stratified by interventional or observational study subgroups. Model variables were drawn from the ClinicalTrials.gov database and deemed pertinent to the study. Odds ratios (ORs) were visualized using forest plots, and Wald tests determined the statistical significance of each predictor. Given that associations were expressed as ORs, conversion to absolute risk was not appropriate. A P value below .05 indicated statistical significance. This study adhered to a predefined analytical framework, thus no subgroup, interaction, or sensitivity analyses were pre-specified or performed.
This observational study, adhering to STROBE guidelines, did not involve human participants or identifiable human data. Consequently, institutional review board approval, informed consent, and participant remuneration were not required. Furthermore, no images or figures within the manuscript or its multimedia appendix contain information that could identify individuals, ensuring privacy and ethical conduct throughout the research.
From 2012 to 2022, 28,034 oncology studies were identified in the ClinicalTrials.gov database. Of these, 24,491 studies provided at least one described outcome. A traditional expert-based algorithm was applied to this dataset to identify the inclusion of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) or patient-reported experience measures (PREMs). This method found that 7,549 studies, representing 31% of the eligible oncology studies, utilized at least one PROM or PREM. To assess its effectiveness, the expert-based algorithm's performance was evaluated against a manually labeled "gold standard" from a subset of 4,770 study outcomes. This comparison revealed the algorithm achieved an accuracy of 83% (95% CI 82%-84%), a sensitivity of 86% (95% CI 85%-88%), and a specificity of 81% (95% CI 80%-82%).
The AI-enriched algorithm successfully identified at least one PROM or PREM in 8029 studies, accounting for 33% of the total. Its performance was evaluated against a manually labeled expert consensus, demonstrating superior accuracy. Specifically, within a subset of 1065 outcomes, the AI algorithm achieved 90% accuracy (95% CI 88%-92%), 91% sensitivity (95% CI 88%-93%), and 89% specificity (95% CI 86%-92%). This significantly outperformed the expert-based algorithm (P<.001) in identifying PROMs and PREMs in oncology research. Subsequent findings in this study are based solely on the AI-enriched algorithm's identifications, with expert-based results detailed in Appendix 1.
Clinical studies incorporating PROMs or PREMs steadily increased from 27.6% in 2012 to 36.2% in 2021. This trend indicates a growing integration of patient perspectives in research over time.
The 24,491 studies analyzed were categorized by their use of PROMs/PREMs, with 8,029 studies incorporating them and 16,462 not. Interventional trials constituted 79% (19,344) of all studies. This categorization, based on the AI-enriched algorithm, highlights the prevalence of interventional designs.
Interventional trials frequently incorporated PROMs or PREMs, with Phase 2 studies being the most common (27%, n=5228), followed by Phase 1 (13.1%, n=2536), Phase 3 (8.5%, n=1650), and Phase 1/2 (7.9%, n=1528). Notably, 37.7% (n=7285) of these studies had missing data for trial phases, though this was deemed missing at random. Randomization was the most common allocation method, present in 44.4% (n=8586) of interventional studies. The primary intervention models were parallel assignment (48.2%, n=9330) and single-group assignment (44.5%, n=8613). Treatment was the main purpose for 66.2% (n=12,815) of studies, with supportive care following at 10.6% (n=2058). For observational studies, cohort designs were most prevalent, accounting for 64% (n=3295), and 72.3% (n=3295) were prospective.

The AI-enriched algorithm identified that 32.8% (n=8029) of all analyzed studies included at least one PROM or PREM. The majority of these studies (84.7%, n=6800) were interventional, with observational studies comprising the remaining 15.3% (n=1229). Within interventional studies utilizing PROMs and PREMs, phase 2 and phase 3 trials were most prevalent, accounting for 19.5% (n=1323) and 12.0% (n=814) respectively. However, over half (55.4%, n=3767) of these interventional studies lacked clear data on their clinical phases. Most interventional studies incorporating PROMs and PREMs were randomized (64.7%, n=4401). Common interventional models included parallel assignment (65%, n=4420) and single-group assignment (29.3%, n=1994). The primary objectives for these interventional studies were predominantly treatment (55.7%, n=3789) and supportive care (24.1%, n=1638). Among observational studies that used PROMs or PREMs (n=1229), cohort studies represented the largest proportion at 67.9% (n=835), followed by case-only studies at 12.7% (n=156). Furthermore, prospective studies were the most frequent type of observational study to incorporate PROMs or PREMs, accounting for 79.8% (n=981).
The AI-enriched algorithm revealed that breast and digestive cancers collectively comprised nearly 41% of all oncology studies (2818 out of 6918) that incorporated at least one Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (PROM) or Patient-Reported Experience Measure (PREM). Following these, prostate cancers represented 11% (756/6918) of such studies, lung cancers 10% (673/6918), and urogenital cancers 8% (579/6918). These proportions, illustrating the distribution of PROM/PREM usage across different organ-specific oncology studies, are detailed further in Figure S3 of Multimedia Appendix 1.
Of the 8029 studies identified by the AI-enriched algorithm as incorporating at least one Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (PROM) or Patient-Reported Experience Measure (PREM), 4823 (61.1%) utilized one or more of the nine most prevalent PROMs or PREMs. The European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) emerged as the most frequently employed instrument within oncology clinical studies, appearing in approximately 33.2% (n=1602) of the analyzed studies. Following this, the FACT-G and EQ-5D-5L questionnaires were the subsequent most common, found in 15.2% (n=734) and 11.5% (n=552) of the studies, respectively (Table 3). Further analysis of these 4823 studies, which employed the most common PROMs/PREMs, categorized the health dimensions assessed by these instruments (Table 4). A significant proportion, 40.7% (n=1969), of the identified PROMs or PREMs primarily concentrated on patient satisfaction related to care. Organ-specific quality of life was another prominent focus, accounting for 16.2% (n=783) of the measures, while scales addressing depression and anxiety constituted 9.1% (n=441). This observed distribution of health dimensions is a direct outcome of the zero-shot, multilabel classification model used, which frequently grouped satisfaction with quality of life. The quality of life category was subsequently refined into general and cancer-specific subcategories. These proportions were derived without applying any additional filters based on study type or trial phase.
The use of PROMs and PREMs varied across different cancer sites in clinical studies. Breast and digestive cancers demonstrated the highest representation, comprising 22% (1521/6918) and 20% (1307/6918) of cases, respectively. Prostate cancer followed at 11% (756/6918), with lung cancer at 10% (673/6918), urogenital cancer at 8% (579/6918), and head and neck cancers at 6% (408/6918). Conversely, PROMs and PREMs were less frequently employed in studies concerning hematopoietic (3%, 193/6918), brain (3%, 118/6918), thyroid (1%, 57/6918), skin (1%, 52/6918), bone (1%, 118/6918), and neuroendocrine cancers (0.4%, 24/6918).
The AI-enriched algorithm revealed variations in PROM and PREM utilization across different study characteristics. Interventional studies, for instance, showed a higher propensity to incorporate PROMs and PREMs in later phases. Specifically, phase 2 studies were 1.8 times more likely (95% CI 1.1-2.8), phase 3 studies 3.6 times more likely (95% CI 2.3-5.8), and phase 4 studies 2.6 times more likely (95% CI 1.6-4.4) to include these measures compared to preclinical or early phase 1 studies. Randomized clinical trials also exhibited a twofold increase in PROM and PREM usage compared to nonrandomized trials (OR 2, 95% CI 1.7-2.4). Furthermore, clinical trials focused on supportive care demonstrated significantly greater odds of integrating PROMs and PREMs than those with other objectives (OR 4.1, 95% CI 2.7-6.2). Conversely, certain organ-specific interventional trials were less likely to use PROMs and PREMs. This included studies on breast (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.5-0.9), digestive (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.5-0.8), lung (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.4-0.7), hematopoietic (OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3-0.8), and thyroid (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2-0.9) cancers, compared to non-organ-specific studies. Observational studies also displayed distinct patterns in PROM and PREM incorporation. Cross-sectional (OR 4.6, 95% CI 3.3-6.4) and prospective (OR 3.2, 95% CI 2.5-4.1) studies utilized PROMs and PREMs more frequently than retrospective studies. After accounting for confounding factors, cohort studies had significantly higher odds of using these measures than case-control studies (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.3-2.1). Similar to interventional studies, several organ-specific observational trials showed reduced odds of PROM and PREM use, including those focusing on breast (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.5-0.8), digestive (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.3-0.5), head and neck (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.4-0.8), hematopoietic (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.4-0.8), lung (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.3-0.5), thyroid (OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3-0.9), and urogenital (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.3-0.6) cancers, when compared to non-organ-specific studies.
This observational study assessed an AI-based tool's effectiveness in identifying PROMs and PREMs within oncology trials from ClinicalTrials.gov, benchmarking it against an expert-based standard. Analyzing 24,491 studies, the findings revealed that an AI-enriched algorithm surpassed traditional expert methods in identifying PROMs or PREMs. Furthermore, the integration of PROMs and PREMs in clinical studies has shown a consistent increase over time. Notably, nearly half of all studies incorporating these measures focused on breast and digestive cancers. The research also indicated that interventional prospective studies demonstrated a higher propensity for utilizing PROMs or PREMs compared to other study types. This highlights the AI tool's superior performance and the evolving landscape of patient-reported measures in oncology research.
Our newly developed AI-enriched algorithm offers a highly promising solution for researchers seeking to identify the presence and specific types of Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) within oncology studies. This tool demonstrates superior efficiency and accuracy compared to traditional manual methods. For instance, classifying 4770 outcomes, representing 890 studies, took several weeks with a team of 16 skilled experts. In stark contrast, our algorithm processed the entire dataset of 24,491 studies in mere minutes, ranging from 10 minutes to 3 hours depending on computational resources. While the initial development of this AI-enriched algorithm demanded substantial investment in time and specialized expertise, this upfront effort yields significant long-term returns by enabling much faster processing of large datasets than human-centric approaches. The increasing adoption of PROMs and PREMs in clinical research, particularly in Phase 2 to 4 studies, is a notable trend, driven by heightened interest from various stakeholders. This includes industry, regulatory bodies, and patient advocacy groups, all committed to advancing value-based healthcare (VBHC) practices. Regulatory agencies like the FDA and HAS now encourage the appropriate use of PROMs and PREMs, integrating them into quality and safety indicators, though their inclusion in clinical trials remains non-mandatory. Our findings, which highlight a growing incorporation of these measures in clinical studies, further reinforce this positive outlook. The AI-enriched algorithm's utility is not limited to oncology; it can be adapted for other medical conditions, thereby fostering broader research into patient-centered measures. More broadly, artificial intelligence is poised to become an indispensable asset for medical stakeholders in managing, conducting, and monitoring clinical studies. However, it is crucial to acknowledge potential limitations of the AI-enriched method, specifically the risk of misclassifying conceptually similar clinical outcome assessments, such as observer-reported or clinician-reported outcomes, as PROMs or PREMs. This could lead to an overestimation of their actual prevalence when compared to expert-based reviews. Conversely, expert-based reviews, while more resource-intensive, provide the benefit of manual verification by trained professionals, ensuring greater accuracy and contextual understanding. Despite this, the AI-enriched algorithm's ability to process vast amounts of data with high accuracy represents a significant advancement in the field.
Studies on breast and digestive cancers showed a higher frequency of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) compared to other organ-specific cancer research. This prevalence is likely due to several factors. These cancer types are highly prevalent, significantly impacting patients' quality of life, which necessitates extensive research and patient feedback as treatments advance. The diverse demographics of patients affected by breast and digestive cancers, encompassing various ages, genders, and ethnicities, further emphasize the need to consider their experiences and outcomes. While not exclusive to these cancer sites, breast and digestive cancers are among the most thoroughly investigated regarding patient-reported outcomes. This is largely attributable to their high incidence rates and the robust support from advocacy groups and dedicated research funding. This heightened visibility has been instrumental in integrating Value-Based Health Care (VBHC) principles, which prioritize patient-centered care in medical research. Consequently, breast and digestive cancers are more prominently featured in VBHC literature than other cancer sites, indicating a research gap for less-studied malignancies. It is also plausible that in therapeutic areas where PROMs and PREMs are less common or standardized, the performance difference between AI-driven and expert-based identification methods might be less pronounced. This highlights the crucial role of context in interpreting such findings. In our dataset, where 33% of oncology studies included PROMs and PREMs, the AI algorithm demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity. However, the algorithm's performance could diminish in settings where PROMs and PREMs are reported less frequently or consistently, as sensitivity and specificity are influenced by the prevalence of the measured outcome. Therefore, future research is essential to evaluate the reliability and broader applicability of AI-driven approaches across various medical conditions beyond oncology.
Interventional and prospective studies demonstrate a higher integration of Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) compared to observational and retrospective designs. This increased adoption is largely driven by regulatory bodies and sponsors, including industry and investigators, who are increasingly interested in incorporating these measures into clinical trials. Organizations like the FDA, European Medicines Agency, and Health Technology Assessment bodies advocate for PROMs and PREMs to support approvals and decision-making, with some governments officially recommending their use in interventional studies. Interventional studies are inherently well-suited for PROMs and PREMs due to their structured protocols, which facilitate systematic data collection. The direct patient insights provided by these measures are particularly valuable in interventional studies, where the goal is to assess the impact of specific treatments. Conversely, integrating PROMs and PREMs into observational studies often necessitates their inclusion in routine care or leveraging patient-centered measures from registries and real-world data sources. To further encourage their widespread use, incentives for incorporating PROMs and PREMs could be implemented through revised funding and publication guidelines. Collaborative efforts, such as involving patient advocacy groups and multidisciplinary teams, are also crucial. These groups often champion patient-centered care and bring diverse clinical perspectives that highlight the significance of patient-reported data.
To mitigate bias, several strategies were employed. Selection bias was minimized by analyzing all eligible oncology studies from ClinicalTrials.gov, not a sample. Information bias from outcome misclassification was reduced by training and evaluating the AI algorithm against an expert-consensus standard. Inconsistent PROM and PREM naming, a source of misclassification, was partially addressed through expert review and consensus labeling during model development. Analytical bias was contained by using a consistent preprocessing pipeline and evaluation metrics across all studies. However, the lack of prespecified subgroup or sensitivity analyses might restrict the investigation of remaining variability.
A significant limitation of the AI tool is its potential for misidentifying patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs). The model occasionally struggles with multiword PROMs or PREMs, failing to recognize them as single entities. For example, "Short Form 36 Health Survey" might be incorrectly parsed into separate components like "Short Form" and "Health Survey," preventing its accurate identification as the standardized SF-36 PROM. Furthermore, the AI can be misled by uppercase abbreviations. Given that many PROMs and PREMs are often abbreviated in all capital letters, the model sometimes erroneously classifies general clinical terms, such as "PAIN" (a symptom), as a PROM or PREM. This can lead to both false positives and false negatives, impacting the accuracy of PROM/PREM identification.
Our study identified a 33% prevalence of PROMs and PREMs in oncology trials, a figure slightly higher than the 29% reported by Vodicka et al. (2015) for the period of November 2007 to December 2013. This difference could stem from either an actual increase in the adoption of PROMs and PREMs over time or improvements in our identification methodology. Both studies utilized the ClinicalTrials.gov database, but our analysis encompassed a larger number of studies. A significant challenge in accurately identifying PROMs and PREMs lies in the inconsistent classification within the ClinicalTrials.gov database. The absence of standardized nomenclature means that researchers and healthcare providers often use varied names for the same PROMs and PREMs. This lack of standardization likely contributed to the lower data quality encountered by our AI-enriched algorithm. While both our AI-enriched and expert-based algorithms faced this issue, the AI-enriched approach demonstrated superior efficiency. Consequently, classification inconsistencies emerged as a primary source of potential bias in our study. We did not quantify the clinical significance of this bias; future research should investigate whether the observed 10% error rate in our tool is clinically meaningful. Beyond the challenges in PROM/PREM identification, the trends in their use over time and their distribution across oncology trials also exhibited performance rates below 100%, partly due to misclassification of cancer types. To mitigate such biases, an AI-driven tool could be developed to assist researchers in identifying, labeling, and naming PROs, thereby fostering greater consistency in classification, terminology, and acronym usage. Our study also had several limitations related to the dataset and methodology. First, the heterogeneity of the registries included presented a challenge. Second, our reliance on English-language parsing tools restricted the scope of our analysis. Third, our approach did not involve a comprehensive full-text scrutiny of the studies, which might have led to missed information. Another limitation is the lack of external validation. Since our study utilized the entire population of oncology studies from the ClinicalTrials.gov database, external validation would necessitate applying our methodology to a different dataset. This could involve studies from a non-oncology field or from an alternative database, ideally a large one, to assess the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, our comparison was limited to an expert-based algorithm against a human-based gold standard. We did not include comparisons with other machine learning-based algorithms, which represents another limitation. Future research should test newly developed algorithms against the one presented here. Despite these limitations, our study highlights the potential of AI in overcoming the challenges associated with identifying and classifying PROMs and PREMs in clinical research. The observed increase in PROM/PREM usage underscores their growing importance in oncology, emphasizing the need for robust and efficient identification methods. Addressing the issues of standardization and external validation will be crucial for further advancing the application of AI in this domain and ensuring the widespread and accurate integration of patient-reported measures into clinical practice and research.
In oncology, the routine collection of Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) is understood to improve patient-provider communication, aid in identifying symptoms and concerns, and boost patient satisfaction. Despite these recognized benefits, concrete evidence demonstrating their impact on overall healthcare quality or system performance within oncology remains limited. Consequently, further research is crucial to bridge these knowledge gaps. This includes evaluating the applicability of this methodology across various pathologies and assessing its adaptability for integration with additional open-access databases. Such investigations are vital for determining the generalizability and scalability of the approach, thereby facilitating its widespread adoption in diverse healthcare data systems and supporting its potential for broader application in health data analysis and patient-centered research.
This study introduces an innovative AI-enriched method for identifying patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs). By integrating expert-labeled data with artificial intelligence, this approach automates the process, overcoming the limitations of previous manual or keyword-based methods. This enables large-scale, reproducible monitoring of patient-reported measures across numerous trials, thereby quantifying trends, identifying gaps, and suggesting improvements in patient-centered data collection.
This method aids researchers, regulators, sponsors, and healthcare systems. It improves trial design, simplifies PROM/PREM tracking, informs funding/policy, and advances value-based, patient-centered care.
This study concludes that an AI-enhanced algorithm surpasses traditional expert methods in accurately and rapidly identifying patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) within oncology clinical trials. The integration of expert-labeled data with AI offers a novel, scalable, and reproducible method for identifying these measures, a significant improvement over prior manual or keyword-based approaches. The research also highlights an increasing trend in PROM and PREM utilization, especially in interventional and prospective studies. To further improve their identification and application in research, better standardization of PROM and PREM naming and classification is recommended, potentially through new AI-powered tools. Future research should explore applying this methodology to other diseases and databases to broaden its impact in clinical research.
