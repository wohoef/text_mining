This retrospective study investigated the risk factors and complications associated with forgotten double-J stents (DJSs) in postoperative urology patients. Ureteral stents are sometimes necessary for patients following urological surgery to aid urine drainage and prevent obstruction, but delayed removal or replacement can lead to severe issues. The study analyzed 479 patients who had DJSs inserted. DJSs were categorized into 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month maximal stent life (MSL) groups based on their brand. A forgotten DJS was defined as one not removed or replaced within two weeks past its MSL. Data collected included the reasons for DJS insertion, as well as the timing and method of placement. The most frequent indication for DJS insertion was urolithiasis, accounting for 69.7% of cases. Ureterorenoscopy was the predominant method of insertion, used in 86.2% of patients. A total of 18 patients (3.8%) had forgotten DJSs, with an average delay of 63.17 days beyond their MSL. Multivariate analysis identified two significant risk factors for forgotten DJSs: patients over 60 years of age (odds ratio [OR] = 3.626) and DJSs exchanged via fibrocystoscopy (OR = 5.437). Among the 18 patients with forgotten DJSs, three (16.67%) developed symptomatic complications. These included one case of acute pyelonephritis and two cases of stone encrustation. The findings indicate that individuals over 60 are substantially more likely to forget their DJSs, as are those whose stents were exchanged using fibrocystoscopy compared to ureterorenoscopy. Consequently, enhanced tracking and recall mechanisms are recommended for high-risk patient groups to mitigate the occurrence of forgotten DJSs and their associated complications.

Ureteral stents, or double-J stents (DJSs), are sometimes necessary for postoperative urology patients to aid urine drainage, facilitate stone removal, and prevent obstruction. As our hospital currently lacks fully biocompatible and biodegradable DJS options, these stents require eventual surgical removal or replacement. Unfortunately, some patients forget their scheduled appointments, leading to overdue DJSs. This oversight can result in serious complications such as stone formation, stent fragmentation, infections, and migration. Severe encrustation and stone buildup can cause hydronephrosis and subsequent renal impairment. Addressing these complications often necessitates additional treatments like extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy and endourological procedures, which carry risks of kidney failure, increased costs, and diminished patient quality of life.
Our hospital currently provides patients and their families with paper-based instructions and precautions regarding double-J stents (DJSs). These documents detail the stent's insertion side, the insertion date, and the recommended timeframe for removal or replacement. A signed acknowledgement slip confirms the patient's or family's understanding, noting only the insertion date and medical record number. Despite this method being in use for over two decades, forgotten DJSs continue to occur periodically, highlighting the need to address ureteral stent management as a patient safety concern. Consequently, this study focused on identifying patient profiles at higher risk of forgetting their DJSs and the complications that arise from such instances.
This retrospective observational cohort study was approved by the IRB. We analyzed data from patients over 20 years old who had DJS inserted at our hospital between June 1, 2017, and August 31, 2018. Initial identification yielded 518 participants, excluding those with insertions outside the hospital or by radiology. Patients who had died (25), had DJS removed elsewhere (13), or experienced home stent displacement (1) were excluded. Consequently, 479 patients formed the final study group.
Data collected on patients as of October 31, 2018, included various demographic details such as age, sex, marital status, and education level. Researchers also recorded the reason for DJS placement, the method and time of insertion, and the maximal stent life (MSL). Information on the current DJS status, including whether it was due for change or removal, the duration of any overdue period, and the stated reason for forgetting the DJS, was gathered. Following the identification of patients with forgotten DJSs, their presence or absence of complications was documented. These patients were then contacted to schedule a follow-up appointment. Subsequently, analyses were conducted on the average indwelling time of the stents and the proportion of patients who delayed addressing their forgotten DJSs. The MSLs for the DJSs were determined based on their material type and manufacturer, aligning with the hospital's clinical practices and patient diagnoses. Specifically, certain Cook and Bard stents had a 3-month MSL, while other Bioteq and Bard stents had a 6-month MSL. Cook Medical's black silicone stents were assigned a 12-month MSL. For the purposes of this study, a forgotten DJS was defined as one that remained in place for more than 14 days past its designated MSL, excluding instances where delays were due to medical treatment or scheduled surgery.
Logistic regression analysis in SPSS for Windows identified risk factors for forgotten DJSs. Correlations between overdue periods and complications were explored using an independent sample t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with all tests conducted two-sided.
A total of 479 patients were analyzed, with a mean age of 57.69 years. The majority, 51.1%, were male. Most patients (87.5%) received a unilateral DJS insertion, while 12.5% had bilateral insertions. Urolithiasis was the predominant reason for DJS placement, accounting for 69.7% of cases. Other indications included stricture/polyposis/angulation (18%), external compression (5.6%), and genitourinary cancer (2.5%). DJS insertion was most frequently performed via ureterorenoscopy (86.2%), with fibrocystoscopy being the second most common method (11.5%). The majority of patients (87.5%) were assigned to the 3-month maximal stent life (MSL) group.
A significant majority of patients, 80.8% (387/479), had their double-J stents (DJSs) removed. Among the remaining 19.2%, 16 patients received new DJS insertions, and 76 underwent DJS replacements. The average duration the DJSs remained in place was 42.5 days. Notably, 8.8% (42/479) of patients had overdue DJSs. Of these, 11 patients (2.3%) had already addressed the issue after a phone notification, explicitly stating they had forgotten about their overdue stents. The other overdue patients, excluding three lost to follow-up, had proactively removed or replaced their DJSs before being contacted.
A total of 18 patients, representing 3.8% of the study population, had forgotten double-J stents (DJSs), meaning they exceeded their maximal stent life (MSL) by at least two weeks. These forgotten stents had an average overdue period of 63.17 days, ranging from 18 to 189 days. Initial univariate analysis identified several factors associated with forgotten DJSs. These included being over 60 years old (OR = 4.448), being illiterate (OR = 5.517), and having DJSs exchanged via fibrocystoscopy (OR = 4.475). Further multivariate analysis confirmed significant predictors of forgotten DJSs. Patients older than 60 were 3.6 times more likely to forget their DJSs compared to those 60 and younger. Additionally, DJSs exchanged using fibrocystoscopy were 5.4 times more likely to be forgotten than those inserted via ureterorenoscopy. Notably, six of the forgotten DJSs were exchanged via fibrocystoscopy, and these were all cases scheduled for routine replacement at least twice.
Among the 18 patients with forgotten double-J stents (DJSs), symptomatic complications arose in three individuals (16.67%). One patient, whose 12-month maximal stent life (MSL) DJS remained in place for approximately 15 months, developed acute pyelonephritis necessitating hospitalization and intravenous antibiotics. The remaining two patients experienced stone encrustation. In one instance, a DJS with a 3-month MSL was left in for about 4 months, leading to stone formation. This required percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) for stone removal, followed by further encrustation on the stent coils that necessitated combined endoscopic cystolithotripsy (ESCL), ureterorenoscopic lithotripsy, or PCNL for DJS removal. The third patient, who had a 6-month MSL DJS for stenosis and endoureterotomy, experienced encrustation on the lower coil after a 20-day delay in removal (total indwelling time around 6.7 months), which was treated with ESCL. The other patients with forgotten DJSs did not encounter significant complications and had already arranged for their stent's replacement or removal. Statistical analysis revealed no significant correlation between the duration the DJS was left in place and the occurrence of complications (p = 0.655).
Prior research has established varying definitions for forgotten double-J stents (DJSs). For instance, Tang et al. and Monga et al. considered DJSs forgotten after 6 months of use, while Ziemba et al. based their definition on the planned removal date. In contrast, this study categorized DJSs into 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month maximal stent life (MSL) groups. The majority of DJS insertions, specifically 69.7%, occurred following urolithiasis procedures, typically resulting in shorter indwelling periods. Consequently, some DJSs were assigned a 3-month MSL, a duration shorter than manufacturer recommendations, to create a safety margin for patients who might exceed this timeframe. This approach aligns with findings from Farsi et al. and Joshi et al., who indicated that prolonged DJS indwelling times correlate with increased bacterial colonization, potentially leading to urinary tract infections. Therefore, reducing the MSL to 3 months was deemed a sensible measure to mitigate the occurrence of such infections.
The management of forgotten double-J stents (DJSs) presents significant challenges and incurs increased medical costs. Previous research highlights the potential for severe complications. For instance, a study by Monga et al. on 12 patients with forgotten DJSs (indwelling for over six months) found that a substantial majority experienced calcification (68%) and DJS fragmentation (45%). Similarly, Park et al. observed that among nine patients with DJSs in place for over a year, all developed pyuria, with three experiencing severe encrustation, three minor encrustation, and three none. However, these studies did not detail DJS materials or maximal stent life (MSL), limiting the ability to correlate complications with the duration of the overdue period. Further investigations into stone encrustation have identified contributing factors. Sighinolf et al. determined that proximal coil encrustation was linked to a history of urolithiasis and recurrent stone formation, while distal coil encrustation was associated with urinary tract infections and patient age, though no link to DJS indwelling time was found in their study. Conversely, Kartal et al. reported that 73.9% of 69 patients with DJSs for over six months experienced encrustation, with a longer indwelling time correlating with a greater burden. Jain et al. echoed these findings, noting that prolonged DJS indwelling and a prior history of stones were associated with increased encrustation. In the present study, two patients developed stone encrustation, both with a history of urolithiasis and indwelling times of 200 and 123 days, respectively. Despite these instances, no statistical correlation was found between the overdue period and the occurrence of complications in this cohort.
Studies report a wide range of forgotten double-J stent (DJS) incidence, from 3% to 51%, likely due to varying definitions of maximal stent life (MSL). Even with a paper-based notification system, our study found a 3.8% rate of forgotten DJSs, highlighting the need for robust tracking and recall mechanisms. For instance, Tang et al. reported a 5.4% overdue DJS rate with a 6-month MSL, despite using a stent card registry. Their system's effectiveness was limited by reliance on theater logs for identification and insufficient regular data reviews, hindering the prevention of forgotten DJSs.
Previous research has explored various methods for tracking and reminding patients about their double-J stents (DJSs). Ather et al. pioneered the use of integrated computerized programs for patient tracking and reminders, successfully lowering the incidence of forgotten DJSs from 12.5% to a range of 1.2%-1.5%. Similarly, Lynch et al. developed a web-based stent registry with an automatic recall feature, finding that 51% of patients exceeded their maximal stent life (MSL), with an average overdue period of 20.89 days. In comparison, our study observed that 8.8% of patients exceeded their MSL, but only 3.8% were classified as having forgotten DJSs, with a significantly longer average overdue period of 63.17 days. This suggests that implementing a recall system can effectively reduce the occurrence of forgotten DJSs. Another study by Ziemba et al. utilized a mobile point-of-care application, achieving an even lower rate of overdue stents at just 3%. While these technological solutions demonstrate promise in reducing forgotten DJSs, current systems often require manual database management and patient notification, introducing the potential for human error. Future efforts aim to integrate recall systems with tools like apps, QR codes, and calendar applications to create two-way reminder mechanisms, further minimizing the incidence of forgotten DJSs.
Previous research on the risk factors for forgotten double-J stents (DJSs) is limited, with only two studies specifically addressing this issue. Jain et al. conducted a univariate analysis comparing patients with forgotten DJSs to a control group, identifying significant differences in insurance coverage. Their findings indicated that patients with forgotten DJSs were more likely to be covered by Medicaid or have no insurance at all. In contrast, the present study's participants were all covered by Taiwan's National Health Insurance program, thus precluding an examination of insurance-related factors. Further investigation by Divakaruni et al. using multivariate analysis revealed that uninsured individuals were six times more prone to forgetting their DJSs compared to insured patients. This study also found that males were 2.8 times more likely than females to forget their stents, but no associations were found with variables such as age, English proficiency, or marital status. In the current study, patient instructions and precautions, including the overdue date, were provided in Chinese. The ability to read these instructions and return for timely treatment was crucial. While illiteracy was significantly associated with forgotten DJSs in univariate analysis, this association did not hold in the multivariate analysis. This aligns with Divakaruni et al.'s findings, suggesting that language proficiency is not a significant predictor of forgotten DJSs. A review of 102 studies by Jin et al. explored the relationship between various factors and compliance. Their findings regarding elderly patients were mixed; some studies suggested better compliance in older individuals, while others indicated poorer compliance due to age-related declines in vision, hearing, and memory. The present study found a significant association between age and forgotten DJSs, with individuals over 60 years old being 3.6 times more likely to forget their stents. This highlights the importance of clearly communicating the necessity of scheduled DJS removal to elderly patients or ensuring their family members are aware and can provide reminders.
This study is the first to investigate the relationship between double-J stent (DJS) insertion methods and the likelihood of a stent being forgotten. Findings indicate that patients are more prone to forgetting DJSs when these stents are inserted or exchanged using fibrocystoscopy. Specifically, six patients with forgotten DJSs had undergone replacement via fibrocystoscopy. These individuals were scheduled for regular DJS exchanges, but by the time they presented for follow-up, their stents had already surpassed their maximal stent life (MSL). This situation may stem from patients forgetting the appointment or from physicians not adequately assisting with scheduling or reminders. Therefore, it is crucial for both patients to actively monitor their DJS replacement schedule and for physicians to proactively remind patients of upcoming visits or arrange replacements.
This study's retrospective design presents several limitations. The accuracy of the findings is contingent upon the quality of previously recorded case data, precluding retrospective examination of factors like private insurance coverage or employment status. Furthermore, as a single-center study primarily involving patients from northern Taiwan, the results may be subject to regional bias. The specific reasons for forgotten double-J stents (DJSs) were not thoroughly investigated. While it was confirmed that 11 patients notified by phone missed their scheduled return visits due to forgetfulness, the motivations behind forgotten DJSs in other patients who either resolved the issue or were lost to follow-up remain unclear. However, existing literature suggests that forgetting is a common reason for prolonged DJS indwelling times, often exceeding six months or a year. Finally, stone analysis was not conducted to characterize the composition of encrustations found on DJSs. Previous research indicates that these encrustations commonly comprise calcium oxalate and phosphate, with whewellite being a frequent component.
Older patients (over 60) are significantly more prone to forgetting their double-J stents (DJSs), being 3.6 times more likely than younger individuals. Furthermore, DJSs inserted via fibrocystoscopy are 5.4 times more likely to be forgotten compared to those placed using ureterorenoscopy. To mitigate this, high-risk patients require tailored reminders, and implementing robust recall systems or utilizing biodegradable stents could decrease forgotten DJS incidents.
