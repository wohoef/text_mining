Colorectal cancer (CRC) arises through a progressive sequence, beginning with normal colon lining and advancing through a pre-malignant adenoma stage to a malignant carcinoma. This carcinoma can invade local tissues and spread to distant sites via metastasis. Crucially, only a small fraction, approximately 5%, of adenomas will ultimately progress to cancer. This study aimed to elucidate the biological mechanisms driving adenoma to carcinoma progression using mRNA expression profiling. A key objective was to identify potential biomarkers that could flag adenomas with a high risk of malignant transformation. To achieve this, genome-wide mRNA expression profiles were generated from 37 colorectal adenomas and 31 colorectal carcinomas using oligonucleotide microarrays. The identified differentially expressed genes were then validated in an independent dataset. Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to pinpoint alterations in the expression of gene sets linked to specific biological processes, thereby deepening our understanding of the progression. The analysis revealed significant differences in mRNA expression for 248 genes between adenomas and carcinomas, with 96 genes showing upregulation and 152 showing downregulation in carcinomas. The expression patterns of these genes proved highly accurate in classifying adenomas and carcinomas, a finding that held true even when tested on an independent dataset. Notably, gene sets associated with aging (and senescence) and chromosomal instability were found to be upregulated in carcinomas, while a gene set related to fatty acid metabolism was downregulated. Additionally, gene sets based on chromosomal location indicated that loss of expression on chromosome 4q22 and gain of expression on chromosome 20q are relevant to this progression. In conclusion, these findings support the view that adenomas and carcinomas represent distinct biological entities. The disruption of critical biological processes, including senescence, the maintenance of chromosomal stability, and altered metabolic pathways, appears to be central to the transition from adenoma to carcinoma.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) poses a significant global health challenge. In Europe, it ranks second for cancer incidence and mortality, often diagnosed late when symptoms appear.
Colorectal cancer (CRC) progresses through distinct stages, beginning with normal epithelium, developing into a pre-malignant adenoma, and finally a malignant carcinoma capable of invasion and metastasis. Although adenomas are readily detectable via colonoscopy, the majority, approximately 95%, do not advance to cancer. Understanding the biological mechanisms driving adenoma to carcinoma progression is therefore essential for pinpointing biomarkers that identify adenomas with a genuine high risk of malignant transformation.
DNA copy number analysis revealed that even benign components of progressed adenomas already possess most chromosomal aberrations found in their invasive counterparts. This suggests that genes within these chromosomal regions are crucial for the adenoma-to-carcinoma progression.
The development of colorectal cancer (CRC) involves numerous genes. Early models, such as the one by Fearon and Vogelstein [6], identified APC, KRAS, TP53, and DCC. More recent research, including sequencing of 11 colorectal cancers by Wood et al. [7], revealed 140 candidate cancer genes (CAN genes) with mutations in CRC. Gene expression can be altered through various mechanisms, including changes in DNA copy number and epigenetic silencing. Studies combining copy number variations with point mutations [8, 9], or mutations with promoter hypermethylation [10], have uncovered further genes and pathways crucial for CRC development. AURKA is a notable example of a gene involved in CRC progression, frequently amplified and overexpressed in CRCs [5, 11, 12], and is currently a target for anti-cancer therapies [13].
Analyzing gene expression using microarrays, a powerful method for simultaneously examining thousands of genes, has been extensively applied to colorectal cancer (CRC). Previous research has utilized this technique to compare carcinomas with normal colon tissue, differentiate between microsatellite instable and stable CRCs, and investigate metastasis mechanisms, recurrence risk prediction, and treatment response. However, studies specifically examining differential gene expression between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas are less common, and often involve a limited number of samples, particularly adenomas.
Building on prior work analyzing specific cancer pathways, this study employed an unbiased approach. Using the same dataset of 37 adenomas and 31 carcinomas previously analyzed by oligonucleotide microarrays, the research aimed to identify genes with altered expression during adenoma-to-carcinoma progression.
To understand the molecular drivers of colorectal adenoma to carcinoma progression, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to identify altered expression in gene sets linked to specific biological processes.
This study analyzed genome-wide mRNA expression profiles from 68 snap-frozen colorectal tumor samples, comprising 37 adenomas and 31 carcinomas. These samples were prospectively collected at the VU University Medical Center in Amsterdam, Netherlands, following ethical guidelines and with informed consent. The cohort consisted of 31 females and 34 males, with a mean age of 69 years. Detailed clinical information for these specimens is provided in Supplementary Table 1. To ensure data quality, a pathologist assessed tumor cellularity on cryo sections, with only samples containing at least 70% tumor cells included. The expression microarray data from this study is publicly accessible through the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE8067.
Comparisons between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas were relative due to dual-channel hybridizations with a common reference.
Carcinomas and adenomas were compared using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. Genes with a p-value below 1e-5 (FDR < 0.1) were identified as differentially expressed across the genome.
To assess classification accuracy, PAM was employed using Leave One Out Cross Validation (LOOCV) on the gene expression signatures. This cross-validation was repeated 100 times. The same methodology was then applied to an independent dataset from GEO (accession GSE20916), which comprised 45 adenomas and 36 carcinomas.
To understand how differential gene expression relates to biological processes, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA v2.0) was employed. GSEA leverages the principle that biological changes often involve coordinated expression shifts within groups of genes, known as gene-sets, that govern specific activities. Unlike individual gene expression changes, which can be subtle and statistically insignificant, GSEA can detect group-wise variations in pre-defined gene-sets, indicating altered pathway activity. The GSEA process involves ranking all genes analyzed by expression arrays based on their differential expression between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas. This ranking is then used to perform a competitive analysis of pre-defined gene-sets. GSEA calculates an Enrichment Score (ES) to determine if genes within a specific gene-set are disproportionately found among the highest or lowest ranked genes, or if their distribution is random. The analysis utilized default settings, with significance thresholds established through 1000 permutation analyses. A False Discovery Rate (FDR) of less than 0.1 (q-values < 0.1) was considered statistically significant, accounting for multiple testing.
Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) utilized 3,732 predefined gene-sets. The analysis incorporated three categories: ontology gene-sets (1,454 total, 1,057 selected based on having at least 15 genes with microarray results), curated gene-sets (1,892 total, 1,434 selected), and gene-sets organized by chromosomal position (386 total, 257 selected).
Key genes for pathway activity were identified as individual genes within cancer-related gene sets exhibiting the most significant differences in mRNA expression between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas. For genes contributing to the enrichment score, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test p-value less than 1e-5 (implying a False Discovery Rate < 0.1) indicated significant differential expression at the single-gene level. This analysis was conducted on microarray data from 37 adenomas and 31 carcinomas.
Analysis of the entire dataset of 28,830 probes revealed significant differences in mRNA expression for 248 genes between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas, with a p-value less than 1e-5 according to the Wilcoxon rank test. Among these, 96 genes were found to be upregulated in carcinomas relative to adenomas, while 152 genes were downregulated. Table 1 presents the top 20 upregulated and downregulated genes, with complete lists provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. The most highly upregulated genes in carcinomas compared to adenomas were Spondin 2 (SPON2), located at chromosome arm 4p16.3, and Regulator of G-Protein signalling 16 (RGS16), found at 1q25.3. Conversely, the most downregulated genes were family with sequence similarity 55, member D (FAM55D) at 11q23.1, and protein kinase, cAMP-dependent, catalytic, beta (PRKACB) at 1p36.1. These findings highlight specific genes whose altered expression may be crucial in the progression from adenoma to carcinoma.
To classify tumors as adenomas or carcinomas, we employed PAM analysis with Leave One Out Cross Validation (LOOCV) on the entire gene dataset. This process consistently identified eight genes that were frequently selected across 100 runs. Notably, all these eight genes were among the 248 differentially expressed genes identified in the study. This included SPON2, the top upregulated gene, and FAM55D, the top downregulated gene, highlighting their significance in distinguishing between these two tumor types.
To assess the predictive power of the identified differentially expressed genes, we evaluated their ability to classify adenomas and carcinomas in an independent dataset. Using the expression data from Skrzypczak and collaborators [34], we matched 169 of the 248 differentially expressed genes. This set of genes achieved a 71% accuracy in distinguishing between adenomas and carcinomas (Table 3). Recognizing that upregulated genes are more practical for clinical biomarker applications, we performed a similar analysis using only the 96 upregulated genes. From this subset, we matched 71 genes. This focused set demonstrated a significantly improved classification accuracy of 89% for adenomas and carcinomas (Table 4). This higher accuracy with upregulated genes suggests their strong potential as biomarkers for clinical use.
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was employed to compare mRNA expression between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas at the pathway level. This analysis utilized three distinct categories of gene sets: ontology gene sets (categorized by biological process, molecular function, and cellular component), curated gene sets (compiled from various sources including pathway databases and expert knowledge), and gene sets organized by chromosomal position (representing individual chromosomes and cytogenetic bands with at least one gene).
Genes with significantly altered individual expression between adenomas and carcinomas, within these biological processes, pathways, or locations, were identified as biomarkers. These key genes reflect the pathway changes during adenoma to carcinoma progression.
Analysis of 1,057 ontology gene sets revealed 21 that were significantly differentially expressed between adenomas and carcinomas, with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.1. All of these gene sets showed increased expression in carcinomas compared to adenomas. The majority of these upregulated gene sets were associated with processes such as chromosome binding, chromosome segregation, mitosis, and the cell cycle. Several key genes were consistently observed within these pathways, including Structural Maintenance of Chromosomes 1A (SMC1A), Aurora Kinase A (AURKA), TPX2 (microtubule-associated, homolog (Xenopus laevis)), Polo-like Kinase 1 (PLK1), Sjogren Syndrome/Scleroderma Autoantigen 1 (SSSCA1), and Polymerase (DNA directed), delta 1 (POLD1). These findings highlight the significant alterations in cellular processes related to chromosome handling and cell division during the progression from adenoma to carcinoma. The consistent upregulation of genes involved in mitosis and the cell cycle underscores their critical role in the transition to a malignant state. The identification of specific key genes within these pathways provides potential targets for further investigation and biomarker development. The table (Table 5) details these gene sets, their FDR values, the direction of differential expression, and the associated key genes with their respective p-values from the Wilcoxon signed rank test. This comprehensive analysis at the pathway level offers a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving colorectal cancer progression.
Two curated gene-sets, "progeria" and "old age," exhibited significantly increased expression in colorectal carcinomas (CRCs) versus adenomas. Conversely, the "fatty acid metabolism" gene-set showed decreased expression, with FDR < 0.1.
Within the progeria and old age pathways, prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2/COX2) showed significantly increased expression in carcinomas compared to adenomas (p < 1.7e-6), identifying it as a key gene for these pathways. Similarly, PLK1 was a significantly upregulated gene (p < 3.7e-7) in the old age pathway, also considered a key gene. For the fatty acid metabolism pathway, Alcohol dehydrogenase 1 C (ADH1C) was significantly downregulated in carcinomas relative to adenomas (p < 4.1e-6) and was therefore identified as a key gene for this pathway.
Chromosomal instability, characterized by alterations in chromosome copy number and structure, is a significant factor in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression. To investigate this, gene sets organized by genomic position were analyzed. Among the 257 such gene sets, those on chromosomes 20q13 and 20q11 showed significantly increased expression in carcinomas compared to adenomas, with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.1. Conversely, the gene set on chromosome 4q22 was significantly downregulated. Key genes within the 20q11 set, identified with a p-value less than 1e-5, included C20orf24 and TPX2. The 20q13 set contained AURKA, BMP7, C20orf20, C20orf11, BCAS4, and RBM38 as key genes. Notably, no individual key gene within the 4q22 gene set exhibited significant differential expression between carcinomas and adenomas.
This study analyzed genome-wide gene expression differences between 37 colorectal adenomas and 31 carcinomas. Building upon previous analyses of this dataset concerning 20q oncogenes and carcinogenic pathways, the current research sought to identify genes with altered expression during adenoma-to-carcinoma progression in an unbiased manner. These identified genes are potential contributors to colorectal cancer (CRC) progression. Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to assess the impact of these expression changes on tumor biology, utilizing three categories of predefined gene sets: ontology-based, curated, and those organized by chromosomal position.
A total of 248 genes exhibited significantly altered mRNA expression between colorectal adenomas and carcinomas (p < 1e-5), with 96 genes showing upregulation and 152 showing downregulation in carcinomas. Some of these genes, such as Frizzled-related protein 4 (SFRP4), AURKA, Bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7), and Centromere protein H (CENPH), have been previously identified as upregulated in colon cancer. Conversely, prostaglandin E receptor 4 (PTGER4) and inhibitor of DNA binding 4 (ID4) were found to be downregulated in cancer, lending support to the reliability of the study's methodology. However, a majority of the identified genes had not been previously reported in expression microarray studies comparing colorectal adenomas and carcinomas. This divergence may be attributed to differences in the platforms and gene selection strategies employed by prior research. Despite this, eight of the 248 differentially expressed genes identified in this study, including two of the most downregulated genes, were also noted to be differentially expressed during CRC progression by Skrzypczak and colleagues.
In carcinomas compared to adenomas, SPON2 and RGS16 showed the highest upregulation. SPON2 functions as an extracellular matrix adhesion molecule, while RGS16 inhibits signal transduction by enhancing G protein alpha subunit GTPase activity. Conversely, FAM55D and PRKACB were the most downregulated genes. While FAM55D's role is largely unknown, PRKACB is implicated in regulating processes such as proliferation, cell cycle, and microtubule dynamics.
Twelve of the top 20 upregulated genes, including SPON2, RGS16, SFRP4, and AURKA, have previously been identified as overexpressed in various epithelial cancers such as colon, prostate, and breast cancer. Similarly, five of the top 20 downregulated genes, such as ENTPD5, PTGER4, and ID4, have been linked to epithelial cancers, including colon cancer. The downregulation of UGT1A6 and PRKACB has also been reported by other researchers. Furthermore, ten genes that showed differential expression between adenomas and carcinomas in this study, including TPX2 and TCFL5 (upregulated), and SPTBN2 and SLITRK6 (downregulated), have previously been found to be mutated in colorectal cancer. This concordance with existing findings supports the validity of the study's methodology.
Several genes identified in this study have been previously investigated, with AURKA notably serving as a target for anticancer therapy. Recent research has highlighted AURKA as a gene with substantial evidence of involvement in human cancer development, particularly in colorectal cancer (CRC), where it has been found to be amplified and overexpressed. Our previous work demonstrated a correlation between AURKA overexpression in CRC and copy number increases in the 20q13.2 genomic region, linking it to chromosomal instability during adenoma-to-carcinoma progression. AURKA's function in centrosome duplication and chromosome segregation is crucial; its aberrant expression can lead to centrosome abnormalities and chromosomal instability, ultimately inducing malignant transformation. Another extensively studied gene is BMP7, which has been implicated in various cancers, including lung, prostate, breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer. BMP7 can function as a tumor suppressor, for instance, by inhibiting cellular motility in lung cancer or promoting growth arrest in cervical cancer. However, in colorectal cancer, BMP7 has been shown to enhance invasive potential and is associated with liver metastasis and poorer patient prognosis.
A set of 8 genes, all among the 248 differentially expressed, were identified by PAM analysis as crucial for optimal classifier performance. When tested on an independent dataset, these genes achieved 71% accuracy in classifying adenomas and carcinomas. This accuracy improved to 89% when considering only the upregulated genes, a promising finding given their potential as clinical biomarkers.
Examining gene expression differences between adenomas and carcinomas revealed that biological processes related to aging, such as progeria and old age, along with ontology-based gene sets for chromosomal instability and chromosomal position gene sets 20q11 and 20q13, were upregulated during colon adenoma to carcinoma progression. As expected, many of the previously discussed differentially expressed genes were identified as key players within these altered pathways.
The link between cancer and aging is well-established, with DNA damage being a significant factor in both. Cellular senescence, a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest, is a hallmark of aging. Early neoplastic DNA alterations, such as KRAS oncogene mutations, can induce this senescence, a phenomenon known as oncogene-induced senescence (OIS). The subsequent bypass of OIS by specific genes can then drive oncogene-mediated transformation and promote tumor progression.
While senescence acts as a tumor suppressor, it can paradoxically fuel malignant progression. Senescent cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines that can damage the surrounding microenvironment. Notably, PTGS2 (COX-2), a key gene in the aging-related gene sets identified in this study, plays a role in inflammation and mitogenesis. PTGS2 is known to be overexpressed in colorectal cancer.
The ontology gene-set analysis highlighted genes crucial for chromosome binding, segregation, mitosis, and the cell cycle as major contributors to the differences observed between adenomas and carcinomas. These biological functions are commonly impaired in tumors exhibiting chromosomal instability. Notably, these findings align with the upregulation of key genes within gene sets located on chromosome 20q (specifically 20q11 and 20q13). This observation supports the established fact that gains on the long arm of chromosome 20 are a frequent chromosomal alteration in colon cancer, and our prior research has linked 20q gain to the progression from colorectal adenoma to carcinoma. Within these 20q regions, eight key genes were identified whose expression significantly differed between adenomas and carcinomas. Four of these genes—C20orf24, AURKA, C20orf20, and RBM38—have previously been implicated by our group in the progression process. The remaining four identified genes were BMP7, TPX2, BCAS4, and C20orf11.
The collagen gene set was also found to be overexpressed in carcinomas compared to adenomas. This highlights the importance of the stroma in the transition from adenomas to invasive cancer, where fibroblast proliferation within the tumor stroma plays a significant role. Prior research by the authors indicated that a gain on chromosome 8q24 is linked to a higher stroma percentage in CRCs with a 20q gain, a crucial step in adenoma-to-carcinoma progression.
Carcinomas showed downregulated gene sets related to fatty acid metabolism and chromosome 4q22 compared to adenomas.
Fatty acid metabolism's role in cancer is multifaceted. It supports cancer development and cell growth by providing essential components for cell and organelle membranes. Furthermore, through beta-oxidation, it interacts with glucose metabolism and hypoxia to supply energy to cancer cells. Oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, which are involved in proliferative and survival signaling pathways, can either stimulate or inhibit these metabolic alterations.
Alcohol dehydrogenase ADH1C is a key gene in the fatty acid metabolism pathway, showing significant downregulation in colorectal carcinomas compared to adenomas. This enzyme produces carcinogenic acetaldehyde from ethanol oxidation. While ADH1C polymorphisms have been linked to CRC risk with alcohol consumption, its precise role in adenoma to carcinoma progression remains unclear based on this study's findings.
The gene set located on chromosome 4q22 was found to be significantly downregulated in colorectal carcinomas compared to adenomas through GSEA. Notably, no individual key gene within this set demonstrated significant downregulation, nor were any 4q22 genes among the top 248 differentially expressed genes. This suggests that subtle alterations at the individual gene level might collectively contribute to the observed downregulation of the entire gene set. While deletions of 4q are present in carcinomas but not adenomas, and no significant gene dosage effects have been identified at this locus, other mechanisms such as mutations or epigenetic regulation may be involved. Previous research has indicated that deletions spanning 4q22.1–4q35.2 are linked to poorer outcomes in stage II colon cancer patients.
This study did not detect significant differences in mRNA expression between adenomas and carcinomas for genes like APC, KRAS, TP53, and DCC, which are historically linked to CRC development. This lack of detection for early-stage genes such as APC or KRAS is expected, as their expression is often already altered in many adenomas. Additionally, technical limitations may contribute to these findings. If a gene's baseline expression is low, a reduction in its expression might produce a signal too subtle to be identified by the analysis.
Comparing whole genome mRNA expression in colorectal adenomas and carcinomas revealed 248 significantly differentially expressed genes, suggesting their involvement in adenoma-to-carcinoma progression. Key findings include altered expression of gene sets on chromosomes 4q22 and 20q. Furthermore, biological processes such as aging (linked to senescence), chromosomal instability, and fatty acid metabolism were found to be differentially expressed during this progression.
Adenomas and carcinomas are distinct biological entities. Key factors in adenoma to carcinoma progression include the disruption of senescence, maintenance of chromosomal instability, and altered metabolism.
