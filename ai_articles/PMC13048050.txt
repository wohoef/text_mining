This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and risks associated with docetaxel-combined induction chemotherapy (IC) in advanced head and neck cancers, a treatment approach that, despite its controversial role in certain cancer types like oral cavity, oropharynx, and sinonasal tract cancers, is utilized for inoperable cases and organ preservation in laryngeal or hypopharyngeal cancers. Taxane-based regimens, while demonstrating improved overall response, are also linked to increased acute toxicity. To achieve this, medical records of 40 advanced head and neck cancer patients treated between 2011 and 2017 were retrospectively analyzed. These patients had undergone an initial tumor assessment, received docetaxel-combined IC, and subsequently completed definitive treatments such as concurrent chemoradiation or surgery. The analysis encompassed demographic information, laboratory findings, overall treatment response, and the occurrence of acute toxicities. The findings revealed that 14 patients (35.0%) achieved at least a partial response. However, a significant proportion, 24 patients (60.0%), experienced at least one acute toxicity exceeding grade III. Statistical analyses, including univariate and multivariate linear regression, identified key predictive markers: a platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) below 8.5 was significantly associated with a better overall response (p < 0.05), while a neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) of 3.5 or higher correlated with a greater likelihood of severe acute toxicity, particularly hematologic side effects, within one month post-IC (p < 0.05). In conclusion, a pretreatment PLR of less than 8.5 can predict a more favorable overall response, and a pretreatment NLR of 3.5 or greater can predict more severe acute toxicity following docetaxel-combined IC. These simple hematological markers could aid in predicting tumor regression and anticipating potential adverse effects of IC.

Globally, head and neck cancer ranks as the seventh most prevalent cancer, predominantly squamous cell carcinoma. Its incidence, frequency, and anatomical sites differ significantly across regions. A substantial majority (two-thirds) of patients present with advanced-stage disease, often affecting regional lymph nodes.
For head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), treatment often involves surgery, radiotherapy, or systemic therapy, used individually or combined, based on the patient's specific situation. While definitive treatments like surgery and concurrent chemoradiation are essential for disease eradication, induction chemotherapy (IC) has gained increasing attention over the last ten years. IC, administered before definitive intervention, has shown improved oncologic outcomes in other cancers, such as gastric, esophageal, and bladder cancers, but its effectiveness in locally advanced head and neck SCCs is still debated.
Research into induction chemotherapy (IC) for advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs) has highlighted the efficacy and toxicity of docetaxel-cisplatin-fluorouracil (TPF) regimens. The TAX-323 and TAX-324 trials in 2007 demonstrated improved survival rates for inoperable cases treated with TPF IC compared to the conventional cisplatin-fluorouracil (PF) regimen. However, these trials also noted an increase in acute toxicities within the first month with TPF. Subsequent meta-analyses reinforced these findings. A 2013 meta-analysis by Blanchard et al., utilizing individual patient data from MACH-NC, reported superior 5-year overall survival, progression-free survival, and both loco-regional and distal control with TPF. While some studies indicated a higher incidence of grade 3-4 neutropenia and febrile neutropenia with TPF, the presence of substantial missing data limited definitive conclusions on the extent of increased toxicity. A 2015 meta-analysis by Xu et al. reached similar conclusions.
For locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), induction chemotherapy (IC) regimens containing taxane may offer improved oncological outcomes compared to PF regimens. This approach has also shown greater success in chemoselection and preserving the larynx in laryngeal and hypopharyngeal SCCs. The docetaxel-PF regimen, specifically, has been recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines since 2016 for both initial and salvage treatment of various advanced head and neck cancers, a recommendation maintained in their 2018 update.
Taxane-containing induction chemotherapy (IC) is associated with significant myelosuppression, often leading to treatment interruptions. Currently, no specific markers exist to predict which patients are at higher risk for these severe acute toxicities. This study aimed to uncover demographic and laboratory indicators that could forecast both the severity of acute toxicities and the efficacy of docetaxel-combined IC.
Patient data from April 2011 to March 2017, excluding nasopharyngeal carcinoma, was retrospectively analyzed from the Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital's Cancer Registry Database. This study received institutional review board approval.
Routine clinical evaluations, encompassing physical exams, pan-endoscopy, and laboratory tests, were performed during initial surveillance and follow-ups. Radiological assessments, such as chest X-rays, head and neck CT/MRI, abdominal sonography, bone scintigraphy, and PET/CT when needed, also contributed to disease status determination. This comprehensive data informed treatment strategy decisions, which were formulated or modified by a multidisciplinary tumor board comprising oncologists, otolaryngologists, maxillofacial surgeons, radiotherapists, and pathologists.
Patients meeting specific criteria, such as advanced stage (III or IV), an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 to 1, inoperability, or the goal of organ preservation, received docetaxel-combined induction chemotherapy (IC) as their initial treatment. The standard regimen at our institution involved 60-75 mg/m2 of docetaxel and 60-75 mg/m2 of cisplatin, optionally including 600-800 mg/m2 of 5-fluorouracil. Each IC course was separated by a three-week interval. Prophylactic antibiotics were not routinely given, only if severe toxicity developed. Following the final IC, patients underwent re-evaluation, including physical exams, pan-endoscopy, and imaging, three to four weeks later to assess treatment response and plan subsequent therapies.
This study included patients who had finished at least one cycle of docetaxel-combined induction chemotherapy and subsequent evaluation. Excluded were individuals with nasopharyngeal cancers, non-squamous cell carcinoma pathologies, pre-existing other cancers, unfinished treatment, or those lost to follow-up. Prior to the initial treatment, and following diagnosis, demographic and laboratory data were collected. This encompassed pretreatment body mass index, complete and differential blood counts, biochemical markers, the specific chemotherapy regimen used, and the observed responses and side effects post-IC. If multiple serum tests were conducted before treatment, the one chronologically nearest to the diagnostic biopsy was selected.
Within one month following induction chemotherapy (IC), treatment efficacy was assessed using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) definitions, encompassing overall response rate, overall survival (OS), and progression-free survival (PFS). This evaluation relied on endoscopy, direct observation, or imaging. Acute toxicities were clinically gauged from the initial IC course through one month post-completion of all courses, adhering to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03. Prophylactic antibiotics were not administered after each IC course; instead, supportive care was provided only when severe toxicity manifested.
Following data collection, ROC curves were generated for each factor to determine optimal cut-off points. Patients were categorized into milder (grade 0-II) or more severe (grade III-V) groups to evaluate acute toxicities. For initial response assessment, patients were classified based on RECIST definitions into complete response, partial response, stable disease, or progressing disease categories.
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software. Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were employed to analyze response and acute toxicity data. A p-value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
This study included 40 patients, predominantly male (95%), with a median age of 55 years (ranging from 38 to 80). Half of the participants received intravenous TPF regimens, while the other half received TP regimens. Most patients presented with locally advanced disease (90% at clinical T3-4) and a significant portion had regionally advanced cancer (72.5% at clinical N2-3). The average number of IC courses administered was 2.18 ± 0.71.
Out of 40 patients, 14 (35.0%) achieved a complete or partial response (CR/PR) to induction chemotherapy (IC), as observed through image studies, physical examination, or pan-endoscopy within one month of completing all IC courses. The remaining 26 patients (65.0%) experienced stable or progressive disease (SD/PD). Univariate analysis identified several pre-chemotherapy hematological factors significantly associated with the overall response to IC. These included a white blood cell (WBC) count of ≥10.3 × 103/μL, a hemoglobin level of ≥14.8 mg/dL, a neutrophil percentage <73.5%, a lymphocyte percentage ≥16.5%, a neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) ≥4.4, and a platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) <8.5. No significant correlations were found with pre-chemotherapy serum biochemistry studies or patient characteristics such as age, gender, BMI, alcohol or betel nut use, smoking, primary cancer site, or cell differentiation.
To determine predictors of overall response after induction chemotherapy (IC), a multivariate linear regression analysis was performed using a stepwise backward method. Factors identified as statistically significant in univariate analysis were included: a white blood cell (WBC) count ≥ 10.3 × 103/μL, a hemoglobin level ≥ 14.8 mg/dL, a neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) ≥ 4.4, and a platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) < 8.5. Of these, only a PLR < 8.5 demonstrated statistical significance for predicting overall response following IC.
During the study period, 24 of 40 patients (60%) experienced acute toxicities exceeding CTCAE v4.03 grade III, with five (12.5%) fatalities due to severe sepsis. Conversely, 35 patients tolerated the induction chemotherapy sufficiently to proceed with definitive treatments.
Univariate analysis revealed several pretreatment factors significantly associated with a higher grade of acute toxicity (beyond grade III). These included a hemoglobin level below 12.2 mg/dL, a neutrophil percentage of 67.5% or more, a lymphocyte percentage below 19.5%, an NLR of 3.5 or greater, a PLR of 15.0 or greater, a serum creatinine level of 1.12 mg/dL or higher, and a potassium level below 3.9 mEq/L. Additionally, alcohol consumption was the sole patient characteristic with statistical significance.
To pinpoint factors significantly increasing severe acute toxicity risk, a multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted. From the chosen variables—hemoglobin <12.2 mg/dL, NLR ≥ 3.5, PLR ≥ 15, creatinine ≥ 1.12 mg/dL, and potassium <3.9 mEq/L—only an NLR ≥ 3.5 emerged as a statistically significant predictor of heightened severe acute toxicity.
Following the finding that NLR ≥ 3.5 could indicate more severe acute toxicities, we investigated its link to specific acute side effects. A statistically significant correlation (p = 0.026) was observed between NLR ≥ 3.5 and more severe acute hematologic toxicities. Within this category, only the risk of grade III-IV anemia showed a significant association (p = 0.006).
Our research identified pretreatment biomarkers for advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients undergoing docetaxel-combined induction chemotherapy. Using ROC curves, we established cut-off values for various parameters, including PLR and NLR, to predict treatment outcomes. Specifically, a platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) below 8.5 was associated with a better overall response, while a neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) of 3.5 or higher indicated a greater risk of severe acute toxicity. These findings offer valuable insights for clinicians to weigh the benefits and risks of induction chemotherapy, guiding more informed treatment decisions.
In cancer patients, a notable reduction occurs in circulating hematopoietic precursors with lymphoid potential, while granulocytic progenitor cells preferentially expand. This dynamic suggests that peripheral blood neutrophil and lymphocyte counts could serve as valuable prognostic indicators, reflecting myeloid-lymphoid interactions. Furthermore, the entry of cancer cells into the bloodstream initiates platelet-mediated recognition, a process amplified by various cellular and extracellular components, including immune cells and cell surface receptors. Consequently, platelet counts are crucial in understanding cancer dissemination. Recently, blood-based neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have gained recognition as significant predictors of treatment outcomes across different anatomical sites, stages, and treatment approaches for head and neck cancer. For instance, Chen et al. found that preoperative PLR was a more effective independent predictor of disease-free survival and overall survival than NLR in patients undergoing oral squamous cell carcinoma resection. Despite these findings, the specific relevance of NLR and PLR to the effectiveness and risks of induction chemotherapy (IC), particularly with docetaxel-combined regimens, remains largely unexplored.
Induction chemotherapy (IC) offers promise for advanced head and neck cancers, particularly in managing microscopic metastasis and curbing tumor growth pre-treatment. It also facilitates chemoselection for organ preservation in locally advanced laryngeal or hypopharyngeal cancers. While platinum-based IC has gained traction as an initial therapy, and TPF regimens have shown improved prognosis over PF in numerous studies, their definitive impact on survival remains debated. Nevertheless, the 2018 NCCN guidelines suggest considering organ preservation for laryngeal or hypopharyngeal SCCs when IC achieves a partial or complete response.
For patients with advanced-stage cancer who cannot or choose not to undergo radical surgery, the overall response to induction chemotherapy (IC) is crucial, as successful organ preservation can significantly improve their quality of life. Our research found that the platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) can predict the overall response to docetaxel-combined IC. This allows clinicians to identify appropriate candidates for organ preservation strategies and avoid unnecessary initial IC treatments for those unlikely to benefit. Previous studies have also explored similar predictive markers. Sato et al. identified pretreatment neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as a predictor of overall response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Consistent with our findings, Georgia et al. showed that a lower pretreatment PLR correlated with a better overall response rate in advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinomas. While the connection between platelet metabolism, cancer dissemination, and antiplatelet therapy is an area of ongoing interest, the benefits and risks of such treatments remain a subject of debate.
Docetaxel-combined induction chemotherapy (IC) can lead to common side effects like alopecia and diarrhea. However, more serious hematological toxicities, including leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, have also been reported, some with fatal outcomes. The combination of leukopenia and sepsis, in particular, can be catastrophic. Such severe adverse effects can impede subsequent vital treatments, like surgery or radiotherapy, aimed at tumor eradication. In fact, acute toxicity, rather than tumor progression, has been the cause of death for many patients. Therefore, anticipating and preventing acute hematological toxicities following docetaxel-based IC is crucial. Despite this, a reliable predictive factor for acute toxicities after docetaxel-combined IC has been lacking. Our study identified a pretreatment Neutrophil-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) of ≥3.5 as a predictor for these acute toxicities. This finding is particularly interesting given that Patrice et al. previously proposed NLR = 3.5 as a cutoff in healthy individuals. An elevated NLR prior to treatment may indicate underlying subclinical inflammation, potentially increasing the risk of post-chemotherapy complications.
Our findings indicate a strong correlation between a pre-treatment Neutrophil-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) of 3.5 or higher and an increased risk of severe hematologic acute toxicities following docetaxel-combined Induction Chemotherapy (IC), particularly Grade III-IV anemia. While existing literature, such as studies by Vermorken et al. and Posner et al., reported Grade III-IV anemia in 9% to 12% of HNSCC patients receiving docetaxel-combined IC, our data showed a higher incidence at 20.0%. Examining patients with pre-existing anemia (n=9), none had a hemoglobin level below 8 mg/dL before treatment; however, five (62.5%) developed Grade III or higher anemia after their initial IC cycle. In contrast, among the 31 patients without pre-treatment anemia, only three (7.5%) experienced Grade III or higher anemia after the first IC. This highlights a significant impact of IC on anemic patients. We hypothesize that chronic malnutrition, a common issue in head and neck SCC patients, might exacerbate the severity of anemia during IC-induced myelosuppression, potentially due to deficiencies in factors like folic acid and iron. Although chronic bleeding or renal dysfunction can also cause severe anemia in this population, they are less frequent. Furthermore, NLR and anemia may serve as indicators of subclinical inflammation and could be co-variates. Nevertheless, additional research is necessary to fully elucidate the precise relationship between NLR and the etiology of anemia in this context.
While inflammation-based scores like NLR and PLR have been widely applied to predict prognosis across various malignancies, more recent prognostic models, such as the Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS), modified GPS (mGPS), and C-reactive protein/albumin ratio (CAR), utilize pretherapeutic serum CRP and albumin levels. A key distinction is that GPS, mGPS, and CAR rely on serum proteins, whereas PLR and NLR are derived solely from blood cell counts. CRP offers a more accurate indicator of acute infection, and albumin levels are superior for identifying cachexia compared to NLR/PLR. Nevertheless, our study aimed to establish a straightforward hematologic model using PLR/NLR to forecast the immediate benefits and risks of induction chemotherapy in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas.
Our retrospective study faced limitations in data collection, preventing us from assessing the relationship between GPS, mGPS, and CAR with overall responses and acute toxicities following docetaxel-combined IC in head and neck SCCs. While Chang et al. showed that pretreatment GPS can forecast treatment tolerance, toxicity, and survival in advanced head and neck cancer patients receiving concurrent chemoradiation, no prior research has explored these correlations specifically for IC with docetaxel.
Despite the elevated risks associated with chemotherapy, a definitive, comprehensive guideline for prophylactic medical interventions is lacking. Patients undergoing chemotherapy are routinely monitored with close follow-up and laboratory data rechecks, enabling early intervention for any abnormalities. To combat chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, antiemetics are administered following high-dose platinum treatments. In cases of severe leukopenia, granulocyte-colony stimulating factor is commonly employed, with continuous observation until white blood cell counts normalize. While most cancer patients on chemotherapy recover without antibiotics, fluoroquinolone proves highly effective for preventing severe diarrhea and sepsis in leukopenic individuals.
Our study suggests that a higher NLR warrants more proactive medical intervention to mitigate severe toxicity. Conversely, a lower NLR could allow for the omission of unnecessary medications, such as antibiotics and iron supplements, thereby reducing costs and potential side effects. However, the limited sample size necessitates further research to fully establish NLR's predictive utility and guide prophylactic treatment decisions.
A simple hematological test can help predict outcomes for patients undergoing docetaxel-combined induction chemotherapy (IC). Specifically, a pretreatment platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) below 8.5 suggests a better overall tumor response. Conversely, a neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) of 3.5 or higher before treatment indicates a greater likelihood of severe acute toxicity, particularly anemia. Identifying these risks and benefits allows for tailored support strategies to optimize IC effectiveness and minimize adverse effects.
