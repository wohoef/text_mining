Clinical trials investigating mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) for treating autoimmune and alloimmune conditions have yielded inconsistent results, lacking a clear mechanistic explanation. This variability may stem from inherent differences in the immunosuppressive capabilities of MSC from different donors. Our research shows that MSC, when activated by tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), exhibit varied suppression of T cell proliferation in vitro. This effect is associated with IFN-γ-induced upregulation of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO). Furthermore, we found that MSC's IDO activity influences monocyte differentiation into IL-10-secreting M2 immunosuppressive macrophages. These monocyte-derived M2 cells then contribute to T cell suppression through an IL-10-independent mechanism, amplifying the overall immunosuppressive effect of MSC. In essence, the immune responsiveness of MSC to IFN-γ and TNF-α varies between donors, with inducible IDO activity playing a crucial role and exerting a bystander effect on other immune cells.

Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) is an intracellular enzyme induced by interferon-γ (IFN-γ). It initiates tryptophan breakdown via the kynurenine pathway. Kynurenine, the initial product, can be further processed into various metabolites, including kynurenic acid, anthranilic acid, and 3-hydroxykynurenine, which in turn yields quinolinic acid and picolinic acid. IDO's role was initially recognized as an antimicrobial defense, as it depletes tryptophan, an essential nutrient for microbial growth. In dendritic cells and macrophages, IDO expression is implicated in inducing immune tolerance, primarily by promoting T cell apoptosis or anergy and fostering the development of regulatory T cells (Treg). Both tryptophan depletion and kynurenine pathway metabolites have been shown to activate the stress response kinase GCN2, leading to T cell arrest and apoptosis. Furthermore, GCN2 activation has differential effects depending on T cell state: it causes arrest and apoptosis in activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, but promotes differentiation into Treg in resting CD4+ T cells.
Previous research has established that both mouse and human mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) can be induced to express indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) upon exposure to interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). Studies investigating MSC immunosuppressive mechanisms have identified IDO as the primary contributor to T cell suppression in human and simian MSC, contrasting with murine MSC which largely utilize nitric oxide. In this study, we utilized an in vitro T cell proliferation model to demonstrate that human MSC from healthy adult donors exhibit varying levels of immunosuppressive capacity. Analysis of MSC from seven distinct donors revealed significant differences in their ability to inhibit T cell proliferation. Our findings further confirm that IDO enzymatic activity is the principal mechanism behind MSC-mediated T cell suppression, with higher IDO expression levels correlating directly with greater suppressive potential. Additionally, we observed that IDO enzymatic activity plays a partial role in prompting the differentiation of monocytes within peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) into IL-10-secreting M2 immunosuppressive macrophages. Experiments involving the removal of monocytes from PBMC, as well as the addition of extra monocytes, corroborated the bystander role of these cells in amplifying MSC-induced T cell suppression.
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) for this research were obtained from bone marrow aspirates of patients undergoing elective hip replacement surgery, with consent. Consequently, the donor pool primarily consisted of females over 60 years old, as detailed in Table 1. MSCs were isolated through adherence to plastic, and their homogeneity was verified via flow cytometry. All MSCs exhibited the characteristic surface markers: CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105, with minor variations in expression levels (Supplementary Figure S1a). Contamination by endothelial and hematopoietic cells was ruled out by the absence of CD31+ and CD45+ cells, respectively. Furthermore, all tested MSCs demonstrated differentiation potential into adipocytes and osteocytes when cultured in appropriate media, though the extent of differentiation varied qualitatively (Supplementary Figure S1b).
The immunosuppressive capabilities of human mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) on T cells have been investigated in vitro using various T cell activation methods. For this study, we selected anti-human CD3 and CD28 antibodies to stimulate T cell proliferation. This approach was chosen because it mimics physiological T cell activation, unlike methods employing phytohaemagglutinin or ionomycin, and it consistently yielded comparable T cell proliferation levels across experiments, a benefit not observed with mixed lymphocyte reactions using T cells from unrelated donors. In our experiments, allogeneic peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) and activated with CD3/CD28 antibodies, were co-cultured with MSC for four days. The ratios of MSC to PBMC tested were 1:3, 1:9, and 1:27. T cell proliferation was quantified by measuring the proportion of CFSE-low T cells within the leukocyte population, identified through forward and side scatter plots. Initially, we examined the impact of pre-stimulating MSC with pro-inflammatory cytokines. MSC were either left untreated or pre-treated overnight with interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), or a combination of both. These pre-treated MSC were then added to the PBMC proliferation assay at the aforementioned ratios. Notably, MSC stimulated with both TNF-α and IFN-γ demonstrated a more potent inhibitory effect compared to those treated with a single cytokine. For instance, donor 304 showed a 5.3-fold decrease in T cell proliferation with combined cytokine stimulation, compared to 3.9-fold with IFN-γ alone and 2.3-fold with TNF-α alone. Similarly, donor 316 exhibited a 22.7-fold decrease with combined stimulation, versus 7.6-fold with IFN-γ and 4-fold with TNF-α. Consequently, the combined cytokine stimulation protocol was adopted for all subsequent experiments. Further analysis using real-time quantitative PCR confirmed that the expression of several immune factors in MSC was significantly upregulated by TNF-α and IFN-γ activation (Supplementary Figure S2). Following this, we assessed T cell activation and proliferation in co-cultures with either naive, unstimulated MSC or MSC pre-treated with IFN-γ and TNF-α. These experiments, conducted across three independent trials using allogeneic PBMC from different donors, were performed at MSC:PBMC ratios of 1:3, 1:9, and 1:27 (Figure 1a,b). Consistent with previous findings, we observed that pre-treatment of MSC with TNF-α and IFN-γ substantially enhanced their in vitro immunosuppressive capacity. This enhancement was evident in the reduced T cell proliferation (Figure 1b (i, ii)) and decreased production of IFN-γ (Figure 1c (i, ii)), irrespective of the MSC:PBMC ratio employed. Specifically, mean T cell proliferation across all donors at ratios of 1:3, 1:9, and 1:27 showed a fold decrease of 1.9 (P > 0.05), 1.7 (P < 0.001), and 1.2 (P < 0.01) respectively, following cytokine pre-treatment of MSC. Crucially, our results also revealed variability in the immunosuppressive potential among MSC derived from different individuals. At a 1:3 MSC:PBMC ratio, all donors, whether untreated or cytokine-stimulated, exhibited statistically significant differences in T cell proliferation compared to the PBMC-only control (P < 0.01). However, at a 1:9 ratio, only donors 308 and 303 (cytokine-activated) and donor 308 (non-treated) showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) from the control across all three independent experiments. These findings underscore the existence of differential immunosuppressive capacities between MSC donors, independent of the PBMC source used.
To elucidate the primary mechanism by which human mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) suppress T cell proliferation in vitro, our investigation focused on four key immune factors: interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) produced via cyclooxygenase-2 (Cox2) activity, and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO). We assessed the gene expression of these molecules in MSC donors under basal conditions and following a 24-hour exposure to a combination of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). For comparative purposes, we also analyzed the gene expression in the adherent fraction of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), which are predominantly monocytes, after activation with TNF-α and IFN-γ. This comparative approach allowed us to identify genes expressed at significantly lower levels in MSCs compared to immune cells, such as IL-12, and thus exclude them from our primary analysis. Our findings revealed that IL-10 mRNA, which has been reported in other studies to be expressed by MSCs, was barely detectable in either naive or cytokine-stimulated MSCs when contrasted with cytokine-activated monocytes (Figure 2a (i)). Similarly, while TGF-β mRNA was present in all MSC donors, its expression levels were consistently lower than those observed in activated monocytes. Furthermore, cytokine stimulation of MSCs did not alter the expression levels of TGF-β mRNA (Figure 2a (ii)). In contrast, Cox2 mRNA was constitutively expressed in naive MSCs, while IDO mRNA was largely absent. However, both Cox2 and IDO mRNA demonstrated strong induction following TNF-α and IFN-γ exposure when compared to monocytes. Notably, we observed significant inter-individual variability in the induction levels of IDO mRNA (P < 0.05 to P < 0.01), whereas this variability was not significant for Cox2 mRNA (P > 0.05) (Figure 2a (iii, iv)). Donors 303 and 308, identified as the most effective inhibitors of T cell proliferation, exhibited higher levels of IDO mRNA expression compared to other donors (Figure 2a (iv)). Subsequently, we investigated the functional role of each of these molecules in MSC-mediated suppression using a proliferation assay and specific inhibitors. Treatment with 1-methyl-DL-tryptophan (1-MT), a specific inhibitor of IDO, not only completely abolished the immunosuppressive capacity of MSCs on T cell proliferation but also led to a subsequent increase in T cell proliferation (Figure 2b). In contrast, indomethacin (a Cox2 inhibitor) and anti-TGF-β neutralizing antibodies had no discernible impact on the immunosuppressive activity of MSCs. These results were consistent even when using untreated MSCs (data not shown). Based on these observations, we hypothesized that MSCs secrete a balance of factors that both stimulate and inhibit T cell proliferation. In the absence of inhibitory factors, such as IDO, the stimulatory factors secreted by MSCs would dominate. We noted that activation of MSCs with IFN-γ and TNF-α upregulated the mRNA expression of genes encoding IL-6 and IL-7 (Supplementary Figure S2b), both of which are known to promote T cell proliferation. To further explore the role of IL-6, we added anti-IL-6 neutralizing antibodies to activated PBMCs cultured with supernatant from pre-activated MSCs. As anticipated, this intervention resulted in a further inhibition of T cell proliferation (P < 0.01) (Figure 2c). These findings collectively confirmed that IDO serves as the primary mechanism underlying human MSC-mediated T cell immunosuppression in vitro.
Having established indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) as the primary mechanism by which activated mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) inhibit T cell proliferation in vitro, we proceeded to investigate the protein levels of IDO across different MSC donors. Immunoblotting confirmed that IDO protein expression could be induced by exposing MSCs to a combination of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) for 24 hours. The relative intensity of the resulting protein bands was quantified through densitometry analysis. Subsequently, we analyzed the correlation between the quantity of IDO produced, as indicated by the relative band intensity, and the percentage of T cell proliferation observed for each donor. This analysis, employing a one-tailed Pearson's test, revealed a statistically significant association between the amount of IDO and the level of T cell proliferation (P < 0.05). Given that the transcription of IDO is known to be tightly regulated by IFN-sensitive response elements within its promoter region, we aimed to assess the sensitivity of each MSC donor to IFN-γ signaling. This was achieved by examining STAT-1 phosphorylation. Following a 20-minute exposure to 10 ng/ml of IFN-γ, we observed varying levels of STAT-1 phosphorylation among the donors. However, no significant correlation could be established between the extent of STAT-1 phosphorylation and either the amount of IDO produced or the overall immunosuppressive capacity of the MSCs. Therefore, while the quantity of IDO produced by a particular donor is directly linked to its ability to suppress T cell proliferation, the observed variability in IDO production among donors does not appear to stem from differences in their responsiveness to IFN-γ.
In cocultures of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), we observed a notable production of interleukin-10 (IL-10), reaching levels up to 300 pg/ml. This IL-10 production varied depending on the cytokine preactivation status of the MSC and the specific MSC donor. Given that MSC, whether unstimulated or cytokine-stimulated, do not express significant levels of IL-10 mRNA (as shown in Figure 2a), our investigation focused on identifying the source of this detected IL-10. Previous research has indicated that MSC can influence other cell types to produce IL-10. For instance, studies have reported that human MSC can promote the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into IL-10-producing regulatory T cells (Tregs) in vitro. Additionally, lipopolysaccharide-activated mouse MSC have been shown to recruit and stimulate macrophages to produce IL-10 in vivo. PBMC responders are a heterogeneous population comprising lymphocytes and monocytes, typically in a 4:1 ratio. To pinpoint the origin of IL-10, we conducted cocultures involving MSC with purified CD14+ monocytes, purified CD3+ T cells activated with anti-CD3 and CD28 antibodies, or a combination of both. Individually, each cell type produced only low levels of IL-10, less than 20 pg/ml. Combining monocytes and T cells did not elevate IL-10 levels beyond baseline, nor did combining MSC and T cells (Figure 4a). However, it's important to note that we did confirm MSC's ability to induce Treg generation (Supplementary Figure S3). In contrast, when naive or cytokine-pretreated MSC were cultured with monocytes, we observed a significant increase in IL-10 levels, a 4-fold and 7.3-fold rise, respectively (Figure 4a). The inclusion of activated T cells in MSC-monocyte cocultures led to a reduction in IL-10 production, although levels remained substantially higher than baseline (Figure 4a). We further explored the role of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) enzymatic activity from MSC in monocyte IL-10 production. The addition of 1-methyl-DL-tryptophan (1-MT), an IDO inhibitor, to MSC/monocyte cocultures significantly reduced IL-10 production. For cocultures with naive MSC, IL-10 levels decreased from 76 to 62 pg/ml (a 1.2-fold reduction, P < 0.01), while cocultures with prestimulated MSC showed a decrease from 138 to 89 pg/ml (a 1.5-fold reduction, P < 0.001) (Figure 4a). This inhibitory effect of 1-MT was also observed when naive MSC were combined with monocytes and T cells. Given that CD14+ monocytes cultured with MSC secreted substantial amounts of IL-10, a characteristic of alternatively activated immunosuppressive macrophages (M2), we investigated the expression of CD206, a marker for M2 macrophages, in the CD14+ population using flow cytometry. We found that the CD14+ population within activated PBMC cocultured with cytokine-preactivated MSC exhibited significantly upregulated CD206 expression (Figure 4b(i)). This upregulation of CD206 correlated with the detection of IL-10 in the supernatant via ELISA (Figure 4b(ii)). Therefore, the enzymatic activity of IDO in MSC appears to drive the differentiation of monocytes into IL-10-secreting CD206+ M2 macrophages.
To further investigate the role of M2-differentiated monocytes in MSC-mediated T cell suppression, we conducted proliferation assays. These assays utilized either whole peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or PBMCs with the CD14+ monocyte population depleted, in co-culture with cytokine-preactivated MSCs at ratios of 1:3 and 1:9 (MSC:PBMC). At a 1:3 MSC:PBMC ratio, the removal of CD14+ cells had a minimal impact on MSC's immunosuppressive capacity. However, when this ratio was reduced to 1:9, the depletion of the CD14+ fraction resulted in a partial or complete reversal of the immunosuppressive effect, with the extent of this reversal varying depending on the MSC donor. Furthermore, when 1-methyl-DL-tryptophan (1-MT), an inhibitor of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), was added to co-cultures of prestimulated MSCs and CD14-depleted PBMCs, the immunosuppressive effect of the MSCs was entirely abolished, irrespective of the cell ratio. In a separate set of experiments, we observed a dose-dependent and statistically significant reduction in T cell proliferation when additional monocytes were introduced into the PBMC proliferation assay. Specifically, with the addition of monocytes to cultures from donor 303, T cell proliferation was reduced by 1.7-fold and 1.8-fold for the two tested doses (P < 0.0001 for both). Similarly, for donor 305, reductions of 1.7-fold and 2.3-fold were observed for the respective doses (P < 0.0001 for both). These additions of monocytes also led to a decrease in the levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) detected in the supernatant. An interesting observation was made prior to normalizing the T cell proliferation data. In PBMC-only control groups, the addition of monocytes significantly increased T cell proliferation. However, after normalization, the addition of purified monocytes consistently decreased T cell proliferation. This suggests a dual role for MSCs: they directly inhibit T cell proliferation, and they also indirectly contribute to suppression through the differentiation of monocytes into M2-like macrophages. This indirect pathway, mediated by M2 macrophages, amplifies the overall immunosuppressive effect.
Our research has identified indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) as the primary direct mechanism by which mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) suppress T cell proliferation in vitro, aligning with prior studies on human and nonhuman primate MSCs. We observed that human MSCs, when exposed to interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), increase their production of IDO. Consequently, inhibiting IDO's enzymatic activity completely abolished the immunosuppressive capacity of MSCs on T cells. While IFN-γ alone can induce IDO, we utilized a combination of IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) for MSC stimulation, as this synergistic approach further enhanced T cell suppression. Beyond this primary finding, our study revealed two significant insights. Firstly, MSCs sourced from healthy donors exhibit a variable range of immunosuppressive potential. The induction of IDO following TNF-α and IFN-γ stimulation differed among donors, with those producing higher levels of IDO demonstrating greater potency in inhibiting T cell proliferation in vitro. This established a direct correlation between the quantity of IDO produced and the degree of T cell inhibition. Our examination of various immune-modulatory factors showed that each donor expressed and upregulated these molecules to different extents. Furthermore, our investigation into IFN-γ-induced STAT-1 phosphorylation indicated that variations in MSC donor sensitivity to IFN-γ stimulation did not correlate with either IDO expression levels or their overall immunosuppressive potential. These findings suggest an inherent variability in the immune responsiveness of MSCs to inflammatory cytokines within the general human population.
Our research revealed that the immunosuppressive effects of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) produced by human mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) extend beyond T cells to influence monocytes. Previous work on IDO's role in dendritic cell-mediated tolerance has established that its enzymatic activity breaks down tryptophan into kynurenine. This kynurenine, and its subsequent metabolites, are known to possess immunoregulatory properties affecting T cells. In this study, we demonstrated that IDO activity can directly and indirectly suppress T cell proliferation. As depicted in Figure 6, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), produced by T cells in an inflammatory environment, triggers IDO expression in MSCs. This leads to the conversion of tryptophan into kynurenine, which directly inhibits T cell proliferation. Furthermore, we showed an indirect pathway: IDO activity, potentially in conjunction with other factors secreted by MSCs, promotes the differentiation of monocytes into IL-10-secreting, CD206+ M2-like macrophages. These M2 macrophages possess their own T-cell immunosuppressive capabilities. Our findings were corroborated by experiments where either the monocyte population was depleted or additional monocytes were introduced into peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) proliferation assays, confirming this observation (Figure 5).
In our in vitro experiments, the addition of anti-IL-10 neutralizing antibodies did not significantly alter T cell proliferation when monocytes were activated. While this finding doesn't negate the role of IL-10 from monocytes in vivo, it suggests that MSC-influenced monocytes produce additional immunosuppressive factors in vitro. We investigated the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) as a potential contributor, observing that MSC promote the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs, consistent with previous research. This Treg generation was diminished when the monocyte fraction was removed. It's plausible that the enzymatic activity of IDO, along with other factors secreted by MSC like TGF-β, contributes to Treg induction. Although these Tregs may not exert a pronounced effect on T cell proliferation in vitro, they could play a significant role in vivo.
Our research showed that when mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) are cultured with CD14+ monocytes, these monocytes exhibit increased CD206 expression and secrete IL-10, hallmarks of an M2-like macrophage phenotype. This observation aligns with findings from two other studies that also reported MSC inducing IL-10 production in monocytes/macrophages through unspecified MSC-derived factors or conditioned media. We found that 1-methyl-DL-tryptophan (1-MT) partially reduced IL-10 production by MSC-influenced monocytes, indicating that tryptophan depletion or kynurenine pathway metabolites may play a role in differentiating monocytes into immunosuppressive M2 macrophages. This is supported by a study by Wang et al., which demonstrated that kynurenic acid can decrease pro-inflammatory TNF-α production in lipopolysaccharide-activated CD14+ monocytes, thus modifying their immune-modulating characteristics. The partial inhibition of monocyte IL-10 expression by 1-MT also suggests that other factors secreted by MSC contribute to monocyte reprogramming.
The interpretation of studies on the in vitro immune biology of human mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) warrants careful consideration due to these findings. While responder T cells are commonly employed in assays to quantify MSC-mediated suppression, our research highlights a critical distinction. The use of whole peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) versus purified T cells or monocyte-depleted PBMC can yield significantly different in vitro outcomes. Consequently, existing literature investigating the mechanisms by which MSC interact with human immune effector cells may require re-evaluation. A critical review of past studies, considering the specific responder cell populations used, could offer valuable insights into the often-observed variability in results within this field.
The findings presented offer valuable insights into how mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) exert their immunosuppressive effects in vivo. Clinical trial outcomes, even with relatively low doses of MSCs (1-2 million cells/kg), may be attributed to a cascading effect on local immune cells. Specifically, MSCs can induce monocytes to differentiate into immunosuppressive M2 macrophages and promote the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs). These processes likely augment the overall T cell suppression mediated by administered MSCs. This understanding can guide the optimization of MSC-based therapies. For "universal" donor MSCs, selecting individuals with high IDO expression and pre-activating MSCs with cytokines prior to administration could bolster their immunosuppressive capacity, leading to improved therapeutic efficacy. In personalized cell therapy, where autologous or donor-matched MSCs are employed, pre-treatment assessment of MSC immune plasticity in response to specific cytokines could help predict and align their activity with key effector pathways like IDO induction.
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) were obtained from bone marrow aspirates of consenting patients undergoing hip replacement surgery, specifically donors 303 through 308 and 311. Following dilution with phosphate-buffered saline, the aspirates were subjected to a Ficoll density gradient to isolate mononuclear cells. These cells were then cultured in complete human MSC medium, which included α-MEM, 20% fetal bovine serum, 20 mmol/l l-glutamine, and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin, at a high density. After five days, nonadherent hematopoietic cells were removed by changing the medium. The MSC were subsequently passaged weekly, reseeded at 200 cells/cm², and exhibited a 40–60-fold increase in cell number per passage. Prior to passage three, MSC cultures underwent flow cytometry analysis to confirm the absence of CD45+ and CD31+ contaminants and to assess the expression of CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105. Additionally, differentiation into adipocyte and osteocyte lineages was evaluated. All experiments utilized MSC between passages 3 and 5 to ensure the elimination of residual hematopoietic cells and to prevent senescence.
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were obtained from healthy volunteers via leukapheresis and isolated using a Ficoll density gradient. T cells and monocytes were subsequently separated using negative selection kits targeting CD3+ and CD14+ markers, respectively. A separate kit was employed for the depletion of monocytes from PBMC.
Recombinant human IFN-γ and TNF-α, used for cell activation at 10 ng/ml and 3 ng/ml respectively, were obtained from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN). Neutralizing antibodies targeting human IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β, also from R&D Systems, were applied at doses between 20 and 100 µg/ml. BD Bioscience provided antihuman CD3 and CD28 antibodies, both at 0.2 µg/ml. Indomethacin and 1-MT were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO), with indomethacin used at 10 µmol/l (dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide) and 1-MT at 1 mmol/l (dissolved in 0.1 N NaOH). CFSE, purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA), was utilized at a concentration of 5 µmol/l.
To analyze protein expression, whole cell extracts were prepared from mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) that were either untreated or activated for 24 hours with a combination of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These extracts were subjected to electrophoresis on a 12% Tris–glycine gel and subsequently transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Detection of specific proteins involved rabbit anti-human antibodies for indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and phospho-STAT-1, along with a goat anti-human antibody for total STAT-1. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as a loading control, using rabbit anti-human GAPDH antibodies. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies facilitated the visualization of protein bands via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system. Quantitative analysis of the protein bands was conducted using Adobe Photoshop 7.0 to determine band intensity. Absolute intensity was calculated by multiplying the mean intensity value by the number of pixels within each band. Relative intensity was then determined by normalizing the absolute intensity of IDO or phospho-STAT-1 to that of their respective GAPDH or total STAT-1 control bands. Finally, a linear regression model and statistical correlation analysis performed with GraphPad Prism 5 software were used to establish the relationship between the relative intensity of IDO produced by each MSC donor and their corresponding percentage of T cell proliferation inhibition.
To perform real-time quantitative PCR, DNA-free total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy kit with DNase digestion from Qiagen. Two micrograms of RNA underwent reverse transcription with MuLV reverse transcriptase from Applied Biosystems, utilizing random hexamers and RNase inhibitor. A 1/100th aliquot of the complementary DNA was then employed for PCR amplification in a 20-µl reaction volume. Duplicate real-time quantitative PCR assays were conducted on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system, employing SYBR Green Mastermix from Applied Biosystems. Specific human primer sequences were used for IL-10, TGF-β, IDO, COX2, and the reference gene 18S. Data analysis relied on the relative quantification method, normalizing target gene mRNA expression to 18S mRNA levels. PCR amplification specificity was confirmed through melting curve analysis, and the absence of genomic DNA contamination was verified by PCR analysis of total RNA with each primer set.
To assess T cell proliferation, carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were cultured in 24-well plates. These PBMC were cocultured with either untreated or cytokine-preactivated (interferon-gamma [IFN-γ] and/or tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α] overnight) mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) at ratios of 1:3, 1:9, and 1:27 (MSC:PBMC) in R10 medium. T lymphocyte stimulation was achieved by adding anti-human CD3 and CD28 antibodies. In specific experiments, purified monocytes, indomethacin, 1-methyl-DL-tryptophan (1-MT), anti-IL-10 antibody (20 µg/ml), and anti-TGF-β antibody (20 µg/ml) were incorporated into the cocultures. T cell proliferation was quantified four days post-coculture via flow cytometry, analyzing CFSE fluorescence intensity. Additionally, T cell activation was evaluated by measuring interleukin-2 (IL-2) and IFN-γ levels in the supernatant using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. To ascertain the potential toxicity of indomethacin and 1-MT on MSC, an MTT assay was conducted on MSC cultured with these inhibitors for four days. Furthermore, the cellular toxicity of all employed inhibitors was assessed on activated CFSE-labeled PBMC, with toxicity determined by measuring any inhibition in cell proliferation. Importantly, no significant cellular toxicity was observed with any of the inhibitors at the specified concentrations for either MSC or PBMC.
To investigate the role of monocytes and T cells in MSC-mediated immunosuppression, cocultures were established. MSC, CD14+ monocytes, and CD3+ T cells were combined in various configurations within 24-well plates, stimulated with anti-CD3 and CD28 antibodies, and cultured for four days. These cultures were assessed for IL-10 levels in the supernatant via ELISA. Additionally, flow cytometry was employed to analyze the M2 macrophage phenotype by examining CD14 and CD206 double positivity. The impact of 1-MT on these cultures was also evaluated.
Flow cytometry utilized antibodies for human CD14, CD31, CD44, CD45, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD206.
Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Group comparisons utilized ANOVA, while the correlation between T cell proliferation and IDO expression was assessed via a one-tailed Pearson's test. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ns (P > 0.05), * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), and **** (P < 0.0001).
