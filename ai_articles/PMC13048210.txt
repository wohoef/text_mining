This retrospective cohort study investigated the influence of various factors on pulmonary cavities in diabetic patients with culture-proven pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB). Previous research indicated a heightened risk of cavities in this population, potentially linked to inadequate glycemic control. Given that cavities negatively affect PTB treatment, our study aimed to thoroughly evaluate the interplay of clinical factors, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, and antidiabetic agents, particularly considering other diabetes-related variables that could confound these associations. We analyzed chest X-rays (CXRs) and computed tomography (CT) scans from 128 diabetic PTB patients. Our findings revealed that patients exhibiting pulmonary cavities on both CXRs and CT scans were younger, less likely to be on metformin, and had considerably higher HbA1c levels compared to those without cavities. Multivariate logistic regression analysis strongly associated higher HbA1c levels with the presence of cavities on CXRs (OR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.12-1.61) and CT scans (OR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.13-1.64). Furthermore, patients with multiple cavities on CT scans displayed significantly elevated HbA1c levels compared to those with a single cavity (p = 0.002). Notably, after multivariate analysis, other variables, including metformin use, did not show significant differences between groups. In conclusion, our study underscores poor glycemic control as the primary risk factor for cavitary lesions in diabetic PTB patients, even when accounting for potential confounders like metformin use. Therefore, improving glycemic management in these patients could significantly enhance the effectiveness of antimycobacterial treatment.

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health concern, despite advancements in its control. The 2015 Global Burden of Disease Study reported a substantial impact, including 10.2 million new cases, 10.1 million existing cases, and 1.3 million deaths. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is increasingly recognized as a crucial risk factor for TB. Its influence extends beyond raising the likelihood of developing both active and latent TB infections; DM is also linked to unfavorable TB treatment outcomes. These include a higher risk of delayed sputum conversion, treatment failure, multidrug resistance, relapse, and increased mortality.
Pulmonary cavities, a hallmark of PTB, are linked to a high mycobacterial load. This makes them a primary driver of disease spread, drug resistance, and treatment failure. Diabetic PTB patients face a significantly elevated risk of developing these cavitary lesions, including multiple cavities, compared to non-diabetic individuals. Consequently, understanding the factors contributing to cavities in diabetic PTB patients is crucial for effective prevention and management of the disease.
The current study aimed to investigate the influence of clinical and diabetes-related factors on pulmonary cavities in diabetic PTB patients, as seen on CXRs and CT scans. This research was prompted by a gap in previous studies, which, despite noting a positive link between glycemic status and cavitary lesions in diabetic PTB patients (with worse control leading to higher risk), failed to explore potential confounding variables. Metformin, for instance, has garnered interest as an immunomodulator in PTB due to its role in promoting autophagy and mitigating lung damage via inflammation reduction. Singhal et al. observed fewer TB cavities in metformin users, but their work, like others, didn't fully account for interacting factors that might influence cavity formation in this patient population.
The study, a retrospective analysis, received approval from the National Yang-Ming University Hospital's institutional review board (NYMUH IRB No. 2017A030). It was carried out at two teaching hospitals affiliated with National Yang-Ming University in northern Taiwan, covering the period from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2016. Eligibility for inclusion was based on medical records of patients who had positive cultures for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* from respiratory samples, such as sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, lung aspiration, or lung biopsy. Exclusion criteria included individuals with extrathoracic TB, HIV infection, those receiving immunosuppressant therapy, patients with underlying malignancies, or concurrent pulmonary conditions like lung cancer, pneumoconiosis, or other pulmonary infections. Data on sputum acid-fast bacilli (AFB) testing, specifically Ziehl-Neelsen staining results and CDC guideline-based grades, were also collected for enrolled patients, with grades of 3+ or 4+ indicating high AFB smear grades.
To be included in the study, individuals had to meet specific criteria for diabetes mellitus (DM). This included patients already receiving hypoglycemic agents, those with at least two ICD-9 codes for DM from outpatient visits or admissions, or individuals with two separate fasting blood glucose readings exceeding 126 mg/dL. Enrollment was limited to patients whose DM was either pre-existing at the time of PTB diagnosis or diagnosed simultaneously. Patients who developed DM after their PTB diagnosis were not included. For glycemic status assessment, only patients with glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels documented within one month of their PTB diagnosis were considered. Data on the duration of DM and the specific antidiabetic medications, such as metformin, other oral agents, or insulin, being used at the time of PTB diagnosis were collected. To avoid confounding effects related to metformin use, patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 3b or higher were excluded, as metformin is contraindicated in these cases.
The study exclusively enrolled diabetic PTB patients who had both pretreatment chest X-rays (CXRs) and thoracic CT scans available, aligning with their positive cultures. This was crucial because CT scans offer superior sensitivity for identifying pulmonary issues, such as cavitary lesions, compared to CXRs. Clinical data, including age, gender, smoking history, sputum AFB smear grade, HbA1c levels, and antidiabetic medication use, were standardized and extracted from electronic medical records, with all patient identifiers removed.
To ensure accuracy, two radiologists (L.K.H. and L.D.J.), each with eight years of chest imaging experience, independently evaluated CXRs and CT scans using a standardized form, unaware of patient clinical details. Their interpretations were reconciled through consensus. Any discrepancies were resolved by a conference involving these two readers and a senior radiologist (M.H.W.), who had 25 years of experience, with the agreed-upon reading becoming the final result.
In this study, cavitary lesions, encompassing cavitated nodules, masses, and consolidations, refer to gas-filled spaces appearing as lucencies or low-attenuation areas, consistent with the Fleischner Society's definition of a pulmonary cavity.
Chest X-rays were analyzed for pulmonary cavities, noting their presence. The severity of the disease, categorized as minimal, moderately advanced, or far advanced, was also assessed using the U.S. National Tuberculosis and Respiratory Disease Association scheme.
Thoracic CT scans, known for their superior sensitivity and accuracy compared to CXRs, were conducted using two Philips Brilliance 64-multidetector scanners. Beyond simply identifying pulmonary cavities, the CT analysis also quantified their number, distinguishing between single and multiple occurrences. Furthermore, a comprehensive evaluation of other CT findings was performed. This included identifying mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy (defined as lymph nodes exceeding 10 mm in short-axis diameter), pleural effusion, and characteristic patterns like centrilobular nodules or branching linear opacities (tree-in-bud appearance). The presence of bronchogenic spread, indicated by consolidation or nodule clusters in lobes distinct from the primary TB lesions, was also noted. Finally, the radiological distribution and location of parenchymal abnormalities, including cavities, were categorized as either usual (primarily in the apical/posterior upper lobes and superior lower lobes) or unusual (involving other lung segments).
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 22; IBM-SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), with significance set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). To identify independent risk factors for pulmonary cavities in diabetic PTB patients, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed. For comparing categorical variables between two groups, Fisher’s exact test was utilized. Continuous variables were compared using an independent Student’s t-test. However, for PTB symptom and diabetic durations, which were reported as medians (interquartile range), the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Patient demographics and clinical features were summarized, with categorical data presented as percentages and continuous data as means and standard deviations.
During the six-year study, 1671 patients were identified with culture-proven PTB. Of these, 377 also had diabetes at their PTB diagnosis. A significant number were then excluded: 159 lacked thoracic CT images at diagnosis, and 17 had no HbA1c data within a month of diagnosis. Further exclusions were made for patients using immunosuppressants, those with HIV, underlying malignancies, CKD stage 3b or higher, or other lung diseases. Ultimately, 128 diabetic PTB patients (105 men, 23 women) with available CXRs and CT scans were included in the study.
Of the 128 patients studied, 43 (33.6%) exhibited pulmonary cavities on their chest X-rays (CXRs), while 85 did not. Table 1 details the clinical and radiological features of these two groups. A higher incidence of elevated sputum AFB smear grades was noted in patients with CXR cavities (p < 0.001). Univariate analysis revealed that patients with cavities were significantly younger (p = 0.003), had higher HbA1c levels (p < 0.001), shorter diabetes durations (p = 0.01), and less frequent metformin use (p = 0.004) compared to those without cavities. However, in multivariate analysis, after accounting for age, diabetes duration, and metformin use, only higher HbA1c levels (OR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.12-1.61; p = 0.001) remained a significant independent factor for the presence of pulmonary cavities.
Of the 128 patients studied, thoracic CT scans revealed pulmonary cavities in 57 individuals, representing 44.5% of the cohort. A comparison of patients with and without cavities, detailed in Table 2, highlighted several significant differences in both clinical and radiological findings. Radiological features such as tree-in-bud appearance, bronchogenic spreading, lesions predominantly in unusual locations, and disease affecting all lung lobes were more frequently observed in patients exhibiting cavities. Furthermore, high sputum AFB smear grades were also more prevalent in those with cavities on their CT scans. Univariate analysis of clinical factors indicated that patients with cavities tended to be younger, have elevated HbA1c levels, shorter durations of diabetes, and less frequent metformin use. However, when these factors were subjected to multivariate analysis, adjusting for age, diabetes duration, and metformin use, only higher HbA1c levels maintained a statistically significant association with an increased risk of pulmonary cavities on thoracic CT scans (OR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.13-1.64; p = 0.001).
When examining patients with pulmonary cavitary lesions on CT scans, we categorized them by the number of cavities. Our findings, detailed in Table 3, revealed that individuals with multiple cavities, as depicted in Fig. 3, exhibited a greater propensity for bronchogenic spreading (p = 0.035) and lung involvement across all lobes (p = 0.045) compared to those with a single cavity. Among clinical factors, only elevated HbA1c levels showed a significant correlation with the presence of multiple cavities (p = 0.002).
Our study's key finding is that glycemic control profoundly affects pulmonary cavities in diabetic PTB patients. Those with cavitary lesions, visible on both CXRs and CT scans, exhibited poorer glycemic status compared to patients without cavities.
The formation of TB cavities originates from the central necrosis and eventual rupture of granulomas. Hyperglycemia-induced immune dysfunction in diabetic individuals is a potential mechanism contributing to the development of these cavities. Prior research indicates that TB cavities correlate with smear positivity, and diabetic patients exhibit a greater bacillary load in their sputum compared to non-diabetic individuals. Our current investigation further revealed that diabetic PTB patients with cavities were significantly more likely to have high PTB smear grades in their sputum. A similar trend of elevated sputum PTB smear grades was observed in patients with multiple cavities compared to those with a single cavity. Furthermore, our study participants with CT-detected cavities frequently presented with bronchogenic spreading, consolidation, or clustered nodules, particularly in cases of multiple cavities, which are CT indicators of smear-positive active PTB. Consequently, identifying and mitigating the risk factors for cavitary diabetic PTB is crucial for curbing TB transmission.
Our investigation reinforces earlier findings that poor glycemic control in diabetic patients elevates the risk of developing TB cavities. Even after accounting for clinical and other diabetes-related variables, our study confirmed this connection between glycemic status and pulmonary cavities in diabetic PTB patients. Specifically, individuals with multiple cavities on CT scans exhibited significantly higher HbA1c levels compared to those with only a single cavity. This is particularly concerning because cavitary lesions, especially in the context of multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant TB, not only promote the emergence of drug-resistant TB strains but also impede disease eradication. Pulmonary cavities are negatively correlated with successful sputum culture conversion and favorable treatment outcomes in drug-resistant PTB. Moreover, the diverse resistance patterns within TB strains found in patients with numerous lung cavities can further complicate treatment strategies. Consequently, the elevated risk of TB cavities in diabetic patients due to hyperglycemia can hinder effective PTB treatment. Therefore, achieving proper glycemic control is crucial for successful PTB treatment, as better blood glucose management has been linked to the resolution of pulmonary abnormalities.
Our research explored metformin's influence on pulmonary cavities in diabetic TB patients. Metformin, a widely used first-line diabetes treatment, has garnered interest as a potential immunomodulator in TB. Previous studies have indicated several benefits of metformin in this context. For instance, adjuvant metformin use has been linked to a reduced risk of TB and disease relapse in diabetic individuals. It has also been associated with a higher rate of two-month sputum culture conversion in diabetic PTB patients with cavities. Furthermore, diabetic PTB patients receiving metformin exhibited a significantly lower mortality rate compared to those not on metformin, with metformin users showing a mortality risk comparable to PTB patients without diabetes. The formation of PTB cavities is thought to involve severe tissue hypoxia and uncontrolled proteolytic destruction, characterized by the accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α and elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) concentrations in lung tissues. Metformin has been shown in vitro to inhibit both HIF-1α and MMP expression. A recent study also reported a connection between metformin use and reduced plasma MMP levels in diabetic PTB patients. However, our findings diverge from those of Singhal and colleagues. Our multivariate analysis revealed no significant difference in metformin use between diabetic PTB patients with and without pulmonary cavities once HbA1c levels were accounted for. Notably, metformin users in our study population had significantly lower HbA1c levels (8.28 ± 2.13) compared to non-users (9.65 ± 2.70; p = 0.003). This suggests that the observed correlation between metformin use and fewer pulmonary cavities in our univariate analysis might stem from improved glycemic control rather than a direct immunomodulatory effect of metformin. Therefore, further investigation with a larger cohort is warranted to fully understand metformin's potential immunomodulatory role in reducing diabetic PTB cavities. Additionally, preclinical studies have not consistently shown promising adjunctive sterilizing activity of metformin against TB, and potential drug interactions between metformin and anti-TB medications have been documented. Given these considerations, a more practical approach to managing diabetic PTB patients might involve tailoring antidiabetic regimens to achieve optimal glycemic control, thereby reducing the risk of cavitary lesions.
Several limitations affect this study's conclusions. The retrospective design, coupled with a small and highly selective sample (excluding 159 diabetic patients without CT images), introduces potential selection bias. This bias might lead to misinterpretations, particularly regarding metformin's impact on TB cavities, suggesting a need for larger future studies. Furthermore, the generalizability of these findings may be limited to regions where TB is endemic. Our results could also be confounded by the fact that HbA1c values can be artificially high or low in various clinical situations. Finally, the specific strains of *M. tuberculosis* were not analyzed, despite prior research indicating their potential influence on PTB radiological features, including cavities.
Our research indicates that poor glycemic control significantly elevates the risk of pulmonary cavities, detectable on both CXRs and CT scans. This association holds true even after accounting for various clinical factors and antidiabetic medications, such as metformin. Notably, patients exhibiting multiple cavities on CT scans demonstrated substantially worse glycemic control than those with no cavities or only a single cavity. Given the strong connection between diabetes and tuberculosis, and the clear link between poor glycemic management and cavitary lesions, routine dual screening for these conditions is crucial. Furthermore, achieving proper glycemic control in diabetic PTB patients is vital for improving antimycobacterial treatment outcomes.
