This study aimed to assess the physical fitness of individuals experiencing their first episode of schizophrenia and to identify factors associated with their fitness levels. A certified fitness instructor administered the Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA) fitness test to 70 participants with recent-onset schizophrenia within three months of their enrollment. The findings revealed that the participants' percentile ranks for muscular strength and endurance, muscular flexibility, and cardiorespiratory fitness all fell below the 50th percentile when compared to national U.S. norms. Consistent with expectations, higher body mass index and smoking status were linked to poorer cardiorespiratory fitness. While not statistically significant, a trend suggested that a longer duration of illness was associated with reduced cardiorespiratory fitness. Importantly, exposure to antipsychotic medication did not appear to be related to cardiorespiratory fitness. The study concludes that physical fitness seems to be impaired in these patients and may deteriorate over time, though further longitudinal research is needed to confirm this.

Individuals with schizophrenia experience a 20% reduction in life expectancy and an increased susceptibility to conditions like diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease. Those experiencing their first psychotic episode are especially prone to negative physical consequences from antipsychotic medications, including abdominal fat accumulation and metabolic issues. While health monitoring guidelines exist internationally, the assessment of physical fitness in schizophrenia patients is not yet a standard practice.
While chronic schizophrenia patients engage in less physical activity, no studies have assessed physical fitness in first-episode schizophrenia. This is crucial, as early illness stages show increased central obesity, metabolic syndrome, glucose abnormalities, and reduced insulin sensitivity.
This study aimed to assess the physical fitness of first-episode schizophrenia patients by measuring three key components using the standardized Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA) fitness assessment protocol. This protocol is a recognized method for adult physical fitness testing in the United States. Researchers compared the participants' fitness test results against the YMCA's age- and gender-specific normative data to ascertain if there were significant differences compared to the general young adult population. Additionally, the study investigated potential factors influencing fitness levels in these young adults with schizophrenia, specifically examining body mass index (BMI), how long they had experienced the illness, whether they were taking antipsychotic medication, and their smoking habits.
The UCLA Aftercare Research Program, an NIMH-funded longitudinal outpatient study for first-episode schizophrenia patients, recruits participants from Los Angeles area facilities or via direct referral. For 18 months, participants receive psychiatric medication management, psychoeducation, group skills training, and individual case management.
Seventy patients experiencing recent-onset schizophrenia participated in this study, drawn from the Developmental Processes in Schizophrenic Disorders project. These individuals had experienced their first major psychotic episode within the preceding two years. The university's institutional review board provided approval for the protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent. Adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki was maintained, and every participant received medical clearance before undertaking the fitness testing.
The study sample consisted of 70 recent-onset schizophrenia patients with a mean age of 21.8 years. Participants reported a mean of 12.3 years of education, with parental education averaging 13.7 years. The average age of illness onset was 20.7 years. The sample's racial makeup was diverse, including 42% mixed race, 27% African American, 17% White, 13% Asian, and 1% American Indian or Alaskan Native. Additionally, 39% identified as Hispanic. The majority of the participants, 76%, were male. All patients were receiving oral risperidone and were awaiting randomization to either oral or injectable risperidone as part of the ongoing research protocol.
Developed by US experts in fitness and sports medicine, the YMCA Fitness Assessment protocol is notable for its extensive normative database, compiled from over 35,000 tests. A certified instructor administered this protocol to participants within three months of their program entry. The average time to testing was 4.2 months, with a median of 3.0 months.
Cardiorespiratory endurance, flexibility, and muscular strength/endurance were assessed using the 3-minute step test, modified sit-and-reach, and half sit-up tests, respectively. These tests were conducted indoors to standardize environmental conditions like temperature and humidity.
The modified sit-and-reach test employed an Acuflex® I sit-and-reach box. Participants positioned themselves with their back, hips, and head against a wall, then reached forward three times. The final reach involved pushing the sliding device as far as possible along the indicator and holding it for at least two seconds. The score, representing the best of three trials, was the distance in centimeters achieved.
To assess muscular strength and endurance, participants completed the half sit-up test. This involved performing abdominal crunches on a yoga mat marked with four 15-cm tape strips, spaced 9 cm apart. Participants flexed their spine, reaching their fingertips to the second strip, then returned to a reclined position with shoulders touching the mat. This motion was repeated for one minute, with the total number of correctly executed repetitions serving as the score.
Participants in the step test ascended and descended a 30.5-cm bench for 3 minutes at a pace of 96 beats per minute (24 steps). Cardiovascular fitness was assessed by measuring the recovery heart rate in beats per minute for 1 minute, starting 5 seconds after the final step. A lower recovery heart rate indicated better cardiorespiratory fitness. A digital heart rate monitor (Acumen® EON Basix ES) was used for these measurements.
To evaluate the participants' fitness, raw scores from the YMCA fitness tests were transformed into age- and gender-specific percentile ranks, referencing the YMCA fitness test manual's normative data. Histograms were created to examine the distribution of these scores, as shown in Figures 1-3. The study found that, on average, most patients fell around the 20th percentile for muscular strength and endurance, the 11th percentile for muscular flexibility, and the 40th percentile for cardiorespiratory fitness. The percentile rank distributions for muscular strength, endurance, and flexibility exhibited a limited range and a positive skew, leading to their exclusion from correlational analyses. In contrast, the percentile ranks for cardiorespiratory endurance showed a wider spread, indicating greater variability within the sample for this particular fitness component.
At baseline, the study sample had a mean BMI of 28.2 (SD = 5.6), with half of the participants being smokers. Normal BMI ranges from 18.5 to 24.9, while 25.0 to 29.9 indicates overweight, and 30.0 or higher signifies obesity. Correlational analyses revealed a significant link between poorer cardiorespiratory endurance and higher BMI (r = −0.40, P = 0.001). A trend, though not statistically significant, suggested that a longer duration of illness was associated with worse cardiorespiratory fitness (r = −0.22; P = 0.099). Smokers demonstrated slightly poorer cardiorespiratory endurance compared to non-smokers (F(1,63) = 3.8; P = 0.057). Importantly, cardiorespiratory endurance showed no relationship with medication exposure, measured by prescribed days weighted by adherence (r = −0.05, P = 0.78).
Utilizing a standardized fitness test protocol, this study offers novel insights into the physical fitness of individuals experiencing their first episode of schizophrenia. Across all measured components—muscular strength and endurance, flexibility, and cardiorespiratory fitness—our patient sample consistently scored below the 50th percentile when benchmarked against national age- and gender-specific norms in the United States. Furthermore, the data for muscular flexibility and strength/endurance revealed restricted score ranges, with most participants falling into the lower performance categories. These findings collectively indicate a significant impairment in physical fitness among individuals with recent-onset schizophrenia.
The study also investigated factors associated with cardiorespiratory fitness. As anticipated, individuals with higher body mass indexes and those who smoked demonstrated poorer cardiorespiratory fitness. A trend, though not statistically significant, suggested that a longer duration of illness was linked to worse cardiorespiratory fitness. This finding implies a potential decline in physical health during the early stages of schizophrenia, a hypothesis that warrants confirmation through longitudinal research. Notably, exposure to antipsychotic medication did not correlate with cardiorespiratory fitness in this sample. This observation aligns with existing research indicating physical health deterioration in first-episode patients before the manifestation of antipsychotic side effects. However, it's acknowledged that antipsychotic side effects could contribute to reduced muscular flexibility, despite the lower prevalence of muscular stiffness with newer antipsychotic medications.
The study's findings of impaired physical fitness in first-episode schizophrenia patients suggest the need for targeted interventions. Regular physical activity is known to yield significant health benefits, including reduced cardiovascular disease and diabetes mortality and morbidity, alongside improvements in mental well-being, physical function, and weight management. Developing specialized fitness programs could further integrate physical and mental healthcare for individuals with severe mental illnesses, building upon the effectiveness of preventative behavioral programs. Employing standardized fitness assessment tools, such as the YMCA fitness test, will be crucial for researchers and clinicians to effectively evaluate the outcomes of initiatives aimed at encouraging physical exercise in this vulnerable population.
