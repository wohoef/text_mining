United Phosphorus Ltd. submitted an application to Spain, the evaluating Member State, requesting an adjustment to the maximum residue level (MRL) for mancozeb in persimmons. This request, made under Article 6 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, sought to increase the current MRL from 0.05 mg/kg (the limit of quantification) to 0.2 mg/kg, reflecting the planned application of mancozeb. Spain subsequently prepared an evaluation report, as mandated by Article 8 of the same regulation, and forwarded it to both the European Commission and EFSA. EFSA's review determined that the provided data support a proposed MRL of 0.2 mg/kg for mancozeb on persimmons, with residues measured as carbon disulfide. Furthermore, EFSA confirmed the availability of suitable analytical methods for enforcing carbon disulfide residue limits in persimmons. The risk assessment conducted by EFSA indicated that the proposed mancozeb use on persimmons would not lead to consumer exposure levels surpassing toxicological reference values, thereby posing no significant health risk to consumers.

United Phosphorus Ltd. submitted an application to Spain, the evaluating Member State, requesting a change to the maximum residue level (MRL) for mancozeb in persimmons. This request, made under Article 6 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, sought to increase the current MRL from 0.05 mg/kg (the limit of quantification) to 0.2 mg/kg, reflecting the planned use of the substance. Following this, Spain prepared an evaluation report, as mandated by Article 8 of the same Regulation. This report was then sent to the European Commission and subsequently forwarded to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on February 5, 2016.
EFSA's assessment draws upon several key documents. These include the EMS's evaluation report, the DAR from Council Directive 91/414/EEC, the Commission's mancozeb review, the JMPR Evaluation report, and prior EFSA opinions concerning dithiocarbamates.
An acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0.05 mg/kg bw/day and an acute reference dose (ARfD) of 0.6 mg/kg bw were established for mancozeb. This was based on a toxicological assessment conducted within the Directive 91/414/EEC framework, where sufficient data were available.
Foliar applications of mancozeb were studied across various primary crop groups, including fruit, root, cereal/grass, and pulses/oilseeds, to understand its metabolic pathways. These metabolism studies informed the proposed residue definition for mancozeb, expressed as carbon disulfide (CS2), for both enforcement and risk assessment purposes. Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 established CS2 as a screening residue definition for dithiocarbamates like maneb, mancozeb, metiram, propineb, thiram, and ziram, due to CS2 being the common analytical marker for these substances. EFSA determined that mancozeb's metabolism has been adequately characterized for its use in persimmons, confirming the applicability of the derived residue definition.
Sufficient residue trials support a proposed MRL of 0.20 mg/kg for mancozeb in persimmons. Furthermore, effective analytical methods exist to enforce CS2 residue monitoring in persimmons, with a quantifiable limit of 0.05 mg/kg.
This assessment excluded mancozeb residues in animal products and rotational crops. This is because persimmons are permanent crops and are not used as livestock feed.
No acute consumer health risk was identified concerning the proposed MRL for persimmons, with the highest acute exposure reaching only 1% of the ARfD. The EFSA Pesticide Residues Intake Model (PRIMo), specifically revision 2, was utilized for the consumer risk assessment. Across all European diets included in the PRIMo model, no long-term consumer intake concerns were found. The maximum chronic intake observed was 72% of the ADI in German children. Furthermore, residues in persimmons contributed minimally to overall consumer exposure, accounting for less than 0.01% of the ADI (WHO Cluster B).
The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has determined that the proposed application of mancozeb on persimmons is not expected to lead to consumer exposure levels that surpass established toxicological thresholds. Consequently, this use is deemed unlikely to present a risk to public health. Based on this assessment, EFSA recommends an adjustment to the current Maximum Residue Level (MRL) for mancozeb in persimmons. The existing MRL, currently at the limit of quantification (0.05 mg/kg), is proposed to be increased to 0.2 mg/kg. This revised MRL is supported by trials conducted in Southern Europe, with the residue defined as mancozeb expressed as carbon disulfide (CS2).
The European Union's Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 sets the framework for pesticide maximum residue levels (MRLs). Under Article 6 of this Regulation, any interested party or applicant seeking plant protection product authorization, as per Council Directive 91/414/EEC (now superseded by Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009), must submit an MRL modification request to a Member State, following Article 7's stipulations.

Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) for dithiocarbamates, including mancozeb, are established within Annex II and IIIB of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. While mancozeb itself lacks a dedicated MRL, its residues, along with those of maneb, metiram, propineb, thiram, and ziram, are measured as carbon disulfide (CS2). This is because CS2 is a common component produced by analytical techniques for all these dithiocarbamates. Nevertheless, propineb, thiram, and ziram do have individual MRLs due to the availability of specific analytical methods for their detection. Since the regulation's implementation, EFSA has provided numerous opinions regarding MRL adjustments for dithiocarbamates, influencing subsequent EU legislation.
While Codex Alimentarius has set maximum residue limits (CXLs) for many products, dithiocarbamates in persimmons lack specific CXLs. Appendix A provides details on the intended Good Agricultural Practice (GAP) for mancozeb.
EFSA's assessment drew upon various key documents. These included the evaluation report from Spain (2015) and the draft assessment report (DAR) from Italy (2000), prepared under Directive 91/414/EEC. Further input came from the European Commission's 2009 review of mancozeb and the 2012 JMPR Evaluation report by FAO. Additionally, EFSA considered its own prior opinions on dithiocarbamates from 2009, 2010, and 2011. The entire evaluation adhered to the legal framework of Commission Regulation (EU) No 546/2011, specifically the Uniform Principles for the Evaluation and Authorisation of Plant Protection Products, alongside relevant guidance documents for pesticide residue risk assessment.
The RMS (Italy, 2000) evaluated the analytical methods provided by applicants for detecting mancozeb residues in plant commodities.
Analytical techniques for mancozeb lack a dedicated enforcement method. Instead, common analytical approaches convert dithiocarbamates into their CS2 component, which is subsequently quantified through chromatographic methods like HS-GS, GC-ECD, GC-FPD, HPLC-UVD, and HPLC-MSD, or via colorimetry. These methods can achieve a limit of quantification (LOQ) between 0.01 and 0.10 mg/kg across various commodities, including those with high water or acid content, and dry/starchy goods. However, these screening tools cannot differentiate the specific dithiocarbamate initially applied to a crop. Consequently, if residues are detected, further analysis using specific methods is required to pinpoint the exact active substance, though such specific methods are presently limited to thiram, propineb, and ziram.
When analyzing dithiocarbamates, laboratories face challenges. Sample preparation is crucial due to the low stability of these compounds in plant matrices, which can lead to CS2 losses. Furthermore, current analytical methods cannot differentiate between naturally occurring CS2 from certain crops (like Brassicaceae and Caricaceae) and pesticide residues, potentially causing false-positive results.
Since persimmons are high in water and not Brassicaceae or Caricaceae crops, no natural carbon disulfide (CS2) is anticipated.
Sufficient analytical methods exist to enforce the mancozeb MRL in persimmons.
The current application does not evaluate analytical methods for residues in animal-derived food, because persimmons are typically not part of livestock feed.

Persimmons are typically consumed raw, so the formation of ethylenethiourea (ETU) is not a concern for this intended use. ETU, a more toxic degradation product of mancozeb, maneb, and metiram, forms at high temperatures. Toxicological reference values for ETU are provided.
The RMS assessed mancozeb's metabolism in primary crops, including fruit, root, cereal, and pulses/oilseed groups, as part of the Directive 91/414/EEC evaluation (Italy, 2000). Table 3 provides a summary of these metabolism studies.
Research into mancozeb's plant metabolism reveals significant breakdown, with most radioactive material integrating into natural biological compounds. These include simple and complex sugars (up to 41% of total radioactive residue), starch (up to 27%), proteins and amino acids (up to 45%), oils or lipids (up to 14%), and lignin (up to 23%). The metabolite ETU was generally undetectable, remaining below the limit of quantification across all crop matrices, with the sole exception of a trace amount (0.0007 mg/kg) found in potato. Given these low levels and the absence of ETU detection in other studies, it was excluded from the residue definition for unprocessed primary crops.
Following metabolism studies, mancozeb (expressed as CS2) was proposed as the residue definition for enforcement and risk assessment. Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 later established CS2 as a screening residue definition for dithiocarbamates, encompassing maneb, mancozeb, metiram, propineb, thiram, and ziram.
For consumer risk assessment, a conversion factor of 1.78 is necessary to re-express CS2 as mancozeb, aligning with the toxicological reference values based on the parent compound.
Given persimmons are fruit, and mancozeb metabolism is consistent across all crop types, the residue definition of mancozeb (as CS2) is suitable for both enforcement and risk assessment.
The applicant provided four outdoor trials from Southern Europe, all conducted in Spain in 2013. These trials supported the MRL application and followed the proposed GAP (3 x 1,800 g/ha, 100-day PHI).
Analysis of samples for mancozeb (expressed as CS2) revealed residue levels between 0.05–0.07 mg/kg in whole fruits. This led to a proposed MRL of 0.20 mg/kg, determined by the OECD MRL calculator.

The integrity of residue data was confirmed by assessing mancozeb stability in plant matrices during storage before analysis.
