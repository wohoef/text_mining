Previous research on complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) use in Taiwan has often concentrated on specific illnesses or particular CAM modalities. This focus has resulted in findings that vary considerably and are challenging to compare with international studies. To address this, the International CAM Questionnaire (I-CAM-Q) was developed as a standardized tool for global comparability. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of CAM treatments received from various healthcare providers in Taiwan, employing an adapted version of the I-CAM-Q, known as I-CAM-QT. The I-CAM-QT was created by translating the Korean version of the I-CAM-Q into traditional Chinese. This translation was chosen due to the shared characteristics of CAM usage and physician licensing systems in both countries. The research involved two phases: an initial pretest survey to refine the questionnaire, followed by an internet-based survey to gather data from the general population. The survey included 1200 respondents. Findings indicated that 37% of participants were advised by Western medicine (WM) physicians to use Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), while 37.7% received similar advice from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) doctors. Beyond CHM, dietary supplements and massage were the most frequently recommended CAM forms by both WM and TCM practitioners. For self-help practices, walking and relaxation techniques were most prevalent, utilized by 61.9% and 40.4% of respondents, respectively. Furthermore, 70.3% of participants reported using at least one type of dietary supplement within the preceding year. In conclusion, this internet-based survey highlights that CHM, dietary supplements, and massage are the CAM modalities most commonly prescribed or recommended by WM physicians and TCM doctors in Taiwan.

Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) has seen widespread adoption globally in recent decades, with Taiwan being no exception. Evidence from international surveys highlights this trend: a 2012 US National Health Interview Survey reported 36.8% of adults used CAM, while a 2013 UK study found a 41.1% prevalence. More recently, a 2017 German study indicated an even higher usage rate of 86.0%. These figures collectively demonstrate the growing acceptance and utilization of CAM worldwide.
Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), particularly traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), has a long history of widespread use in Taiwan. While numerous studies have investigated CAM usage in Taiwan, many have focused on specific patient populations with particular illnesses, such as depression or diabetes. In contrast, broader investigations of the general population have revealed high CAM utilization rates. For instance, three telephone surveys indicated that between 75.5% and 86.9% of respondents had employed at least one CAM therapy annually. A 2009 survey by Lin KC et al. identified religious rites and exercises (qigong or yoga) as the most prevalent CAM at 55.3%, followed by Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) at 45.1%, dietary supplements at 40.2%, massage at 25.9%, scraping (guasha) at 22.2%, and acupuncture at 13.6%.
Many individuals incorporate complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) alongside conventional Western medicine (WM), rather than replacing it entirely, often employing multiple approaches for health maintenance and disease treatment. A significant challenge arises because patients frequently do not disclose their CAM use to healthcare providers during hospital visits. This lack of transparency necessitates a thorough investigation into CAM utilization to inform clinicians. Historically, a unified tool for CAM research was absent, hindering comparability due to variations in national contexts and CAM definitions. Consequently, studies on CAM use across different countries yielded disparate results, making cross-national comparisons difficult.
To address the lack of standardization in CAM research and the growing need for comparable data, the International CAM Questionnaire (I-CAM-Q) was created in 2009 by Norway's National Research Center in Complementary and Alternative Medicine. This standardized tool allows for consistent and comparable results across different countries, facilitating global studies on CAM usage. The I-CAM-Q has since been employed in numerous international surveys.
To assess CAM use in Taiwan, the I-CAM-Q was translated into Traditional Chinese, creating the I-CAM-QT. A web-based survey was then conducted using this adapted questionnaire. This marked the first investigation of CAM utilization in Taiwan with the I-CAM-Q, enabling comparisons with international data.
The International CAM Questionnaire (I-CAM-Q) was originally created in 2009 by a Norwegian research center and has since been adapted for use in various countries. For this study in Taiwan, the Korean version of the I-CAM-Q was translated into Traditional Chinese. This choice was made due to the cultural similarities in CAM use and the comparable doctor licensing systems in Korea and Taiwan, where both have separate licensing for Western medicine (WM) physicians and traditional medicine practitioners. The Korean version itself was based on the Japanese version of the I-CAM-Q. Furthermore, a section on "complications of CAM use" was incorporated to assess any adverse effects experienced by respondents during CAM treatments. The revised questionnaire underwent further adjustments for face validity through expert review by five medical and epidemiological specialists in Taiwan, covering both WM and TCM.
The study employed a cross-sectional design with two phases. Initially, a pretest questionnaire was administered to 10 volunteers at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. This step aimed to assess the questionnaire's applicability and accuracy, and to refine the I-CAM-QT for the subsequent data collection.
The second phase involved an internet-based survey administered by a Japanese company, MACROMILL Inc. The goal was to collect comparable data with international studies, so the survey aimed for 1200 participants, with an equal representation of 120 individuals from each gender within five age groups: twenties, thirties, forties, fifties, and sixties. To ensure comparability across different countries, an equal number of responses were gathered from each generation and gender. Participants working in healthcare or media were excluded from the study.
The study utilized a general population internet address database. Initially, 240 individuals from each generation group were emailed the survey. Follow-up emails were sent to groups that did not meet their target response numbers. The survey period ran from January 25 to February 6, 2018. Importantly, no demographic group was over-represented, and the data collection did not involve stratification by region or any other factor.
Prior to commencement, this web-based survey secured approval from the Taipei Veterans General Hospital IRB (2017-09-003BC) and the Nihon Pharmaceutical University ethics committee (29–03). Registration was also completed with the University Hospital Medical Information Network in Japan (UMIN000029161).
The survey included 1200 participants, with an even distribution across age and gender groups. Demographic details are provided in Table 1. A university education was reported by 45.8% of respondents. The majority of participants described their current health as "good" or "acceptable" (38.3% and 43.1%, respectively), with only 1.3% reporting "very bad" health. Chronic health issues were present in 39.1% of respondents, most commonly hypertension (35.4%), dental disease (26%), and gastrointestinal disease (21.2%). Over two-thirds of participants held private insurance.
In the past year, a significant majority of respondents, 82.5%, accessed healthcare from Western medicine (WM) physicians. Dentists and pharmacists were also frequently consulted, with 66.7% and 45.4% of respondents receiving services from them, respectively. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) doctors were visited by 39.7% of participants. Among other CAM providers, massage practitioners or acupressure therapists served 24.3% of respondents. Participants primarily sought TCM for chronic conditions or general well-being, whereas WM physicians were most often consulted for acute illnesses.
In the past year, Western medicine (WM) physicians most frequently advised dietary supplements (44.8%) for CAM treatments, followed by Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) (37.0%) and massage (24.9%). Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) doctors also commonly prescribed or recommended CHM (37.7%), dietary supplements (19.9%), and massage (18.2%). For both WM and TCM physicians, these CAM treatments were primarily recommended for chronic conditions or general well-being. Further details on CHM use in Taiwan revealed that prescribed manufactured CHM was the most utilized form (39.1%), with herbal dietary supplements (19.8%) and prescribed CHM decoctions (10.8%) also being common.
Walking and relaxation techniques were the most popular self-help practices, utilized by 61.9% and 40.4% of respondents, respectively. Magnet therapy and traditional healing ceremonies were the least common. Participants primarily used these practices to improve their well-being, finding them very or somewhat helpful.
Over 70% of survey respondents reported using at least one dietary supplement in the past year. Of these, nearly 10% continued use at the time of the survey. The primary reason for use was well-being improvement, with over half of respondents finding supplements somewhat helpful.
Pharmacies were the most frequent purchase location for CHM and dietary supplements, with 41.0% of respondents buying there. Drug stores followed at 38.1%, and internet shopping sites at 30.1%. Direct purchases were the least common method.
A small number of respondents, 31 out of 1200, reported experiencing complications from CAM treatments advised by healthcare providers. Approximately half of these complications occurred within the past year. Massage, Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), and chiropractic treatments were the most frequent causes, accounting for 25.8%, 22.6%, and 19.4% of complications, respectively. In contrast, acupuncture, moxibustion, and homeopathy resulted in fewer reported issues. TCM doctors, folk physiotherapists, and massage practitioners were associated with about 60% of these complications. The most common issues were musculoskeletal problems like fractures and pain (64.5%), followed by general, neurological, and allergic symptoms.
Taiwan's healthcare system, similar to those in Korea and China, features two distinct medical doctor types: Western medicine (WM) physicians practicing conventional medicine and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) doctors specializing in traditional medicine. WM physicians can incorporate CAM into their practice, with some dual-licensed doctors proficient in both. TCM doctors, in turn, offer TCM alongside other CAM modalities. This study aimed to determine how frequently various CAM treatments are prescribed or recommended by both WM physicians and TCM doctors in Taiwan.
In Taiwan, 82.5% of respondents sought treatment from Western medicine (WM) physicians, primarily for acute illnesses. In contrast, 39.7% consulted Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) doctors, most often for general well-being. This suggests that Taiwanese individuals choose healthcare providers based on their specific health needs and symptoms.
In Taiwan, our findings indicate that 37.0% of participants used Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) following advice from Western medicine (WM) physicians, while 37.7% did so based on recommendations from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) physicians. These figures can be compared with prior research utilizing the International CAM Questionnaire (I-CAM-Q). Studies in Cambodia, Thailand, and Vietnam reported herbal medicine usage rates of 44.5%, 34.6%, and 42.9%, respectively. Similarly, Korean and Japanese I-CAM-Q studies found prevalence rates of 36.2% for Korean medicines and 31.1% for Kampo medicines. In contrast, a study in Australia showed only 18.5% using herbal medicine, and a Swedish survey reported 23% usage. More recently, a Japanese survey of private clinic doctors indicated Kampo medicines as the most popular CAM at 34.8%. Collectively, these data suggest that herbal medicine remains more prevalent in Asian countries compared to other regions globally.
A prior telephone survey by Lin et al. identified religious rites and exercises (qigong or yoga), Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), and dietary supplements as the most prevalent CAM types, with CHM use at 45.1%. In contrast, our current study found that CHM, dietary supplements, and massage were the CAM modalities most frequently prescribed or recommended by Western medicine (WM) physicians and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) doctors. Specifically, 37.0% and 37.7% of respondents reported CHM use under the advice of WM and TCM physicians, respectively. This discrepancy in CHM usage rates between the studies can be attributed to methodological differences. Lin et al.'s survey broadly inquired about general CHM use, while our study specifically differentiated between CHM recommended by WM physicians and that recommended by TCM doctors. It is important to note that in Taiwan, WM physicians do not directly prescribe CHM but may refer patients to TCM doctors. Furthermore, our study's exclusion of individuals over 69 years old, a demographic potentially more inclined to use CAM, might also contribute to the observed differences in CHM prevalence.
In Taiwan, the utilization of acupuncture was reported by 12.3% of respondents receiving care from Western medicine (WM) physicians and 12.6% from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) doctors. Moxibustion was received by 14.9% of respondents from WM physicians, a slightly higher percentage than the 14.1% who received it from TCM doctors. Massage was prescribed or advised by WM physicians for 24.9% of respondents and by TCM doctors for 18.2%. Cupping therapy was utilized by 12.3% of respondents under WM physician guidance and 10.7% under TCM doctor guidance. Notably, WM physicians demonstrated higher rates of prescribing or advising moxibustion, massage, and cupping compared to TCM doctors. This aligns with previous research suggesting a commitment among senior WM physicians in Taiwan to integrate Chinese medicine with Western medicine. While this integration is still developing, it indicates that WM physicians are increasingly familiar with TCM and CAM therapies. Interestingly, moxibustion use, at 14.9%, was reported more frequently than acupuncture use (12.3%) in this study. This observation is made despite acupuncture and moxibustion often being billed together for national health insurance. A potential explanation lies in the fact that many WM physicians may not differentiate clearly between acupuncture and moxibustion when advising patients. Furthermore, moxibustion is widely practiced not only in hospitals but also by folk therapists and is a common self-care method accessible to individuals at home. The prevalence of moxibustion as a self-care option might lead some patients to mention it first, subsequently receiving medical advice on its use. This widespread accessibility and patient-initiated discussion could contribute to the slightly higher reported use of moxibustion compared to acupuncture.
A significant portion of respondents, 70.3%, reported using dietary supplements in the past year. While 44.8% received recommendations from Western medicine (WM) physicians and 19.9% from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) doctors, a notable 19.8% used herbal dietary supplements without consulting TCM doctors. The study also revealed that 30% of participants purchased these supplements online. This suggests that a considerable number of individuals were using dietary supplements without professional guidance.
Walking and relaxation techniques were the most frequently employed self-help practices among respondents, utilized by 61.9% and 40.4% respectively. Walking's popularity as a self-help method extends to other countries like China, Japan, and Korea. Research has demonstrated walking's positive impact on physical fitness and serum lipids. Relaxation techniques have also been consistently identified as common self-help practices in various studies.
A total of 31 out of 1200 participants reported complications from CAM treatments. These complications were predominantly attributed to TCM doctors and folk physiotherapists, who accounted for half of the reported cases. The study noted that folk physiotherapists in Taiwan often acquire their skills independently and are not required to undergo national certification, potentially contributing to instances of unqualified practitioners causing adverse effects, though the number of such occurrences in this study was small. Nearly 70% of the reported complications stemmed from massage, Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), and chiropractic treatments. Massage and chiropractic therapies were linked to side effects like ecchymosis, pain, and dizziness. CHM, on the other hand, was associated with gastrointestinal issues such as diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. These findings help explain the prevalence of musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, and neurological problems among those experiencing complications. Interestingly, acupuncture resulted in fewer complications in this study compared to other CAM modalities. Previous research identified infection, subcutaneous bleeding, hematomas, discomfort, and pain as the primary adverse effects of acupuncture. The lower complication rate observed in Taiwan may be due to the comprehensive training TCM doctors receive, which includes WM anatomy and clear practice guidelines. Furthermore, acupuncture in Taiwan is restricted to certified TCM doctors or WM physicians who have completed specific training, and the mandatory use of disposable needles by practitioners helps prevent infections.
This study encountered several limitations. Firstly, its web-based nature meant that only individuals aged 20 to 69 were included, as older adults are less likely to use the internet. This exclusion is significant because the elderly tend to use CAM more frequently, potentially leading to an underestimation of overall CAM utilization. Secondly, the absence of face-to-face interviews prevented participants from seeking clarification on questionnaire items. This lack of immediate support for any doubts or questions they might have had could have resulted in respondents selecting answers that did not accurately reflect their experiences.
This internet-based survey on CAM utilization in Taiwan found that Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), dietary supplements, and massage were the most frequently prescribed or recommended CAM therapies by both Western medicine (WM) physicians and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) doctors. A significant proportion of respondents, however, used dietary supplements without professional consultation, highlighting the need for safety warnings. The study also indicated a low incidence of complications from acupuncture treatments in Taiwan. This may be attributed to the comprehensive biomedical training of TCM doctors and the adherence to safe and clear practice guidelines.
