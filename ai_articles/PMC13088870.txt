This study estimated the chronic dietary exposure to four individual Alternaria toxins: alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN). The analysis utilized 15,563 analytical results from 4,249 food samples, with specific counts for each toxin: 3,648 for AOH, 3,654 for AME, 4,168 for TeA, and 4,093 for TEN. A significant challenge in the data was the high proportion of left-censored results, accounting for 92% of the total. Only 1,290 results were quantified, predominantly from 'Oilseeds' and 'Grain milling products'. The highest estimated exposure to AOH was observed in toddlers, with mean exposures ranging from 3.8 to 71.6 ng/kg body weight (bw) per day (lower bound to upper bound, LB–UB). The 95th percentile exposure for this group fell between 11.4 and 270.5 ng/kg bw per day (LB–UB). Fruit and fruit products were identified as the primary contributors to AOH dietary exposure. Similarly, toddlers exhibited the highest exposure to AME, with mean exposures between 3.4 and 38.8 ng/kg bw per day (LB–UB) and 95th percentile exposures between 10.3 and 97.3 ng/kg bw per day (LB–UB). Vegetable oil and pome fruits, specifically pears, were the main sources of AME exposure. Tenuazonic acid (TeA) showed the highest overall exposure levels. Toddlers experienced the highest mean TeA exposure, ranging from 100 to 1,614 ng/kg bw per day (LB–UB). For the 95th percentile, infants showed the highest estimated exposure at 98–3,603 ng/kg bw per day (LB–UB). Cereal-based food for infants and young children was the leading contributor to TeA exposure in infants and toddlers, while tomatoes and tomato-based products were significant for other age groups. Tentoxin (TEN) exposure was highest in toddlers, with mean exposures between 1.6 and 33.4 ng/kg bw per day (LB–UB). The 95th percentile exposure for TEN was approximately 55 ng/kg bw per day (UB) across various age classes. Fruiting vegetables, particularly tomatoes, were the major contributors to TEN dietary exposure. Limited data suggested that vegetarians may have higher dietary exposure to Alternaria toxins compared to the general population.

Fungi from the genus Alternaria are ubiquitous in soil, functioning as both saprophytes and plant pathogens. These fungi synthesize over 70 secondary metabolites, many of which are characterized and known to act as mycotoxins affecting humans and animals. Alternaria fungi commonly infest crops such as cereals, oilseeds, fruits, and vegetables, and their presence in these food items is frequently documented in scientific literature.
In October 2011, the EFSA Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM Panel) released its inaugural European and international risk assessment on Alternaria toxins in food and feed. This initial evaluation focused on four specific toxins: alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN). A significant characteristic of the available data at that time was a high proportion of left-censored results. The highest concentrations of these four toxins were identified in sunflower seeds. The primary contributors to dietary exposure were found to be grain and grain-based products, vegetables (especially tomato products), fruits and fruit products (including juices), alcoholic beverages, oilseeds, and vegetable oils (predominantly sunflower-based). The CONTAM Panel noted a scarcity of toxicity data, leading them to employ the threshold of toxicological concern (TTC) approach to gauge the relative health risks. For the genotoxic toxins AOH and AME, estimated mean chronic dietary exposures at the upper bound (UB) and 95th percentile exceeded the TTC value of 2.5 ng/kg body weight (bw) per day, highlighting a need for more specific toxicity data. In contrast, for the non-genotoxic TeA and TEN, with a TTC of 1,500 ng/kg bw per day, exposure estimates were deemed unlikely to pose a human health concern.
This scientific report focused on four Alternaria toxins: AOH, AME, TeA, and TEN. Initially, 24,461 analytical results from 7,916 food samples were gathered for these toxins. After data evaluation, a refined dataset of 15,563 results from 4,249 samples was utilized for dietary exposure estimations. This final set included 3,648 results for AOH, 3,654 for AME, 4,168 for TeA, and 4,093 for TEN. A significant portion of these samples, 3,574, were analyzed for all four toxins, while others were tested for two or three.
In 2014, the most extensive data on Alternaria toxins were gathered, with Germany contributing the majority of samples (70%), followed by the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and Luxembourg. The dataset for dietary exposure estimation predominantly consisted of left-censored data (92%). Out of the total, 1,290 results were quantified, with tenuazonic acid (TeA) being the most frequently reported (n=822), followed by tentoxin (TEN) (n=196), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME) (n=163), and alternariol (AOH) (n=109).
Highest mean levels of alternariol (AOH) were found in grains like buckwheat (27.9–33.1 μg/kg) and oats (35.3–39.7 μg/kg). AOH was also detected in various tomato-based products. These included tomato puree (4.6–17.1 μg/kg), tomato sauce (4.3–17.4 μg/kg), and sun-dried tomatoes (2.5–17.4 μg/kg), among others.
AME levels were generally lower than AOH. However, tree nuts and oil seeds, specifically Chestnuts and Sesame seeds, showed the highest mean AME concentrations.
Tenuazonic acid (TeA) exhibited significantly higher levels compared to other Alternaria toxins. It was frequently detected in tomatoes and various tomato-based products. For instance, dried tomato soup, labeled as 'Vegetable/herb soup', showed a mean TeA concentration of 351.2 μg/kg. Sun-dried tomatoes (226–233 μg/kg) and tomato purée (211–212 μg/kg) also contained substantial amounts. TeA was further identified in tomato sauce ('Vegetable sauce'), tomato ketchup, tomato juice, and fresh tomatoes (39–54 μg/kg). Notably, a group of cereal-based foods for infants and young children, specifically those requiring reconstitution with water or other protein-free liquids, reported average TeA values of 496–497 μg/kg, with over 90% of these samples being quantified.
Tentoxin (TEN) levels were generally the lowest among the four Alternaria toxins studied. The highest concentrations were observed in sunflower seeds, where nearly half of the samples were quantified, with levels ranging from 79 to 82 μg/kg.
Toddlers and other children exhibited the highest estimated mean and 95th percentile dietary exposure to alternariol (AOH). For toddlers, mean exposure ranged from 3.8 to 71.6 ng/kg body weight (bw) per day, with 95th percentile exposure between 11.4 and 270.5 ng/kg bw per day. Other children showed mean exposures of 2.0 to 54.4 ng/kg bw per day and 95th percentile exposures from 9.1 to 218.9 ng/kg bw per day. Across all age groups, fruit and fruit products were identified as the primary contributors to AOH dietary exposure.
Dietary exposure to alternariol monomethyl ether (AME) was found to be lower than that of alternariol (AOH). Similar to AOH, the highest AME exposure was observed in toddlers and other children. For toddlers, mean exposure ranged from 3.4 to 38.8 ng/kg body weight per day, with 95th percentile exposures between 10.3 and 97.3 ng/kg bw/day. Other children experienced mean exposures between 1.6 and 34.4 ng/kg bw/day, and 95th percentile exposures from 6.5 to 89.9 ng/kg bw/day. Vegetable oils and pome fruits, particularly pears, were identified as the primary sources of AME exposure.
Tenuazonic acid (TeA) exposure significantly exceeded that of other Alternaria toxins. Toddlers and infants exhibited the highest mean TeA exposure, with daily intakes ranging from 100 to 1,614 ng/kg body weight (bw) for toddlers and 37 to 1,490 ng/kg bw for infants. For the 95th percentile of exposure, infants showed the highest estimates (98–3,603 ng/kg bw per day), followed by toddlers (209–1,216 ng/kg bw per day). In infants, cereal-based foods for young children were the primary source of TeA. For all other age groups, tomatoes and tomato-based products were the principal contributors to TeA exposure.
Tentoxin (TEN) exposure was highest in toddlers and other children. For toddlers, mean exposure ranged from 1.6 to 33.4 ng/kg body weight (bw) per day, and for other children, from 0.7 to 30.1 ng/kg bw per day. The 95th percentile exposure for toddlers, other children, and adolescents approached 55 ng/kg bw per day. Across all age groups, tomatoes, classified as fruiting vegetables, were the primary source of TEN exposure, contributing a median of 60% to total exposure, with a maximum recorded contribution of 93%.
Vegetarians may experience higher dietary exposure to Alternaria toxins compared to the general population, though this is based on limited data. Significant uncertainties affect these estimations. These include concerns about the representativeness of food samples, as 70% of analytical data originated from a single Member State. The high proportion of left-censored data (92%) also introduces uncertainty. Furthermore, challenges in linking occurrence and consumption data for specific tomato products, such as sauces and soups, contribute to this uncertainty.
Compared to the 2011 EFSA opinion, current dietary exposure estimates for AOH showed similar or slightly lower lower-bound (LB) values, while upper-bound (UB) estimations were considerably lower. For AME, both LB and UB exposure estimates were higher, particularly the UB values. This increase is attributed to the inclusion of occurrence data from staple foods like bread and fine bakery wares, which exhibited significant differences between LB and UB values. Dietary exposure to TeA and TEN also showed higher estimates overall (both LB and UB) compared to the 2011 assessment. This rise is largely due to a greater volume of occurrence data, leading to better food coverage, and higher reported levels in certain food commodities that significantly contribute to exposure. While the primary food contributors to Alternaria toxin exposure remained largely consistent with the 2011 opinion, a notable difference was the increased contribution of tomatoes and tomato-based products, especially concerning TeA exposure.
To reduce uncertainty in Alternaria toxin exposure assessments, more analytical data are needed for key food items like fruits, tomato products, and infant cereals. This requires using sensitive analytical methods to improve data quality.
Fungi of the *Alternaria* genus are ubiquitous in soil, functioning as both saprophytes and plant pathogens. These fungi are known to produce over 70 secondary metabolites, some of which have been identified as mycotoxins affecting humans and animals. The EFSA Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM Panel) has previously assessed several of these, including alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), iso-TeA, altertoxins (ATX), tentoxin (TEN), altenuene (ALT), and *Alternaria alternata* f. sp. *lycopersici* toxins (AAL-toxins). These mycotoxins are categorized into five main structural classes. Dibenzo-α-pyrones encompass AOH, AME, and ALT, while TeA and iso-TeA form another group. Perylene quinones include altertoxins I, II, and III, along with stemphyltoxin III. AAL-toxins, derived from *A. alternata* f. sp. *lycopersici*, are further divided into AAL-TA and AAL-TB, differing in the specific amino-dimethylheptadeca-pentol or -tetrol esterified with 1,2,3-propane-tricarboxylic acid. Tentoxin (TEN), a cyclic tetrapeptide, represents a miscellaneous structural category.
Alternaria fungi, particularly *Alternaria alternata*, are widespread plant pests that produce mycotoxins. These fungi commonly contaminate cereals, oilseeds, fruits, and vegetables, and can even spoil food during refrigeration.
In October 2011, the EFSA Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM Panel) released the first European or international risk assessment on Alternaria toxins in food and feed. This assessment focused on four specific toxins: alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN), whose chemical structures were presented. The risk assessment utilized occurrence data from various food categories, including grains and grain-based products, tomato and tomato-based products, sunflower seeds and oil, fruits and fruit juices, as well as beer and wine. Sunflower seeds exhibited the highest concentrations of these toxins. The primary sources of dietary exposure were identified as grain and grain-based products, and vegetables. The CONTAM Panel also highlighted a lack of information regarding the impact of food processing on these toxins, though some indications suggested potential concentration increases under favorable conditions and chemical stability during processing.
Due to a scarcity of toxicity data, the CONTAM Panel employed the threshold of toxicological concern (TTC) approach to evaluate the potential risks of these mycotoxins. For genotoxic Alternaria toxins (AOH and AME), estimated chronic dietary exposures, both mean at the upper bound and 95th percentile, surpassed the TTC value of 2.5 ng/kg body weight per day. This finding highlighted the necessity for more specific toxicity information. In contrast, exposure estimates for non-genotoxic Alternaria toxins (TeA and TEN), with a TTC of 1,500 ng/kg body weight per day, were deemed unlikely to pose a human health concern.
Following recommendations from the CONTAM Panel, a research project investigating the toxicokinetics and in vivo genotoxicity of Alternaria toxins was conducted. In November 2014, the Panel reviewed the project's findings, concluding that they neither confirmed nor increased concerns about the potential in vivo genotoxicity of these toxins. Consequently, the CONTAM Panel determined that a revision of its 2011 risk assessment was not necessary at that time. Currently, no regulations exist in Europe concerning Alternaria toxins in food and feed. This background information was provided by the European Commission.
The European Commission is considering setting maximum levels for certain Alternaria toxins in food, prompted by EFSA's 2011 scientific opinion and the emergence of new occurrence data. To inform this, an updated dietary exposure assessment for alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN) individually is required. This assessment will leverage newly available occurrence data and the updated comprehensive food consumption database.
The European Commission has requested EFSA to conduct an individual dietary exposure assessment for alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN). This assessment will utilize available occurrence data from the EFSA database and the updated comprehensive food consumption database, as per Article 31(1) of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002.
In February 2016, EFSA's Evidence Management Unit (DATA unit) issued a call for data to supplement existing Alternaria toxin information in its database. This initiative invited European national food authorities, research institutions, academic bodies, and food businesses to contribute analytical data on these toxins in food. Submissions adhered to EFSA's Standard Sample Description guidance, with occurrence data managed according to established standard operating procedures for data collection, validation, and analysis of both food consumption and occurrence data.
As of September 2016, EFSA's chemical occurrence database held 34,072 analytical results for various Alternaria toxins in food. Specifically for the four toxins of interest – alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN) – the database contained 24,461 results. These were broken down as follows: 6,662 for AOH, 6,567 for AME, 5,918 for TeA, and 5,314 for TEN.
To ensure data quality for exposure assessment, the initial dataset underwent rigorous cleaning and validation according to EFSA's Standard Operating Procedures. This process involved meticulous examination of analytical methods, reporting units, expression of results, and FoodEx classification codes for food samples. The comprehensive data analysis is detailed in Section 3.
To address the prevalence of left-censored data, a substitution method was employed, aligning with recommendations from both the WHO/IPCS and EFSA. This approach is suitable for chemicals expected to be present in food, such as mycotoxins. For the lower bound (LB) estimation, values below the limit of quantification (LOQ) and limit of detection (LOD) were set to zero. Conversely, for the upper bound (UB) estimation, results below the LOD were assigned the LOD value, and those below the LOQ were assigned the reported LOQ. A middle bound (MB) scenario was also calculated, using LOD/2 or LOQ/2 for left-censored data, providing an intermediate estimate between the LB and UB. This methodology ensures a comprehensive treatment of the data, accounting for the uncertainty introduced by non-quantified results.
The EFSA Comprehensive European Food Consumption Database, established in 2010, compiles individual-level food consumption data from national surveys. Its latest iteration includes data from 51 dietary surveys across 23 Member States, encompassing 94,532 individuals. Detailed usage guidelines for this database are available in EFSA's guidance documents.
Subjects in dietary surveys were categorized by age. Two surveys also included specific groups: pregnant women in Latvia and lactating women in Greece.
The European Union's Comprehensive Food Consumption Database offers the most extensive data available. It compiles consumption information from 51 surveys across 23 Member States, covering 94,532 individuals. Data collection methods varied, including 24- or 48-hour recalls and 3- to 7-day dietary records, meaning direct country comparisons may be misleading.
To facilitate the linkage of occurrence and consumption data for food-borne hazard exposure assessments, EFSA developed the FoodEx classification system in 2009. This system organizes food into 20 primary groups. These groups are further subdivided into 140 subgroups at the second level, 1,261 items at the third level, and approximately 1,800 specific food names or generic descriptions at the fourth level.
Chronic dietary exposure to four Alternaria toxins (AOH, AME, TeA, TEN) was assessed individually, building upon the 2011 EFSA Scientific Opinion.
For chronic exposure assessment, dietary surveys relying on only one day of data per subject were excluded, as they are insufficient for evaluating repeated exposure. This exclusion also applied to individuals within longer surveys who only provided one day of consumption information. Consequently, food consumption data from 35 recent dietary surveys conducted across 19 European countries, as found in the latest Comprehensive Database, were utilized. The total number of subjects included in this chronic exposure assessment is detailed in Appendix A.
To estimate chronic dietary exposure to individual Alternaria toxins, individual-level food consumption and body weight data from the Comprehensive Database were utilized. Occurrence data were matched with consumption data at the most granular FoodEx level available. Food commodities were then categorized to clarify their contribution to overall dietary exposure for each toxin. Exposure estimates were computed for every dietary survey and age group. Mean and 95th percentile chronic dietary exposures for the four Alternaria toxins were determined by integrating average toxin occurrence values with individual average daily food consumption. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Statistical Software (SAS enterprise guide 5.1).
Before estimating dietary exposure, the 24,461 analytical results from 7,916 food samples for the four Alternaria toxins underwent assessment. This involved a thorough review of food classification, units, and result expression (e.g., fresh vs. dry weight). The dataset incorporated 9,009 results from the German Federal Office of Consumer Protection and Food Safety (BVL), submitted in response to a February 2016 data request. Samples originated from Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Poland, Spain, and the United Kingdom, with a small number unspecified as 'European union'. The majority of data (92%) originated from national competent authorities, with limited contributions from academia (2%) and older literature data (1993–2009) previously used in the 2011 EFSA Scientific Opinion.
To ensure the accuracy of dietary exposure estimations, analytical results predating 2010 were excluded, totaling 6,018 results from 2,936 samples, as they might not reflect current Alternaria toxin levels. Additionally, 929 results from 269 samples identified as 'Grains as crops,' with potential use in either food or feed, were removed. Samples flagged as 'Suspect samples' within the Standard Sample Description (SSD) were also excluded (169 results from 44 samples) due to concerns about non-random sampling potentially biasing estimates. Further data refinement involved excluding samples with a limit of quantification (LOQ) at or above 100 μg/kg for any toxin, following EFSA guidance and literature review. This measure, aimed at mitigating the impact of left-censored data and less sensitive analytical methods on upper-bound (UB) exposure scenarios, resulted in the exclusion of 891 samples (1,782 analytical results), of which only two were quantified for AOH and AME.
A total of 15,563 analytical results from 4,249 samples formed the final dataset for Alternaria toxins. This included 3,648 results for AOH, 3,654 for AME, 4,168 for TeA, and 4,093 for TEN. Most samples (3,574) were tested for all four toxins, with the remaining analyzed for fewer. All results were reported as whole weight.
The highest number of analytical results for Alternaria toxins in the final dataset was collected in 2014. Germany provided the most data, followed by the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and Luxembourg, among the four Member States that reported sampling information.

For dried tomato soup, designated as 'Vegetable soup,' concentrations were adjusted by a factor of 10 to match liquid consumption data. Similarly, consumption instances of 'Cereal-based food for infants and young children' were modified. Depending on whether the product was a 'Simple cereal' reconstituted with milk or another liquid, or a 'Cereal with added high protein' reconstituted with water, a division factor of 7 or 4 was used, respectively. These adjustments ensured accurate linkage between toxin levels and actual food intake.

Consequently, lower bound (LB) estimations were primarily employed for comparing food group contributions to total exposure, though upper bound (UB) estimations were also occasionally considered.

For younger demographics ('Infants', 'Toddlers', and 'Other children'), 'fruit nectar' emerged as a contributor, particularly under the upper bound scenario. In contrast, for adult populations ('Adults', 'Elderly', and 'Very elderly'), 'wine' was a significant factor, contributing over 20% in numerous surveys, while 'breakfast cereals' were also noted, primarily at the upper bound.
Despite buckwheat and oat grains showing high AOH levels, their low consumption across surveys limited their contribution to overall exposure. Generally, grains are not widely consumed, and specifically, buckwheat and oats are less common in grain-based products compared to wheat.

Uncertainties in assessing dietary exposure to Alternaria toxins were qualitatively evaluated, adhering to the EFSA Scientific Committee's guidance on uncertainties in dietary exposure assessment.
Uncertainty in the dietary exposure estimates for Alternaria toxins stems from several factors. Firstly, the representativeness of food samples across Europe is questionable, as 70% of the analytical data originated from a single Member State. Secondly, a significant proportion (92%) of the final dataset comprised left-censored data, meaning results were below the limit of quantification. This is particularly problematic for widely consumed items like bread, where few quantified results exist. While lower bound (LB) estimates may underestimate exposure, upper bound (UB) estimates can overestimate it. The scarcity of quantified results in specific food subgroups also introduces uncertainty regarding mean concentration values and the identification of primary exposure sources. Furthermore, the absence of information on the analytical methods used for approximately 70% of the samples adds further doubt to the reported toxin levels.
Specific uncertainties impact the dietary exposure assessment for Alternaria toxins, particularly concerning the EFSA Comprehensive Food Consumption Database. A key issue is linking occurrence and consumption data for certain tomato products. For instance, tomato sauce (labeled 'Vegetable sauce') and tomato soup ('Vegetable soup') lack specific FoodEx level 4 codes, necessitating linkage at FoodEx level 3. The absence of occurrence data for commonly consumed tomato products like 'Tomato broth' also introduces uncertainty into exposure estimates. Further uncertainty arises when connecting consumption data for reconstituted cereals with occurrence data. Occurrence values for these cereals often pertain to gluten-free products, a detail not consistently available at the consumption level. This discrepancy affects the accuracy of exposure estimations for these food categories and, consequently, the overall assessment.

The dietary exposure estimates for Alternaria toxins in this report likely overestimate European population exposure, especially at the upper bound.
This study estimated the chronic dietary exposure to four Alternaria toxins—alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME), tenuazonic acid (TeA), and tentoxin (TEN)—using data from 2010 to 2015. The final dataset comprised 15,563 analytical results from 4,249 samples, with specific counts for each toxin: 3,648 for AOH, 3,654 for AME, 4,168 for TeA, and 4,093 for TEN. A significant challenge in this analysis was the high proportion of left-censored data, accounting for 92% of all analytical results. Consequently, only 1,290 results were quantified, with TeA having the most quantified results (n=822), followed by TEN (n=196), AME (n=163), and AOH (n=109). TeA exhibited the highest levels among the four toxins. While the food commodities with the highest mean levels varied by toxin, tomato-based products, tree nuts, oilseeds, grains, and fruits generally showed relatively high concentrations. The extensive use of left-censored data significantly impacted the chronic dietary exposure estimations. Generally, the highest exposures were observed in the "Toddlers" and "Other children" age groups. TeA exposure was notably higher than that of the other toxins, with "Infants" and "Toddlers" experiencing the greatest exposure, largely due to its presence in "Cereal-based food for infants and young children." Preliminary findings suggest that vegetarians may have a higher dietary exposure to these toxins compared to the general population. When compared to a 2011 EFSA opinion, this study found higher exposure estimates (both lower and upper bounds), attributed to a greater availability of occurrence data and higher reported levels in certain food commodities that are significant contributors to exposure.
To reduce uncertainty in Alternaria toxin exposure assessments, more sensitive analytical methods are needed to address the high percentage of left-censored data. Additionally, generating more occurrence data for key food commodities, such as fruits, tomatoes, and infant foods, is crucial.

The EFSA Comprehensive European Food Consumption Database, updated in 2016, contains data from 51 dietary surveys across 23 EU Member States, encompassing 94,532 individuals. This database, crucial for chronic exposure assessments, includes detailed food consumption information at the individual level, collected via 24- or 48-hour recalls and 3- to 7-day records. Subjects were categorized into various age groups, with specific surveys also providing data for pregnant and lactating women. Food consumption and occurrence data were standardized using the FoodEx classification system, allowing for linkage and exposure estimations. For chronic exposure, surveys with only one reporting day per subject were excluded, focusing on 35 recent surveys from 19 countries.
