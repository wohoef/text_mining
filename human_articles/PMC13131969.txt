Human papillomavirus (HPV) is well recognized as a causative agent for anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers, however, the biology of HPV infection at different mucosal locations, specifically the oral cavity, is not well understood. Importantly, it has yet to be determined if oral tissues are permissive for HPV infection and replication. We investigated for the first time the titers, infectivity, and maturation of HPV16 in oral epithelial versus genital epithelial tissue. We show that infectious HPV16 virions can be produced in oral tissue. This demonstrates, for the first time, that infectious virus could be spread via the oral cavity. HPV16 derived from oral tissue utilize a tissue-spanning redox gradient that facilitates the maturation of virions over time. Maturation is manifested by virion stability and increased susceptibility to neutralization with anti-HPV16 L1 antibodies. However, susceptibility to neutralization by anti-HPV16 L2 specific antibodies decreases during the maturation of HPV16 virions in oral tissue.

In a recent report to the nation published in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute, it was reported that death rates continued to decline for all cancers combined for men and women of all major racial and ethnic groups and for most major cancer sites (Jemal et al., 2013). However, the incidence rates increased for oropharynx human papillomavirus- (HPV-) associated cancers (Jemal et al., 2013). There have also been substantial changes in the burden of cancer affecting HIV-infected individuals in the U.S. during a 12-year period spanning the introduction of highly active anti-retroviral therapy (HAART). As a result of these temporal changes, non-AIDS-defining malignancies have come to comprise the majority of cancers in HIV-infected persons during the HAART era (Engels et al., 2008). Among these emerging malignancies, HPV-associated cancers of the oral cavity/pharynx increased significantly following an AIDS diagnosis (Engels et al., 2008; Grulich et al., 2010). HPV is rapidly changing the landscape of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). However, the mechanisms and natural history of oral HPV infection and oncogenic progression is poorly understood. Extrapolation of knowledge of HPV-associated infection and oncogenicity in the genital tract is not satisfactory to our understanding of oral HPV disease. HPV is necessary, but not sufficient, to cause invasive cervical cancer. Although HPV is an important cause of oropharyngeal cancer, HPV infection is neither necessary nor sufficient to cause HNSCC that can occur in the absence of HPV. Therefore, HPV-HNSCC needs to be studied as its own entity.
HPV16 is the most dominant HPV type, accounting for (> 90%) HPV-associated HNSCCs. HPV16 is also the same type that commonly leads to HPV-associated anogenital cancers (Marur et al., 2010). While the lifecycle of HPV16 has been extensively studied in anogenital tissues, little has been reported on the HPV16 life cycle in oral tissues. In fact, direct evidence that oral tissue can support HPV replication of infectious virus particles is lacking.
Primary human tonsil (HTLK) and primary human foreskin keratinocytes (HFK) were isolated and grown from tonsil and foreskin tissues, respectively, according to the method described previously (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b). HPV16+ cell lines were generated as previously described (McLaughlin-Drubin et al., 2004). In short, pBSHPV16 plasmid DNA was digested with BamH1 to linearize the viral DNA and separate it from the vector sequence. A total of 10 μg viral DNA was electroporated into HTLKs or HFKs using a Gene Pulser (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b). Immortalized keratinocytes stably maintaining HPV16 genomes following electroporation were cultured with J2 3T3 feeder cells and maintained in E-medium.
Immortalized HTLK cell lines were characterized by Southern blot hybridization, as previously described, to determine the state and quantity of the HPV16 genome within the immortalized keratinocytes (Meyers et al., 1992a). Briefly, total cellular DNA was isolated from the immortalized cell line. To linearize the viral genome, 5 μg of the total cellular DNA was digested with BamHI. Samples were then separated by gel electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel, transferred onto a GeneScreen Plus membrane (New England Nuclear Research Products) and probed with a HPV16 specific, whole genome probe, as previously described (McLaughlin-Drubin et al., 2004, 2003; Meyers et al., 1992a, 1997).
Organotypic (raft) cultures were grown as previously described (Meyers et al., 1992a, 1997). Immortalized HTLK and HFK lines persistently infected with HPV16 were seeded onto collagen matrices consisting of rat-tail type 1 collagen and containing J2 3T3 feeder cells. Following cell attachment and growth to confluence, the matrices were lifted onto stainless steel grids and fed with E-medium supplemented with 10 μM 1,2-dioctanoyl-sn-glycerol (C8:O; Sigma Chemical Company) via diffusion from below. Raft cultures were allowed to stratify and differentiate for 10 days, 15 days, or 20 days.
Raft culture tissues were harvested at different time points, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Four-micrometer sections were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Meyers et al., 1992b, 1997).
HPV infected raft tissues were harvested as has been described (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b). Viral preparations (VPs) were prepared as previously described (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013). Briefly, two rafts were dounce homogenized in 500 μl of phosphate buffer (0.05 M sodium phosphate [pH 8.0]/2 mM MgCl2). Homogenizers were rinsed with 250 μl of phosphate buffer. Then, un-protected genomes were digested by the addition of 1.5 μl (375 U) of benzonase to 750 μl of VP, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Samples were brought to 1 M NaCl by adding 188 μl of ice-cold 5 M NaCl. Then, samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 4 °C and 10,500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were stored at −80 °C for further experiments. Optiprep purification was performed as described previously (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b). Briefly, OptiPrep gradients were prepared by underlaying 27%, 33%, and 39% Optiprep. Gradients were allowed to diffuse for 1 h at room temperature. Then, 600 μl of clarified, benzonase-treated virus preps was layered on top of the gradient. Tubes were then centrifuged in a SW55 rotor (Beckman) at 234,000 × g for 3.5 h at 16 °C. After centrifugation, 11–500 μl fractions were carefully collected, top to bottom, from each tube.
HPV16 titers were measured as previously described (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013). Briefly, viral genomes were released from a benzonase treated virus preparation by re-suspension in 200 μl HPRT DNA extraction buffer (400 mM NaCl/10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4]/10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), 2 μl 20 mg/ml proteinase K, and 10 μl 10% SDS for 2–4 h at 37 °C. Following extraction, the DNA was purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitated overnight at −20 °C. To quantify the viral genomes, a Thermo Scientific Maxima SYBR Green qPCR kit was utilized. Amplification of the HPV16 E2 open reading frame (ORF) was performed using 0.3 μM of forward primer 5′-CCATATAGACTATTGGAAACACATGCGCC-3′ (nucleotides (Zhao et al., 2000) 2839 to 2868) and 0.3 μM of reverse primer 5′-CGTTAGTTGCAGTTCAATTGCTTGTAATGC-3′ (nt 2960 to 2989). Amplification of the E2 ORF of serially diluted pBSHPV16 DNA, ranging from 108 – 104 copies/μl served to generate a standard curve. A Bio-Rad iQ5 Multicolor Real-Time qPCR machine and software were utilized for PCR amplifications and subsequent data analysis.
All infectivity studies were performed using HaCaT keratinocytes. HaCaT cells were seeded 50,000 cells/well in 24-well plates and infectivity assays were performed as previously described (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013). Briefly, cells were incubated with virus for 48 h at 37 °C/5% CO2 followed by mRNA harvest using a RNeasy kit (Qiagen). Infections were analyzed using a RT-qPCR based assay detecting levels of the E1∧E4 splice transcript. HPV16 E1∧E4 was detected using the forward primer 5′-GCTGATCCTAGCAACGAAGTATC-3′, the reverse primer 5′-TTCTTCGGGTGCCCAAAGGC-3′, and the probe 5′-(6-FAM)CCCGCCGCGACCCATACCAAAGCC(BHQ-1)-3′. For neutralization assays, virus was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C prior to infection with the conformation-dependent anti-L1 antibody H16. V5 (1:1000 dilution; a kind gifts from Neil Christensen, Penn State College of Medicine) or the anti-L2 antibodies RG-1 (1:100 dilution; a kind gift from Richard Roden, Johns Hopkins) and L2#4 (1:100 dilution; a kind gift from Tadahito Kanda, the RIKEN institute) followed by the infectivity assay as described previously (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013).
Aliquots (70 μl) from OptiPrep fractions were boiled for 10 min in 6% 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) loading buffer and then loaded onto 8–12% polyacrylamide gels. To detect HPV16 L1, the Camvir-1 anti-HPV16 L1 monoclonal antibody (BD Pharmingen) was used at a dilution of 1: 2500 according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. To determine the relative quantity of L1 present in the bands, a Bio-Rad G8-800 calibrated densitometer along with Quantity One analysis software was used.
Frozen sections of HPV16-infected tonsil and foreskin epithelial tissue were stained with DACM (N-[7-Dimethylamino-4-Methyl-coumarinyl]-Maleimide) to detect free sulfhydryls and disulfide bond-specific sulfhydryls as previously described (Ogawa and Taneda, 1979; Ogawa et al., 1979). Tissue sections were imaged on a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope and images were taken with a CoolSnap Cf2 camera and NIS-elements 3.10 software.
To develop producer cell lines derived from tonsil and foreskin keratinocytes, primary tonsil (HTLK) and foreskin keratinocytes (HFK) were electroporated with wild-type HPV16 genomes. To account for potential host differences, the HPV16 genome was electroporated into multiple batches of HTLKs and HFKs and multiple cell lines for each cell type were selected by immortalization as measured by greater than 20 population doublings. The maintenance of episomal HPV16 genomes in the immortalized cell lines was confirmed by Southern blot. The HPV16 HTLK cell lines generated stably maintain approximately 100 episomal copies of HPV16 DNA per cell (Fig. 1A). These HPV16 persistently ‘infected’ cell lines were then grown in organotypic culture. The organotypic tissues were harvested after 10-, 15- and 20-days of growth. H&E staining shows that the HPV infection slightly disturbs differentiation morphology as compared to the uninfected tissues (Fig. 1B). Unlike epithelia in vivo, organotypic tissues are always horizontal and dead cells are not sloughed off, leading to a buildup of the cornified layer of tissue, which displays an increased thickness over time. Therefore, the accumulation of cells in the top, cornified layer is much thicker than what you would observe from epithelia in vivo. The HPV16 infected tonsil tissues has large amounts of vacuolated cells in the upper strata, similar to uninfected tonsil tissue (Fig. 1Bi–iv). The HPV16 infected foreskin tissue often has a thickened top layer composed of cornified cells (Fig. 1Bv–viii) as we have previously demonstrated (McLaughlin-Drubin et al., 2004, 2003; Meyers et al., 1997).
To compare the production of HPV16 virions in foreskin versus tonsil epithelia, we measured viral titers after 10-, 15- and 20-days of organotypic tissue growth (Fig. 2A and B). HPV16 virion production in tonsil tissue showed a time dependent increase in the quantity of encapsidated, endonuclease resistant genomes. HPV16 replication in foreskin tissue peaked after 15-days of tissue growth then dropped about 1/3rd after 20-days of tissue growth, as we have previously reported for foreskin tissue (Conway et al., 2009b).
To compare the infectivity efficiency of HPV16 from tonsil and foreskin tissues, we infected HaCaT cells with equal viral genome equivalents (VGEs) of 5 and measured infectivity by RT-qPCR as previously described (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013). The level of infectivity for 15-d and 20-d virus is shown as relative infectivity, compared to the infectivity of 10-d virus. The infectivity efficiency of HPV16 increased in a time dependent manner (Fig. 2C and D) for virus grown in both tissues, similar to what we previously reported for HPV16 in foreskin tissue (Conway et al., 2009b). HPV16 from foreskin tissue grown for 15-days demonstrated a 50% increase in infectivity over 10-d virus, while HPV16 infection from 20-d foreskin tissue was nearly twice as efficient as virus from 15-d foreskin tissues (Fig. 2D). Time dependent changes in infectivity of HPV16 grown in tonsil tissues for 10-, 15-, and 20-days differed in relative changes in infection, with 15-d and 20-d HPV16 showing 7-times and 10-times more relative infectivity than virus from 10-d tissues (Fig. 2C). There has always been speculation that the oral cavity could replicate and spread infectious HPV. However, assays previously performed only measured the presence of viral genomes. This is the first direct evidence that oral tissues can replicate infectious HPV.
To assess potential differences in stability and maturation between HPV16 grown in tonsil and foreskin tissues, we fractionated the viral preparations on OptiPrep step gradients and then measured viral titers and infectivity in each fraction. OptiPrep gradient fractionation of HPV virions has been reported to destabilize capsids (Buck et al., 2004; Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011b). The majority of viral genomes from fractionated 10-d foreskin tissue derived HPV16 was detected in fractions 1–4 rather than in the high infectivity fractions 6–8 (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; Cruz and Meyers, 2013), suggesting that high shear forces and hydrostatic pressure associated with ultracentrifugation may have led to the destruction of capsids, as suggested by other studies (Buck et al., 2005). When 15- and 20-d foreskin tissue derived HPV16 were OptiPrep fractionated and viral genomes quantitated, viral genomes were less prevalent in fractions 1–4 and more prevalent in the denser fractions 5–9, suggesting that 15- and 20-d virions were more stable than 10-d virions (Fig. 3B), similar to what we have previously reported (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b). Conversely, HPV16 derived from tonsil tissues showed an OptiPrep gradient profile different from the foreskin derived HPV16 (Fig. 3A). After 10-days of tonsil tissue growth, the virus particles were spread evenly across all gradient fractions, suggesting an equal mixture of viral particles at all levels of stability. As the tonsil tissue growth time increased to 15- and 20-days, the virus particles shifted to having the highest proportions of particles being in fractions 6–8, the more stable fractions (Fig. 3A). This suggests a fundamental difference between HPV16 grown in tonsil and foreskin tissues. HPV16 maturation in tonsil epithelium appears to occur earlier and to a greater percentage of the total virus than what is observed with HPV16 grown in foreskin tissues.
As a method to quantify the relative stability of particles within virus preparations, the ratio of unstably encapsidated viral genomes (fractions 1–4) over stably encapsidated viral genomes (fractions 6–8) was taken. In foreskin derived HPV16 viral preparations, we detected 14.8, 1.0 and 0.56 unstably encapsidated viral genomes for every stably encapsidated viral genome in 10-, 15-, and 20-d tissue, respectively (Fig. 3C). This data suggests that many more unstably encapsidated virions exist in 10-d foreskin tissues compared to 15- and 20-d foreskin tissues. This is similar to what we have previously reported (Conway et al., 2009b, 2011b). We then compared the relative stability of HPV16 grown in tonsil tissues and measured ratios of 2.7, 0.48, and 0.34 unstably encapsidated viral genomes for every stably encapsidated viral genome in 10-, 15-, and 20-d tissue, respectively (Fig. 3C). This finding suggests that HPV16 is able to mature faster to become a more stable virion in tonsil epithelium than in foreskin epithelium.
We utilized RT-qPCR to compare infectivity of virions extracted from 10-, 15-, and 20-d foreskin and tonsil tissues (Fig. 4). In order to obtain infectivity from all fractions of the 10-, 15- and 20-d an equal VGE of 10 was used for all infectivity assays. In contrast to the stability profiles, the infectivity profiles of OptiPrep fractionated HPV16 from 10-, 15-, and 20-d tonsil and foreskin tissues very similar (Fig. 4A and B). The infectivity profiles show that HPV16, at all growth periods and from either tissue, had the highest levels of infectivity in fractions 5–8, as we had previously shown for foreskin tissues (Conway et al., 2009b). As expected from the stability profiles, the greatest relative infectivity was observed in the virus grown for 20-days. Infectivity observed in the unstable fractions, 1–4, is at levels similar to what we have observed with free viral genomes (Conway et al., 2009b).
To determine whether stability of virions played a role in the observed increase in specific infectivity, Western blot analysis of the L1 capsid protein from OptiPrep-fractionated 10-, 15-, and 20-d HPV16 preparations was performed. The relative quantity of L1 expression in gradient fraction 7 in 10-, 15-, and 20-d preparations was used to normalize for relative infectivity of these same fractions. This was done to define the “infectivity-to-protein ratio” as a second gauge for specific infectivity. Previously, we have shown that 20-d foreskin derived HPV16 was 2.9-fold more infectious than 10-d HPV16, suggesting that there is an intrinsic difference between 10- and 20-d HPV16 taken from fraction 7 rather than a difference in the overall stability of particles within 10-, 15-, and 20-d foreskin tissue preparations (Conway et al., 2009b). Western blot analysis of L1 from OptiPrep-fractionated 10-, 15-, and 20-d tonsil derived HPV16 were performed. The infectivity-to-protein ratio for tonsil derived HPV16 showed that 20-d HPV16 was 3.1-fold more infectious than 10-d HPV16 (Fig. 4C).
Formerly, in HPV16 extracted from foreskin tissues we found by Western blot two bands representing two different sizes of capsid derived L1 in OptiPrep fractions 6–8 (Conway et al., 2009b). Here we found that, like foreskin tissue, the L1 protein from HPV16 particles derived from tonsil tissue also exists as two bands (Fig. 4C).
The highly oxidizing environment of the stratum corneum compared to suprabasal strata allowed us to hypothesize that the temporal phenotypic changes observed over time were due to the exposure of virions to the different redox environments of epithelium (Buck et al., 2005; Ogawa et al., 1979; Pang et al., 1993). We have previously reported that in foreskin epithelial raft tissues, similar to what has been observed in vivo, a differential localization of free versus disulfide-bonded sulfhydryls confirmed that a natural redox gradient exists within human foreskin epithelial tissue whereby the basal and suprabasal compartments can be generally described as reducing while the cornified compartment is oxidizing (Conway et al., 2009b). To confirm that tonsil epithelia have a similar reducing and oxidizing environment, frozen sections of 15- and 20-d (Fig. 5 and data not shown, respectively) HPV16-infected tonsil and foreskin epithelia were stained with the fluorescent, sulfhydryl-alkylating, reagent, DACM. DACM is not fluorescent until it reacts with free sulfhydryl groups, thus emitting a fluorescent color (Ogawa et al., 1979; Pang et al., 1993). We detected that all epithelial strata, except the stratum corneum, contained significant amounts of detectable free sulfhydryl groups when stained with DACM alone (Fig. 5A-iii and B-iii). Treatment with DTT to break all disulfide bonds, followed by staining with DACM showed that sulfhydral groups are found in all strata (Fig. 5A-iv and B-iv). When we irreversibly blocked free sulfhydryls with the sulfhydryl-alkylating reagent N-ethylmaleimide (NEM), followed by reduction of disulfide-bonded sulfhydryls with dithiothreitol and then stained with DACM, only the stratum corneum contained significant amounts of detectable disulfide bonds (Figs. 5A-v and B-v). Only faint auto-fluorescent signals can be detected in either unstained samples or samples blocked with the NEM (Fig. 5A-i and ii and B-i and ii). The differential localization of free versus disulfide-bonded sulfhydryls confirms that a natural redox gradient exists within human tonsil epithelial tissue whereby the basal and suprabasal compartments can be generally described as reducing, while the cornified compartment is oxidizing. At 10-days, most of the virus is found within the reducing compartment of the host tissue. At 15-days, the virus has migrated into the oxidizing (cornified) compartment of the host tissue, therefore allowing for disulfide bonding to occur within the viral capsid, stabilizing the particle (Conway et al., 2009b, 2011b).
Previously, we detected a differential susceptibility to neutralization by anti-L1 and anti-L2 antibodies in virions isolated at different time points from foreskin tissues (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a). We tested whether tonsil tissue derived HPV16 showed a similar differential susceptibility to neutralization. A VGE of 10 was used for all neutralization assays. We used the conformation dependent anti-L1 MAb H16. V5 (epitope amino acids 266–297 and 335–365) (Christensen et al., 2001), the anti-L2 MAb RG1 (epitope amino acids 17–36) (Gambhira et al., 2007), and the anti-L2 antiserum L2#4 (epitope amino acids 56–75) to test the neutralization profiles of HPV16 derived from tissues grown for 20-days.
As previously reported, infection by foreskin tissue-derived HPV16 grown for 20-days was efficiently neutralized by all three antibodies utilized. In contrast, the tonsil derived HPV16 was efficiently neutralized by the L1 antibody, H16. V5 and only moderately neutralized by either of the L2 specific antibodies (Fig. 6A). Additionally, as indicated by the large error bars present, the tonsil tissue showed a wide variation in its ability to be neutralized with all three antibodies tested. Therefore, virus from four different HTLK cell lines, each derived from an individual patient sample, was tested for infectivity and neutralization utilizing the L1 and L2 specific antibodies previously mentioned (Fig. 6B). Overall, tonsil-derived HPV16 was neutralized by the conformation dependent anti-L1 H16. V5 antibody. However, this neutralization was not as efficient as the neutralization of infection by foreskin-tissue derived HPV16 (Fig. 6A). Additionally, when neutralization by H16. V5 was tested on the individual tonsil preparations, only two of the four samples were neutralizable, indicating variation between virus preparations from different tonsil tissue samples (Fig. 6B). Neutralization with both of the L2 specific MAbs was only moderately neutralizable overall (Fig. 6A) and was also highly variable between virus preparations, as can be seen when looking at neutralization of virus from cell lines created from individual patient tissue (Fig. 6B). Since tonsil tissue-derived HPV16 is highly infectious and shows an OptiPrep gradient profile similar to foreskin tissue-derived HPV16 virus, this strongly suggests a conformational difference between foreskin derived and tonsil derived HPV16.
Viral capsids are typically composed of numerous repeating identical or of a few different species of subunits. These subunits are arranged in precisely arranged patterns. Commonly, the first form that can be observed with the electron microscope is the capsomere. Multiple capsomers come together to form the viral capsid. The simplicity of the capsid minimizes the space on the viral nucleic acid necessary for it’s coding and increases the efficiency of assembly. The two main functions of the capsid are to protect viral genomes from the extracellular environment and to deliver the genomes from one cell or host to the next. While the general instructions for capsid assembly may on the surface appear simple, the host environment can induce numerous potential changes in the stability and efficiency of assembly. Additionally, there is the potential for the host cell environment or the extracellular environment to induce changes in the viral surface conformation that can then affect binding efficiency to a naïve host cells, immune interactions, and/or disassembly of the virus within a new host cell. Examples of this are numerous with enveloped viruses, with changes in enveloped proteins affecting infectivity and surveillance by the immune system. Little has been reported for a similar phenomenon with non-enveloped viruses. In our present study, we demonstrate that while much of the phenotype of HPV16 grown in foreskin and tonsil tissue is similar, virus derived from the two tissues differs in two significant ways. First, a greater percentage of the total HPV16 virions grown in tonsil epithelium become fully mature and stable and this occurs faster than what is observed with HPV16 virions grown in foreskin tissue. Similar differentiation associated redox potential gradients, which we have previously shown to be important for the stability and maturation of HPV16 (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011b), are shown for both tissue types. However, the stability and maturation of virus from foreskin and tonsil differ, suggesting the involvement of other host cell factors in virion assembly and maturation. It is possible that there are cell type specific chaperones that assist in viral capsid assembly, though future investigations are needed to determine this. The second major phenotypic difference between viruses from tonsil versus foreskin is the overall inability of L2-specific antibodies to neutralize tonsil-derived virus. This suggests that the final surface conformation of the tonsil-derived HPV16 differs at least in respect to the capsid conformation and availability of L2. Additionally, the difference in neutralization profiles between virus preparations produced from different HTLK cell lines indicates that not all tonsil-tissue derived virions are structurally identical. Interestingly, the surface conformation of the L1 capsid protein did not appear to differ, as both foreskin and tonsil derived virions are susceptible to neutralization by the conformational dependent L1 antibody.
The HPV life cycle is intimately connected to the differentiation program of its host tissue, squamous epithelium. Using the organotypic culture system, our laboratory has previously studied the life cycle of different HPV types in genital tissues (Conway et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2011a, b; McLaughlin-Drubin et al., 2005, 2004; McLaughlin-Drubin and Meyers, 2004, 2005; Meyers et al., 1992a, 1997; Ozbun and Butel, 1995; Ozbun and Meyers, 1996, 1997, 1998a, b, 1999). In the present study, we investigated for the first time the life cycle of HPV16 in oral tissue with direct comparison to foreskin tissue. While it has commonly been believed that the oral cavity is capable of replicating infectious HPV particles and therefore a source of viral spread, this has never been directly demonstrated. We have shown here that tonsil epithelial tissue culture is suitable for the replication of high titers of infectious, native HPV16. This study focused on the assembly and infectivity of infectious virus, suggesting that while many aspects of the phenotype of HPV16 grown in foreskin and tonsil tissue are similar, that there are significant differences that relate to the maturation and final conformation of the virus when grown in the two different tissues.
