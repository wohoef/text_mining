The precise development expenses for any specific drug are never publicly disclosed, but economists estimate that the average investment required to bring an innovative drug to market exceeds $1 billion and continues to increase.1,2 These substantial costs associated with pharmaceutical research and development are frequently cited to justify the high prices of new medications. While the initial price point of effective new drugs—ranging from hepatitis cures to novel agents for heart failure—may be unaffordable for many patients, market principles suggest that drugs with high demand should become more accessible once market exclusivity ends and generic competition commences. Since 1984, when the Drug Price Competition and Patent Term Restoration Act was enacted, pharmaceutical innovation and access have been balanced on this premise: innovative drugs are rewarded with high prices during their period of patent protection, and generic competition subsequently reduces prices.3,4
What occurs when this expectation is not fulfilled for a drug that is crucial for managing a disease with significant public health implications? When insulin was discovered in 1921, it was celebrated as one of the 20th century's initial "wonder drugs," capable of transforming a fatal condition into a manageable chronic illness.5 Currently, there are 21 million individuals living with diabetes in the United States alone, 6 million of whom use insulin.6 Yet, insulin is exclusively available in brand-name forms—out-of-pocket costs for uninsured patients range from $120 to $400 per month. While many other essential medications are available as $4 generics, no similarly low-priced versions of insulin exist. For numerous patients—particularly those without insurance—insulin's price remains too high to afford, leading to disastrous consequences for both individual and systemic diabetes management.7
The perplexing question of why a medication discovered nearly a century ago is still not available as a low-priced generic agent has historical roots—and implications for current policy and practice.
In a widely acclaimed narrative of biomedical serendipity, insulin was discovered in 1921 by an improbable scientific team at the University of Toronto, led by Frederick Banting, a young orthopedic surgeon without laboratory training, and Charles Best, a medical student. After refining their technique for extracting the active insulin (initially called isletin) from whole animal pancreas, they produced enough to treat the first patient, Leonard Thompson, in 1922. A patent was not filed until later, however, partly because academic medicine viewed the patenting of biomedical research products with some disapproval. When the team applied for a U.S. patent in January 1923 (which they later sold to the university for $1), they stated that their objective was not profit, but rather ensuring the rapid and safe availability of their discovery to the public.8 The patent, they wrote to their university's president, served as a form of publication: "When the details of the method of preparation are published anyone would be free to prepare the extract, but no one could secure a profitable monopoly."9
Patenting insulin also enabled its creators to regulate the product's quality by controlling who could manufacture it.10 After attempting to produce insulin in a manufacturing facility on the university campus, the researchers realized they lacked the necessary expertise to produce sufficient quantities of the drug for North American markets. In 1923, they collaborated with Eli Lilly, an established pharmaceutical company experienced in producing glandular extracts.9 Lilly was permitted to obtain U.S. patents for any manufacturing-process improvements, but the university would retain the patent rights for the rest of the world.9 The Toronto team licensed the rights to produce insulin to numerous other companies in various countries. One of these companies, Denmark's Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium (which later merged with Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium to form Novo Nordisk), would soon become a significant innovator in insulin products.
Insulin was immediately recognized as a life-saving drug of immense clinical and public health importance. Nevertheless, the initial animal extracts had limitations.
Firstly, their brief duration of action necessitated frequent injections. In the early 1930s, Hans Christian Hagedorn and his colleagues at Nordisk discovered that adding protamine to insulin altered its absorption and prolonged its action.11 These initial protamine insulins represented a significant innovation, but their physical form prevented mixing with crystalline fast-acting (regular) insulin. A subsequent innovation, the addition of small amounts of zinc to form the crystalline protamine-isophane insulin, now known as neutral protamine Hagedorn (NPH),12 was patented in 1946. This advancement made it possible to combine long-acting and short-acting insulin, allowing many individuals with diabetes to be treated with a single daily injection. Soon thereafter, a method was discovered for extending insulin's action without adding protamine, which led to the introduction of the lente ("slow") insulins in the mid-1950s.13 These discoveries provided more options for titrating insulin regimens but extended the reach of insulin patents into the 1970s.
Secondly, these initial beef and pork insulins were plagued by problems inherent to animal-tissue extracts. Impurities could cause local site reactions, and immunologic reactions to nonhuman proteins could diminish insulin's efficacy and trigger allergic responses. A series of innovations in the insulin manufacturing process in the early 1970s helped to improve purity and reduce these side effects. In quick succession, Novo introduced "monocomponent" insulins and Lilly introduced "single-peak" insulins. These safety improvements extended insulin patents into the late 1980s.
By the late 1970s, however, further purity improvements were superseded when it became feasible to produce human insulin through recombinant technology. Biotechnology investors viewed insulin as an ideal product for the nascent industry after Genentech scientists produced the first recombinant DNA human insulin in 1978 by inserting the cloned insulin gene into the bacteria Escherichia coli.14 This technology enabled Lilly to introduce the first recombinant human insulins—Humulin R (rapid) and N (NPH)—to the U.S. market in 1982. Around the same time, Novo and Nordisk developed methods for chemically converting bovine insulin into human insulin, which allowed them to compete in the human insulin market. Novo Nordisk eventually brought its first recombinant insulin to market in 1988. A new network of insulin patents, held by Lilly, Novo Nordisk, and Genentech, promised to extend into the 21st century.
Once recombinant technology opened the door to using the genetic code to create insulin, scientists quickly began modifying insulin's structure, aiming to enhance its physiological effects. In the late 1980s, it was demonstrated that single–amino-acid substitutions could lead to substantially faster insulin absorption,15 theoretically allowing injected insulin to more closely mimic prandial insulin release by the pancreas. In 1996, lispro became the first short-acting insulin analogue to be approved; aspart followed in 2000, and glulisine in 2004.
The same principle that enabled fast-acting analogues also allowed for the engineering of long-acting analogues. Since NPH has an unpredictable peak and a duration of action of less than 24 hours,16 long-acting synthetic insulins could theoretically reduce hypoglycemia and improve glycemic control. Glargine became the first long-acting analogue insulin in 2000, followed by detemir insulin in 2005; the initial patents on these products expired in June 2014.
Why, then, is a drug originally patented in 1923 not available in generic form in 2014? It is true that biologic drugs are larger, more complex, and more challenging to copy than the small molecules upon which the generic-drug industry was built, but the widely anticipated entry of biosimilar insulins may promise more competitive pricing, now that the most recent insulin patents have expired. Last summer, Lilly and Boehringer Ingelheim announced that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) had granted tentative approval to a biosimilar version of insulin glargine.17 Other companies have also declared plans to produce biosimilar analogue insulins in the United States. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) recently moved closer to approving the first biosimilar glargine on the European market,18 and unregulated biosimilar insulins have already appeared in countries with less-stringent regulatory bodies, including China, India, Mexico, and Peru.19
New biologic drugs are orders of magnitude larger than small-molecule drugs, and it is often impossible to determine on an atom-by-atom basis whether one large-molecule drug is identical to another. Subsequent versions of off-patent biotech drugs are therefore termed biosimilar rather than generic. Differences in the tissue systems that produce them can lead to variations in protein folding and glycosylation, which could theoretically result in differences in efficacy or safety. However, the FDA, the EMA, and the generic drug industry hope these issues can be addressed by new regulatory sciences of biosimilarity.20
Although regulatory pathways for biosimilar insulin are being finalized in the United States and Europe, economists caution that the introduction of biosimilars may not lead to price reductions equivalent to those observed with typical generic medicines. Even an abbreviated approval process for biosimilars will require substantially more original data than the typical abbreviated new drug application required for small-molecule generics and will necessitate immunogenicity and other safety studies in humans.21 Price reductions for biosimilar insulins in the United States are predicted to be approximately 20 to 40%—much less than the 80%-or-greater reductions for most small-molecule generics.22 Europe, which has already approved numerous noninsulin biosimilars, has experienced similarly disappointing price reductions in biosimilar products.21
Reducing the problem of generic insulin to the contemporary debate over biosimilarity overlooks the historical reason why we have consistently lacked generic insulin: incremental innovation has repeatedly prevented the formation of a generic insulin industry in North America when earlier patents expired. The history of insulin has not followed the standard chronology of pharmaceutical innovation, in which patent monopolies predictably give way to generic competition.
Viewed from a historical perspective, insulin is not a singular entity but a family of related products that has evolved through incremental improvements. Subsequent iterations of insulin represented actual innovations, each one being safer, more effective, or more convenient than its predecessor. Yet, after generations of incremental innovation, insulin may be no more affordable than it was when the original patent holders sold their stake for $1 to ensure access to this essential medicine.
Pharmaceutical-industry analysts have described a repatenting tactic called evergreening, in which a series of related patents—often on metabolites or optical isomers—extend the life of a product after initial patent expiration.23 Evergreening can shift market share within a family of products: for example, after Pfizer lost patent exclusivity on the antiepileptic agent gabapentin (Neurontin) in 2004, it maintained a healthy share of the market through patents on a metabolic cognate, pregabalin (Lyrica). Critics of evergreening often contend that the incremental innovations leading from a given drug to a "me-too" drug are trivial: pregabalin, for instance, is not clearly safer or more efficacious than gabapentin.
However, the cascading generations of insulin products can hardly be dismissed as merely "me-too" medicines. Protamine insulin offered a distinct advantage over regular, NPH insulin offered a distinct advantage over protamine, and so on. On the whole, today's insulin is demonstrably safer and more convenient to use than products available in 1923. But whether each incremental innovation is worth the price we pay, in a world where insulin remains unaffordable to many diabetic patients, is less certain. When lente insulin was introduced in the 1950s, some observers questioned whether its minimal theoretical advantages over NPH warranted the complexity introduced by adding another insulin formulation to the market.24 The theoretical advantages offered by the monocomponent extract insulins may sometimes have been outweighed by the inconvenience and risk caused by transitioning patients to an insulin of different potency.25 Although recombinant insulin was heavily advertised as a clinically superior agent in the 1980s (see image), almost no evidence was provided to demonstrate its superiority to the best available animal-extract insulins.26 Although long-acting analogues cause less hypoglycemia than NPH does,27 it has yet to be demonstrated that analogues lead to better long-term outcomes than standard recombinant human insulin does.28
Undoubtedly, for many patients, these incremental innovations were worth the added price. What is surprising is that the trailing edge of old insulin products did not generate a market for generic competition but rather became a set of obsolete products that were promptly removed from the U.S. market. Pork and beef insulins are not merely underutilized; they are unavailable for human use in the United States. Even when practitioners prescribe NPH and R insulin in place of glargine and aspart insulin, these cheaper prescriptions are filled with newer recombinant products sold under brand-name monopolies. And yet, on the whole, it is difficult to assert that contemporary patients who cannot afford their insulin (let alone the patent-protected glucometers and test strips required to titrate its use) are well served by only having the option of an agent that is marginally more effective than those that could have been generically available 50 or 30 or 10 years ago, had generics manufacturers introduced cheaper versions when patents expired.
Generic drug companies have evidently not considered it worthwhile to invest in the additional good manufacturing practices needed to produce a version of insulin that may already be obsolete, when off-patent small-molecule drugs represented lower-hanging fruit. Only recently, with insulin-analogue patents expiring and no next-generation products on the horizon, have prominent generics manufacturers shown serious interest in the insulin market.
It is difficult to overstate the economic and public health impact of generic drugs in improving access to safe, effective, inexpensive medications in the United States. In the early 1960s, less than 1 in 10 medicines dispensed in pharmacies were generic, and most prescription drugs were effectively monopolies. Today, more than 80% of prescriptions are filled with generics, which saves the health care system billions of dollars each year.29,30 These savings are critical both for payers that are squeezed by rising health care costs and for patients, since lower medication costs are associated with better compliance31 and better outcomes.32
However, the case of insulin demonstrates that the generics market is like other markets—not an automatic phase in a drug's lifecycle. As the increasing waves of generic-drug shortages in the past decade also remind us, the drugs that ultimately see extensive generic competition differ from those that attract few, if any, manufacturers. The history of insulin highlights the limits of generic competition as a public health framework. Nearly a century after its discovery, there is still no inexpensive supply of insulin for people living with diabetes in North America, and Americans are paying a steep price for the continued rejuvenation of this oldest of modern medicines.
