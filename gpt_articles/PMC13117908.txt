Obesity is the most prevalent metabolic disorder globally. It arises when caloric intake surpasses energy expenditure (1) and is primarily characterized by an excess accumulation of adipose tissue (2,3). Individuals with obesity appear to face a high risk of developing type 2 diabetes (2). Increasing energy expenditure has emerged as a promising and appealing strategy for obesity prevention (4). While the precise regulators of energy expenditure are not fully understood, adipocytes seem to play a crucial role in modulating energy balance and nutrient flow in vertebrates.

Two distinct types of adipose tissue, white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT), have been extensively investigated. White adipocytes function in energy storage (e.g., triglycerides), whereas brown adipocytes are involved in energy consumption (5,6). Numerous studies have demonstrated that alterations in BAT activity can significantly impact adaptive thermogenesis and glucose homeostasis (7,8). The thermogenic capacity of brown fat is primarily mediated by the presence of mitochondrial uncoupling protein-1 (UCP-1), which uncouples the electron transport chain from energy production, leading to the release of potential energy from food as heat. The expression of UCP-1 is regulated by several transcriptional factors, including peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ (PPARγ) coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), which can be induced by cold exposure and/or β-adrenergic signaling (9). In various rodent models, the browning of WAT depots appears to offer protection against diet-induced metabolic disorders, such as obesity and diabetes (10,11).

Irisin, a small polypeptide hormone secreted by skeletal muscles and whose levels increase with exercise, consists of 111 amino acids and has an estimated molecular mass of 22 kDa. Research has indicated that PGC-1α and exercise upregulate the expression of fibronectin type III domain containing 5 (Fndc5), a type I transmembrane protein found in skeletal muscle. Fndc5 is subsequently proteolysed at amino acid positions 30 and 140 to produce irisin (12). Overexpression of irisin via an adenoviral vector leads to an increase in total body energy expenditure, induces modest weight loss, and improves glucose intolerance in mice fed a high-fat diet (12). Age and skeletal muscle mass are the primary determinants of circulating irisin levels, with young male athletes exhibiting irisin levels several times higher than those in middle-aged obese women (13). Circulating irisin is significantly lower in individuals with type 2 diabetes compared to non-diabetic control subjects (14,15). Furthermore, plasma irisin levels are negatively correlated with blood urea nitrogen levels in patients with chronic kidney disease (16) and positively correlated with aerobic performance in heart failure patients (17). Considering the relationship between irisin and energy expenditure, irisin can now be recognized as another secreted protein that stimulates brown adipocyte thermogenesis, alongside liver fibroblast growth factor 21 (18) and cardiac natriuretic peptides (19). However, the molecular mechanism and signaling pathways that mediate the induction of the functional “brown-like” adipocyte phenotype by irisin remain largely unknown.

In the present study, we developed an efficient system for the expression and purification of human recombinant irisin (r-irisin) in Pichia pastoris and administered r-irisin to mice through intraperitoneal injection. We also treated primary adipocytes and 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes with r-irisin to investigate its direct effects on the fat cell browning process, aiming to elucidate the underlying “browning” mechanisms. Our study demonstrated that in vivo administration of r-irisin resulted in decreased body weight, promoted brown fat–specific gene expression in subcutaneous white adipose tissue, and improved glucose tolerance, which aligns with a previous study (12). Additionally, cell culture experiments revealed that irisin stimulated brown fat–specific gene expression through the activation of the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) and extracellular signal–related kinase (ERK) pathways.

Human irisin cDNA (360 bp) was designed and synthesized by Life Technologies. The synthesized cDNA was cloned into the EcoR1/Xba1 sites of the pPICZαA plasmid. Pichia pastoris X-33 was transformed with a linearized pPICZαA-irisin plasmid following the instructions of the kit manual (Pichia Easycomp Transformation Kit; Invitrogen). Yeast culture and induction of protein expression were carried out as previously described (20).

The r-irisin protein present in the supernatant was purified using a two-step method (20). First, r-irisin was precipitated with 60% saturated ammonium sulfate. The precipitated proteins were collected, dissolved in buffer A (25 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.9, 10% glycerol, 0.1 mol/L KCl, 0.2 mmol/L EDTA, and 0.5 mmol/L dithiothreitol), and dialyzed against the same buffer. The resulting sample was then loaded onto a concanavalin A–agarose column. The column was initially washed with 0.2 mol/L KCl in buffer A, and proteins were subsequently eluted with 1.5 mol/L KCl in buffer A. The elution fractions were analyzed by Western blotting. The purified irisin was dialyzed against 10% glycerol in PBS and stored at −80°C.

Human irisin protein contains two N-glycosylation sites (Asn7 and Asn52). To confirm glycosylation, we constructed two mutant irisin cDNA clones, N7H and N52H, using a site-directed mutagenesis kit (Stratagene). Briefly, the pPICZαA-irisin plasmid served as a template for site-directed mutagenesis PCR with point-mutation primers. Following Dpn1 digestion, the PCR fragments were transformed into Escherichia coli Top10. The cDNA of the clones was purified using a Qiagen plasmid midi-purification kit. Each mutant cDNA was fully sequenced to ensure that no other base changes had occurred. The mutation primers used were as follows: N7H forward, 5′-tccatccgctccagtgcacgttactgttagacacttgaaggc, and reverse, 5′-tgtctaacagtaacgtgcctggagcggatggagagaattc; N52H forward, 5′-gattcatccaagaggtgcacactactactagatcctgtgctttg, and reverse, 5′-gatctagtagtagtgtgcacctcttggatgaatctcaacattc.

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 g) were utilized in the study, and adipocytes were isolated using a previously described method (21). In short, subcutaneous fat pads were minced with scissors and digested in type I collagenase (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C in a water bath shaker. After 90 minutes, the contents were immediately filtered through a mesh and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 10 minutes. The layer of floating cells was then washed three times with PBS. Adipocytes were incubated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 units/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C.

Murine preadipocyte (3T3-L1) cells were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China) and induced to differentiate into mature adipocytes as previously described (22). In brief, preadipocytes were cultured in basic medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% bovine calf serum and 100 units/mL penicillin-streptomycin) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Upon reaching confluence (day 1), the cells were initiated for adipocyte differentiation by incubation with induction medium (basic medium supplemented with 0.5 mmol/L isobutylmethylxanthine, 0.25 μmol/L dexamethasone, and 10 μmol/L insulin). Two days after induction (day 3), the medium was replaced with basic medium containing only insulin for an additional 2 days. On day 5, the medium was switched back to basic medium for another 2 days. R-irisin was added to the 3T3-L1 cells on day 3 of adipocyte differentiation for a total of 4 days of stimulation. On day 7, 3T3-L1 mature adipocytes, with or without r-irisin treatment, were processed for Oil Red O staining to confirm adipocyte differentiation, RNA extraction for reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), immunocytochemistry (ICC) with anti–UCP-1 antibodies, and Western blotting.

On day 7 of differentiation, 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes were incubated with or without irisin for 30 minutes. Following washing, the cells were incubated with anti-irisin rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:1,000; Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, Inc.) for 1 hour. Subsequently, FITC–anti-rabbit IgG antibody was added and incubated for another hour. After washing, the cells were examined using a confocal fluorescence microscope. Additionally, a luciferase-irisin fusion protein was constructed into the pREN2-irisin expression vector and produced as previously described (23). 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes were incubated in culture with either luciferase-irisin or luciferase. ICC was performed using antiluciferase antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as outlined above.

Total RNA was extracted from cells, with or without irisin treatment, using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription of 2 μg of total RNA was performed with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Real-time PCR in triplicate was conducted with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), and gene-specific primers are listed in Table 1. The 2−∆ct method was used to calculate the relative expression of genes, with β-actin RNA serving as an internal control.

3T3-L1–derived adipocytes were fixed with ice-cold methanol for 10 minutes at 4°C. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and blocked in hydrogen peroxide blocking solution for 10 minutes at room temperature. The cells were incubated with primary antibody for UCP-1 (1:500; Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, ONT, Canada) overnight at 4°C. After washing, the cells were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody for 30 minutes at room temperature. Following washing with PBS, the cells were incubated in avidin-biotinylated HRP for an additional 30 minutes. Diaminobenzidine was used for ICC, and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining nuclei. Positive and negative expression of protein UCP-1 was indicated by dark brown staining in the cytoplasm, respectively.

An equal amount of proteins from cell lysates was loaded into each well of a 12% SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, blocked for 1 hour with 5% fat-free milk at room temperature, and blotted with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C at a 1:1,000 dilution (rabbit anti-irisin, anti–UCP-1 [Sigma-Aldrich], anti-ERK1/2, anti–phospho-ERK1/2, anti-AKT, anti–phospho-AKT, anti-JNK, anti–phospho-JNK, anti–p38 MAPK, and anti–phospho-p38 MAPK antibodies [Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.]); rabbit anti-GAPDH antibody was used at 1:10,000 (Sigma-Aldrich). Following washing with Tris-buffered saline with Tween, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently, immune complexes were detected using the ECL method, and immunoreactive bands were quantified by densitometric analysis using Alpha Imager 2200.

C57BL/6 mice, fed a high-fat diet (HFD) (60% fat) for 10 weeks with an initial body weight ranging from 35–43 g, were purchased from the animal center of Shandong University. Obese mice were randomly assigned to two groups: control (n = 8) and r-irisin treated (n = 8). The mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of purified r-irisin at a dose of 0.5 μg/g body weight/day for 14 days, while mice in the control group were given daily saline at the same volume. At the conclusion of the r-irisin treatment (day 14), mouse body weights were recorded, and some mice underwent an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test before being euthanized. Mouse subcutaneous fat tissues were collected for immunohistochemistry and total RNA extraction for subsequent RT-qPCR.

Results are presented as the mean ± SE of at least three independent experiments, with each experiment conducted in triplicate. Statistical significance among multiple groups was analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Student's t-test for comparing results between two groups, utilizing the SPSS software package (SPSS 17.0). A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To achieve high-yield expression of the human irisin gene in a yeast expression system, we designed and constructed an optimized irisin cDNA coding sequence tailored for yeast codon usage. The synthesized gene and protein sequence of r-irisin are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. The resulting plasmid pPICZαA-irisin was transformed into P. pastoris. Eight clones were selected from 50 single colonies that grew on yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) containing zeocin (100 μg/mL) to assess their capacity for r-irisin protein expression. To examine protein expression, secreted r-irisin at various time points was separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Three protein bands were observed on the gel with molecular weights of 25, 22, and 15 kDa (Fig. 1A). Western blotting analysis confirmed that all three bands corresponded to human irisin protein (Fig. 1B). The protein identity of all three bands was further verified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Human irisin protein possesses two putative N-glycosylation sites at asparagine 7 (Asn7) and asparagine 52 (Asn52). The two higher molecular weight forms (25 and 22 kDa) of the secreted irisin protein, when compared to the native form of irisin, were likely glycosylated. Treatment of the purified r-irisin with PNGase F to remove N-linked glycans, followed by Western blotting analysis (Fig. 1C), revealed that the enzyme-treated r-irisin exhibited a single band at a low molecular weight (15 kDa), confirming that the majority of the secreted r-irisin expressed by P. pastoris was glycosylated.

To confirm the specific glycosylated sites, we individually mutated each of the two glycosylation sites at Asn7 or Asn52. The mutant r-irisin protein from each mutant (N7H or N52H) displayed only two bands with molecular weights of 22 and 15 kDa. The 22-kDa protein likely represents the mutant irisin that was glycosylated on one of the two glycosylated sites (N7H or N52H), as this band remained sensitive to PNGase-F treatment (Fig. 1D). Our experiments confirm that human irisin indeed has two glycosylated sites at Asn7 and Asn52.

To investigate the browning effect of r-irisin on adipocytes, we utilized both 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes and primary rat adipocytes as cellular models, treated with or without human r-irisin as detailed in RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODS. Gene expression studies indicated that treating 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes with r-irisin (20 nmol/L) led to a rapid upregulation of brown cell markers: UCP-1, PGC-1α, transmembrane protein 26 (TMEM26), early B cell factor 3 (Ebf3), elongation of very long chain fatty acids-like 3 (Elovl3), cell death-inducing DNA fragmentation factor (Cidea), and cytochrome c oxidase, subunit VII α (Cox7α) (all P value <0.01) (Fig. 2A). UCP-1 is a specialized mitochondrial protein that dissipates chemical energy as heat. PGC-1α is a potent transcriptional coactivator and a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, including UCP-1 and Fndc5 in brown fat cells. The increased expression of the beige cell marker TMEM26 (24) and brown cell markers such as Cidea and Cox7α suggested that r-irisin might exert effects on converting white adipose cells into beige-like adipocytes and brown adipose cells. Interestingly, treatment of adipocytes with r-irisin also significantly increased the expression of the betatrophin (ANGPTL8) gene (3.53 ± 0.97–fold, P < 0.01). Betatrophin, a newly identified pancreatic β-cell growth hormone, is primarily expressed in the liver and fat of mice (25). This result implies that the increased betatrophin expression induced by irisin may, at least in part, account for the improved insulin resistance observed with irisin.

The increased UCP-1 expression at the protein level by r-irisin in 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes was also demonstrated by both Western blotting (Fig. 2B) and ICC study (Fig. 2C, bottom), with confirmed adipocyte differentiation (Fig. 2C, Oil Red O staining, top). Quantification of UCP-1 ICC studies in r-irisin–treated 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes revealed a significant increase in UCP-1–positive adipocytes over controls (2.78 ± 0.63–fold, P < 0.01) with multilocular lipid droplets compared with those treated with saline (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, treatment of primary cultured rat adipocytes with r-irisin across a range of concentrations (2, 20, and 100 nmol/L) (Fig. 3) was capable of significantly increasing the expression of the UCP-1 and PGC-1α genes. Given that UCP-1 mRNA was increased fourfold compared to untreated cells using 20 nmol/L r-irisin, we selected 20 nmol/L r-irisin for subsequent experiments.

To ascertain whether glycosylation in irisin significantly impacts its biological function, we next examined the expression of browning markers in 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes treated with irisin protein mutated at either Asn7 or Asn52. As depicted in Fig. 2D, the effects of irisin in upregulating UCP-1 and PGC-1α were significantly diminished by the mutated irisin at either site. This loss of function was also confirmed at the protein level, with a significant reduction in UCP-1 compared to treatment with wild-type irisin (Fig. 2E), suggesting that the posttranslational glycosylation of secreted irisin from yeast enhances the biological function of the browning effect, as evidenced by increased expression of UCP-1 and PGC-1α. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that the decreased biological activity observed in the N7H and N52H mutant irisin was due to mechanisms other than altered glycosylation status.

Although the browning function mediated by irisin has been extensively investigated, the specific molecular signaling pathways that mediate browning remain unclear. Here, we demonstrated that p38 MAPK and ERK play a crucial role in mediating the irisin browning function. As shown in Fig. 4, phosphorylated p38 MAPK (P-p38) and phosphorylated ERK (P-ERK) were significantly increased as early as 5 minutes, peaked between 10 and 20 minutes, and decreased at 30 minutes after r-irisin treatment in both primary rat (Fig. 4A and C) and 3T3-L1 adipocytes (Fig. 4B and D). The increased levels of P-p38 and P-ERK proteins following r-irisin treatment were statistically significant, as quantified by densitometry.

To further investigate the potential involvement of p38 MAPK and ERK pathways in irisin-induced browning effects, 3T3-L1 adipocytes were treated with or without SB203580 (p-38MAPK inhibitor) or U0126 (ERK inhibitor) individually in the presence of irisin. Irisin-induced p38 and ERK phosphorylation was gradually suppressed with increasing concentrations of the p38 and ERK inhibitors (Fig. 4E), while the total amount of p38 or ERK protein remained unchanged. The irisin-induced UCP-1 upregulation was significantly reduced by either SB203580 or U0126. Moreover, the combination of both inhibitors almost completely abolished the effect of irisin-mediated increased expression of UCP-1 mRNA (Fig. 5A and B) and protein (Fig. 5C and D) levels. These findings indicate that the irisin-induced browning effect, characterized by enhanced expression of the browning gene UCP-1, was mediated via the activation of p38 MAPK and ERK signaling pathways.

A previous study demonstrated that irisin significantly improves glucose tolerance and insulin resistance in mice fed a high-fat diet (12); however, the mechanism underlying this effect was unknown. AKT is an important signaling molecule in the insulin signaling pathway. Therefore, we investigated the effect of irisin on the activation of AKT using an antibody specific for phosphorylated serine 473 of AKT. Unlike insulin (100 nmol/L), which can significantly promote the phosphorylation of AKT (Supplementary Fig. 3B), irisin stimulation of 3T3-L1 adipocytes showed no significant effects on the phosphorylation of AKT at any of the tested time points (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Furthermore, there was no additive or synergistic effect between insulin and irisin on the level of AKT phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 3B), suggesting that the AKT system is not involved in the browning action of irisin. Additionally, we showed that treatment of adipocytes with irisin had no effect on the phosphorylation of c-Jun NH2-terminal kinases (JNKs), indicating that the JNK signaling pathway is not involved in the irisin-mediated browning process (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Currently, there is no direct evidence demonstrating a specific membrane receptor for irisin in adipocytes. Based on the results showing the activation of P-p38 and P-ERK signaling transduction by irisin, we infer that the adipocyte membrane might possess receptors for irisin. Ligand binding to its membrane receptor has historically been determined using a radioactive isotopically labeled ligand. However, the potential hazards associated with radioisotope handling and the environmental impact of radioisotope disposal make this approach less desirable. Therefore, we employed live cell binding methods to observe the binding property of irisin to the cell membrane. The 3T3-L1 adipocytes were briefly incubated with r-irisin. The r-irisin bound to the cell surface membrane was visualized by immunofluorescence after sequential incubation with anti-irisin antibody and FITC–anti-rabbit IgG antibody. As shown in Fig. 6A, green fluorescence was observed only on the membrane of irisin-treated cells. To further confirm irisin binding to the cell membrane, we added luciferase-irisin fusion protein or luciferase alone (as a control) to the cell culture medium. Irisin binding to the cell membrane was visualized using an antiluciferase antibody. As shown in Fig. 6B, clear green fluorescence with increasing intensity was observed along the membrane on luciferase-irisin–incubated cells, whereas no clear membrane signal was observed on luciferase-treated cells. These data suggest that 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes may possess a yet-to-be-identified irisin receptor on their cell membrane.

To investigate the potential therapeutic effects of r-irisin, we treated both normal and obese mice with daily injections of r-irisin for 14 days. Figure 7A illustrates that the mRNA levels of brown adipocyte marker genes, including UCP-1, PGC-1α, Cox7α, Elovl3, PPARα, and Cidea, were significantly elevated in the adipose tissues of normal mice receiving r-irisin (**P < 0.01). Similar gene expression results were also observed in adipose tissue isolated from obese mice fed a high-fat diet (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with findings in 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes, the expression of betatrophin (ANGPTL8) was also significantly increased (*P < 0.05) in the adipose tissue of mice treated with r-irisin compared to control mice (Fig. 7B). The changes in browning gene expression in the subcutaneous adipose tissues were accompanied by an increase in the number of UCP-1–positive, multilocular adipocytes (Fig. 7C). The body weights of the r-irisin–treated obese mice were reduced after 14 days compared to the saline-treated control obese mice (Fig. 7D). R-irisin treatment of obese mice significantly improved glucose tolerance, as demonstrated by a quicker recovery curve in the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (Fig. 7E) and significantly reduced levels of fasting insulin (Fig. 7F) when compared to the control obese mice. These results suggest that in vivo treatment of obese mice with r-irisin can decrease body weight, promote the expression of brown-specific genes, and improve HFD-induced insulin resistance.

PGC-1α–dependent irisin, a novel myokine, originates from the cleavage of Fndc5 protein. Irisin promotes brown fat–like development and thermogenesis in WAT both in vitro and in vivo. The discovery of irisin has opened up opportunities to further understand the role of adipocytes in obesity, diabetes, and other associated metabolic disorders (12,13,26,27). However, the molecular mechanisms and cellular signaling pathways responsible for the browning effect of irisin have not yet been elucidated.

In this study, we successfully constructed a yeast expression plasmid containing a synthesized optimal codon usage, human irisin-coding sequence, and generated pure human recombinant irisin protein in P. pastoris with high yield that is fully biologically functional. The P. pastoris system is widely employed for heterologous protein expression, possessing the capability to produce posttranslationally modified proteins (28). The human recombinant irisin protein expressed in yeast exhibited a predominant band of approximately 22 kDa, which is similar to the glycosylated irisin previously reported (12). Through PNGase-F digestion and site-directed mutagenesis analysis, we for the first time confirmed two glycosylation sites in human irisin located at asparagine 7 and asparagine 52. Given that glycosylation is a common characteristic of secreted proteins, including irisin in mice and humans (12), we site-directedly mutated the two glycosylated sites to investigate the biological effect of the mutant irisin. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, posttranslational glycosylation of the secreted irisin enhances the expression of UCP-1 and PGC-1α, compared to mutated irisin, indicating that yeast-produced irisin is superior to that produced in a bacterial expression system.

It is now clear that there are two distinct types of brown adipose cells. One is classical brown fat, which originates from a myf5, muscle-like cell lineage (29,30). The other consists of brown adipocytes found interspersed within WAT, appearing in response to chemical or hormonal stimulation, environmental changes, cold exposure, and defined genetic manipulation (31–33). These UCP-1 positive brown fat cells are referred to as beige or brown-in-white (brite) cells (11,34). In the current study, we demonstrated that r-irisin indeed increased the expression of thermogenic genes, including UCP-1, PGC-1α, Cox7α, Ebf3, and Elovl3, in both primary and cell line–derived adipocytes. We also found that the expression of TMEM26, a marker of beige cells, was increased when r-irisin was added to cultured 3T3-L1 preadipocytes during differentiation. Wu et al. (24) also showed that beige cell precursors exhibit preferential sensitivity to the browning effect of irisin. Our results indicated that irisin significantly elevated the expression of TMEM26, suggesting that irisin may influence the number of beige-like cells.

Irisin functions as a muscle-derived energy expenditure signal that directly communicates with adipose tissue and induces browning. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the molecular pathways mediating the browning function of irisin. Boström et al. (12) identified PPARα as a potential factor for UCP-1 expression, but other pathways may also be involved. PGC-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and function, acts as a cold-inducible protein that controls adaptive thermogenesis in BAT, and its expression is regulated by the activation of cAMP/PKA/p38 MAPK signaling pathways (35,36). Moreover, other molecules, such as AMPK and PPARδ, have been suggested as exercise mimetics (37,38). Our data show that irisin significantly increased the levels of P-p38 MAPK and P-ERK in primary adipocytes and 3T3-L1–derived adipocytes, and this effect was abolished by inhibiting p38 and ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 5) to prevent irisin-induced UCP-1 expression. We propose that the p38 and ERK signaling pathways play a central role in the irisin-induced emergence of brown adipocytes.

Our study further suggests the possible existence of an irisin-specific receptor on the membrane of adipocytes. While more experiments are necessary to characterize and validate the existence and specificity of this receptor, our study at least provides the initial evidence for its presence.

In conclusion, recombinant irisin enhances the expression of thermogenic genes both in vitro and in vivo. It stimulates the expression of brown cell–specific genes in adipocytes through the p38 and ERK signaling pathways. This effect is likely mediated via a specific membrane receptor on adipocytes. Our findings provide initial experimental evidence supporting the potential therapeutic use of recombinant irisin for the treatment of obesity and diabetes, as well as associated metabolic disorders.
