ObjectiveThere is an ongoing debate about the relationship between obesity and morbidity in the elderly, the clinical relevance of overweight and obesity in older patients and the need or harms of treatment. The main purpose of our study was to investigate whether a higher BMI is associated with a worse cardiovascular risk in all age groups, especially in the older ones.Subjects and DesignWe performed a retrospective evaluation of clinical data from 3926 patients who visited a medical outdoor center for diagnostic and/or therapeutic interventions in the period from January 1995 to July 2010. Patients were assigned to eight age groups of one decade from <20 years to ≥80 years.ResultsThe Body Mass Index (BMI) of our patients showed a continuous increase with increasing age with peak values in the age decade 61–70 years (26.29 ±4.42 kg/m2. This was paralleled by an increase in cardiovascular events and need for continuous medication, demonstrating peak values in the age decade 61–70 years (22.3% in the female and 24.7% in the male group). In all age decades up to 80 years the BMI values were higher in patients with events compared to those without it. multivariable linear regression analysis — including confounding variables (blood pressure, fasting glucose, HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, physical activity, smoking) — revealed for all age groups a strong positive relation of BMI and a negative relation of fat free mass (FFM) to the probability for a cardiovascular event and need for medication.ConclusionIn all age groups, the percentage of cardiovascular events was directly correlated with the BMI. Having in mind the transition to an aging society, therapeutic and preventive strategies should, therefore, include weight management strategies also for the elderly.

Obesity revealed a remarkable increase during the past decades. A high prevalence is also observed in the elderly population 1, 2. Overweight and obesity are associated with an increased risk for cardiovascular disease, diabetes, several forms of cancers, cognitive disability and overall mortality (3). Despite these strong relationships in general, there is an ongoing debate on whether the relationship between obesity and morbidity/mortality also persists in old age and to which extent it is of clinical relevance for the elderly 4, 5. Under certain conditions, such as in heart failure, overweight and obesity were found to be associated with a lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (6). Retrospective analysis indicate that there exists a U-shaped relationship between the body mass index (BMI) and the mortality of patients older than 75 years, with a high mortality rate for overweight and obese persons as well as for those with a lower BMI. This was confirmed by the Bergen Clinical Blood Pressure study, where the 20 years cumulative risk of death related to baseline BMI was U-shaped in the elderly with an increased mortality associated with overweight and obesity, but also with a low BMI (7). It should also be considered that weight loss might occur voluntary as part of a therapeutic regimen or unintentional, due to co-morbidities with adverse effects on the prognostic outcome.
Especially in the elderly population an unintentional weight loss can occur in the course of the syndrome of frailty, with a high risk for falls, disability, hospitalization and mortality (8).
Independent of age, overweight is defined as a BMI of 25.0-29.9 kg/m2 and obesity as a BMI≥30 kg/m2 (9). In elderly the relationship between BMI and the percentage of body fat changes as a consequence of the age-associated loss of body height caused by compression of vertebral bodies, the changes of body composition with a decrease in muscle mass and an increase in fat mass 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14. The age-associated height modification induces a somewhat flawed BMI increase of 1.5 kg/m2 in men and 2.5 kg/m2 in women. Being aware of this finding, the definition of overweight adapted for elderly is a BMI ≥27.0 kg/m2 (10). The validity of this age-adapted definition of overweight is supported by the fact that no increase in cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in elderly could be detected for the BMI range between 25.0 and 27.0 kg/m2 (11). It is widely assumed that body weight tends to increase with age, peaks between 50 and 59 years, and then decreases 15, 16, 17. This gradient is based on observational data. However, it remains a matter of debate whether obese patients reveal a high premature mortality and a shorter life-expectancy, compared to non-obese patients with a significantly higher chance to reach an advanced age (3). The risks of obesity in the elderly might be underestimated by a number of cofounders such as competing mortality, relatively shortened life expectancy and unintentional weight loss. It has to be emphasized that many younger obese individuals did not survive to an older age due to premature mortality, and overweight individuals, who have survived may possess longevity genes, which offer protection from an earlier mortality. In contrast to the results from observational data, longitudinal cohort studies show that body weight and BMI values decrease only slightly in older adults 17, 18, 19.
More conclusive evidence is available about the age-associated changes in body composition. Muscle mass decreases up to 40% from 20 to 70 years of age (4), fat mass increases with the predominance of abdominal fat accumulation (20), resulting in a higher prevalence of most of the metabolic and cardiovascular abnormalities contributing to the metabolic syndrome 21, 22, 23, 24. Therefore, central fat accumulation and a relative loss of fat-free mass may become relatively more important as risk markers than the body weight as defined by the BMI. In elderly, a high waist circumference as an indicator for central fat accumulation as well as a high BMI were both found to be related to a decreased physical functioning 24, 25, 26. This relationship enhances the risk for the metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular morbidity and mortality (27). Glucose values, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia and systolic blood pressure reveal a linear relationship with aging and with BMI in general 28, 29. Evidence from long-term follow-up studies suggest that the presence of overweight and of cardiovascular risk factors - especially at middle age, and not at an advanced age -is of importance for the further life-long risk for morbidity and mortality 30, 31, 32.
With respect to the controversial debate on the relevance of obesity and morbidity in old age, we performed a retrospective analysis of clinical data of a large cohort of patients assigned to eight age decades from <20 to >80 years. The main purpose of our study was to analyze the BMI in different age groups and the relation with cardiovascular events and medication - including the relevance of fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol and blood pressure values as symptoms of the metabolic syndrome 33, 34.
Between January 1995 and July 2010 a total of 3926 persons attended the medical outdoor center for diagnostic and/or therapeutic interventions. All patients underwent clinical routine controls and laboratory measurements. Laboratory tests were performed from blood samples taken in the morning after an overnight fasting period. The kind, dosage and application rate of medication was available from the prescriptions, which were noted for all patients.
Information on the BMI and on fat free mass (FFM) was collected for all patients. FFM was determined by bioimpedance (BIA) applying the OMRON BF 302 body fat monitor (OM-RON Healthcare Europe B.V. JD Hoofdoorp, Netherlands). By measuring conductivity and the degree of hydration BIA allows determination of total body water and fat free mass. Fat free mass was calculated as the difference between body weight and fat mass 35, 36.
Blood pressure measurements were undertaken by a nurse or technician using a standard sphygmomanometer, with the patients resting for 5-10 min in the supine position. The blood pressure was taken as the mean of three or more readings at two separate visits.
The patients enrolled in the study revealed euthyroid function. No changes in life-style during the last 3 months were reported by the patients, and none of the patients reported about an actual weight reduction program. The level of physical activity was determined by the patient's report about the number of sports activities per week. Among all 3926 patients 1060 (around 27%) reported to be actual smokers. The smoking status was evaluated according to the cigarette consumption per week. All other patients were non-smokers for their lifetime or had stopped smoking for a period longer than a year.
Patients with acute or chronic inflammatory disease, severe renal or hepatic failure or chemotherapy were excluded from our study. Beside the clinical status, an increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein and leucocyte count were the diagnostic criteria for inflammatory disease, renal disease was defined by a serum creatinine>1.5 mg/dL, and impaired liver function by a double or higher increase of hepatic enzyme levels. Among all patients 509 (9.5%) were diagnosed as manifest diabetics.
Patients were assigned to eight age groups from <20 years to >80 years, thereby separating patients with and without events and continuous medication. The term “event” summarized all cardiovascular events, including a history of angina with typical clinical symptoms and findings in electrocardiography and/or coronary angiography, status post myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, as well as peripheral arterial disease, reported stroke or cerebral ischemia. The term “medication” summarized all kinds of continuous drug therapy including antihypertensive, lipid lowering and antidiabetic medication, as well as diuretics and antiaggregatory substances. Informed consent about the participation in the study was obtained from all patients.
Data are summarized by their means and standard deviations. (Within and between) Group comparisons were conducted by Student t-tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Further, we applied a logistic regression (logit model) to disentangle the driving forces behind the probability of a cardiovascular event and/or the need for continuous medication (47). The parameter estimates reported below are evaluated at the means of the explanatory variables. They should be interpreted as the separate percentage impact of an explanatory variable on the probability of a cardiovascular event and/or the need for a continuous medication.

When patients with and without cardiovascular event and medication were separately analyzed, a continuous increase of the BMI with increasing age and peak values in the age decade 61-70 years could be observed again in both groups (Table 3). In all age decades between 21 and 80 years the BMI values of patients with cardiovascular events were higher than in patients without it. This difference was statistically highly significant, except in the age decade 31-40 years. In patients >80 years the BMI values of patients with cardiovascular events and/or medication and without it revealed no significant difference.
In order to evaluate the influence of a weight modulation by drug therapy a further analysis of patients with cardiovascular events was performed, including (full sample) or excluding (restricted sample) patients under insulin therapy, antidepressive medication or hormone replacement therapy (Table 3). No significant difference could be observed between these 2 groups (Table 3).
A logistic regression analysis was performed in patients with cardiovascular events and/or need for continuous medication. Specifically, the dependent variable is an indicator variable with entry one (zero) if a patient had a (no) cardiovascular event and/or continuous medication. The probability of an event/medication was estimated with a logit model, including BMI, FFM, blood pressure (systolic and diastolic), fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterin, the sex of the patient, her physical activities per week and the smoking behaviour as explanatory variables. Two versions of the logit model were estimated: Model A belongs to the full population of patients, and Model B excludes patients with possible weight modulating drugs, i.e., patients under therapy with insulin, antidepressive medication and hormone replacement therapy. Patients were assigned to 4 age groups (<20, 21-40, 41-60, 61-75 years) and to an age group >75 years, corresponding to geriatric patients. Table 4 reveals that in all age decades the BMI-values were positively related to the probability for a cardiovascular event and/or need for continuous medication. This can be observed in both models A and B. FFM was negatively related to the probability for a cardiovascular event. Further, it turned out that all other variables had a varying impact on the probability for a cardiovascular event (Table 4).
In our study of a large number of 3926 patients, who were assigned to age groups from <20 to >80 years, BMI values revealed a continuous increase with age up to 80 years. In all age-groups, also in the elderly up to 80 years, patients with cardiovascular events and/or with a need for continuous medication revealed higher BMI values as compared to those without events/medication. This observation suggests that overweight is related to a higher morbidity not only in the younger population, but also in the elderly 1, 2, 38, 39.
The BMI values and event rate revealed peak levels in the age decade 61-70 years. In the oldest group comprising patients>80 years BMI values between patients with and without cardiovascular events/medication showed no difference. This supports previous findings about a lower relevance of overweight in the geriatric patients. The existence of a so-called “reverse metabolic syndrome” was discussed recently, summarizing the clinical symptoms of unintended weight loss caused by malnutrition and/or chronic disorders, a low BMI, a low diastolic blood pressure and high insulin sensitivity. This state is predictive for a high mortality risk with a worse outcome when body weight is low (40). Frailty - a geriatric syndrome associated with a high risk for mortality -reveals a U-shaped pattern in its relationship to BMI and body fat, with the lowest risk in the normal BMI range of 25.0-19.9 kg/m2 (40). Because of the fact that the older patients included in our study were community-dwelling active elderly people, without pronounced cognitive or functional impairments, an evaluation of our data about the association of BMI and FFM to frailty could not be performed. This is a limitation of our study. A further limitation lies in the fact that measurements of waist circumference were not available in a representative number of our patients, so that we cannot relate the cardiovascular risk to abdominal obesity. Senescence is characterized by a loss of subcutaneous and gain of visceral fat mass, and thus in an increased risk for a metabolic syndrome 38, 39, 41, 42. However, the diagnostic criteria for the metabolic syndrome, including waist-circumference, have not yet been adapted adequately to age-related changes in body composition, blood pressure values or laboratory results 27, 37, 40, 43.
We have performed a logistic regression analysis based on BMI and including classical cardiovascular risk factors as confounding variables. For this analysis all patients with cardiovascular events and/or medication were assigned to age decades <20 years, 21-40 years, 41-60 years, 61-75 years and a group >75 years focused on geriatric patients. This age assignment was chosen to obtain an adequate number of patients for a separate analysis of the total sample and after excluding patients under therapy with weight modifying medication.
The BMI values revealed in all age group >20 years a positive relationship with the probability for cardiovascular events and medication. A comparable stable negative relationship was observed for the FFM, while a varying impact on the probability for a cardiovascular event was found for the classical cardiovascular risk factors. The varying effect of classical risk factors in the different age-groups could be explained by the influence of therapeutic strategies, such as antihypertensive and lipid lowering drug therapy, but also by aging associated changes of the risk factors. HDL-cholesterol shows a tendency to decrease with age 44, 45, arterial stiffness enhances systolic hypertension, and fasting plasma glucose levels increase with increasing age (42). In the logistic regression analysis especially physical activity was negatively associated with the probability for a cardiovascular event in the elderly patients >75 years of age. This could support the importance of exercise also in the elderly 46, 47. Finally, it turned out that smoking is positively associated with the probability of cardiovascular events and/or medication, at least in the age decade 21-40 years.
Overall, our study supports the hypothesis that overweight is a risk factor not only for younger and middle-aged, but also for active elderly patients. In all age groups cardiovascular events and a need for continuous medication were related to a higher BMI and a higher percentage of body fat. With respect to the aging society, many countries that are confronted with therapeutic and preventive strategies should include weight management strategies also for the elderly.
