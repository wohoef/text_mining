Healthcare has gradually shifted toward treating patients as active partners rather than passive recipients of care. This shift is particularly visible in cancer treatment, where understanding what patients actually experience can dramatically improve the quality of care delivered. Two specific tools have emerged as central to this effort: patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs). Together, these belong to a broader family known as patient-centered measures, or PCMs.

The distinction between these tools is straightforward but important. PROMs capture standardized information directly from patients about their health status, things like symptoms, daily functioning, mental wellbeing, and overall quality of life. They work like measurement instruments, recording how patients feel at specific points in time without any filtering or interpretation by clinicians. PREMs, by contrast, focus on the experience of receiving care itself: whether patients felt satisfied, how they perceived their interactions with providers, and what their journey through treatment was actually like.

PROMs come in two flavors. Generic versions like the EQ-5D apply across virtually any health condition and measure broad dimensions such as mobility, self-care, and emotional state. Specific versions zero in on particular conditions or concepts, with the EORTC QLQ-C30 being a widely used example designed specifically for cancer patients. The original EQ-5D used three severity levels per dimension, but the 2010 revision expanded this to five levels to improve sensitivity and address ceiling effects. For cancer alone, researchers have developed and validated more than 300 different PROMs, including specialized modules layered on top of the EORTC core questionnaire for specific cancers like lung or head and neck.

Regulatory bodies including the FDA in the United States and the Haute Autorité de Santé in France have begun incorporating these patient-centered measures into their quality and safety frameworks, encouraging their use in both clinical trials and routine practice. However, no universal naming convention exists for these instruments, which creates a real practical problem: identifying when and where they are actually being used in research becomes surprisingly difficult.

The authors wondered whether artificial intelligence could solve this identification problem more effectively than traditional methods. Specifically, they wanted to estimate how often oncology clinical studies actually include PROMs or PREMs, and they wanted to compare two different ways of finding out: a conventional approach relying on expert-curated keyword lists, and an AI-enriched approach using modern language models.

The team performed a retrospective cross-sectional analysis using ClinicalTrials.gov, the publicly accessible registry of clinical research. They focused on oncology studies with start dates between January 2012 and January 2022. Studies needed at least one described outcome to qualify; those without any outcome description were excluded. When studies had multiple outcomes, each was extracted individually for analysis.

Two parallel identification methods were developed. The expert-based approach searched outcome descriptions for matches against a curated list combining 346 oncology-specific PROMs and PREMs drawn from the PROQOLID database, plus eleven generic terms agreed upon by expert consensus. These generic terms included phrases like "quality of life," "patient-reported outcome," "satisfaction," and "experience."

The AI-enriched approach used a BERT model, a type of language model that converts text into numerical representations capturing meaning and context. The team trained this model on 2,399 outcomes that human experts had manually labeled, validated it on 479 more, and tested it on 1,065. They tuned the model by experimenting with different batch sizes and learning rates, ultimately selecting the version with the best accuracy.

To create a gold standard for evaluation, sixteen independent experts were divided into eight pairs and asked to manually label a random subset of outcomes. Each expert worked blind to their partner's decisions and to the algorithm's output. After initial labeling, partners were unblinded and worked through any disagreements. The initial agreement rate was 59 percent, reflecting the genuine difficulty of the task, but the consensus process eventually produced fully agreed-upon labels grounded in additional literature review.

A separate model called SKEP'IA, built on named entity recognition principles, was developed to identify which specific PROMs and PREMs appeared in the texts. This involved manually annotating over 2,500 outcomes, applying BIO tagging to mark entity boundaries, and fine-tuning a BERT-cased model with hyperparameter optimization. To address inconsistent naming, results from SKEP'IA were cross-referenced against the PROQOLID database using text similarity matching.

For categorizing cancer types, which are entered as free text in ClinicalTrials.gov and consequently appear under many different names for the same condition, the team used k-means clustering followed by a few-shot classification model that achieved 96 percent accuracy.

Statistical analyses included descriptive characterization, year-over-year trend analysis, and multivariate logistic regression to identify factors associated with PROM and PREM use, stratified by whether studies were interventional or observational.

The search yielded 28,034 oncology studies, of which 24,491 had at least one described outcome and entered the analysis. The expert-based algorithm flagged 7,549 studies (31 percent) as containing at least one PROM or PREM. The AI-enriched algorithm identified slightly more: 8,029 studies, or 33 percent.

The performance gap between methods was meaningful. The expert-based approach achieved 83 percent accuracy, 86 percent sensitivity, and 81 percent specificity when measured against the human consensus gold standard. The AI-enriched approach reached 90 percent accuracy, 91 percent sensitivity, and 89 percent specificity. The improvement was statistically significant.

Use of these measures has grown over the decade studied, rising from 27.6 percent of studies in 2012 to 36.2 percent in 2021.

Looking at study characteristics, interventional trials dominated the dataset (79 percent), with phase 2 being the most common stage. Among studies using PROMs or PREMs, parallel-assignment designs and randomized allocation were particularly common. Treatment was the most frequent primary purpose overall, but supportive care studies showed especially high odds of including patient-reported measures (odds ratio 4.1). Compared to preclinical or early phase 1 work, later-stage trials were substantially more likely to include these measures, with phase 3 showing the strongest association (odds ratio 3.6).

Among observational studies, prospective and cross-sectional designs incorporated PROMs and PREMs far more often than retrospective ones, with odds ratios of 3.2 and 4.6 respectively. Cohort studies showed higher odds than case-control designs.

Cancer site mattered too. Breast and digestive cancers together accounted for roughly 41 percent of all studies using these measures. Yet paradoxically, when adjusted for other factors, organ-specific studies in several common cancer types showed lower odds of using PROMs or PREMs compared to non-organ-specific research, suggesting these measures might be underutilized in some specialty areas relative to general oncology research.

The EORTC QLQ-C30 emerged as the most popular instrument, appearing in about a third of studies that used any PROM or PREM. The FACT-G followed at 15 percent and the EQ-5D-5L at roughly 12 percent. When the team categorized what these instruments measured, care-related satisfaction was the most common focus, followed by organ-specific quality of life and depression or anxiety scales.

The study delivers a clear practical message: AI substantially outperforms traditional keyword-based methods for identifying patient-reported measures in clinical trial registries, and it does so vastly faster. The manual review of 4,770 outcomes (covering 890 studies) required several weeks of effort from sixteen highly skilled reviewers. The algorithm processed all 24,491 studies in minutes to a few hours depending on available computing power. While building the AI tool required significant upfront investment, the long-term efficiency gains are substantial.

The growing use of PROMs and PREMs in later-phase trials likely reflects converging pressures from regulators, sponsors, patient advocacy groups, and the broader value-based healthcare movement. Although neither the FDA nor European agencies currently mandate these measures, their inclusion in quality frameworks creates strong incentives.

The concentration of PROM and PREM use in breast and digestive cancer research probably reflects the high prevalence of these conditions, their substantial impact on quality of life, and the well-organized advocacy and research funding ecosystems that have grown around them. This visibility has helped these areas integrate value-based healthcare principles, though it also points to gaps in less-studied cancer types where patient-centered measurement remains underdeveloped.

The authors are forthright about constraints on their findings. The AI tool sometimes mishandles multi-word instrument names, breaking up compound terms like "Short Form 36 Health Survey" rather than recognizing the unified SF-36 instrument. All-caps abbreviations occasionally trip up the model in the opposite direction, with general clinical terms like "PAIN" being misclassified as instrument names.

The fundamental challenge underlying both approaches is the lack of standardized naming for PROMs and PREMs in ClinicalTrials.gov, where the same instrument can appear under many different labels. The dataset is also limited to English-language content, does not involve full-text examination of trial documents, and was not externally validated against other databases or non-oncology fields. The AI approach was not benchmarked against alternative machine learning methods, leaving room for future comparisons.

The performance metrics reported depend partly on the prevalence of PROMs and PREMs in the dataset (roughly one-third of studies), so the algorithm's behavior in domains where these measures are rarer or more inconsistently reported remains an open question.

Despite these caveats, the study demonstrates that combining expert human judgment with AI scaling can transform how researchers monitor patient-centered measurement in clinical research. The approach could readily extend beyond oncology to other medical fields and to other databases, supporting better trial design, more informed regulatory decisions, and more effective tracking of how thoroughly research actually integrates patient voices. The authors suggest that an additional AI tool dedicated to standardizing PROM and PREM naming and classification could further reduce the noise that currently complicates this work, ultimately strengthening the foundation for value-based, patient-centered care.
